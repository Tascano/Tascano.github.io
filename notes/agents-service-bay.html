Four Agents Walk Into a Service Bay

or: why the most important agent is a state machine

A customer books a service appointment, gets a link, and a team of AI agents turns "it makes a noise sometimes" into something a technician can act on before the car even arrives. Here's how I'd build that to be fast, scale on a Monday morning, survive a model outage, and stay visible the whole time, mostly in pictures.

Ambrose TuscanoOctober 20268 min read
Before we start: this is not how it is at Nissan

At Nissan I designed and shipped Tell Me More, a multi-agent intake chatbot for dealership service appointments. This post is not that system. It's my own recreation of the idea, expanded with what I'd build next, in my own words. Every design, name, number and line of pseudocode here is mine and illustrative; none of it is Nissan's architecture, data, prompts or code.

the intake

The short version, for whichever hat you're wearing

  • One agent talks; three work as its tools, and a plain state machine decides what happens next.
  • Every turn has a deadline, tools run in parallel, and the reply streams.
  • When the model fails, the conversation doesn't: retry, another region, a smaller model, then scripted questions.
  • Agents as tools, not a group chat: one caller, typed outputs, schema-checked JSON.
  • Save state every turn, keyed by turn ID, so a retry or a reconnect never duplicates or loses anything.
  • Fan out tool calls with a deadline; a slow tool is skipped and flagged, never waited on.
  • Separate the conversation from the workflow: agents for language, a state machine for control, Step Functions for hand-off.
  • Admission control in front of the model: a token bucket and a queue that favours live conversations.
  • A failover ladder, ending in a deterministic script that needs no model at all.
  • The LLM is the least reliable component; design as if it will be slow, wrong or gone.
  • Quality is a metric: rubric scores per dimension sit on the same dashboard as p99.
  • Prompts are code: versioned, tested against golden conversations, shipped by canary, rolled back by alias.
  • Plan for Monday 9 am: quotas are a capacity plan, like hosts.
  • Page on symptoms: p99 turn time, errors, cost per session, completion rate.
  • Every prompt change gets a golden-set run and a canary, with a one-command rollback.
  • The product is a better first visit: the technician knows what, where, when and how to reproduce before the car arrives.
  • Safety concerns jump the queue, and the agent never guesses a diagnosis.
  • Success is completed, confirmed summaries, in few turns, that a technician can act on without calling the customer.

01

"It makes a noise sometimes"

That sentence is where most service visits start, and a technician can't do much with it. They need what, where, when, how to reproduce it, and whether it's safe to drive. Usually they get that by calling the customer, after the car is already on the lift.

The idea: when a customer books an appointment, the dealership sends a personal link, and a short conversation collects everything before the visit. It sounds like a chatbot. The engineering is everything around the chatbot.

    03reliability

    Four agents, one conductor

    There are two ways to build "multi-agent". In a group chat, agents talk to each other freely: flexible, and almost impossible to reason about. I'd build the other way: one agent talks to the customer, and the other three are its tools, each with one job and a typed output.

    That split is the reliability story in miniature. The model can phrase a question beautifully and still be wrong about whether we're done, so "done" is decided by code: the Rubric's typed output, checked against a schema, moves a concern from intake to summary.

      One turn, against the clock

      A conversation feels broken after a few seconds of silence. One turn touches a guardrail, the agent and up to three tools, and the order you do that in decides how long the customer waits. Same work, three schedules:

      turn · pseudocode
      async def handle_turn(session, message):
    message = guardrails.check(redact_pii(message))      # nothing raw reaches a model
    state = await sessions.load(session.id)              # DynamoDB: the source of truth
    if state.already_answered(message.turn_id):          # a retried or duplicate turn
        return state.last_reply
    concern = state.queue.current()                      # highest-priority open concern
    fields, history, dealer = await gather_with_deadline(
        rubric.extract(concern, message),                # what did that answer tell us?
        vehicle_history.lookup(state.vin),               # past repairs, cached per VIN
        dealership.check(state.vin, concern),            # warranty, parts, advisories
        deadline=2.0)                                    # a slow tool is skipped, not waited on
    concern.merge(fields)
    reply = await conversation.next_turn(concern, history, dealer)   # streams to the browser
    await sessions.save(state, turn_id=message.turn_id)  # saved every turn: resumable
    return reply

      Three rules fall out of that picture. Fan out: tools that don't depend on each other run at once. Deadlines: a tool that misses its two seconds is skipped and flagged, not waited on. Stream: the first words reach the customer while the rest is still being written. And every turn is saved before it's acknowledged, keyed by its turn ID, so a retried message can never produce two answers.

      08scale

      Monday, 9 am

      Appointments cluster, so links get opened in waves, and every model has a quota on requests and tokens per minute. Quotas are a capacity plan, exactly like hosts. Without admission control, the wave overshoots the quota, calls get throttled, retries pile on, and live conversations fail. Drag the wave:

      Interactive · a morning of model calls against the quota
      calls wanted per minutequotathrottled (errors)

      admit · pseudocode
      def admit(call, bucket, queue):
    if bucket.take(call.tokens):          # under quota: go now
        return run(call)
    if call.session.is_active:            # mid-conversation beats new arrivals
        return queue.push_front(call)
    if queue.wait_seconds() < 20:         # a short wait is better than an error
        return queue.push(call)
    return offer_callback(call.session)   # shed gracefully: "we'll text you when it's quiet"

      A token bucket spends the quota smoothly, a queue absorbs the burst, live conversations go to the front, and when the wait gets long, new sessions are shed gracefully with a "we'll text you" instead of an error. The chat service itself is the easy part: stateless containers on ECS, scaling on open connections.

      09reliabilityfailover

      When the model says no

      The language model is the least reliable part of this system: it gets throttled, it times out, and now and then it returns JSON that isn't. Design as if it will be slow, wrong or gone. Break something:

      Animation · break something
      1. Pick a failure above.

      Every failure lands somewhere on one ladder. Each rung is slower or plainer than the one above, and the bottom rung needs no model at all:

      ladder · pseudocode
      FAILOVER = [
    retry_with_jitter(attempts=2),          # most throttles clear in a second
    cross_region_inference(),               # same model, another region's capacity
    smaller_model(),                        # faster and cheaper; still good at intake
    scripted_questions(),                   # deterministic: the rubric's questions, in order
    text_me_later(),                        # save progress, send the link again
]

      Cross-region inference handles the common case, a busy region, with the same model. Session state in a DynamoDB global table means a customer can reopen their link anywhere and pick up at the same question. And the hand-off to the dealership is a Step Functions workflow with retries and a catch, so a confirmed summary is never lost in transit.

      Quality is a metric too

      For an agent, "up" isn't enough. It can be fast, error-free and still asking terrible questions. So the dashboard puts quality next to latency: completion rate, turns per concern, fields per question, and the rubric's own scores. Ship a bad prompt and watch:

      Every turn is a trace: guardrail, agent, each tool, with timings and token counts, through AgentCore observability into CloudWatch. Alarms fire on symptoms: p99 turn time, error rate, cost per session, and completion rate, because a prompt regression shows up there first.

      13

      Ten decisions, one page

      The options I'd weigh, the one I'd pick, and why. All of them are my calls for this recreation.

      1. How do the agents work together?
        group chatone big agentone talker, three tools

        One caller and typed outputs keep every turn explainable. Cost: the talker is a bottleneck you must keep fast.

      2. Who decides what happens next?
        the modela state machine

        "Confirmed" and "done" are facts, not opinions. Cost: new flows need code, not just a prompt.

      3. How do messages travel?
        pollingserver-sent eventsWebSocket

        Two-way and streaming, managed by API Gateway. Cost: idle timeouts and reconnects to handle.

      4. Where does session state live?
        in memorya relational databaseDynamoDB, saved every turn

        Key-value by session, global tables for failover. Cost: a write on every turn.

      5. How do tools run?
        one after anotherin parallel, with a deadline

        The turn takes as long as the slowest tool, capped. Cost: sometimes a summary without history.

      6. What does the model remember?
        the full transcriptfacts + recent turns, tiered

        Smaller prompts are faster and cheaper. Cost: extraction has to be right.

      7. What happens at the quota?
        let it throttlebuy more quotatoken bucket, queue, shed

        Live conversations never fail because of new ones. Cost: some customers wait, or get a text later.

      8. What if the model is down?
        show an errorwait it outthe failover ladder

        The bottom rung needs no model at all. Cost: scripted questions feel plainer.

      9. How does a summary reach the dealership?
        call it inlineStep Functions + retries + DLQ

        A slow dealership system never blocks a customer. Cost: delivery is asynchronous.

      10. How do prompts ship?
        edit in placemanual spot checksgolden set, judge, canary, alias

        A regression stops before a customer sees it. Cost: a golden set to curate and keep fresh.

      14

      The well-earned drink

      Every Brew Miles trip ends with a well-earned drink. Here's this one's:

      1. Let models talk and let code decide; the most important agent is a state machine.
      2. Fan out with deadlines, stream the reply, and save every turn by its ID.
      3. Treat quota like capacity: a token bucket, a queue, and a graceful "later".
      4. Build a failover ladder whose last rung needs no model.
      5. Put quality on the dashboard, and ship prompts like code.
