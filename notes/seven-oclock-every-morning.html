Seven O'Clock, Every Morning

or: building a daily scripture service, from docker compose to AWS

Prefer to watch? Every animation in this post, narrated in about 6.5 minutes, with captions built in. The narration is a local AI clone of my voice.

Daily Bible sends every subscriber the day's reading, a saint's quote, an AI image and a video, in English, Hindi, Marathi or Spanish, on WhatsApp or email, at 7 AM their time. Here's how I built it as microservices, tested it all on my laptop, then moved it to AWS. Everything you need to rebuild it is in the diagrams.

Ambrose TuscanoNovember 202618 min read
What this is

My own project, designed and built end to end. Latency, throughput and cost figures come from local load tests or are illustrative estimates, and are labelled that way.

The short version, for whichever hat you're wearing

  • Make the day's content once, send it many times. Generation is a fixed daily cost; delivery is the only part that grows.
  • Every step can fail and the morning still happens: retries, fallbacks, and a ladder that ends in plain text.
  • Built and tested on a laptop first, then each piece swapped for its AWS twin.
  • 7 AM depends on almost nothing: a frozen list, a queue and the provider. The database, AI and payments can all be down at 7:00.
  • One package per date, written with a conditional put: reruns are free, double-runs are impossible.
  • Outbox + dedupe key (subscriber, date, channel): a crash never sends twice.
  • Scripture is never generated: it's read from a stored, versioned text; models only write around it.
  • Stripe events are hints: stored by event id, then the subscription is re-fetched; a nightly reconciliation catches the rest.
  • Small services behind queues, each with one job, its own data and its own failure budget.
  • Same contracts locally and in the cloud: LocalStack, MinIO and Mailpit stand in for SQS, S3 and SES.
  • Per-channel pacing and circuit breakers, so a slow WhatsApp never delays email.
  • A lane per tenant with fair share, codes on their own lane, and a pilot-light second region instead of a costly warm one.
  • The promise is "7 AM, every day": on-time delivery rate is the headline metric.
  • Four languages, one review gate, with a native-speaker check before any language goes live.
  • Cost is mostly messaging, not AI; the slider in section 12 shows where it goes, and nine levers keep it low.
  • Every failure has an owner and a price: section 11·b shows what each one costs to protect against.

01·dscalesecurity

A thousand sign-ups a minute

A parish shares the link after Sunday Mass, or a creator mentions it in a video, and sign-ups arrive in a wall. The sign-up itself is easy to scale. The trap is the verification code: it has to reach a phone through the same rate-limited providers as the morning messages.

Interactive · a sign-up rush: 400 a second for two minutes

Pick a design.

illustrative: 400 sign-ups a second for two minutes, then 30; a shared sender limit of 80 a second

How I built it so a rush is boring:

  1. The page can't fall over. The sign-up page is static files on S3 behind CloudFront. A million visits cost almost nothing and need no servers.
  2. The request does one thing. POST /signup runs on API Gateway and Lambda. It checks the bot token, normalises the contact (lower-case email, phone in E.164), does one conditional write keyed by tenant + hash(contact), and returns 202. A double tap gets the same answer, not a second row. Everything slow happens later, off a queue.
  3. Bots never cost me a message. A WAF rate rule per IP, a bot check before any code is sent, at most 3 codes per number an hour, and a daily spending cap per country. If the share of codes that get entered drops below about 60%, an alarm fires: that's what SMS/WhatsApp pumping fraud looks like.
  4. Codes are short-lived and stored hashed: 6 digits, 10 minutes counted from when the code is sent (not requested), 5 attempts, then a new code.
  5. Codes get their own lane. A separate queue and a separate WhatsApp number using authentication templates, with its own throughput. A sign-up rush never slows the 7 AM wave, and the 7 AM wave never delays a code. Email verification links go through SES, which is quick and cheap.
  6. What it costs: idle, close to zero (Lambda and on-demand DynamoDB bill per request). A rush costs cents, plus the messages themselves.

    06·bspeedscale

    Exactly 7:00:00, for a million people?

    "Exactly 7 AM" is a promise about throughput, not just a timer. A million messages at 80 a second takes three and a half hours. Precision comes from three things: everything rendered and queued before the moment, clocks that agree, and enough parallel senders (and provider capacity) to drain the wave in seconds.

    Interactive · how long one time zone's wave takes

    Diagram · one time zone's wave, minute by minute

    How each zone hits 7:00:00, step by step:

    1. T−60 min, freeze. A wave planner (a Lambda) reads the zone's entitled, unsuppressed subscribers from a read replica and streams them to S3 as shards of 5,000 lines. Each line is a ready-to-send request: template variables filled in, a signed unsubscribe link, and the idempotency key. Changes made after the freeze apply tomorrow, except unsubscribes, which senders check again just before sending.
    2. T−15 min, scale up. A schedule sets the sender service's task count for this zone (shards ÷ shards per task). EventBridge Scheduler is only accurate to about a minute, so it starts the wave, never the send.
    3. T−5 min, warm. Each task claims shards with a conditional write, loads them into memory, opens keep-alive HTTP/2 connections to the provider, fetches tokens, and checks its clock against Amazon Time Sync. An offset over 20 ms raises an alarm.
    4. T−50 ms, wait precisely. Tasks sleep until 50 ms before the moment, then spin on the monotonic clock to 7:00:00.000 and start together. Nothing is computed now; the requests are already built.
    5. T, fire. Token buckets add up to just under the provider's limit, so there are no 429s. Each shard is shuffled every day, so nobody is always last.
    6. T+N s, drain and measure. Every send is logged with its timestamp. Dispatch p50 and p99 against 7:00 go to the dashboard, per zone.
    7. T+10 min, sweep. Anyone expected but not delivered is sent again with the same key (09·c).

    Honest fine print: "to the millisecond" holds for when we dispatch. Arrival on a phone takes a few seconds more, decided by WhatsApp, mail servers and the network. So the promise I measure is "99% delivered within N seconds of 7:00", where N = subscribers ÷ throughput. If N creeps up, I buy throughput (a higher WhatsApp tier, a bigger SES quota, more shards) before it matters. WhatsApp also caps how many different people a number can message per day, so I raise that tier ahead of sign-ups.

    From my work · Amazon Ads

    I remember chasing a 10% request drop that no error log explained. The cause was autoscaling policies reacting a step behind the traffic. Ever since, I don't let a known spike wait for autoscaling, which is why senders scale up on a schedule at T−15, not when the queue fills.

    07·bfailoverreliability

    Bounces, complaints and failed messages

    Sending isn't the end. Every provider reports back: delivered, bounced, complained, failed, throttled. Ignore those reports and SES pauses your account, or WhatsApp lowers your limits. Every report lands on one feedback queue, and each kind has exactly one rule.

    Animation · what happens after "send"
    1. Pick an outcome.
    1. Wire it once. An SES configuration set publishes bounce, complaint, delivery and reject events to SNS, then SQS. WhatsApp status webhooks (sent, delivered, read, failed) are checked by signature and land on the same queue. One small worker applies the rules.
    2. Never retry a permanent failure. It only damages reputation. Suppress the address at once, in my table and in SES's account-level suppression list.
    3. Keep the two numbers that matter low: bounces well under 5%, complaints under 0.1%. Both have alarms at half those values. Double opt-in and one-click unsubscribe are what keep them low.
    4. The domain is set up properly: SPF, DKIM, DMARC, a custom MAIL FROM, and a slow warm-up for a new domain or IP.
    5. Recycled phone numbers: numbers get reassigned. I ask for a one-tap reconfirmation every 12 months, and a long run of failures on a number ends that subscription instead of messaging a stranger.

    09·breliability

    Payment events lie about time

    Stripe's webhooks can arrive late, twice, or in a different order from the events themselves, and our endpoint can be down when they're sent. So we never trust the order, never trust a single delivery, and never trust ourselves to have caught everything.

    Animation · three ways a payment event goes wrong
    1. Pick a problem.

    The payment side, end to end:

    1. Trials without a card. Stripe Checkout creates a subscription with a 14-day trial, collecting a payment method only if required, and set to pause if the trial ends with no card. Mornings stop; nothing is charged by surprise.
    2. One trial per person, forever. Before a trial starts, I look up hash(normalised email or phone) in trials_used. That hash outlives account deletion, so delete-and-re-sign-up doesn't reset the trial, and it isn't personal data I can read back.
    3. A reminder before the trial ends. Stripe sends customer.subscription.trial_will_end three days before; the subscriber gets one message in their language and channel, with a link to add a card.
    4. The webhook inbox is tiny on purpose. Verify the signature, insert into stripe_events (primary key: event id), reply 200 within milliseconds. A queue does the work, so a slow database never makes Stripe retry.
    5. Events are hints; the API is the truth. The worker fetches the subscription and maps its status to one thing: may we send? trialing and active: yes. past_due: yes, during a 7-day grace period. paused, unpaid, canceled: no. The row is updated only if the fetched copy is newer than the stored one.
    6. Every call to Stripe carries an idempotency key, such as checkout:sub_8f2c:2026-11-05, so a retried request never creates two subscriptions or two charges.
    7. Failed renewals: Stripe's Smart Retries plus card-updater, and a message after the first failure. Mornings continue during the grace period, then pause. They don't get deleted.
    8. Nightly reconciliation lists every subscription Stripe changed in the last 48 hours and corrects any that differ. If it fixes anything, an alarm fires, because something upstream is wrong.
    9. Who may receive tomorrow's message is decided once, at the T−60 freeze, from that one field.
    the tables that keep payments and sends safe
    subscribers   (id, tenant_id, contact_hash, lang, channel, zone,
               status, may_send, stripe_sub_id, fetched_at)
trials_used   (contact_hash PRIMARY KEY, first_trial_at)       -- survives erasure
stripe_events (event_id PRIMARY KEY, type, received_at, processed_at)
deliveries    (key PRIMARY KEY, status, provider_msg_id, sent_at, ttl)
              -- key = tenant:subscriber:date:channel, TTL 30 days
suppressions  (contact_hash, channel, reason, at)              -- bounces, complaints, STOP

    09·dscale

    Many tenants, one pipeline

    Parishes, schools and ministries sign up whole communities under their own name. One tenant must never slow down, break or see another. The classic failure: a parish with 200,000 members imports first, and every small parish waits behind it.

    Interactive · one zone: a 200,000-member parish, 50 small parishes, 30,000 individuals

    illustrative: 1,000 messages a second in total; seconds after 7:00 until the last member of each group is sent

    1. A tenant id on everything: every row, every partition key, every message key (tenant:subscriber:date:channel), every log line and cost tag.
    2. A tenant is configuration, not code: its name and branding, its calendar, its languages and channels, its quota, and optionally its own WhatsApp number (with its own throughput and quality rating, so its mistakes stay its own).
    3. Members still opt in themselves. A parish shares a link or QR code carrying its tenant code. A CSV import sends invitations, never subscriptions.
    4. Fair sending: fan-out writes a lane per tenant per zone. Senders take from the lanes round-robin, and a lane that empties gives its share to the rest. Small tenants finish in seconds; the big one finishes when it would have anyway.
    5. Content is still made once: packages are keyed by date × calendar × language, so ten tenants on the same calendar share one package.
    6. Billing: the tenant is one Stripe customer on a per-seat subscription, updated nightly from its active member count. Members pay nothing.
    7. Blast radius: per-tenant API rate limits, per-tenant quotas, and a bad import or a paused template fails only that tenant's lane.

    12scale

    Where the money goes

    Interactive · monthly cost as subscribers grow

    illustrative unit costs; half the subscribers on WhatsApp, half on email

    How I keep the bill small without making it fragile:

    1. Make content once. The biggest saving by far (04·b): AI cost doesn't grow with subscribers.
    2. Nothing runs while it isn't needed. Senders exist only during waves, scaled from zero by the zone schedules. The pipeline runs on Fargate Spot (up to 70% cheaper; an interruption is just a retry from the last checkpoint) on ARM (Graviton).
    3. Low-traffic APIs are serverless. Sign-up, settings, webhooks and unsubscribe run on API Gateway and Lambda: close to free at this size, and they scale on their own.
    4. Never attach media. Messages carry links to S3 behind CloudFront (the first terabyte a month is free); homilies link to where they're hosted.
    5. Email is the cheap channel. SES costs $0.10 per 1,000 emails; a WhatsApp template message costs tens to hundreds of times more, depending on the country. Both are offered; the price page says so honestly.
    6. No NAT gateway. Free gateway endpoints for S3 and DynamoDB; senders run in public subnets with no inbound access, so they don't pay roughly $30+ a month per NAT plus data.
    7. Data expires. The delivery log has a 30-day TTL (deletes are free), old packages move to cheaper S3 storage after 90 days, and logs are kept 14 days.
    8. Pay for safety where it's cheap. Replicating packages to a second region costs pennies; a fully warm second region would roughly double the bill. So I keep a pilot light (11·b) and accept a slower recovery.
    9. Watch the bill like an SLO. AWS Budgets alarms, a cost tag on every service and tenant, and a monthly cost-per-subscriber figure.
    From my work · Microsoft · Azure Resource Graph

    I worked on a five-region scale-out that roughly doubled processing capacity with no extra cloud spend. The cheapest capacity is the capacity you're already paying for. Here, that means senders that run only during waves, and content made once.

    13

    Rebuild it yourself

    1. Load the Bible once. Public-domain text per language into a verses table; a liturgical calendar maps each date to a reading.
    2. Write each service against an interface: Queue, Blob, KV, Mailer, WhatsApp, LLM. One local and one AWS implementation each.
    3. Run the stack with compose, models in fixture mode, then real calls. Mailpit shows every email; the WhatsApp mock can return 429s on demand.
    4. Test the failures, not just the path: kill containers mid-run, fire the scheduler twice, make the mock throttle.
    5. Port one piece at a time: containers to ECS Fargate, LocalStack to SQS/SNS/DynamoDB, MinIO to S3, Postgres to Aurora, Mailpit to SES, the mock to the WhatsApp Cloud API, all in CDK.
    6. Add the safety nets before the users: the feedback worker (bounces, complaints, WhatsApp statuses), the Stripe inbox and nightly reconciliation, the sweeper ten minutes after each wave, and canary inboxes.
    7. Add the wave machinery: a planner that freezes each zone at T−60 into shards in S3, a schedule that scales senders at T−15, and a watchdog at T−10.
    8. Load-test the waves locally: a million fake subscribers, a mock provider at its real limit, and a stopwatch on dispatch p99.
    9. Canary first: one time zone and one language for a week, watching the on-time rate, then everyone.

    Decisions, one page

    1. One package or one per subscriber?
      per subscriberone per date

      AI cost stays flat. Cost: no personalisation yet.

    2. Monolith or services?
      one appsmall services + queues

      A slow renderer can't stall sending. Cost: more to deploy.

    3. Where does scripture come from?
      the modela stored, versioned text

      Never misquote the Bible. Cost: one-time loading.

    4. How do sends get timed?
      delayed queue messagesa release schedule per time zone

      Queues only delay 15 minutes; schedules can wait hours. Cost: ~40 schedules to manage.

    5. How do retries stay safe?
      hopededupe key + idempotency key

      Crashes never double-send. Cost: a write per message.

    6. What if there's no video?
      skip itthe ladder

      Something always arrives. Cost: a renderer to run.

    7. What does 7 AM depend on?
      the whole stacka frozen shard, a queue, a provider

      The database, AI and Stripe can all be down at 7:00. Cost: changes after T−60 wait a day.

    8. Verification codes?
      share the senderstheir own lane and number

      A rush never touches the morning. Cost: a second number to manage.

    9. Tenants?
      one queuea lane each, fair share

      No parish waits behind another. Cost: more queues to watch.

      warm standbypilot light

      Pennies, not double. Cost: ~30 minutes to recover, rare duplicates.

    15

    The well-earned drink

    Every Brew Miles trip ends with one:

    1. Make it once, send it many times.
    2. Claim the date, checkpoint every stage, and reruns become free.
    3. Key every message, pace every channel, and keep a breaker per provider.
    4. Build a ladder whose last rung is plain text.
    5. Freeze before you fire: at 7:00, nothing is computed, only sent.
    6. Test it on a laptop, failures and all, then port one piece at a time.
