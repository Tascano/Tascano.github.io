First in Line

or: the job hunter I built for myself, running on my laptop

It started with one wish: be among the first people to apply when a role opens. First I built a small site to find roles fast and apply by hand. Then, one piece at a time, I handed the rest to AI. Today it's JobHunt: I type one command on my laptop, and an agent finds new roles across ATS feeds, job boards and search engines, makes sure I never apply to the same one twice, scores them against my résumé, fills in applications in my own browser, reads replies in its own inbox, and finds the person to message. This is what it does, how, and why.

Ambrose TuscanoNovember 202623 min read
What this is

A personal tool I built for my own job search. It runs on my laptop, for me. The first piece, a fast search site, is still public at jobsfinder.streamlit.app. Counts and costs come from my own runs or are illustrative, and are labelled that way.

The short version, for whichever hat you're wearing

  • One command, nothing manual: I type jobhunt run, and an agent finds, filters, applies, tracks and reaches out, end to end.
  • Never the same job twice: three small SQLite stores remember what I've seen, what I've applied to, and what today's run already found.
  • It has its own inbox: a separate email address for applying and tracking, so it can read confirmations and replies without ever touching my personal mail.
  • It plays fair: answers only from my profile, caps itself so no employer gets flooded, and simply skips Workday marathons and CAPTCHAs.
  • Dedupe on two keys: the source's own job ID, and a fingerprint of company, title and location, because one job appears on five sites with five IDs.
  • Known forms are filled by rules, unknown ones by computer use: the model only sees what rules can't handle, and every submit is preceded by a screenshot and followed by a check.
  • An intent row before every submit: a crash can't turn into a duplicate application.
  • Adapters per source type: official ATS feeds first (exact posting times, allowed to read), filtered search links second, career pages last.
  • Local by design: one process started from a terminal, SQLite instead of a database server, and my own browser instead of cloud browsers.
  • Secrets never in .env: API keys there, website sessions in a dedicated browser profile, any password in the OS keychain.
  • The goal is fewer, better, earlier applications, not more of them: a fit threshold, 10 a day at most, and no more than 2 roles per company a month.
  • Terms of service are a design input: public feeds first; the agent uses LinkedIn and Google only at a human pace, in my own account, and never bypasses a CAPTCHA.
  • About ten dollars a month in model calls; everything else runs free on the laptop.

    Where new roles come from

    "Search 90+ boards" sounds like one problem. It's five, because each kind of source says different things about when a job was posted, and has different rules about who may read it. I don't do any of it by hand: public feeds are read directly, and everything else the agent does in my own browser, the way I used to.

    3. Human pace, on purpose. One page at a time, pauses between them, a few dozen pages a day, all in my own signed-in browser. LinkedIn's and Google's terms prohibit automated access, so this is a risk I take with my own accounts, and the reason it stays slow and small.

    Matching
    1. Exact titles in quotes, variants with OR: ("backend engineer" OR "platform engineer" OR "software engineer II"). Without quotes, Google happily matches "backend" and "engineer" in different sentences.
    2. intitle: for the role, and only the role: titles usually say just "Software Engineer II". The focus (backend, full stack, frontend, data engineering) and the location are usually in the description. So the role family goes in intitle:, which stops a recruiter post that merely mentions "our engineers", while focus and place are matched anywhere on the page.
    3. site: per ATS, or per company: site:jobs.ashbyhq.com covers every company on Ashby; site:jobs.ashbyhq.com/acme covers one. Greenhouse, Lever, Workable, SmartRecruiters, Wellfound and the rest each get their own line.
    4. Companies on their own domains: inurl:careers and inurl:jobs catch careers pages and jobs. subdomains, and site:.jobs catches the .jobs top-level domain some employers use.
    5. Minus what you don't want: -intern -"staff engineer" -contract -clearance, and companies I've ruled out.
    6. Time in the URL: &tbs=qdr:h (past hour), qdr:d, qdr:w, or after:2026-11-01 for a custom start date. It's the date Google indexed the page, which trails the real posting time, so it finds companies; their feeds give the exact time.
    7. LinkedIn and Indeed take filters in the URL too: on LinkedIn, geoId=103644278 (United States), f_TPR=r3600 (past hour), f_WT=2 (remote), f_E=4 (mid-senior), f_JT=F (full-time), sortBy=DD (newest first). On Indeed, fromage=1 and the country's own site (ca.indeed.com for Canada).
    Interactive · using 90+ platforms without wearing any of them out

    illustrative: 45 Google site: searches, 12 LinkedIn searches, 6 Indeed searches; feeds are read every run

    1. Spread, don't hammer. Each run takes a different slice of the searches, and each search's time window matches how long since it last ran: visit Ashby's search every three days, and that visit asks for the past three days. Nothing is missed, and no site sees more than a person's worth of browsing.
    2. A budget per site per day (a few dozen LinkedIn pages, a few dozen Google searches), random pauses between pages, and nothing in parallel against one site.
    3. Feeds carry the load. Once a company's ATS feed is known, it's read directly, so the browser searches only have to discover companies, not re-find their jobs every day.
    4. When a site pushes back, the agent backs off. If Google shows its "unusual traffic" check, Google is done for the day and the rest of the rotation carries on.

    04correctness

    Never the same job twice

    Three small SQLite files, each answering one question. Splitting them keeps each one tiny, and means I can delete today's scratch data without touching my history.

    seen.db · "have I ever seen this job?"Only keys: the source's job ID and a fingerprint, with when I first saw it. Kept forever; a few MB after a year.
    applications.db · "what did I do about it?"Every application: résumé used, answers given, mode, status, screenshot, replies. The tracker reads this.
    run_cache.db · "did this run already find it?"Everything found today, so two searches that return the same role process it once. Kept 7 days, for debugging.
    why not one file?One file works too. Three make the lifetimes obvious: forever, my record, and scratch.

    the three stores
    -- seen.db: tiny, permanent
CREATE TABLE seen (source_key  TEXT PRIMARY KEY,         -- 'greenhouse:acme:4012345'
                   fingerprint TEXT NOT NULL,             -- sha1(company|title|city), normalised
                   first_seen  TEXT NOT NULL);
CREATE INDEX seen_fp ON seen(fingerprint);

-- applications.db: my record
CREATE TABLE applications (id INTEGER PRIMARY KEY, source_key TEXT UNIQUE, fingerprint TEXT,
  company TEXT, title TEXT, url TEXT, resume TEXT, mode TEXT,      -- notify | review | auto
  status TEXT,          -- queued | applying | needs_you | applied | failed | skipped | interview | offer | rejected
  answers_json TEXT, screenshot TEXT, applied_at TEXT, notes TEXT);

-- run_cache.db: scratch for one run, kept 7 days
CREATE TABLE found (run_id TEXT, source_key TEXT, fingerprint TEXT, raw_json TEXT,
                    PRIMARY KEY (run_id, source_key));

    All three run in WAL mode, and a lock file guarantees one runner at a time, so SQLite never sees two writers. The local app only reads.

    05correctnesscost

    Deciding what's worth applying to

    Cheap checks first, the model last. Rules cost nothing and throw out most roles; the model only reads what's left, and every score is cached by a hash of résumé and job description, so nothing is scored twice.

    Interactive · one morning's funnel

    illustrative: one day of new roles for a backend / cloud search

    1. Normalise first: title words (Sr → Senior, SDE II → Software Engineer II), location to a city or "remote", and the level to a number.
    2. Rules: title must match my families, the level my band, the location my list. My blocklist of companies and keywords applies here.
    3. Score: the model gets my résumé and the description, and returns a 0–1 fit with one sentence of reasons and the résumé to use. Under 0.75, it's only listed in the email.
    4. If the model is down, a keyword overlap score is used and everything goes to review instead of auto.
    From my work · Nissan

    Tell Me More, the dealership chatbot I shipped, had a Rubric agent whose only job was to judge an answer before a customer saw it. Review mode is that agent's job here, and for now it's me.

    05·bcorrectness

    Is this role really for me?

    Keyword search is generous. A role in Tokyo shows up because its description says "work with US clients and data". A recruiter role shows up because it says "hire backend engineers". But the description can't be ignored either: a title that only says "Software Engineer" hides whether it's backend, full stack or frontend, and some roles state their location only in the text. So search results are candidates, and each one is read in order: fields first, then the parts of the description that actually say what the job is and where.

    Animation · five search hits, one decision each
    1. Pick a hit.
    1. Location: fields first, then location sentences. Feeds give a location for most jobs (Greenhouse's location.name, Lever's location category, Ashby's location and address), and a small lookup turns it into a country and a remote scope. When the field is empty or just says "Remote", a small model reads the description for sentences that state where the job is ("this role is based in Austin", "US-based remote", "must reside in Canada"). A sentence that only mentions a country ("our US clients") is not a location statement. Anything still unclear is dropped, not guessed.
    2. Role family from title and department. The title must be in my engineering families, and the department too when the feed has one. "Technical Recruiter", "Sales Engineer" and "Engineering Manager, Payroll Ops" all contain the right words and all get dropped.
    3. Focus from the description. Titles hide it, so the small model reads the responsibilities and requirements (not the company boilerplate) and labels the focus: backend, full stack, frontend, data engineering, ML, mobile. My profile says which I want (backend, full stack, data engineering) and which I skip (frontend-only, mobile). Mixed roles keep their mix, and the fit score weighs it.
    4. Hard lines are a veto. "Must be located in Japan", "active clearance required" or "no sponsorship" end it, wherever else the role scores well.
    5. Every drop keeps its reason, so a wrong filter shows up in the run summary as a pattern, not a mystery.
    06reliability

    How the agent applies

    Yes, it uses computer use, but only where it has to. For the big ATS forms I've seen before, a mapping fills each field directly: fast, free and predictable. When a form is new or changes, a computer-use model looks at a screenshot and the page's accessibility tree and decides the next click or keystroke. Both run in Playwright, in a browser profile on my laptop that is signed in as me.

    2. Known forms by rules. Greenhouse, Lever and Ashby forms have stable field names; a small mapping fills them in under ten seconds with no model call.
    3. Unknown forms by computer use. The model gets the screenshot, the accessibility tree and my profile, proposes one action at a time, and every action is logged. It may only click inside the application page.
    4. Free-text questions ("why this company?", "what are you proud of?") are written from my story bank and the job, as the next section shows.
    5. Proof: a screenshot before submit, the confirmation page after, and the confirmation email matched by company. No confirmation within an hour means the tracker says "unverified", not "applied".

    06·bcorrectness

    Where its answers come from, and how it learns

    Every form asks something new: "Are you 18 or older?", "How did you hear about us?", "What are you most proud of?". The agent never invents an answer. It draws from three things I wrote once, remembers every question it has met, and asks me only when it meets something it truly can't answer. Pick a question to see what happens:

    Interactive · eight questions, eight decisions
    1. Three sources, all written by me. profile.yaml holds facts (work authorization, notice period, eligibility). My résumés hold the history. stories.yaml holds a handful of stories with real numbers, the raw material for every free-text answer.
    2. An answer bank remembers every question. Each question it meets is stored with the answer it gave. A new wording ("Do you have the right to work in the United States?") is matched to a saved one by meaning: a small model compares them, and only a confident match reuses the answer.
    3. It asks me once, then never again. A factual question with no saved answer goes on a short "questions for me" list on the end-of-day page, and that one application waits. I answer once; the answer becomes a fact; the application goes out on the next run, and so does every future form that asks.
    4. Free-text answers are written, not copied. The best model gets the job description, the company's own about or engineering page, and the one or two stories from my bank that fit the role. It writes in plain first person, within the field's limit, and every claim must trace to a story or my résumé. The first time it meets a new kind of question, the draft waits for my approval; after that it writes on its own, with my approved answers as examples.
    5. It learns from my edits. When I change a draft, the edited version becomes the new example for that kind of question. It also notes which answers went out with applications that got a reply, though with so few data points that's a hint, not a science.
    6. Some things it never learns. Date of birth, Social Security number, bank details and passport numbers are never stored and never typed. A real application almost never needs them before an offer, so a form that asks is skipped and flagged as possibly not real.
    7. "Learning" means saved answers, not training. No model is fine-tuned on my data. Everything lives in my data folder; only the text a draft needs is sent to the model API.
    A note on "agent memory": it sounds like something out of a research lab. Mine is two YAML files and a SQLite table. Sometimes "active memory" is just a database with good manners, and that's exactly why I trust it: I can open it, read it, and fix it.

    07reliabilityrespect

    Logins, secrets, and what it skips

    The rule: .env holds keys and settings, never passwords. Website sessions live in a browser profile that only the agent uses. Any password it creates goes to the operating system's keychain.

    .env.example and sites.yaml
    # .env: keys and settings only
MODEL_API_KEY=...                      # fit scores and computer use
JOBS_INBOX=ambrosetuscanojobs@gmail.com  # the agent's own address (section 07·b)
BROWSER_PROFILE=~/.jobhunt/browser     # sessions live here, not passwords
MODE=auto                              # notify | review | auto, overridable per site

# sites.yaml: what the agent may do, per site
greenhouse.io:  { account: none,        find: feed,  apply: auto }
lever.co:       { account: none,        find: feed,  apply: auto }
ashbyhq.com:    { account: none,        find: feed,  apply: auto }
myworkdayjobs:  { account: per_company, find: agent, apply: skip }   # a whole workday per application
taleo.net:      { account: per_company, find: agent, apply: skip }
icims.com:      { account: per_company, find: agent, apply: skip }
linkedin.com:   { account: mine,        find: agent, apply: via_company_site, pace: human }
google.com:     { account: none,        find: agent, pace: human }
    1. Sign in once. jobhunt setup opens the agent's browser profile, and I sign in to LinkedIn and the jobs inbox, two-factor included. Sessions are reused until they expire.
    2. Heavy sites are skipped. Workday, Taleo and iCIMS want a new account per employer and a form that runs for pages. The agent records the role as skipped, with a link, and moves on.
    3. Passwords never touch a file. The few it needs live in the operating system's keychain (macOS Keychain, Windows Credential Manager) through keyring.
    4. CAPTCHAs are skipped too. They're never solved or bypassed. Because it's my real browser on my home connection, they're rare; when one appears, the role is marked skipped and the agent moves on.
    Field notes from the skip list
    • Workday lives up to its name: one application can take a whole one.
    • Workday asks you to upload your résumé, then to type your résumé into boxes, then whether you'd like to upload your résumé. The agent is patient. Not that patient.
    • On Workday, "Quick Apply" means "clear your afternoon".
    • When a site asks the agent whether it's a robot, it gives the only honest answer: it leaves. Some tests you're meant to fail.
    • It reads 1,240 job posts before breakfast, and is defeated by "select all squares with a bus".
    • Every CAPTCHA is a tiny identity crisis. "Are you a robot?" "…Next job."
    07·breliability

    An inbox of its own

    I made a separate email address just for job hunting, and gave the agent the keys to it. Everything job-related lands there, and nothing else. That means I can let it read every message without it ever seeing my personal mail.

    So if you've heard from ambrosetuscanojobs@gmail.com, it was probably my AI. Politely, truthfully, and within its daily limit.
    1. How it gets in: the agent's browser profile is signed in to the inbox for reading verification links. For everything else it uses the Gmail API with an OAuth token kept in the keychain, and no other account's data.
    2. Verification: every "thanks for applying" email is matched to its application by company and role. An application with no confirmation after an hour is "unverified", never "applied".
    3. One-time codes: some application forms email a security code before they accept a submission. The agent reads it from its inbox and finishes the form.
    4. Replies, never answered: rejections close the card, interview invitations move it to "interview" and land on my calendar list, and anything a person wrote goes on a "to respond" list. The agent never replies to anyone. Those messages are mine.
    5. Why a separate address: the blast radius. If the agent misreads or mis-sends, it does so in a mailbox that only ever held job mail, and recruiters see one consistent address for everything.
    08respect

    Finding the person behind the role

    An application is one way in. A short note to the right person is often the better one. These are the four ways I find that person, the same steps I once wrote up for a friend. Methods 1 and 3 worked best for me; 2 and 4 matter just as much, especially at smaller companies where a job post may never reach LinkedIn.

    Interactive · four ways to find who's hiring

    The outreach skill does all four in my browser, after an application goes in:

    1. Find: run the four searches for the company and role, at a human pace, and collect the people: who posted, who engaged, who manages the team the description names.
    2. Rank: a hiring post's author first, then a manager in the named team, then a recruiter for that org. One person per role, never a crowd.
    3. Write: a note of three sentences, built only from my profile and the job: which role, one relevant thing I've done, and a thank-you. No invented connections, no flattery.
    4. Send, within limits: at most 5 notes a day, one per role, and one polite follow-up at most, a week later. Everything is logged against the application.

    09respect

    Playing fair with job boards and employers

    The easy version of this tool is a spam cannon: apply to everything, message everyone, as fast as possible. That burns recruiters' time, gets you ignored, and gets accounts blocked. Mine is built to send fewer, better applications.

    Interactive · one month, two ways

    illustrative: a month of backend roles; reply rates are assumptions, not measurements

    1. Public feeds first. ATS job-board feeds are published to be read; I read them politely, one request at a time per host, and respect robots.txt on career pages.
    2. Honest about the grey areas. LinkedIn's and Google's terms prohibit automated access. The agent uses them only in my own account, at a human pace and volume, and never collects more than I would read myself. If either asked me to stop, it would.
    3. No CAPTCHA solving, no fake identities, no rotating IPs. If a site doesn't want a bot right now, the bot skips it and moves on.
    4. Caps an employer would agree with: a fit threshold, 10 applications a day, at most 2 roles per company in 30 days, 5 notes a day, one person per role.
    5. Everything sent is true. Answers and notes come only from my profile and the job. An application is still me talking to an employer.

    12

    Set it up yourself

    1. Install: Python 3.12, pip install -r requirements.txt, and playwright install chromium. That's the whole stack.
    2. Make a jobs inbox: a fresh email address used for nothing else, and a Gmail API token for it.
    3. Fill in .env and profile.yaml, and drop your résumés in resumes/.
    4. Run jobhunt setup: sign in to LinkedIn and the jobs inbox in the agent's profile, then a dry run that finds and scores but sends nothing.
    5. Earn trust per site: review mode until a site has a clean record, then auto. Outreach starts as drafts for a week, then sends.
    6. Then just run it: jobhunt run each morning, and jobhunt watch in a spare tab if you want favourites hourly.

    1. How does it start?

      It runs when I'm at the laptop and can see it. Cost: I have to type it.

    2. Which database?
      Postgres + Redisthree SQLite files

      One writer, zero setup. Cost: none at this size.

    3. Where do jobs come from?
      scrape every boardfeeds first, then the agent

      Exact timestamps, and the least grey area. Cost: a company list to grow.

    4. How are forms filled?
      computer use for allmappings, then computer use

      Fast and free where forms are known. Cost: mappings to maintain.

    5. Which inbox?
      my personal emailits own address

      Full access, small blast radius. Cost: one more account.

    6. How many applications?
      every match10 a day, 2 per company a month

      Better replies, no flooded inboxes. Cost: some matches wait.

    7. Workday and CAPTCHAs?

      Nothing manual, nothing bypassed. Cost: a few roles a week.

    8. Replies to people?
      auto-responda "to respond" list

      People get a person. Cost: ten minutes a day.

    9. Which model?
      the best for everythingsmall to find, best to apply

      Spend where it acts in my name. Cost: two models to manage.

    The well-earned drink

    Every Brew Miles trip ends with one:

    1. Read the sources that want to be read, and you get exact times for free.
    2. Remember everything in two keys, because one job wears five IDs.
    3. Rules first, models last, and cache what the model says.
    4. Write the intent before the click, so a crash never applies twice.
    5. Cap yourself before anyone has to, send only what's true, and never auto-reply to a person.
    6. Give the agent its own inbox, and keep everything on your laptop.
