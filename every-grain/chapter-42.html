Period covered: from the apostolic traditions of the first centuries to the Year of St Francis, November 2026

In this chapter. Thread four: the Catholic story from the apostolic traditions to the Year of St Francis, 2026.

Four bodies carried to Sopara

In the spring of 1321 or 1322, a friar walked north along the coast from Thana carrying the dead. His name was Jordanus, a Dominican from Séverac in the south of France, and he had come to India with a small party of Latin missionaries: three Franciscans, Thomas of Tolentino, James of Padua and Peter of Siena, and a Georgian lay-brother named Demetrius. [n38; Gazetteer XIV pp. 356–357] At Thana they had been taken in by Nestorian Christians, “of whom there were fifteen families”. Jordanus had gone on ahead to preach at Sopara. While he was away, his companions were brought before the town’s Muslim governor and his qadi. [n38; Gazetteer XIV pp. 356–357]

What happened next survives in Jordanus’s own account, as the 1882 Gazetteer of the Bombay Presidency relays it in its footnotes. Thomas, asked his opinion of the Prophet, called Muhammad “the son of perdition”. James was thrown into a fire and came out unharmed, and still the qadi urged the governor to kill them. Thomas, James and Demetrius were beheaded. Peter, who had stayed behind at their lodging, was cut asunder the next day. [n38; Gazetteer XIV pp. 356–357 notes] When the news reached Jordanus at Sopara, he came back to Thana, took up the four bodies, carried them to Sopara and buried them there. [n38; Gazetteer XIV p. 357, citing Jordanus’s Mirabilia and Yule’s Cathay]

At Sopara he found something he had perhaps not expected: “many Nestorian Christians and a church dedicated to St Thomas the Apostle”. He stayed fifteen days, instructed and baptised “many people”, and gave Holy Communion to about ninety Christians. [n38; Gazetteer XIV p. 322] [n15; Gaz. XIV pp. 321–322] Then he wrote to his superiors in Europe. “There are three places where the friars might reap a great harvest,” he said, “and where they could live in common. One of these is Supera, where two friars might be stationed.” [n15; Hobson-Jobson quoting Yule’s Cathay p. 227] He commended the place, the Gazetteer says, as “an important place, not only for missions in India, but as a good starting point for missionaries to Ethiopia”. [n38; Gazetteer XIV p. 322]

Within about a year another friar, the Franciscan Odoric of Pordenone, reached Thana, visited the tomb and carried the martyrs’ bones away. [n38; Gazetteer XIV p. 357] Through him the dust of this coast went to China and to Italy.

This chapter follows a single thread through the whole biography of the coast: why Sopara and Vasai matter to the Catholic faith, in India and in the wider Church. It is a thread that runs from a grave dug by a foreign friar in 1321 to a pilgrim centre declared in a village church in June 2026. Along the way it passes through a Franciscan orphanage burned in 1540, a college founded by Francis Xavier, a city of seven churches inside a sea fort, an Inquisition prison, a boy from Agashi crucified at Nagasaki, a treaty clause that kept the faith alive under the Marathas, church bells that now ring in Hindu temples, a quarrel between Lisbon and Rome that ran for a century, a pope’s visit, a diocese, a Marathi Bible and a green movement. It also passes through the lives of the people who carried the faith from generation to generation in the villages, the Kuparis of the northern garden belt, the Christian Vadvals of the south, the Koli Christians of the shore and all the families who in 1887 began to call themselves East Indians.

Every part of it has to be read with care. Some of the story is tradition, honoured and old, but not evidence. Some is evidence, but written by people with their own quarrels: Portuguese missionaries proud of their numbers, a Goan scholar contemptuous of the local converts, British officials suspicious of Goa, Hindu writers angry at the conversions, Catholic writers defending them. The chapter tries to keep each voice in view and to say plainly which kind of claim each one is.

Before the record: Thomas, Bartholomew and a bishop from Persia

The Catholic story of this coast is usually told as if it began with the Apostles. It is important to be clear at the outset that, for Sopara and Vasai, that beginning is tradition, not documented history.

The apostle most closely tied to India in Christian memory is Thomas. Across South India, the Thomas Christians of Kerala trace their church to him. On this northern coast, though, the research behind this book found no source for any tradition that Thomas himself came to Sopara. [n38; §1 Gaps] The only “Thomas” link is a dedication: the church of St Thomas that Jordanus found at Supara in 1321. A church named for an apostle is not proof that the apostle preached there. It shows only that, by the early fourteenth century, the Christians of Sopara honoured Thomas as their patron, as Christians of the Persian tradition across India did. [n38; §1 Inferences]

The second apostolic tradition is Bartholomew’s. It begins with a sentence in the Church History of Eusebius of Caesarea, written in the early fourth century. Eusebius tells how Pantaenus, a teacher of Alexandria, travelled to “India” late in the second century and found there the Gospel of Matthew in Hebrew letters, left behind, it was said, by the apostle Bartholomew. [n38; Eusebius, Church History V.10] Eusebius does not name the place, and the word “India” in his time could cover a great deal of the world east of Egypt, including Arabia and Ethiopia. Some recent authors, among them Perumalil and Moraes, have placed Bartholomew’s mission in the Bombay region and identified it with Kalyan. But, as a summary of the debate puts it, “the majority of scholars are skeptical”, and there is no ancient local tradition here to support it. [n38; Eusebius V.10; nasrani.net summary, a partisan site] The Wikipedia article on the Bombay East Indians repeats that Portuguese-era converts included descendants of an ancient Christian community “reportedly founded by Bartholomew the Apostle”, which is a tertiary source restating the same claim. [n38; Wikipedia: Bombay East Indians]

The Diocese of Vasai itself traces Christianity in its territory to St Bartholomew. [n38; Wikipedia: Diocese of Vasai; Catholics India directory] That is a living claim of a living church, and it deserves to be recorded as such. It is not independent proof. The honest position is that the Bartholomew mission to this coast is possible, unprovable and doubted by most historians. It belongs in this book as the oldest story the Church here tells about itself.

The first firm evidence is later, and it comes from a merchant. In the sixth century, about 535 to 550, an Alexandrian trader who later became a monk wrote a book of Christian geography. He is known as Cosmas Indicopleustes, “Cosmas who sailed to India”. Among the Christian communities he lists along the Indian coasts is one at a place he calls Kalliana, where there was a bishop “appointed from Persia”. [n38; Gazetteer XIV p. 322] [n37; Gaz. XIII-I p. 200] Kalliana is Kalyan, the old port at the head of the Ulhas creek, the same river whose mouth the Portuguese would later call the Bassein creek. Cosmas also has a doubtful reference to “Sibor”, a place of great trade on the coast near Kalyan, which some have read as Sopara; the Gazetteer itself calls the identification doubtful. [n15; Gaz. XIV p. 320]

So by the sixth century there was an organised church at the head of this creek system, with a bishop sent out by the Church of the East, the church of the Persian empire, whose head sat at Seleucia-Ctesiphon on the Tigris. Westerners later called that church “Nestorian”, after the fifth-century patriarch Nestorius whose teaching it was accused of following. The 1882 Gazetteer goes one step further and suggests, with a cautious “seem”, that the Christian settlements at Kalyan and Sopara were strengthened in the fifth century by refugees from persecution in the Byzantine and Persian lands. [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 432 n.4] [n03; TG-II p. 420 n.4] That is the Gazetteer’s inference, not a record.

Between Cosmas and Jordanus lie almost eight centuries with only scattered Christian traces on this coast. There were Christians at Chaul in 942. [n37; Gaz. XIII-I p. 200] The ports traded with the Persian Gulf, the Red Sea and East Africa, and the Church of the East moved along those trade routes. But no source in the notes behind this book names a Christian at Sopara in those centuries. When the Gazetteer looked at the Sopara church Jordanus found, it concluded: “there seems to have been a relic of the old Kalyan bishopric at Sopara”. [n38; Gazetteer XIV p. 322] The phrase “seems to have been” is exactly right. The bishop at Kalliana in Cosmas’s day and the ninety communicants at Sopara in Jordanus’s day are two points on a line, and the line between them is drawn by inference.

1321: the martyrs of Thana and the tomb at Sopara

The Thana martyrdom is the first event in the Catholic story of this coast that rests on a contemporary witness. It deserves to be told carefully, with its conflicts.

The friars had come to India in the age of the great Latin missions to Asia, when the papacy and the mendicant orders, the Franciscans and the Dominicans, sent men overland and by sea to the Mongol courts, to Persia and to China. Jordanus and his companions had sailed from Hormuz in the Persian Gulf. [n38; §1] They landed at Thana on the island of Salsette, a port that, like Sopara, faced the creeks and the sea. There, the Gazetteer says, they were received by Nestorian Christians, fifteen families of them. [n38; Gazetteer XIV pp. 356–357]

Thana was then under a Muslim governor. The Gazetteer is clear, elsewhere, that Thana “seems never to have been the scene of any forcible conversion of Hindus to Islam” and that Muslim presence on this coast had come through “traders, refugees and adventurers” since the seventh century. [n37; Gaz. XIII-I p. 216] The friars were killed not for being Christians among Muslims, but, as Jordanus tells it, for what Thomas said about the Prophet under questioning. That detail matters. It shows that the martyrdom was a confrontation, and it is the friars’ own side that records Thomas’s words.

The date is not settled. The Gazetteer gives 1322 as probable, following the Acta Sanctorum. Modern Italian cult sources relayed on Wikipedia give 8 April 1321, with a feast on 9 April. [n38; Gazetteer XIV pp. 356–357; Wikipedia: Thomas of Tolentino] The research behind this book recommends taking the date from the Gazetteer or from Yule’s Cathay, the scholarly edition of the medieval travellers, rather than from the cult sources. [n38; §1 Cited Findings] Either way, the martyrdom falls in 1321 or 1322.

Jordanus’s actions afterwards are the heart of the Sopara connection. He carried the bodies to Sopara, buried them there, and, in the fifteen days he stayed, baptised, taught and gave communion. [n38; Gazetteer XIV p. 322 and p. 357] Another passage of the Gazetteer says that he found the “pagans”, Parsis and Hindus, “willing to listen”, and that he made thirty-five converts between Thana and Supara. [n37; Gaz. XIII-I p. 200] His letter asking for two friars to be stationed at Supera is, in the words of the research note, “the Catholic Church’s first formal request for more missionaries at Supera”. [n38; §1 Inferences] Jordanus was later made a bishop, of Quilon on the Malabar coast, according to Latin sources the research did not re-check. [n38; §1 Inferences]

Then came Odoric. He reached Thana within a year of the killings, visited the tomb, and “carried off the martyrs’ bones”. [n38; Gazetteer XIV p. 357] The Wikipedia article on Thomas of Tolentino, drawing on Italian sources, places the removal in 1323 or 1326. It says Thomas’s skull travelled with Odoric to Quanzhou, the great port of southern China, and to Khanbaliq, the Mongol capital that is now Beijing, and then reached the Franciscans of Tolentino in Italy in 1330, where it was later placed in the cathedral in a silver bust. [n38; Wikipedia: Thomas of Tolentino, citing da Cunha pp. 174, 176] The same article says Thomas and his companions were “unofficially regarded as beatified since the 14th century”, that Pope Pius VII approved their cult in 1809 and Pope Leo XIII in 1894. [n38; Wikipedia: Thomas of Tolentino] Secondary sources disagree about whether the act of 1894 was a beatification or a confirmation of an existing cult, and the research did not read the decrees. [n38; §1 Gaps] The Thana martyrs have never been canonised. [n38; §4 Inferences]

How much of the martyrs stayed at Sopara is unknown. Odoric took “the bones”; whether anything was left in the grave, and where the grave lay, no source in the notes records. [n38; §1 Gaps] There is no shrine to them at Sopara today that the research found.

Even so, the episode places Sopara in a world map of Catholic memory. A grave dug at Sopara links the coast of Thana to Tolentino in the Marche, to the Franciscan Order’s story of its early martyrs, and to the first Latin Christian communities in China. [n38; §1 Inferences] The Gazetteer’s 1882 judgement that Sopara’s Nestorian church was “a relic of the old Kalyan bishopric” also means that, in 1321, two Christian traditions met at Sopara: the old Church of the East, Persian in its bishops and liturgy, and the Latin Church of the friars. Whether the ninety communicants Jordanus served belonged to the Persian Church or simply pre-dated the Portuguese, the research could only infer. [n38; §1 Inferences]

Two silent centuries

After Jordanus, the Christian record of Sopara goes silent for two hundred years.

The silence is not a sign that nothing happened, but it is a real gap in the evidence, and it should be named as one. The 1882 Gazetteer, compiling everything it could find about the Christians of Thana, concluded: “No trace seems to be left either of the Nestorian or of the Latin converts.” [n34; Gaz. XIII p. 200] A footnote on the same page records a single tantalising report: “One recent report mentions a Christian village in Bassein that claims to be older than the Portuguese. No confirmation of this statement has been received.” [n34; Gaz. XIII p. 200 n.7] The village is not named. It is the only hint in the record that any Christian community on this coast might have survived from the Nestorian age into the Portuguese one, and the Gazetteer’s editors could not confirm it.

What the neighbouring record does show is a coast under the Delhi and then the Gujarat sultans, its trade in the hands of Muslim, Hindu and Jain merchants. In 1514 the Portuguese writer Duarte Barbosa described “Baxay” as a town of “Moors and Gentiles”, a good seaport of the King of Gujarat. [n37; Gaz. XIV p. 28] The Naitas of Bassein, Muslims descended from Arab and Persian traders who had married local women, were rich traders when the Portuguese came. [n37; Gaz. XIII-I pp. 216–217] The Jain bronzes found around Sopara and Gass, dated 1458 to 1555, show a Jain community living there to the eve of Portuguese rule. [n42; Shah 2020] Christianity of the old Persian kind may have faded with the decline of the Church of the East across Asia in these centuries; the notes offer no direct evidence either way.

When Christianity came back to this coast, it came by sea, with cannon, under a European flag, and it came to stay.

1534: the Franciscans come first

The Portuguese took Bassein by force in 1532, and on 23 December 1534 Sultan Bahadur of Gujarat, pressed by the Mughals on his northern frontier, ceded to Portugal Bassein with its dependencies, Salsette, Bombay and Karanja. [n38; Hull I pp. 1–2] [n07; D’Mello blog] The treaty itself was not a religious document. One of its clauses, recorded by Danvers, guaranteed that 5,000 larins of Bassein’s revenue that had been paid to the mosques “was to continue to be so applied”. [n37; Danvers vol. 1 p. 416] That clause did not last long. In March 1546 King João III wrote to the viceroy Dom João de Castro ordering that the “3,000 pardaos formerly employed in the maintenance of the Mahomedan mosques” be given to the priests “who give a helping hand towards the conversion of the infidels”, and that a church be built. [n37; da Cunha p. 215 note] [n32; DC 214–216] From 1550 the Crown paid the Bassein Franciscans 2,070 pardaos a year “derived from a source which before the conquest was solely applicable to the lighting of the mosques in Bassein”. [n34; Da Cunha 1876 p. 235] The money that had lit the mosques now paid for the conversion of the country.

The first missionaries were Franciscans. Ernest Hull, the Jesuit historian of the Bombay mission writing in 1927, states it plainly: the Franciscans “from 1534 were the first to establish churches in Bassein, Salsette, Bombay, Karanja and Chaul”. [n38; Hull I pp. 1–2] Their leader was Fr António do Porto. The Catholic Encyclopedia calls him the “Apostle of Bassein” and credits him with 10,156 converts. [n38; Catholic Encyclopedia, “Bassein”] The Gazetteer gives his record, from 1534 to 1552, in one blunt sentence: he “destroyed 200 temples, made over 10,000 converts, built twelve churches, and, by founding orphanages and monasteries, secured a supply of native priests”. [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 461]

Both halves of that sentence are true, and both matter. Porto threw down temples at Agashi, Bassein and Thana. At many sacred tanks “temples were thrown down” and some pools were filled in. [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 461 n.1] At Kanheri, on Salsette, the great Buddhist cave became a church of St Michael; the Brahmanical caves at Mandapeshvar became a church of Our Lady of the Conception. [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 461 n.1] Two heads of the Kanheri monastery, apparently Buddhist monks, were converted and renamed Paulo Rapozo and Francisco de Santa Maria; Paulo Rapozo was given three villages, which he left to the college at Mandapeshvar. [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 461 n.1] At one famous healing pool between Bassein and Agashi the pool was taken over, a chapel to Our Lady of Healing was built beside it, and “the pilgrimage and cure-working continued”. [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 461 n.1]

The other half of Porto’s work was the orphanage, and its first home was Agashi. As early as 1535, da Cunha writes, Porto built there an orphanage “for the education and maintenance of 40 boys”, under the invocation of Nossa Senhora da Luz, Our Lady of Light. Agashi, he says, “was the first place of this district visited by Franciscan missionaries”. [n34; Da Cunha 1876 pp. 159–160] [n38; Hull I p. 3] Later orphanages followed at Thana (130 boys), at Vehar (800 boys) and at Mandapeshvar (100). [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 461 n.1] Their purpose was explicit: in times of war and famine they were filled “with deserted children”, and from them Porto “prepared a class of native priests”. [n34; Gaz. XIII p. 201] That choice would matter two centuries later, when the Portuguese clergy left and the native priests stayed.

The Agashi orphanage did not survive five years. In April 1540 an army from Gujarat invaded the Portuguese lands and the orphanage was destroyed. Da Cunha reports that five people left behind were burned alive after refusing to accept Islam. [n34; Da Cunha 1876 pp. 159–160; Gazetteer XIV pp. 1–2] A Franciscan chronicle, cited by the research, calls them the “second batch of martyrs”, after the friars of Thana, and notes that they were all native converts. [n37; Gaz. XIV p. 1; da Cunha pp. 159–160] When the Catholic Encyclopedia, early in the twentieth century, described Bassein as “memorable for the martyrdom of five religious”, it was very probably remembering these five of Agashi, though the entry does not say so. [n38; Catholic Encyclopedia, Damão] The research found no names for them. If they were the orphanage’s staff and boys, they were the first local Christians of the Portuguese era to die for the new faith, within five years of its arrival.

A correction to the last two paragraphs comes from a source found later. Frei Paulo da Trindade’s Franciscan chronicle places the burning of the Agashi neophytes in 1571, not April 1540, and says four people, not five; and its editor doubts that Franciscans were settled in the lands of Baçaim before 1547. The orphans’ “college” at Agashi is real in his account, but its date is not. Chapter 34 sets out the evidence. [n64 §4]

In the same years the Portuguese built their great fort. It was founded in 1535 or 1536, and inside it, according to the Diocese of Vasai’s own history page (read only through a search snippet), the first Latin-rite church of the territory was established in 1536 under the title Nossa Senhora da Vida, Our Lady of Life. [n38; Diocese of Vasai, snippet] [n09; Diocese of Vasai, History, snippet] The 1882 Gazetteer calls Nossa Senhora da Vida “one of the oldest churches in Bassein”, with three altars when the Italian traveller Gemelli Careri saw it in 1695; by the nineteenth century its ruin had been turned into a sugar factory, and in digging the foundations the workers found the bones of a man and a horse with a rusty rapier, a cavalier buried with his charger. [n32; BG 40; DC 226–227] The parish church, the Matriz of St Joseph, stood on the site of a church of about 1546, the one King João III had ordered paid for from the mosque money. [n32; BG 38–39; DC 214–216]

Francis Xavier and the College of the Holy Name

The Jesuits arrived in India in 1542. [n38; Hull I pp. 1–2] Their most famous member, Francis Xavier, passed through Bassein several times. Hull, following da Cunha, counts three visits: once in 1544 and twice in 1548. [n38; Hull I p. 4] Da Cunha dates the first “about the end of the year 1544”, when Xavier was on his way to Diu; the second in 1548, to see the viceroy Dom João de Castro; and the third, later in 1548, when he laid the foundation of “a splendid mission, which his Society extended throughout the Northern Konkan”. [n38; da Cunha pp. 231–232] The Gazetteer gives a different set of years, 1544, 1548 and 1552. [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 461] On his visits Xavier stayed with the Franciscans at their convent of Santo António, the oldest and one of the largest churches in the fort. [n32; BG 41–42]

The foundation of 1548 was the Jesuit College and Church of the Holy Name of Jesus, the Nome de Jesus. Hull writes: “The foundations were laid in 1548. Previous to this the mission-work in and about Bassein had been in the hands of the Franciscans only.” The college beside the church was “the great Jesuit college or seminary for the education of native converts, which had previously been manned by the Franciscans”. [n38; Hull I p. 4] The Catholic Encyclopedia dates Xavier’s foundation to December 1548. [n38; Catholic Encyclopedia, “Bassein”] Fr Melchior Gonçalves began the building and became its first rector. [n38; da Cunha pp. 231–232] [n32; BG 41] One web source dates the church to 1549 and says the large college building was begun in 1561. [n09; colonialvoyage.com, snippet] Over the college door, in 1882, the Gazetteer recorded the date 1636, a date of repair. [n32; BG 41]

A year after the founding, Xavier was in Japan. From Kagoshima, on 5 November 1549, he wrote back asking about “the College of Bassein, the Franciscans who before held it”. [n38; da Cunha pp. 231–232] The phrase shows that the college had begun as a Franciscan house and passed to the Jesuits, and that Xavier, at the far edge of the known world, kept it in mind. Its second rector, Fr Francisco Barreto, later became Provincial of the Jesuits in India, in 1553. [n38; da Cunha p. 232] It was, the research concludes, the Jesuits’ first school in the northern Konkan. [n38; §2 Inferences] Within two decades it would be educating a boy who became a saint.

The Jesuits brought new devotions. Passion plays, the Gazetteer says, were “introduced by the Jesuits about the middle of the sixteenth century”: a society of penitents was formed in 1551, and in 1552 Fr Melchior Nunes Barreto brought it to Bassein. [n34; Gazetteer XIII pp. 207–210; Da Cunha 1876 pp. 249–250] The Lenten processions of the Vasai villages today, the candle-lit pirsao or miravnuk that the Kupari community describes, stand in that line of Lenten devotion, though no source connects the two directly. [n07; D’Mello blog]

How the villages became Christian

The Jesuits’ own letters, preserved in the great Roman edition called Documenta Indica, are the best evidence for how conversion actually worked on this coast. They are unusually frank, and they cut against both pious and polemical retellings.

On 5 September 1558 Fr Gonçalo Rodrigues wrote from Bassein. In the three and a half years since he had come from Goa “to Taná and Baçaim”, more than nine hundred souls had been baptised. [n34; Documenta Indica IV doc. 16 p. 98] Then he explained who they were. “Those who come to conversion are commonly the poorest and most needy, and are converted more drawn and moved by hope of some interest and remedy of life than by preaching, which they do not understand and for which there is no interpreter.” And again: “we set them the snares of human things and, once caught in them, we teach them the divine … we invite the rustics, farmers and those who live in woods and hedges.” [n34; Documenta Indica IV pp. 100–101, translation from the research note] He described a new Christian village near Thana, the village of the Holy Trinity, about five hundred souls, all farmers and labourers, built around a converted temple. The converts were given houses, “very cheap lands”, and for many of them properties the Jesuits bought; clothes for men, women and children; rice to eat until their first harvest; seed; “as many oxen as they want to plough, with their ploughs mended”; a common cattle shed; and a granary. [n34; Documenta Indica IV pp. 100–101] This was in Salsette, not in the northern garden villages, but it is the clearest account of the Jesuit method in this region.

Four years later, on 5 December 1562, Fr Francisco Cabral wrote from Bassein to the Jesuit General in Rome. Before his preaching, he said, the Portuguese citizens of Bassein, who “all have villages”, had believed that making one villager Christian would empty the village, and so Christianity “was little favoured”. Now “they no longer hinder Christianity, at least publicly”, and “the inhabitants who before would riot if a vassal of their villages was made [Christian] now come to beg us to go and convert their villages”. [n34; Documenta Indica V doc. 90 pp. 628–629] He described the method: catechumens kept at the college, men and women separately, taught the faith nightly by a lay brother who knew the language; baptism in procession with the captain and the whole city, with garlands, psalms and flutes, followed by a feast; couples married as Hindus married again; about eighty converts’ children kept at the college to learn to read, write and sing, “and if they are taught they may become priests”. [n34; Documenta Indica V p. 629]

Gerson da Cunha, the Goan physician and antiquarian who wrote the standard nineteenth-century history of Bassein in 1876, summarised the Jesuit method more sourly. The Jesuits converted “a high-caste man, a Brahman, who was sure to be imitated by a number of his and other classes”, then celebrated his baptism “with all possible pomp”, with a procession led by the Governor and nobility and “a sumptuous banquet”. “Their bait was alluring and irresistible,” he wrote, “and the native convert was led by the nose.” [n34; Da Cunha 1876 p. 234] He gives the scale: “in the year 1588, when in the church of Bassein alone 9,400 heathen were baptized”. [n34; Da Cunha 1876 p. 234] The Gazetteer repeats it: between 1570 and 1590 the Jesuits “took pains to make Brahman and other high-caste converts, knowing that if the Brahmans became Christians, many of the lower classes would follow”, and baptisms rose “from 1600 in 1573 to 9400 in 1588”. [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 461; Gaz. XIV p. 41] Both figures come from the Jesuit chronicle Oriente Conquistado; the research did not see the original letter. [n34; §9]

Royal law stood behind the missionaries. In 1546 the King of Portugal ordered idols broken and the makers of idols and performers of Hindu rites punished; the order was not enforced and was renewed in 1555, when feasts, Brahman preaching and cremations were forbidden and anyone found with idols was to be sent to the galleys with his property forfeit, half going to the Church. It was “evaded by the grant of licenses, but … enforced in 1581”. [n37; Gaz. XIII-II pp. 462–463] In 1549 the governor Jorge Cabral ordered that “no person of whatever quality shall rebuild a temple or a mosque” and that “no person shall dare to prevent another from becoming Christian”. [n34; Mendiratta p. 210] Each Portuguese settlement had a Father of the Christians, the Pai dos Cristãos, whose office was to “further Christianity, to foster Christians and to gather others to Christ”; da Cunha called it “a dangerous appointment”. [n37; da Cunha p. 102] The Jesuit visitor Valignano’s instructions to that office said that conversion “does not commonly occur as a result of sermons and doctrine” but by obstructing idolatry, punishing, “refusing them favours which can justly be refused, and offering them to those who are newly converted”. [n37; Priolkar p. 58] The poorer converts were freed from tithes and first fruits for fifteen years. [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 462] Orphan laws placed fatherless Hindu children under the Church. [n37; Priolkar pp. 128–129]

Two qualifications matter for the Vasai villages. First, the Portuguese historian of architecture Sidh Losa Mendiratta notes that, “as far as we know”, the Provisions in favour of Christianity “were never enforced in the Northern Province’s subdivisions closest to the mainland” — which includes the subdivisions where the northern garden villages lay. [n34; Mendiratta p. 211] Second, the landlords themselves came to drive conversion. The 1931 Census of Bombay explained the coast’s Portuguese surnames this way: “The Portuguese parcelled out the countryside among certain of their countrymen … With the advent of the missionary a great deal of mass conversion took place, not unaided by competition among the owners of these estates, and in many villages every tenant in the village was baptized and given the surname of the owner of the village, e.g., Demonte, Pereira, Ferreira, etc.” [n34; Census 1931 Vol VIII Part I pp. 363–364] Mendiratta puts the outcome in one sentence: between about 1570 and 1620, Jesuits and Franciscans converted “a majority of the population” of Salsette and the Bassein and Agashi subdivisions, and “whole villages were converted, normally following the conversion of the respective ‘matará’ or headman”. [n34; Mendiratta p. 211]

Was it force or persuasion? The sources divide, and each side should be heard. The 1882 Gazetteer’s editors judged: “Force may have been used in some cases, but the Portuguese accounts seem to show that persuasion and interest were the chief means of conversion.” [n34; Gaz. XIII p. 201 n.2] A. K. Priolkar, the Goan archivist who wrote The Goa Inquisition in 1961, argued that conviction played almost no part, quoting Dr Antonio Noronha that conversions came “some by fear of physical force; others from moral cowardice … not a few to avoid the loss of their properties and interests … and almost none from conviction”. [n37; Priolkar pp. 49–55] The Jesuit historian Fr H. Heras argued the opposite, that the Jesuits acted “only a posteriori … after the Hindus themselves had decided to join the Church”. [n37; Priolkar p. 51] Priolkar’s evidence is mostly from Goa, not Bassein. [n37; §3] A Jesuit letter of 1547 from Goa, written by Fr Nicolau Lancilotto to Ignatius Loyola, is the most damning contemporary witness: “The people of this country who become Christians do so purely for temporal advantage.” [n37; Wicki, Documenta Indica I, via Priolkar p. 55] Again, that letter is about Goa.

The local stories of forced conversion belong in the record as tradition. The best known says that missionaries threw bread, or flesh, into the village wells, and that those who drank were outcasted and so became Christians. The Gazetteer recorded it in 1882 as the story “of the Uraps and other classes”, whose “forefathers were forced to become Christians by having pieces of flesh thrown into their wells”, and cited a Protestant periodical of 1859 on “the Portuguese thrusting biscuit and pork into the mouths of unwilling multitudes”. [n34; Gaz. XIII p. 201 n.2] The story appears today in Christian community blogs and in Hindu polemical writing alike. [n07; D’Mello blog; Swarajya] No Jesuit or Franciscan letter the research read records it. [n34; §3 Inferences] It is a tradition at least as old as the 1850s, attached at first to the Uraps rather than to the Samvedi converts, and the Gazetteer’s own editor discounted it.

The fairest summary is the research note’s: payment, tithe exemption, land, status, protection and orphan law pushed the poor and the vulnerable; converts of high caste were honoured and drawn by ceremony; forced baptism is documented most clearly for orphans; and no source names a mass forced baptism in Bassein. [n37; §3 Inferences] Faith and interest cannot be separated in these sources. Nor can the reader of 2026 know what the converts themselves believed, because none of them left a written word.

The peoples who became the Church of Vasai

The conversions did not make one community. They made several, because, as the Gazetteer noted, “in matters of marriage the lower classes keep to their old caste distinctions”. [n41; Gazetteer XIII-1, Christians chapter] Each of the Christian peoples of Vasai entered the Church from its own starting point.

In the northern garden belt, around Agashi, Nirmal, Sopara, Koprad and Vatar, the best period evidence points to Samvedi Brahmans, the landed farmers and gardeners of the Bassein and Mahim country. The Gazetteer of 1882 says: “The husbandmen of Sopara are Christians, many of them converted Samvedi Brahmans.” [n34; Gazetteer XIV p. 315] At Agashi, Vatar and Koprad it counted “about eighty-five families of Samvedi Brahmans, fifteen families of Pachkalshis, and about seventy-five families of Native Christians originally Samivedis and Pachkalshis”, drying the plantains for which the district was famous. [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 395] The Samvedi community, in other words, was split by conversion, not wholly converted, and Christian and Hindu Samvedis went on living and working side by side. [n37; §3] One popular synthesis says the first converts of all were Samvedi Brahmins settled near the Vaitarna between Agashi and Nalasopara, and that they were “the only Bassein group to receive Jesuit education”. [n34; Rodricks, Sahapedia] These Samvedi Christians are the people known today as the Kuparis. The name itself, remarkably, appears in none of the fifteen primary and early sources the research searched, from the Gazetteer of 1882 to the Census of 1931; its first printed attestations are a community blog of 2007 and Wikipedia. [n34; headline finding] It is usually derived from Portuguese compadre, “co-father” or godfather, which fits a community whose 1882 vocabulary used Portuguese godparent words, padrinho and madrinha, but the derivation is unproven. [n34; §2] A second, unsourced folk etymology from kup, “well”, is tied to the bread-in-wells story and is felt by many as an insult. [n34; §2]

The Samvedi-Brahman origin is not the whole picture, and hostile and scholarly voices both complicate it. Da Cunha in 1876 insisted that “there is no descendant of any high-class native” among Bassein’s Catholics, describing them as clans of Kolis and Bhandaris and the offspring of soldiers and local women; Mendiratta reads his tone as contempt born of the quarrel between Goans and local Christians. [n34; Da Cunha 1876 pp. 250–251; Mendiratta p. 234] Kenneth Ballhatchet found that by 1850 it was primarily the cultivating classes who were Catholics in the Konkan. [n34; via Pinto] Both can be true for different villages. Even a Brahman origin, on this coast, meant a landed farming community, not a priestly one. [n34; §1 Inferences]

In the south, around Sandor, Manickpur, Merces and Chulne, the Christian cultivators descended largely from Pachkalshis (Panchkalshis), the farming caste whose rural division is called Vadval. The Gazetteer’s list of Bassein’s Christian castes includes “Brahmans, Bhandaris, Chavkalshis, Pachkalshis, Kharpatils and Kolis”, the “most hardworking and skilful husbandmen” who grew sugarcane and plantains, and it calls “the Vadvals among the most skilful cultivators in the district”. [n41; Gazetteer XIII-1, Christians chapter] Enthoven’s Tribes and Castes of Bombay (1922) says that Portuguese persecution drove many Panchkalshis south and that “many were then converted to Roman Catholicism by force and their descendants may still be traced at Bassein and Salsette”. [n41; Enthoven III p. 161] That is a community or colonial-era account, not a Portuguese document; the research found no Portuguese text that names the Vadvals. [n41; §5 Inferences] A Marathi book of 1935 noted that “some Christians even now call themselves Panchkalshi Christians”. [n41; Naik 1935 p. 15]

On the shore, the Koli fishing people formed the third great Christian group. The Koli Christians, also called Thankar and Gaonkar Kolis, are remembered as builders of churches and valued boat and warship builders, and are found at Bassein and Agashi, Uttan, Gorai, Madh and elsewhere. [n07; Wikipedia: Koli Christians] Their social life is run by a hereditary patil and karbaris, and statues of Mary in their churches are dressed in Koli saris. [n07; Wikipedia: Koli Christians] The Gazetteer of 1882 records that only the Kolis among the Christians kept a headman or council. [n34; Gaz. XIII pp. 207–210] Kunbis (Kulbis), Bhandaris and a few families of potters, washermen and barbers also entered the Church, keeping their caste identities. [n34; Rodricks; n07; East Indians] Later, in 1887, all of these would gather under one name, East Indians. [n07; §5]

The parish map: churches in the villages

Out of the conversions came a grid of parish churches across the island of Bassein. Their founding dates are contested, because the online sources repeat blog dates and the primary sources give few. The commonly cited list of nine Portuguese-era churches outside the fort, all still used by Vasai’s Christians, is: Nirmal 1557, Remedi 1557, Sandor 1566, Agashi 1568, Nandakhal 1573, Papdi 1574, Pali 1595, Manickpur 1606 and Merces 1606. [n09; Vasaiker; Goanet 2020] Other sources give other years. Hull lists Papdy (Nossa Senhora da Graça) as built in 1565, Sandor (São Tomé) in 1575, “Douli” (Nossa Senhora dos Remédios) in 1583, and Nirmal on the site of a church of 1580. [n38; Hull I pp. 5–6] The church directories give Nirmal’s Holy Cross as 1557 or 1558, and GCatholic dates the shrine of Our Lady of Remedies to 1577. [n38; UCA News; GCatholic] Sandor’s St Thomas is dated 1556 or 1566 and was the first parish of south Vasai outside the fort, the mother of Papdy and, with Merces, of Giriz. [n09; Wikipedia: Sandor] Da Cunha names three parishes as Jesuit foundations: Nossa Senhora da Graça (Papdi), São Tomé (Sandor) and São Miguel Arcanjo (Manickpur). [n34; Da Cunha 1876 p. 234] Rome later ordered the Jesuits to administer only parishes they had founded from a pagan community. [n37; da Cunha p. 234]

In the north, the primary record is firmer for Agashi than the blogs are. The church there, the Gazetteer says, was “built after the destruction of Nossa Senhora da Luz”, Porto’s orphanage of 1535. [n34; Gazetteer XIV pp. 1–2] By 1881 it was the church of St James, “St. Iagos”. [n34; Gaz. XIII p. 206 n.3] The Holy Spirit church at Nandakhal is said to have been built by the Franciscans in 1573; in 1881 the Gazetteer’s Holy Spirit church stood at Koprad, which may be the same parish under an older name, though the research could not confirm it. [n09; Wikipedia: Holy Spirit Church Nandakhal] [n34; §3] Nirmal’s Holy Cross priest served Nandakhal’s people before Nandakhal had its own church. [n38; Wikipedia: Holy Spirit Church Nandakhal]

Nirmal is a place where the two faiths of this coast press especially close. It was a Hindu tirtha, sacred by the Skanda Purana and the Nirmal Mahatmya, with the linga of Vimaleshvar. The Portuguese “pulled down the temples and destroyed the linga”. [n37; Gaz. XIV pp. 292–293] A Christian church of the Holy Cross rose nearby. Two centuries later the Marathas would purify the site and build eight temples. [n37; Gaz. XIV pp. 292–293] Both stories are part of Nirmal’s history, and neither cancels the other.

From the beginning the village churches were served partly by local men. In 1570, Hull says, Bassein’s rural churches were handed over to native secular priests, “a similar experiment being tried in Salsette”, and “there seems also to have been a seminary for secular clergy at Bassein at an early date”. [n38; Hull I p. 22] The Gazetteer notes that “wealthy families who are village headmen and owners of rich garden lands often bring up one or more of their sons for the Church”. [n34; Gaz. XIII p. 205] Porto’s orphan priests, the Jesuit college boys who “may become priests”, the seminary for secular clergy: these were the roots of a local priesthood that would carry the Church through the crisis of 1739.

The five Franciscan rectories of the cassabé, about 1630

The best near-contemporary list of the village churches under the Franciscans is Frei Paulo da Trindade’s chapter “Of five rectories that we have in the cassabé of Baçaim”, written in the 1630s. [n64 §3] The cassabé, he explains, is so called “from the great freshness of the place, being all taken up by many orchards and gardens”, for the word in the language of the country “means a fresh place”. His editor adds that it was not an administrative division but a common name for a fertile tract named after its chief village, and quotes the factor André Ribeiro Coutinho in 1728: the cassabé of Baçaim “extends along the water’s edge from this fortress to Agassaim, over two leagues, and is more than half a league wide”. [n64 §2] The jurisdiction of Baçaim, Paulo says, was “the largest of all in India”: east to the fortresses of Asserim and Manorá, eight leagues of “very strong villages of great revenues”, north to the river of Agashi, and south to the river of Bombay or Karanja. He also gives the day the governor laid the first stone of the fort: 20 January 1535, St Sebastian’s day, “dressed with all the nobles and captains very richly”. [n64 §1]

The five rectories were these. [n64 §3]

Pale, the village opposite the city, held Our Lady Mother of God, “the most perfect finished that is known in India”, a carved figure pierced by a sword. The people of the city went on Saturdays to hear Mass there, bringing oil and candles. On the night of the great storm of 1618 the image was found with her face covered by the altar curtain, which had wound round behind her and tied itself tightly around the wrist that holds the sword. Some saw grief in it, and others shame at the sins of the city. The rector was already a Franciscan in 1585. [n64 §3] This is the Mother of God church of the parish accounts, and the “Palle” of the 1892 report. [n64]

St John the Baptist was by the 1630s “all of Christians” and, “for some inconveniences”, had been given up by the friars about twenty years before to the secular clergy. It is in the Franciscan lists of 1595 but not of 1585 or 1610. Where it stood is not known. [n64 §3]

Holy Cross, called Monte Calvário, at Nirmal, had many Christians, and many orphan boys learned to read, write and sing there, supported by rents that devotees had left. The editor, citing a Jesuit letter of 1561 by Manuel Teixeira, says the Vera Cruz church “was of recent foundation in 1561” and was raised to sanctify a place “where the gentiles made their ‘washings’”, which fits the picture of Nirmal as a holy lake and place of ablutions. [n64 §3]

The Holy Spirit (Nandakhal, on one reading) had two confraternities, of Jesus and of the Conception, founded by Fr Gonçalo da Conceição, a Bassein-born friar who later became the order’s Provincial (elected 27 January 1647; died 1 March 1653). At the Conception festival of 1632 the crown on the Virgin’s head was seen in the air before her, and the man hanging the church’s decorations fell from his ladder and hung by one hand from a beam, calling on her. A lame Christian Brahmin who came to pray at her altar, Paulo says, went home well and “is today a brother of the confraternity”. [n64 §3]

Agashi, the fifth, had a church of Our Lady of the Assumption, two thousand Christians and the college for forty boys described in Chapter 34. Its church, first of reed and straw, was burnt in 1571; the stone church that replaced it sheltered the villagers, Portuguese and local, against a Muslim force in 1594; it was destroyed again in the war of about 1613–14, when the attackers took the Assumption banner from the house, and a Malabar captain was later captured with it, its Virgin’s face cut out; and what stood was brought down by the storm of 1618. By the 1630s, Paulo says, “the whole building is renewed by the great labour and industry of our friars”. [n64 §4–§5]

The storm itself is dated by Paulo to the week of 15 May 1618. It began on Tuesday the 15th with cloud and wind; on the Thursday night, from eight to nine, the wind rose so that everyone thought the buildings would fall. It came in two bursts, two hours long, then a quarter-hour’s lull, then “double fury”. The sea came in through the city’s gates though it was neap tide and low water, and the black sand coated the walls. At the Franciscan church at Pale the roof, the choir and much of the gable fell and the altarpieces of the crossing were broken, while the friars sheltering in the vaulted chancel noticed nothing. Of the city’s churches only the Franciscans’ stood. [n64 §6] The parish source the book used for the statue says it fell on 16 May; Paulo’s storm night is the Thursday, 17 May. [n64 §6]

A city of churches: Dominicans, Augustinians and the court of the North

Inside the walls of Baçaim, the “Court of the North” grew into a city of convents. Within about sixty years of 1534 all four great religious orders of Portuguese Asia were established there. [n38; §2 Takeaway]

The Dominicans came later than the Franciscans and Jesuits. Hull says they founded their first Indian convent only in 1548 and reached Bassein afterwards; their Bassein church and monastery date from 1583. [n38; Hull I pp. 1–2, 4] It was dedicated to São Gonçalo, the Portuguese Dominican Blessed Gonçalo of Amarante, and the Diocese of Vasai’s history page (via a search snippet) calls it the Dominican church of 1583 “dedicated to Blessed Gonsalvo”. [n38; Diocese of Vasai, snippet] One web source dates it to 1564, which conflicts. [n09; Ancient History Sites] In 1882 its walls, tower and chief chapel with its arch still stood, with the ruined tomb of its patron on the gospel side. [n32; BG 42] The Dominicans also had a monastery at Agashi, which Mughal raiders from Gujarat destroyed in 1634. [n34; Gazetteer XIV pp. 1–2]

The Augustinians, Hull says, came to the region in 1572, and their Bassein church and convent date from about 1596. [n38; Hull I pp. 1–2, 4] Their chapel, Nossa Senhora da Anunciada, had a double-arched front and a vaulted roof with painted mouldings. [n32; BG 43] [n09; Ancient History Sites] Last came the Hospitallers of São João de Deus, a poor order that arrived after 1681 and, Careri said, could maintain only three friars. [n38; Hull I p. 4] [n32; BG 41]

An Augustinian chronicle printed in Silva Rego’s Documentação, written after 1696, gives the order’s own account of its Bassein house. The ninth convent of the congregation, it says, was that of Baçaim, founded about 1595 by Fr Domingos da Trindade, sent by the vicar provincial Fr Gaspar de São Vicente with two companions. Its first hospice was a hermitage of Nossa Senhora da Anunciada that Archbishop Dom Frei Aleixo de Meneses gave the order, “the first church there was in that city”, the chronicler says, which the Franciscan and Jesuit foundations contradict. In 1621 the convent moved to another site, where a church of Nossa Senhora do Bom Sucesso was built; the friars later returned to the Anunciada, “the reasons for these changes are not known”; and about 1696 the prior Fr Vicente de Santa Maria built the new church “that exists today”, with four altars: the high altar, Our Lady of Good Success, St Nicholas of Tolentino and St Rita. [n70 §2]

Around these orders stood the secular churches. The Matriz of St Joseph, the parish church that some writers wrongly called a cathedral, carried a Portuguese inscription over its lintel: “In the year 1601, the Most Illustrious Lord Dom Frei Aleixo de Menezes being Archbishop Primate, and Fr Pedro Galvão Pereira vicar, this Matriz was rebuilt.” [n32; BG 38–39; DC 214–216] In 1634 it had a vicar, four canons, two choir boys, a treasurer, four singers and one instrumentalist. [n32; BG 39 n.2] The Misericórdia, the confraternity of mercy, ran a church and hospital in a long pile on the river wall, paid 140 pardaos a month, with an allowance of rice for the poor; Careri, attending Mass there on a Sunday in 1695, called it “the parish of the city”. [n32; BG 40; DC 225–226] The fort also held an asylum for orphans. [n38; Hull I p. 3]

At the very end of the sixteenth century the Jesuit visitor Nicolau Pimenta, in India from 1598 to 1602, founded at Bassein a seminary called the College of the Purification. [n38; Hull I p. 21] The Gazetteer describes it as “a seminary for noble children, natives of those parts, who were brought up as missionaries”. [n34; Gaz. XIII pp. 201–202] It was one of the few institutions anywhere on this coast designed to send local boys out as missionaries.

The city took its patron saints seriously. Over a portal of a large building, which the Gazetteer thought was probably the Augustinian church, an inscription of 10 May 1631 announced that, under the viceroy Dom Miguel de Noronha, Count of Linhares, “this portal was built, on which St Francis Xavier was placed as patron of this city”. [n32; BG 40; DC 219–220] Da Cunha noted that a description of 1634 still gave St Sebastian as the earlier patron. [n32; DC 220 n.] Xavier had been canonised in 1622, nine years before; Bassein, where he had laid the foundations of the Jesuit college, adopted him as its own.

The figures for this age come from the Portuguese chronicler Bocarro’s survey of 1634. Bassein then had 63 religious: 30 Franciscans, 15 Jesuits, 10 Dominicans and 8 Augustinians, “all maintained by the State”. [n38; Hull I p. 4; da Cunha p. 235] [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 462] The same year the Jesuits’ share of the Crown grant, entered under the heading Conversão da Fé, “Conversion of the Faith”, was raised to 3,000 pardaos a year. [n34; Da Cunha 1876 p. 235] The same survey counted, as an earlier chapter of this book notes, about 1,800 slaves against 400 Portuguese and 200 Indian Christian families within the city. [n37; §3] Some of the household slaves, brought from Africa, were “made Christians and sometimes raised to be priests”. [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 460 n.3]

Visitors were impressed. The Italian traveller Pietro della Valle stayed a night at the Jesuit college in 1623 (the Gazetteer says 1628). [n32; DC 228] The English physician John Fryer, in 1674–75, counted six churches, four convents and two colleges, the Franciscan and the Jesuit, the latter “with a fine library” of historians, moralists and expositors, fine cloisters and a copy of Michelangelo’s Resurrection. [n38; Hull I p. 3] [n32; BG 41] He also saw the hard side of the city: the Portuguese gentry living “like petty monarchs” and holding the people “in a perfect state of villainage”. [n34; Gaz. XIII p. 202] Gemelli Careri heard Mass in the “parish church of Our Lady da Sé” on 11 February 1695. [n32; DC 216]

The city of churches was not secure. In 1618 a cyclone unroofed the three largest churches and badly damaged the Franciscan, Augustinian and Jesuit buildings. In 1674 Arab raiders from Muscat plundered the churches outside the walls. [n09; Vasaiker; Wikipedia: Vasai Fort] The Pai dos Cristãos and the orphan laws brought conflict with the Maratha state rising in the hills: an English record of the 1670s says Shivaji quarrelled with the Portuguese daily, “the chiefest cause of his hatred to them being for forcing orphans of his [caste to] turn Roman Catholicks”; in 1675 the Captain-General of Bassein himself took the side of some orphans against the Jesuits and made the Jesuits restore the orphans’ estate. [n37; Orme MSS via Priolkar p. 129] In 1674 the Maratha envoy More Pandit demanded the chauth because the Portuguese “had lately inflicted great severities on many families of the Morattoe religion … because they refused to become Christians”. [n37; da Cunha p. 143] A Jesuit traveller of the early seventeenth century, Goez, had already reported that “the persecution of the Portuguese had made many Hindus, Mussulmans and Parsees abandon their homes”, leaving much village land between Bassein and Daman uncultivated, though the Gazetteer adds that the districts soon recovered. [n37; Gaz. XIII-II pp. 463–464]

The Vicar-General of the North and the commissary of the Inquisition

Two Church offices at Bassein reached far beyond its walls.

The first was the Vicar-General of the North. The whole northern territory belonged to the Archdiocese of Goa, created in 1534, but Goa was far away, and a Vicar-General governed the Church of the North on the archbishop’s behalf. Hull is precise: “Down to the Mahratta invasion of 1739 his headquarters was at Bassein; after that date at Thana or Kurla, and later at Gloria Church, Bombay.” [n38; Hull I p. 6] For two centuries, then, Bassein was the ecclesiastical capital of the coast from Daman to Chaul. Bombay, which the Portuguese ceded to the English in 1661, was a dependency of this northern Church, not its centre. Bassein’s priests served Bombay, Salsette and Thana. [n38; §2 Takeaway] The research note puts it simply: the Vicar-General’s seat at Bassein until 1739, and the papal recognition at Vasai in 1986, together explain why the Church treats Vasai as the “cradle” of the Latin Catholic community around Mumbai. [n38; §2 Inferences]

The second office was darker. “There was also at Bassein a Commissary of the Inquisition,” da Cunha writes. [n38; da Cunha pp. 235–236] The Holy Office was set up at Goa in 1560; by 1580, the Gazetteer says, “agents of the Inquisition, called commissaries, were at work in Chaul, Bassein and Daman, collecting offenders and sending them for trial and punishment to Goa”. [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 463] Another passage dates the Bassein commissary from 1560 and ties him to the Jesuit college. [n32; BG 41] Prisoners were held in the jail near the Captain’s palace until “the fleet from the north” carried them to Goa each year. [n37; da Cunha pp. 235–236] An inscribed slab of the jail survived near the travellers’ bungalow in the nineteenth century; it records that the jail was built under the viceroy Pero da Silva and completed under Captain André Salema, between 1635 and 1639. [n32; BG 39 n.7] The French physician Charles Dellon, whose Relation de l’Inquisition de Goa (1688) became Europe’s most famous account of the Goa Inquisition, was held at Bassein on his way to Goa; the sources date that stay variously to 1583 (an OCR slip in da Cunha), 1673 and 1683. [n38; da Cunha pp. 235–236] [n37; §4]

The Inquisition proper tried baptised Christians, chiefly “New Christians” suspected of keeping Hindu, Muslim or Jewish practices. It was also the force behind the ban on Hindu pilgrimage to the healing tank near Our Lady of Remedies, which the Inquisition stopped “under very severe penalties”; by da Cunha’s day, in the nineteenth century, Hindus again held a jatra there. [n37; da Cunha p. 163] Its records were largely destroyed, so numbers cannot be known. [n37; Priolkar p. 60] The single best local document is a stone found in 1840 by the Goan scholar Filipe Nery Xavier, set up in 1786 where the Inquisition had razed a house at Bassein: the family who lived there, “dogmatists of the said sect”, had been “burnt in the Auto de Fé celebrated on December 30, 1747”, and their houses “demolished and ploughed with salt”. [n37; Priolkar pp. 60–61] The date is striking. By December 1747 Bassein had been Maratha for eight years. The family must have been taken from Portuguese territory elsewhere, or the stone’s wording may refer to a Bassein family tried at Goa; the research did not establish who they were or where the house stood. [n37; §4 Inferences and Gaps]

One modern source, a 1987 article in Bombay’s Catholic weekly The Examiner, states flatly that “the Inquisition was never set up in Bassein”. [n09; The Examiner 1987 via Goanet] Strictly, that is true of a tribunal: the Inquisition’s court sat only at Goa. But both da Cunha and the Gazetteer are clear that its commissary, its prison and its prisoners were at Bassein. [n38; da Cunha pp. 235–236] [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 463] The Inquisition was suppressed in 1774, restored about 1779 and finally abolished in 1812. [n38; da Cunha pp. 235–236] Da Cunha attributed its founding to Jesuit influence; later historians contest that. [n38; §2]

The Inquisition belongs in any honest account of why this coast matters to the Catholic story, because it was part of that story here. It also helps explain why, two and a half centuries later, Hindu writers in Vasai still write about “the scars of the Inquisition”. [n07; Swarajya]

A boy from Agashi: Gonsalo Garcia

Into this world, in the 1550s, a boy was born whose name would one day be read out before three hundred bishops in Rome.

His name was Gonsalo Garcia. The Catholic Encyclopedia says he was “born in Bassein … of a Portuguese father and a Canarese mother”. [n38; Catholic Encyclopedia, “Gonsalo Garcia, Saint”] Documents in the Portuguese national archive at Lisbon, the Torre do Tombo, describe him as “natural de Agaçaim”, native of Agashi, and his mother as a “Canarim”, the Portuguese word for a native of the Konkan. [n38; Indian Catholic Matters 2023; DNA 2014] His father was a Portuguese soldier who later stayed on as a civilian employee in the fort. [n07; Wikipedia: Gonsalo Garcia] He was what the Portuguese called a mestiço, a child of mixed parentage. His Indianness, as the research note puts it plainly, rests on his mother. [n07; §6 Inferences] His birth year is given as 1556 or 1557, and one source says about 1564. [n07; §6]

Where exactly he was born is disputed in a small way. The Lisbon record points to Agashi village. Tradition links him to the fort, where he studied. [n07; §6 Inferences] A third claim, made in 2021 by the Mobai Gaothan Panchayat in a community column, is that Gass is “the place St. Gonsalo Garcia was born”. [n42; Gaothan Voice, Feb 2021] That claim comes without evidence and conflicts with the documented “natural de Agaçaim”. It is recorded here as a tradition of devotion. No source disputes that he was a son of the Bassein country.

He was educated at the Jesuit college in the fort, the college Xavier had founded, and served at its Church of the Holy Name of Jesus. [n07; Wikipedia: Gonsalo Garcia] One account dates his schooling there from 1564 to 1572, under the guidance of Fr Sebastião Gonsalves. [n38; Indian Catholic Matters] Then he went to Japan. When he left is uncertain: the Archdiocese of Bombay says he went at about fifteen, which would be around 1572, while other accounts date his departure to 1580, at about twenty-four. [n38; Archdiocese of Bombay; Catholic Encyclopedia] The Archdiocese adds that he was refused admission to the Society of Jesus. [n07; Archdiocese of Bombay]

In Japan he worked for about eight years as a catechist, a lay teacher of the faith, for the Jesuit mission. [n07; Wikipedia] He learned Japanese well. He then left mission service and became a merchant, trading at Macao, the Portuguese port on the China coast. [n38; Catholic Encyclopedia] [n07; Wikipedia] From there he went to Manila in the Spanish Philippines and joined the Franciscans as a lay brother. In 1592 he returned to Japan with the Franciscan Fr Pedro Bautista as interpreter for an embassy from the Spanish governor of the Philippines to the Japanese ruler. [n07; Wikipedia; Archdiocese of Bombay] The Franciscans built churches, convents and hospitals; the sources credit him with helping to build them at Osaka and elsewhere. [n07; Archdiocese of Bombay]

In 1596 a Spanish ship, the San Felipe, was wrecked on the Japanese coast. In the crisis that followed, the regent Toyotomi Hideyoshi ordered the arrest of the Franciscans and their Japanese followers. Gonsalo was arrested on 8 December 1596. [n07; Wikipedia] On 3 January 1597 the prisoners’ ears were cut, the tips of the left ears. [n38; Catholic Encyclopedia; Archdiocese of Bombay] They were then marched about six hundred miles from Kyoto to Nagasaki, arriving on 4 February 1597. [n07; Wikipedia] On 5 February 1597, on the hill of Nishizaka above Nagasaki, twenty-six Christians were crucified: Franciscans, Jesuits and Japanese laymen, among them boys. Gonsalo Garcia was, the sources say, the first to be nailed to his cross. He was killed by two lances, still singing. He was about forty. [n38; Catholic Encyclopedia; Archdiocese of Bombay] [n07; Wikipedia]

Recognition came in stages, and the sources describe them in slightly different words. Pope Urban VIII’s bull of 14 September 1627 declared the martyrs blessed, according to da Cunha; the Catholic Encyclopedia, usually careful, says they were “declared venerable” in 1627, and that their veneration was permitted to the universal Church in 1629. [n38; da Cunha pp. 211–212; Catholic Encyclopedia] In 1745 a statue of the saint was taken to Recife in Brazil. [n07; Wikipedia] On 8 June 1862 Pope Pius IX canonised the twenty-six martyrs of Japan, before about three hundred prelates. [n38; da Cunha pp. 211–212]

Da Cunha, writing in Bombay only fourteen years later, noticed what this meant for Bassein. The Basseinese, he wrote, “have an undisputed right to flatter themselves with having one of their citizens’ names inserted in the already long file of the Flos Sanctorum”, the roll of the saints, and the Catholics of Bassein and Salsette “worship him, and none more so than the pious Basseinese”. [n38; da Cunha pp. 211–212] He added other remarks, condescending to the community, which the research advises should not be quoted as fact. [n38; §4]

Gonsalo Garcia is called India’s first saint, and every source the research found uses that label. [n38; §4 Inferences] It is accurate in the sense that he is the first canonised Catholic saint born in India. The research suggests a careful wording: the Church canonised the twenty-six together, as the Martyrs of Japan, and Garcia is the Indian-born one among them. The friars of Thana, killed on this coast in 1321, had come from Europe and have never been canonised. [n38; §4 Inferences] The Catholic Encyclopedia drew the connection between the Bassein mission and Japan more broadly, saying that the Franciscan-led conversions “caused a persecution which gave to the Church the first martyrs of Japan”. [n38; Catholic Encyclopedia, “Bassein”] What can be said firmly is narrower: the Bassein Jesuit college formed at least one missionary who went to Japan and died there. The research found no list of other Bassein-trained missionaries sent to Japan, China or the Mughal court, and treats any wider claim as unsupported. [n38; §2 Gaps]

His feast, universally, is kept on 6 February with St Paul Miki and companions, because 5 February is the feast of St Agatha. The bishops of India moved his own feast to 7 February so that he could be honoured apart from the other martyrs of Nagasaki. [n07; Archdiocese of Bombay] In Vasai his great day is kept not in February but in January, at the ruin of the very church where he studied, on “the first Sunday nearest the neap tide after Christmas”, a reckoning that belongs to a people who once came to the feast by boat. [n38; DNA 2014; FPJ 2026] But that feast belongs to a later part of the story. For a century and a half after 1739, devotion to him in Bassein lapsed. [n38; Catholic Encyclopedia, “Gonsalo Garcia, Saint”]

1739: the siege, the capitulation and the faith that stayed

In 1737 the Maratha armies of the Peshwa Bajirao, led by his brother Chimaji Appa, came down into the Portuguese North. The campaign lasted three years. In its course churches outside the walls were wrecked: Hull records that the Holy Cross church at Nirmal, built in 1580, was “destroyed by the Mahrattas in 1737”. [n38; Hull I pp. 5–6] Our Lady of Mercy at Merces was destroyed in the invasion, and its priests fled. [n09; Goanet; Vasaiker] St Bonaventure at Erangal, across the creek near Madh, was destroyed in 1739. [n07; FPJ 2026] The Holy Spirit church at Nandakhal, by contrast, is said to have survived the attacks of 1737 to 1739, which is why it is called the oldest surviving church of north Vasai. [n09; Wikipedia: Holy Spirit Church Nandakhal]

The Marathas took Arnala and put its garrison to the sword, seized the creeks and islands, and laid siege to Bassein on 17 February 1739. [n32; DC 147–148] The defenders sprang mine after mine; the final breach was made beneath the tower of São Sebastião. [n32; DC 148; BG 34] On 16 May 1739 the city surrendered. [n32; BG 34; DC 148] Da Cunha and the Gazetteer put the losses at about 800 Portuguese and “about 12,000” Marathas killed and wounded, a round nineteenth-century figure repeated from older writers. [n32; DC 148; BG 34] Among those who helped the Maratha side, a Marathi book of 1935 lists not only Pathare Kshatriyas, Brahmans, Prabhus, Somvanshi Kshatriyas, Bhandaris, Kolis and Agris, but also Christians. [n41; Naik 1935 pp. 15, 320 ff.] The book is a community history written two centuries later, but its inclusion of Christians among the helpers is a reminder that not every Christian of the coast identified with the Portuguese cause.

The terms of capitulation are the single most important document for the survival of the Church here. Hull, citing the collection of state papers called Materials, summarises them: free passage for the nobles, for Christians who wished to leave, and for all the religious orders; “liberty of Christian worship” for those who stayed; the privileges of three churches in Bassein, one in the district and one in Salsette preserved, with their curates “subject to the Primate of India”, that is, the Archbishop of Goa; and the images in the churches to remain. [n38; Hull I pp. 75–76, citing Materials I p. 213] The Gazetteer gives the same figure of five churches. [n37; Gaz. XIII-I p. 203] Mendiratta adds that the “one in the district” was in the Cassabé subdivision. [n34; Mendiratta pp. 216–218] Which church that was, the research could not establish.

Who left and who stayed is described by the Jesuit geographer Joseph Tiefenthaler, writing in 1750: “Portuguese nobles and all the regular clergy left for Goa; the ordinary people remained, a native priest taking charge of their souls with the approbation of the Mahrattas.” [n38; Hull I p. 76] Mendiratta puts it more starkly: all the Portuguese, the reinóis born in Portugal and the descendentes born in India, “fled or were expelled … leaving only the Indian converted population behind”. [n34; Mendiratta pp. 216–218] The Marathas would accept only non-white priests, so Goan priests, ordained by the secular clergy or by the Oratorians of Goa, took over the parishes, and the Church of the North was reorganised from Kurla from 1740. [n34; Mendiratta pp. 216–218] The Vicar-General moved from Bassein to Thana or Kurla. [n38; Hull I p. 6] Tiefenthaler adds that most of the Christian families of the fort later left for Bombay. [n38; Hull I p. 76]

The churches in the fort were abandoned. Hull says they were “left to ruin”, while those in the district were, over the following century, “rebuilt or restored”. [n38; Hull I p. 6] The Marathas renamed the city Bajipur. [n09; Live History India] Inside the walls, a Hanuman temple rose by the sea gate; da Cunha, in 1876, read it as a Maratha memento: “the monkey-god has kept his ascendancy”. [n32; BG 38–39; DC 214]

Then there are the bells. Chimaji Appa’s armies carried the church bells away as trophies of victory, and at least seven Portuguese-era bells now hang in Hindu temples across Maharashtra. [n09; §4] The best documented are these. At the Meneshwar temple at Menavali near Wai hangs a five-metal bell of about 650 kilograms, cast in 1707, with a relief of the Madonna and Child. [n09; Vasaiker; Atlas Obscura] At Bhimashankar hangs a bell cast in 1729, with a cross and an image of Jesus; its plaque, which says it arrived in 1729, is wrong, because it came after 1739. [n09; Vasaiker] The Baneshwar temple near Pune has a bell cast in 1683 with a cross. Bells hang too at the Bhairavnath temple at Loni Bhapkar, at Nimgaon Khandoba, and at the Durga Devi temple at Murud, the last said to be from Vasai fort. [n09; Vasaiker] The famous Naroshankar bell at Nashik is said to have come not from Vasai but from the church of Our Lady of the Immaculate Conception at Mount Poinsur, Borivali. [n09; Vasaiker] One account says the bells were carried off on elephants, and another that one hangs at the Ballaleshwar temple at Pali. [n09; Goanet 2020] Wikipedia records that Chimaji dedicated church bells to the Vajreshwari temple. [n41; Wikipedia: Chimaji Appa, not read in full] No bell has been reliably traced to a particular church inside the fort, and no source reached by the research transcribes their inscriptions. [n09; §4 Inferences and Gaps] The total number is unknown. [n09; §4]

The bells are a strange monument. They were taken as spoils of war, and to Vasai’s Catholics their loss is part of the memory of 1739. Yet they were not destroyed. They were hung in the houses of other gods and have rung there for nearly three centuries, Madonna and cross still on their bronze.

What did Maratha rule mean for the Christians who stayed? The evidence points both ways. On one side, the Marathas set out to restore a Hindu population. The Gazetteer says that “to restore a Hindu population grants of rent-free land were offered, and a tax was levied for the support of Brahmans who were brought to purify the Christians and make them fit to take their place in their old castes”. [n37; Gaz. XIV p. 34] Da Cunha dates the purification tax to the Peshwa Madhavrao, who ruled from 1761 to 1772, a generation after the conquest. [n34; Da Cunha 1876 p. 149] Some groups did go back. The Census of 1911 says the Kirpal Bhandaris were given the choice of reverting to Hinduism soon after 1739, and accepted. [n34; Census 1911 para 136] Mendiratta judges that “the Marathas probably managed to bring some Catholics back into the Hindu fold through purifying rites, and economic pressure”. [n34; Mendiratta pp. 216–218] At Nirmal the site of the destroyed linga was purified, the footprints of Dattatreya took its place, and the Sarsubhedar Shankarji Keshav built eight temples about 1750. [n37; Gaz. XIV pp. 292–293] A polemical modern writer names “Fatherwadi” as a once-Christian village that is now largely Hindu. [n07; Swarajya]

On the other side, the Christians who remained were allowed to keep their faith openly. The Gazetteer, no friend of Portuguese Catholicism, wrote: “The Marathas allowed them the freest exercise of their religion, their processions and festivals were respected, and many of the Salsette churches were built or rebuilt about this time (1760).” The native Christians, it said, “showed more constancy and the Marathas more toleration than could have been expected”. [n37; Gaz. XIII-I p. 203] Under the Vicar-General at Kurla, “the native or … Kanarin vicars managed the churches and kept the bulk of the people from forsaking Christianity”. [n34; Gaz. XIII p. 203] The French orientalist Anquetil du Perron, passing through Agashi in 1760, found “the lanes full of people going to church as freely as in a Christian country”. [n34; Gazetteer XIV pp. 1–2]

That sentence of du Perron’s is perhaps the best evidence for why the faith survived. The research note draws the conclusion: after 1739 the local Christian community survived because the Portuguese had trained a native priesthood. [n37; §5 Inferences] António do Porto’s orphan priests, the seminary for secular clergy, the sons of headmen brought up for the Church — the clergy the Marathas would accept were precisely those the mission had raised from the soil. And in Nirmal, by 1750, a Hindu revival and a Christian parish stood side by side, as they still do. [n34; §6 Inferences]

Goa’s distant children: Marathas, the Company and the quarrel of jurisdictions

After 1739 the Christians of Bassein lived under a Hindu state while belonging, in Church law, to a Portuguese archbishop. That arrangement, the Padroado, the royal patronage granted by the popes to the Portuguese crown, gave the King of Portugal the right to present bishops and parish priests across Portuguese Asia. By the eighteenth century it had collided with Rome’s own missionary office, the Congregation de Propaganda Fide, which sent its own missionaries, often Carmelites, as Vicars Apostolic to territories the Portuguese no longer ruled.

Bombay was the battlefield. Under English rule its Catholics were split between the Goan clergy and the Carmelite Vicar Apostolic. A division of the Bombay churches in 1794 created “two rival jurisdictions”. [n38; Catholic Encyclopedia, Goa] An episode from the 1720s, recorded in Hull, already shows a Bassein-born native priest, a Fr Fernandez, staying loyal to the Carmelite Vicar Apostolic while the other native priests joined the Goa party. [n38; Hull I, page not captured] The British took Salsette from the Marathas in 1774, and the Bassein country passed to them early in the nineteenth century, after the fall of the Peshwa in 1818. [n34; Mendiratta p. 219] In 1838 the papal brief Multa praeclare deprived the Padroado clergy of jurisdiction within the vicariates apostolic, and in 1839 Rome gave the Vicar Apostolic of Bombay jurisdiction over Salsette. Bassein and Chaul were exempted. [n38; Hull I; Archdiocese of Bombay; Catholic Encyclopedia, Goa] A Goan annalist quoted by Hull found it “a puzzle why Bassein and Chaul were altogether exempted, since they are united to the same regime which predominates in Salsette”. [n38; Hull I, near the 1839 documents]

The effect, as the research reads it, was that Bassein stayed outside the worst of the fight. It had been Portuguese until 1739 and then Maratha, and its parishes remained attached to Goa, while Salsette, Maratha from 1739 and British from 1774, became the ground where Goan and Carmelite parties contested parishes. [n38; §3 Inferences] The research found no source naming a particular Vasai parish contested between the two jurisdictions. [n38; §3 Gaps]

The early British years were hard. When the British took the Bassein subdivision there were, Mendiratta writes, “eight functioning churches and a Catholic population estimated at 5000”. [n34; Mendiratta p. 219] The British feared the Goa-linked clergy; a letter of 1819 complained that “they form a sort of separate caste in the middle of the community”. [n34; Mendiratta p. 220] In the cholera of 1820–21 “about 3000 Christians rejoined the Hindu fold”, which a British report blamed on the lack of “respectable ministers”. [n34; Mendiratta p. 220] The Gazetteer tells a related story: in the cholera years 1818 to 1820 and again in 1828, some Christian Kolis, “finding that they suffered as much as their Hindu neighbours, took to propitiating the goddess of cholera, and either left or were driven from the Church”. [n37; Gaz. XIII-I p. 203] With the help of Palshe Brahmans some became Hindus and were known as Uraps; the Bassein Agris who reverted in 1820 paid Rs 1,200, and their priest, Ramchandra Baba Joshi, was for a time put out of his own caste; a second group was admitted on 12 November 1828 by Vithal Hari Naik Vaidya of Bassein. [n37; Gaz. XIII-I p. 117] Only their surnames remembered the Christian past: Gomes, Soz, Fernan, Frutad, Minez. [n34; Enthoven I, Urap Agris] Others who were expelled did not become Hindus and still came to the parish churches at festivals. [n37; Gaz. XIII-I p. 203] A different explanation, from Vaupell in 1838, blamed “an attempt to extend the priestly prerogative”. [n37; Gaz. XIII-I p. 203 n.3] Christians, the Gazetteer adds, “did not receive any special encouragement from the British Government”, and some early accounts mention them “in terms of contempt”. [n34; Gaz. XIII p. 203]

And yet the nineteenth century was, in the villages, an age of rebuilding. The dates in the Gazetteer and in Hull read like a roll-call of parish effort. Our Lady of Remedies at Dhauli (Remedi) was rebuilt in 1821 for Rs 18,600 (Hull says 1839). Sandor’s St Thomas got a new chapel in 1838 and a new front in 1858, its side walls still sixteenth-century. Pali was rebuilt in 1840, Manickpur’s St Michael in 1851, Papdi’s Our Lady of Grace in 1864 or 1865. [n32; BG 36–37] [n38; Hull I pp. 5–6] At Nirmal the parishioners rebuilt Santa Cruz in 1856 at a cost of about Rs 24,000, on the ruins of the church the Marathas had wrecked. [n34; Gazetteer XIV p. 293] [n38; Hull I p. 5]

The Gazetteer of 1882 lets us see these parishes almost from the inside. In 1881 Bassein had nine churches under nine priests: Sandor (St Thomas), Papdi (Our Lady of Grace), Pali (Mother of God), Manikpur (St Michael), Davli (Our Lady of Help, or of Remedies), Nirmal (Holy Cross), Koprad (Holy Spirit), Agashi (St James) and Bassein (Our Lady of Mercy). [n34; Gaz. XIII p. 206 n.3] Nirmal had a congregation of 2,184, a vicar with a house and a monthly British grant, and a private school whose teacher played the violin in church. [n34; Gazetteer XIV p. 293] Agashi had 1,500 Christians among its 6,823 people, and a school of seventeen pupils taught Portuguese, arithmetic, singing, doctrine and music. [n34; Gazetteer XIV pp. 1–2] Remedies counted 3,238 parishioners, Sandor 1,725, Papdi 1,294, Our Lady of Mercy 974, Pali 900 and Manickpur 800. [n32; BG 36–37] In 1872 the Bassein subdivision held 13,126 Christians. [n34; Gaz. XIII p. 71] The Gazetteer judged that the Christians of the district had “more than doubled in number” in fifty years, and that “some of their villages are as rich as any in the Thana district”. [n37; Gaz. XIII-I pp. 204, 216]

The same pages record the texture of faith. Children were baptised between the eighth and fifteenth day, carried to church by their padrinho and madrinha; the mother did not attend, and was purified at church on the fortieth day. [n34; Gazetteer XIII pp. 210–211] On the third or sixth night after a birth the poorer families strewed gram at the door to ward off the spirit Sathi, the same spirit the Hindu Samvedis worshipped as Sati on the fifth day — a small, telling sign that Christian and Hindu households of the same stock had kept common customs. [n34; Gaz. XIII pp. 82, 210–211] In Bassein, unlike Salsette, the marriage proposal came from the boy’s side. [n34; Gaz. XIII p. 211 n.1] At a death the church bell was tolled, the poor were buried in a habit “in shape like a Franciscan’s”, brotherhoods attended with candles, and prayers and a meal followed on the seventh day. [n34; Gaz. XIII pp. 214–215] Sermons were in Portuguese or Marathi; Latin was the language of the Church. [n34; Gaz. XIII pp. 204, 207] Each church paid one or two music masters who played the violin. [n34; Gaz. XIII p. 207] First fruits of the rice harvest were blessed in church. [n34; Gaz. XIII pp. 207–210] In some outlying villages, the Gazetteer heard, secret offerings were still made to Ganpati and Gauri and vows to Shitladevi, which the priests disapproved. [n34; Gaz. XIII pp. 207–210] The Christa Purana, the long Marathi poem on the life of Christ by the English Jesuit Thomas Stephens, was “still well known and much read”. [n34; Gaz. XIII p. 207 n.1] Stephens is said by one source to have spent about a year at Bassein learning Marathi; his poem of 10,962 verses, completed in 1616, would later be printed in Devanagari in the East Indian dialect at Vasai itself, in 1922, and reprinted in 1927. [n38; Wikipedia: Krista Purana; Sahapedia; Indian Christian Writings blog]

In the middle of this century of rebuilding came the canonisation of 1862. Devotion to Gonsalo Garcia, the Catholic Encyclopedia says, had lapsed “from about 1739” and was now revived, with an annual feast “each year” drawing pilgrims from Bassein, Salsette and Bombay. [n38; Catholic Encyclopedia, “Gonsalo Garcia, Saint”] When da Cunha wrote in 1876 of “the pious Basseinese” and their saint, he was describing a cult barely fourteen years restored. [n38; da Cunha pp. 211–212]

A letter from Lisbon to the Governor of Goa in 1780, printed in Biker’s collection of Portuguese treaties, shows one more face of the quarrel. The Apostolic Nuncio asked the Queen to let the Archbishop of Goa grant faculties to the Vicar Apostolic of the “Mogul Empire” to govern the Christians of Baçaim and the other lands north of Goa. His reason, the letter says, was that, although those lands were under the Archbishop of Goa, “the potentates of gentilism did not want the said peoples governed in matters of religion by the said Archbishop”, and the passage breaks off in the OCR before it says what was decided. [n67 §2] It records what a Maratha-ruled Christian people looked like from Rome: a flock whose bishop belonged to a rival power.

1886, 1887, 1928: Daman, East Indians and Bombay

In 1886 Rome and Lisbon finally settled the quarrel of jurisdictions, at least on paper. The Concordat of 23 June 1886 and the bull Humanae Salutis of 1 September 1886 made Bombay an archdiocese and created a new Padroado diocese of Daman, whose territory took in Bassein and all the Portuguese-patronage northern coast except Bombay island. [n38; Hull I p. 6; Catholic Encyclopedia, Damão; Archdiocese of Bombay] Vasai’s parishes, which had belonged to Goa since 1534, now belonged to Daman. The Catholic Encyclopedia, writing early in the twentieth century, counted 71,000 Catholics, 51 churches and about 85 priests in the diocese; the Bassein district alone had twelve churches and one chapel. [n38; Catholic Encyclopedia, Damão] The Goa Annuario of 1901 counted 19,527 Catholics in the Bassein varado. [n38; Hull I pp. 5–6] The bishops of Daman tried to set up Portuguese schools between 1887 and 1925, but they “don’t appear to have been long-lasting”. [n34; Mendiratta p. 241]

The year after the Concordat, the Christians of the North Konkan renamed themselves. On 26 May 1887 about thirty community leaders met in Bombay “to change the designation ‘Bombay Portuguese’ to ‘Bombay East-Indian’”, and founded the Bombay East Indian Association with a weekly newspaper, the Bombay East Indian. [n34; Mendiratta pp. 234–236, quoting John de Mello’s recollection in the 1937 souvenir] The year was Queen Victoria’s Golden Jubilee, and the name claimed that they were the Crown’s earliest Catholic subjects, the original Catholic people of the Bombay country, as distinct from the Goan and Mangalorean Catholics who had migrated to the city. [n07; §5; Catholic Connect 2026] The 1937 souvenir called the timing a “happy coincidence”; one professor says the community petitioned the Empress, and it is unknown whether she replied. [n07; east-indians.com; The Goan 2025] The Goans answered with a Bombay Portuguese Association of their own. [n34; Mendiratta pp. 234–236]

The East Indian name was coined in Bombay, but it came to cover the villages of Bassein too. The standard academic account is Elsie Baptista’s The East Indians (1967), which the first round of research could not read and which has since been found online. Her scanned text lists five cultural groups: the Samvedi Christians, the Koli Christians, the Vadvals, the Salsette Christians and “the urbanised section”. [n60 §4] An earlier version of this chapter, following a Catholic Connect article, gave the five as Kulbis, Samvedi Christians “commonly called Kuparis”, Koli Christians, Wadvals and Salsette Christians. [n34; Catholic Connect] The text of Baptista has neither “Kulbi” nor “Kupari” in its OCR (checked with fuzzy matching too), so that list and the gloss are the article’s, not hers, unless another edition differs. [n60 §4] Mendiratta notes that the Catholics of Salsette and Bassein came to be labelled “rural” or “samvedi” East Indians. [n34; Mendiratta p. 218] The 1887 politics were Bombay-centred, and in the Bassein villages, which stayed under Padroado parishes, the name meant something different. [n34; §7 Inferences] In the 1920s the Association complained of “the neglect of education in the districts of Salsette and Bassein” and “the paucity of vocations to the priesthood among the community”; while the urban East Indians drifted toward the Archdiocese of Bombay, those of Salsette and Bassein “remained by and large under the jurisdiction of the Padroado”. [n34; Mendiratta p. 236] By 1925 there were twelve parish churches in Bassein, up from nine in 1881. [n34; Mendiratta p. 239 n.50]

In 1928 the double jurisdiction ended. The Archdiocese of Bombay’s history says simply that in 1928 the “double jurisdiction came to an end in India”; the Diocese of Vasai’s history page, as a search snippet shows it, says that Vasai was under Goa from 1535 to 1886, under Daman from 1886 to 1928, and under Bombay from 1928. [n38; Archdiocese of Bombay; Diocese of Vasai snippet] The instrument was the apostolic letter Inter Apostolicam of 1 May 1928, which dissolved the diocese of Daman, according to a Wikipedia entry the research did not check against the document. [n38; §3] [n09; Wikipedia: Sandor]

The research makes a point about these changes that is easy to miss. The moves of 1886 and 1928 each changed the bishop of a Vasai parish without changing its priest, its church or its feast. Parish registers and feast days run unbroken across both. [n38; §3 Inferences] For a family in Nirmal or Papdi, the jurisdiction quarrel that filled volumes in Bombay and Rome meant a new name in the prayers of the Mass, not a new church.

The Census of 1931 looked at the Christians of the Bassein coast with an official’s eye. “The intensive work of the last 60 to 80 years has done much to purge the Indian Christian population of Bassein of practices alien to … Roman Catholicism,” it said, though “the priests admit the Indian Christian population of Bassein and the coastal area around Bombay is more unorthodox than is desirable”. It added: “There is no evidence that this community worships at Christian altars behind which are placed Hindu gods.” [n34; Census 1931 Vol VIII Part I pp. 363–364] Those lines were written about the fishing coast more than the northern gardens, but they record a Church that, after four centuries, was still in conversation with the older religious world around it.

The ten churches of the Bassein district, 1889–91

The first village-by-village count of the Bassein churches in the notes comes from a bishop’s visit. Antonio Pedro da Costa, Bishop of Damão, spent nearly two months, from 30 November 1889 into early January 1890, on a pastoral visitation of the district, and set down what he found in his report on the new diocese, printed in 1892. [n60 §2] He wrote of a district once known as “the Court of the North”, which by then had “ten churches” under the Portuguese Royal Padroado. Its old capital was “entirely abandoned, inhabited only by some thousands of snakes of various kinds”, with a few employees of the English Government guarding the ruins and allowing nothing to be carried off. Several things stand out. The Marathi schools of the English Government appear in nearly every parish: five in Nirmal, four at Nandakhal, three at Papdi, two each at Mercês and Agashi, and one each at Manekpur, Palle, Sandor and Tarapur, with a Gujarati school at Agashi paid for by subscription and English schools at Papdi (by subscription) and Tarapur. Only Remédios is described just as having “others of the Government”. [n60 §2] Where the report names a vicar’s birthplace it is Goa, or Bardez in Goa. Most vicars drew a stipend of 174 rupees a year from the English Government. [n60 §2]

The furnishings of the old city had gone to the villages. The altarpieces at Manekpur, Nirmal, Palle, Sandor and Agashi, the bishop says, came from the old churches and convents of Baçaim, and the pulpit at Manekpur, “the best of all the churches of Bacaim”, with them. Manekpur’s church was the largest in the district. [n60 §2] At Nirmal the bishop’s dates match the Hull and plaque dates in Chapter 9: a first church of 1580, destroyed in 1737, and a new one built in 1856; the cemetery of the first church, a quarter of an hour away, still had an altar where Mass was sometimes said. [n60 §2] Nandakhal was, he says, “the only one that exists as the Portuguese built it, and which by a superstition was spared the destruction of the Marathas”, a reason the report does not explain. [n60 §2] At Papdi he sang a pontifical Mass on 27 December 1891 to mark the end of repairs, “the most remarkable of all that have been celebrated in Baçaim in modern times”, and noted that Papdi was the only church in the district keeping the Good Friday Passo, a procession which drew people of every religion. [n60 §2] At Tarapur, the poorest parish, he found a graveyard with no wall, where wild animals dug up the dead by day, until the villagers built one with a grant from the mission fund; and at the filial chapel of Our Lady of the Angústias at Danhu, built about 1570 and famed for miracles, “gentios e outros de diversas religiões”, non-Christians and others of various religions, celebrated the bishop’s visit as their own. [n60 §2]

Gass has no entry. It was not a parish until 1942, and its people were counted within Nirmal’s 2,291. [n60 §2; n42]

New parishes for old villages: Giriz, Gass, Bhuigaon

In the twentieth century the old parish map began to divide. Villages that had walked to a distant mother church for three or four centuries built churches of their own.

The first was Giriz. Its foundation stone was laid on 14 February 1917, and the villagers built the church of St Francis Xavier in about a year, the parish history says, “without foreign aid”. Before that Giriz had belonged to the parishes of Sandor and Merces. Its first parish priest was Fr Augusto D’Souza, and its feast is kept on 3 December, the feast of Xavier, the saint who had walked these lanes. [n09; Wikipedia: St Francis Xavier’s Church, Giriz]

The second, in 1942, was Gass. The church of St Gonsalo Garcia at Gass “was built in 1942 and later expanded in 1957”, according to the church directories. [n42; Catholics India] [n38; Catholics India] One source, an essay on the saint published in 2023, says it was built by Msgr Louis Caetan D’Souza; the research for the Gass chapter of this book found no parish chronicle and no list of priests, so his role rests on that single report. [n38; Indian Catholic Matters] [n42; §5 Gaps] Gass was carved out of the parish of Nirmal, the Holy Cross church whose priest had served the northern garden villages since the 1550s. [n42; D’Mello blog] [n07; Catholics India; Wikipedia: Diocese of Vasai] Why a parish was founded at Gass in 1942, in the middle of the Second World War, no source states. Distance from Nirmal and a growing congregation are the likely reasons, and Bhuigaon, a few years later, was founded for exactly that reason. [n42; §5 Inferences] The choice of patron, though, speaks for itself. Eighty years after the canonisation, a Kupari village of the old island of Sopara dedicated its church to the boy from Agashi, the saint of its own soil. [n42; §5 Inferences] It is the only church in the diocese named for him, and the diocese calls it the “major shrine” of the saint. [n38; Catholics India; §4] The research found no source for the “national shrine” status sometimes claimed for it, and treats that as unconfirmed. [n38; §4 Inferences] Its feast is a week-long celebration in February, around the saint’s day. [n09; Prayers & Petitions listing]

The third was Bhuigaon, where the Holy Family church was founded in 1946 because Bhuigaon families had had to travel to Nirmal. [n09; travelindiadestinations] [n42; §3] Bhuigaon’s landscape shows how the Church entered the shape of a Kupari village. Behind a steel cross lie about forty large irrigation wells, the bhowkals, that water the bananas, vegetables and flowers. A blog written from inside one Bhuigaon family in 2011 describes the cross as working like a gaondevi, a village guardian, a protector and a territorial marker for the extended family, and says crosses are also fixed on front doors. The church, the same family says, granted the land the community farmed. [n07; Mumbai Paused 2011] That is one family’s account, but it catches something true of the whole Kupari belt: the cross does in these villages much of the work that a shrine of the village goddess does in a Hindu village.

The pattern continued after Independence. The Holy Spirit church at Nandakhal, rebuilt in 1907–10 with government timber and four metal pillars, extended in 1920, given a new sacristy and residence blessed by Cardinal Simon Pimenta on 27 November 1983, celebrated its 425th anniversary in 1996–97. By 2016 it counted 2,150 families and 7,339 members across 29 hamlets. [n09; Wikipedia: Holy Spirit Church Nandakhal] Its daughters were Umrale (1970), Nanbhat (1 June 1975), Rajodi (opened 1 May 1999), Bolinj (a centre from 1988, a parish from 10 June 2001) and Jeladi (a centre in 2006). [n09; Wikipedia: Holy Spirit Church Nandakhal] Giriz celebrated its golden jubilee in 1968 and replaced its twin spires in 1981. [n09; Wikipedia: Giriz] Across the creek, St Bonaventure at Erangal, destroyed in 1739, was repaired in the 1950s. [n07; FPJ 2026] The Jesuit church in the fort, roofless since 1739, was given a temporary roof each year for the Mass on the feast of St Gonsalo Garcia; The Examiner described it so in 1987. [n09; The Examiner 1987]

9 February 1986: a pope at Vasai

In February 1986 Pope John Paul II made a pilgrimage to India. On 9 February, the first stop of his visit to the Bombay region was not Bombay but Vasai. [n38; Vatican: Address at Vasai, 9 Feb 1986]

He spoke at St Augustine High School. The Vatican text of his address survives, and in it he said what no pope had said before about this coast. Vasai, he said, was the place “where the Christian faith was first proclaimed and Christian communities were first established” in this region, and from here “the Franciscans and the Jesuits, the Dominicans and the Augustinians, went forth” to the villages and towns. [n38; Vatican: Address at Vasai] And he named the local saint: “Saint Gonsalo Garcia, the young native Franciscan of Vasai, who was martyred together with his confreres in Japan.” [n38; Vatican: Address at Vasai]

The words matter for the argument of this chapter. A pope, speaking in Vasai, recognised it as the starting point of the faith in the Bombay region and named its son as a saint of India. The research note concludes that this papal recognition, together with the Vicar-General’s seat at Bassein until 1739, explains why the Church treats Vasai as the cradle of the Latin Catholic community around Mumbai. [n38; §2 Inferences]

The papal visit came in a decade of new institutions. St Gonsalo Garcia College in Vasai was founded in 1984, affiliated to the University of Mumbai. [n38; Wikipedia: St Gonsalo Garcia College] In 1983 Fr Francis D’Britto, a priest of the Vasai villages, became editor of Suvarta, the Marathi Catholic monthly, which he would edit until 2007. [n07; Wikipedia: Francis D’Britto] And in the 1980s and 1990s, as builders moved on the green belt north of Mumbai, the same priest would found the movement known as Harit Vasai. [n07; Sabrang; FPJ] Those stories belong to the next movements of this chapter.

The Diocese of Vasai, 1998

On 22 May 1998 Pope John Paul II, by the decree Cum ad aeternam, separated the northern part of the Archdiocese of Bombay and erected the Diocese of Vasai. The decision was announced on 28 June 1998. [n38; Archdiocese of Bombay; Wikipedia: Diocese of Vasai] It is a diocese, not an archdiocese, a suffragan of the metropolitan see of Bombay. [n07; §7] [n09; correction] Its cathedral is Our Lady of Grace at Papdy, the old Jesuit parish of Nossa Senhora da Graça, founded in 1574 according to GCatholic and declared a cathedral on the day the first bishop was installed. [n09; GCatholic; Wikipedia: Diocese of Vasai] Its principal patron is St Gonsalo Garcia. [n26; Diocese of Vasai: history] [n38; GCatholic]

The first bishop was Thomas Dabre. He was born on 23 October 1945 at Bhuigaon, the Kupari village whose families had once walked to Nirmal for Mass, and was ordained priest on 31 October 1971. [n34; Catholic-Hierarchy] He was made an auxiliary bishop of Bombay (in 1990 according to Catholic-Hierarchy, 1988 according to the diocese) with charge of the Vasai region, and was installed as first Bishop of Vasai on 15 August 1998, India’s Independence Day, at the cathedral in Papdy. [n38; Archdiocese of Bombay] [n26; Diocese of Vasai: history] [n34; Catholic-Hierarchy] No source found says in so many words that he is a Kupari; that is an inference from his birthplace. [n34; §8] He was transferred to the Diocese of Poona on 4 April 2009 (the diocese’s history page says 2008). [n26; Diocese of Vasai: history; Wikipedia] He also spoke publicly about the building boom that was swallowing the region’s villages. [n38; AsiaNews]

The second bishop was Felix Machado, born in 1948 at Remedy, the village of the old shrine of Our Lady of Remedies. [n38; Wikipedia: Felix Machado] [n27; Wikipedia: VVCMC] His career carried a Vasai son to the centre of the universal Church. From 1999 to 2008 he was under-secretary of the Pontifical Council for Interreligious Dialogue in Rome, and he helped to organise the Day of Prayer for Peace at Assisi in 2002. He became Bishop of Nashik in 2008, was named Bishop of Vasai on 10 November 2009 and installed on 19 December 2009, and holds the personal title of archbishop. [n38; Wikipedia: Felix Machado; GCatholic] His tenure in Vasai ended on 6 June 2024. [n38; GCatholic] In August 2023 it was he who condemned the attack on the church at Gass. [n42; §5]

The third bishop is Thomas D’Souza, born at Chulne in south Vasai, who had served as rector of the Shrine of Our Lady of Remedies from 2013 to 2019. He was named bishop on 9 November 2024 and consecrated on 15 December 2024. [n38; Fides] [n27; Wikipedia: VVCMC] Above him, in the metropolitan see of Bombay, Archbishop John Rodrigues succeeded Cardinal Oswald Gracias on 25 January 2025. [n38; Catholic Connect]

The diocese is small in share and dense in institutions. As of 31 December 2022, according to GCatholic (page updated 15 November 2026), it had 117,026 Catholics, 2.6 per cent of a population of 4,439,027, in 49 parishes and 4 missions, with 185 priests (102 diocesan, 83 religious), 458 religious (103 brothers and 355 sisters), 4 seminarians, and an area of 7,596 square kilometres. [n38; GCatholic] Other sources give different figures: Catholic-Hierarchy about 137,600 for 2022; Catholics India, undated, 136,800 Catholics in 45 parishes; Vatican Radio in 2009 about 122,000 Catholics, 139 priests and 499 religious; Wikipedia 135,677 in 2010. [n38; §6] The research cautions that the apparent fall from about 136,000 to 117,000 more likely reflects different sources and methods than a real decline, and that like should be compared with like. [n38; §6 Inferences] The Census of 2011 counted about 8.25 per cent of Vasai taluka’s 1,343,402 people as Christians, about 110,800, a figure that includes every Christian group, Kuparis, Vadvals, Kolis, Goan and other migrants, and Protestants. [n34; Census 2011 via Wikipedia]

In 2009 Vatican Radio counted 4 hospitals, 3 senior and 2 junior colleges, 23 high schools and 56 primary schools under the diocese, with orphanages, homes for the aged, dispensaries and leprosy homes. [n38; Vatican Radio 2009] Catholics India lists more, including 8 degree colleges, 82 high schools and 4 minor seminaries. [n38; Catholics India] The Cardinal Gracias Memorial Hospital at Bangli, Sandor, is a Catholic hospital of 147 beds. [n38; search snippet] Remedy has a minor seminary. [n38; Fides] About 7,000 to 8,000 women belong to the sodality federation named for Our Lady of Remedy. [n09; Diocese of Vasai, snippet] In 2020, St Gonsalo Garcia College became a 750-bed COVID-19 care centre. [n38; Wikipedia: St Gonsalo Garcia College] Vocations are not what they were: in July 2025 the diocese held its first diaconate ordination in five years. [n38; Catholic Connect]

Bishops from the villages

One measure of what this coast has given the wider Church is the list of bishops born in its villages. The Wikipedia page of the Vasai-Virar City Municipal Corporation, the only source for most of the list, names: Thomas Dabre of Bhuigaon, first Bishop of Vasai; Felix Machado of Remedy, second Bishop of Vasai; Thomas D’Souza of Chulne, third Bishop of Vasai; Ivan Pereira of Papdy, a bishop in Jammu and Kashmir; Simon Almeida of Gass, Bishop of Poona; Elias Gonsalves of Chulne, Archbishop of Nagpur; and Malcolm Sequeira of Giriz, Bishop of Amravati. The same list names Sr Nandita Pereira of Nandakhal as Superior General of the Missionary Sisters of the Queen of Apostles in Vienna. [n27; Wikipedia: VVCMC] The titles on that page were not individually checked and may have changed. [n27] Malcolm Sequeira’s birth at Giriz in 1961 is also given by search snippets. [n38; §6]

The newest name on the list is a son of Gass. Simon Almeida was born on 4 November 1960 at Gass (Wikipedia spells it “Ghas”; the Diocese of Poona writes “Gass, Bhatori, Vasai”). He went to the St Gonsalo Garcia Primary School in his village and to the Holy Cross High School at Nirmal, the mother parish, entered the Prakash Bhavan minor seminary at Pune in 1978, and was ordained priest for the Diocese of Poona on 1 April 1990. [n42; Wikipedia: Simon Almeida; Diocese of Poona] On 1 November 2025 Pope Leo XIV named him Bishop of Poona, and on 17 January 2026 he was ordained bishop and installed by Cardinal Filipe Neri Ferrão. His motto is “To make all things new in Christ”. [n42; Wikipedia: Simon Almeida] [n38; Fides] His elder brother, Fr Peter Almeida, is a priest of the Diocese of Vasai and was parish priest of Gass in 2023. [n42; §5–6]

There is a symmetry in the Poona succession worth noticing. Thomas Dabre of Bhuigaon left Vasai to become Bishop of Poona in 2009; Simon Almeida of Gass was installed in the same see in 2026. (The notes do not say who held it in between, and this chapter does not guess.) Two boys of neighbouring Kupari villages of the old island of Sopara, from parishes carved out of Nirmal in the 1940s, became bishops of the same diocese across the Western Ghats. The research found no source counting the priests and sisters born in Vasai who serve the Church across India and abroad. [n38; §6 Gaps] The bishops are only the visible part of that number.

A priest of Marathi letters and the green belt: Francis D’Britto

If the bishops show what Vasai gave the hierarchy of the Church, one priest shows what its Catholics gave the Marathi world around them.

Fr Francis D’Britto was born in Vasai on 4 December 1943 (some reports say 1942) and died there on 25 July 2024. [n38; Wikipedia: Francis D’Britto] He was a Franciscan, a member of the order that had been the first to preach on this coast in 1534. [n26; Wikipedia; Sabrang] He edited the diocesan Marathi monthly Suvarta, “Good News”, from 1983 to 2007; the diocese calls it its “premier Marathi monthly”. [n07; Wikipedia] [n38; FPJ] His great work was the Subodh Bible, a Marathi translation of the scriptures with introductions and about two hundred illustrations, published in 2010 after thirteen or fifteen years of work, as reports differ. [n38; Wikipedia; Vatican Radio 2010] [n29; FPJ 2024] Some sources describe it as a translation of the New Testament. [n26; Wikipedia] It won the Maharashtra state translation award in 2013 and the Sahitya Akademi Award in 2014, reported as the first Bible translation ever to win India’s national literary award. [n38; Wikipedia: Francis D’Britto] In 2015 he received the Dnyanoba–Tukaram Puraskar, an award named for the two great saint-poets of Marathi devotion. [n38; Wikipedia] On 22 September 2019 he was elected president of the 93rd Akhil Bharatiya Marathi Sahitya Sammelan, the national assembly of Marathi letters, held at Osmanabad in January 2020; he was described as the first Christian elected to that office. [n07; Wikipedia] [n38; GKToday]

His work stands at the end of a long line. The Gazetteer of 1882 noted that the Christians of Thana spoke Marathi as their home tongue, “very little different from the Marathi spoken by the Kunbis and Kolis of the district”, and heard sermons in Portuguese or Marathi. [n34; Gaz. XIII pp. 204, 207] Thomas Stephens’s Kristapurana of 1616 had already put the Gospel into Marathi verse, and it was printed at Vasai in 1922. [n38; Wikipedia: Krista Purana; Indian Christian Writings] A Catholic priest from the villages of the old Portuguese North, presiding over the Marathi literary world four centuries after the conversions, closes a circle: the community that had been told in 1887 to find a new English name for itself had become, in its own language, a voice of Marathi letters. The research could not establish when Marathi entered the Mass in Vasai; the only evidence it found is Marathi and East Indian dialect printing. [n38; §6 Gaps] The All-India Christian Marathi Literary Conference, which held its twenty-seventh meeting at Nashik, gave Suvarta’s former editor Fr Francis Correa a lifetime award. [n38; FPJ]

D’Britto’s other work was on the land. In the 1980s and 1990s, as builders and planners moved north from Mumbai into the plantain gardens, flower fields and fishing creeks of Vasai, he founded and led the Harit Vasai Sanrakshan Samiti, the committee to protect a green Vasai, against what he called “ecological plunder”. [n07; Wikipedia; Sabrang] The movement fought to protect villages, farms, forests and fishing grounds from urbanisation. [n38; FPJ; Sabrang] It won a ban on tankers drawing water from the green belt and opposed the regional development authority’s draft plan. [n26; Sabrang; CAT] An advocate in the movement said that “the development plan was changed and villages were excluded from the urbanisation plans”; after D’Britto’s death another, Noel Dabre, said: “The greenery that you see now is because of the movement.” The state limited development to 1.5 kilometres on either side of the railway line. [n38; FPJ] [n29; FPJ 25 Jul 2024]

About three thousand people attended his funeral at Nandakhal. [n38; FPJ] [n29; FPJ 2024] It is a fitting place. Nandakhal’s Holy Spirit church is the one said to have survived the Maratha attacks of 1737 to 1739; the priest who had defended the villages against a newer kind of invasion was buried from it.

The faith in the villages: crosses, feasts and carols

The Catholic story of Vasai is told in this chapter mostly through bishops, orders and dates. But it has always lived first in the villages, in a calendar of feasts and a landscape of crosses.

Crosses stand in almost every gaothan, every old village settlement, of Vasai. At each cross’s annual feast the villagers gather to say the rosary. [n09; VasaiCity] A blog of 2015 names some of them: the Community Cross at Cross Naka in Agashi; the cross at St Peter’s Church in Koliwada, the fishing quarter; a cross on the way to Vasai court; the Community Cross at Bangli Naka; the Milagres Cross near the church at Remedi, called miraculous, where devotees are often seen praying; the Parvati Cross toward Vasai station; and the Furtado family cross at Kirawali–Chobare. [n09; Vasai Catholics blog 2015] The tradition, a local site notes, is older than the state of Maharashtra. [n09; VasaiCity] At Bhuigaon the steel cross beside the wells serves, in one family’s telling, as protector and boundary marker; crosses are fixed on front doors. [n07; Mumbai Paused] Among the demands that East Indian leaders put to the Archbishop of Bombay in June 2024 was the preservation of the crucifixes and the gaothans. [n34; FPJ 16 Jun 2024]

The Kupari community’s own vocabulary for the sacraments and the rites of life, recorded by a community blog in 2009, is a map of how the Portuguese parish entered a Marathi-speaking village. Baptism is baltim; confession komsar; First Communion cominyao; confirmation chrisma. A wedding passes through hamdao, lagin and varad, and the celebration after it is saudio. A funeral is yentar, and the seventh-day Mass, saatvoh, is followed by a meal with meat and drink. A new house begins with paya bharni, when jewellery is buried in the foundation and a coconut broken, which the blog likens to the Hindu bhoomi pujan, and is blessed at the bejimat. Lent is kept with the forty-day fast, upas, and the candle-lit procession through the villages called pirsao or miravnuk. [n07; D’Mello blog] These terms come from a community source and are not confirmed by any period text. [n34; §5] But they are consistent with what the Gazetteer saw in 1882: the godparents, the bell, the brotherhoods, the seventh-day prayers. [n34; Gaz. XIII pp. 210–215]

The great feasts double as fairs. The largest crowd of the diocesan year gathers in January at the fort. [n38; §5] On Sunday 11 January 2026 the feast of St Gonsalo Garcia was celebrated in the ruined Church of the Holy Name, the Jesuit church inside Vasai Fort, one of the seven churches that once stood within its walls. The feast and its fair are called locally Killa cha Sann, “the fort’s festival”. Fr Peter Fernandez presided before hundreds of worshippers. [n07; FPJ 11 Jan 2026] The Archaeological Survey of India, which protects the fort, allows a Sunday service there. [n07; FPJ] Families arrive the evening before and camp in enclosures, cooking East Indian moile and stews of meat and vegetables such as kohlrabi; pilgrims once came by animal cart or by boat. [n09; FPJ 2026] In 2014 a newspaper expected “thousands” and noted that the young people of the Koliwada decorated the old church for prayer meetings; that year marked 152 years since the canonisation. [n07; DNA 2014] The same Sunday in 2026, across the creek at Erangal, St Bonaventure’s church, built in 1575, destroyed in 1739 and repaired in the 1950s, kept its own feast, with families camping overnight. “People from Vasai and Versova came by boat,” one villager recalled. [n07; FPJ 2026] The feast at the fort is set by the tide, “the first Sunday nearest the neap tide after Christmas”, a reckoning older than any road. [n07; DNA 2014]

The East Indian community also keeps a “St Gonsalo Garcia Day” with veneration and cultural events. [n09; FPJ] And in 2023 the clergy of Vasai told a Mumbai newspaper that more people ought to know about “India’s first saint”. [n09; Mid-day 2023]

Christmas belongs to the carollers. Churches hold services and choirs, and singers go from door to door through the villages. [n09; FPJ] East Indian families sing and dance to traditional songs played on the ghumat, the clay drum. [n07; DNA, search summary] A Vasai chef described the East Indian Christmas table in 2025: roast chicken stuffed with offal, peas and croutons; fugias, sweetish puffed fried bread; pork indyal, suckling pig, beef tongue, vindaloo and sorpotel made with sugarcane vinegar; fruit cream, guava cheese, coconut rava cake, nevri and spiced wine, all seasoned with the dry “bottle masala” that marks East Indian cooking. [n07; Slurrp 2025] The Kupari table adds fogey, the round Portuguese bread, dodal, fuddi, a pork feijoada, sanna, rice bread, and the fish curries of the creeks. [n07; D’Mello blog]

The songs are fading. The Nandakhal folk songs, also called “Kupari songs”, sang of the bride leaving her father, of women fetching fodder, of the groom’s first shave in the days when boys married young, and of a daughter coming home after rice planting; they “are rarely heard as lifestyles have changed”. [n07; Vasaiker] South Vasai’s music comes from the Vadval community, which one local writer calls “more fun-loving” than the “more conservative Kupari community”, and wedding bands still play. [n07; Vasaiker] The red cap and red-checked sari once worn by Kupari men and women have “largely disappeared from everyday life”. [n34; All About Vasai Virar 2026] (The claim, made in polemical writing, that the Portuguese imposed those red caps on converts as a mark is not supported by the 1882 Gazetteer, which records red broadcloth caps on Hindu Samvedi men as well. [n34; §5 Inferences])

The language of worship is itself a live question. In June 2024 the Mobai Gaothan Panchayat, an East Indian body, asked Cardinal Gracias to use the East Indian dialect of Marathi in the Mass and in hymns, and to promote devotion to St Gonsalo Garcia. [n34; FPJ 16 Jun 2024] A priest from Uttan, Fr Victor Dalmet, composes hymns in that dialect. [n34; FPJ 2023] In 2024 three researchers published a Kadodi–Marathi–English dictionary, “Suman”, built from native speakers of the Kupari dialect. [n34; ACL Anthology 2024]

The faith in the villages has also known fear in these years. On 11 August 2023 vandals broke into the St Gonsalo Garcia church at Gass. They defaced the altar, threw down the statue of Our Lady and scattered the hymn books. A complaint was filed at Manickpur police station; the lack of CCTV cameras hampered the police, and the parish decided to install them. [n42; UCA News; AsiaNews] Archbishop Felix Machado condemned the attack. [n42; §5] Vatican News, reporting it in German, described the church’s patron as “India’s first saint”. [n38; Vatican News 2023] It remained, in November 2026, the most recent attack on a Vasai church the research found. [n38; §6] It should be set beside the words of a Hindu writer from Vasai, otherwise sharply critical of the Portuguese conversions, who wrote in 2019 that he knew of no communal incident in his own lifetime. [n07; Swarajya]

Jubilee 2025 and the Year of St Francis

The most recent chapter of the story was written in the last two years.

The Catholic Church kept 2025 as a Jubilee Year, a holy year of pilgrimage. The Diocese of Vasai opened it on 29 December 2024 at Our Lady of Grace Cathedral, Papdy. [n38; Catholic Connect] On 4 March 2025 Bishop Thomas D’Souza blessed Mini Jubilee Crosses, one for each parish, as a substitute for distant pilgrimages. [n38; Catholic Connect] It was an apt gesture in a country of crosses. Every village that already had its wayside cross now received a jubilee cross too; the pilgrimage came to the parish instead of the parish going on pilgrimage. The research found no list of official Jubilee pilgrim churches for Vasai beyond the cathedral opening and the parish crosses. [n38; §5 Gaps]

On 10 January 2026 Pope Leo XIV opened a Year of St Francis, to run until 10 January 2027, for the eight-hundredth anniversary of the death of Francis of Assisi. [n38; Catholic Connect] The next day, 11 January 2026, Vasai kept the feast of St Gonsalo Garcia, a Franciscan lay brother, in the ruin of the Jesuit church where he had been a schoolboy. [n07; FPJ 2026] On 17 January a son of Gass was installed as Bishop of Poona. [n42; Wikipedia: Simon Almeida] And on 7 June 2026 the Diocese of Vasai designated St Joseph’s Church at Umralla (Umrale), one of the daughter parishes of Nandakhal founded in 1970, as a Pilgrim Centre for the Year of St Francis, at which the faithful may gain a plenary indulgence. [n38; Catholic Connect] [n09; Wikipedia: Holy Spirit Church Nandakhal]

The Franciscan year closes a long arc. The friars of Francis’s order were the first to come to this coast. Three Franciscans, Thomas, James and Peter, died at Thana in 1321, and another, Odoric, carried their bones from the grave at Sopara to China and Italy. António do Porto, a Franciscan, opened the Portuguese mission at Agashi in 1535. Gonsalo Garcia died a Franciscan at Nagasaki in 1597. Francis D’Britto, a Franciscan, gave the Marathi language its award-winning Bible and the villages their green belt. In 2026 a village church in the old Kupari belt, in the land of the island of Sopara, became a place of pilgrimage in Francis’s name.

Why this coast matters

Why, then, does this coast matter to the Catholic faith, in India and beyond?

Not because of size. The Diocese of Vasai holds about 117,000 Catholics, 2.6 per cent of the people within its boundaries. [n38; GCatholic] It matters, the research note concludes, out of all proportion to its numbers. [n38; §6 Inferences]

It matters, first, because of what is old here, and the chapter has tried to be honest about which old things are evidence. The apostolic traditions of Thomas and Bartholomew are tradition. The bishop at Kalliana in the sixth century is evidence, from a merchant who had been there. The church of St Thomas at Sopara and its ninety communicants in 1321 are evidence, from the friar who served them. The grave of the Thana martyrs at Sopara ties the coast to Tolentino and to the Latin Church’s first communities in China. [n38; §1]

It matters, second, because Bassein was for two centuries the ecclesiastical capital of the Portuguese North. Franciscans from 1534, Jesuits with Xavier’s college from 1548, Dominicans from 1583, Augustinians from about 1596; sixty-three religious in 1634; a Vicar-General of the North whose seat was Bassein until 1739, and whose successors, moving to Kurla and Bombay, carried the Bassein Church into the city that would become one of Asia’s great Catholic centres. [n38; §2] The same city held a commissary of the Inquisition, and its history includes temples thrown down and a pilgrimage banned under severe penalties. Both belong to the record.

It matters, third, because of one man. Gonsalo Garcia, born of a Portuguese soldier and a Konkan mother, schooled in Xavier’s college, crucified at Nagasaki on 5 February 1597, canonised on 8 June 1862, is the first canonised saint born in India. He is the patron of the Diocese of Vasai and the second patron of the Archdiocese of Bombay, and of the East Indian community. [n38; §4] [n07; Wikipedia] A pope named him at Vasai in 1986.

It matters, fourth, because the faith survived. The capitulation of 1739 protected Christian worship and five churches, the native priests stayed when the Portuguese left, and the Marathas, as the British Gazetteer admitted, showed “more toleration than could have been expected”. [n37; Gaz. XIII-I p. 203] The Church of Vasai lived through Maratha rule, the cholera reversions, a century of Padroado and Propaganda, and the jurisdictions of Goa, Daman and Bombay, and in 1998 it became a diocese of its own.

And it matters, finally, because of the people. Kuparis, Christian Vadvals, Koli Christians, Kunbis and the other families who in 1887 chose the name East Indian have kept the faith in their own languages and their own villages for nearly five centuries, with crosses at the village edge and violins and later brass bands in the church, with tide-timed feasts and Christmas carols, with priests and sisters sent across India and the world, and with bishops from Bhuigaon, Remedy, Chulne, Papdy, Giriz and Gass. Whatever the mix of faith and interest that first brought their ancestors to the font, and the sources cannot settle that, what they made of it afterwards is their own.

In the old island of Sopara, between the Gass landing and the Nirmal hill, the four layers of this book’s story lie almost on top of one another: the Buddhist stupa mound, the temple stones reused in house plinths, the church of Holy Cross, and the temples rebuilt at Nirmal in 1750. The next chapter, the epilogue, gathers those layers together and asks what, in the end, the coast has kept.
