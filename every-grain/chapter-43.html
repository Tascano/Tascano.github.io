Prologue: a bunch of plantains on a booth by the sea

Late in the year, between October and January, on the sandy edge of the coast near Agashi, a ripe bunch of plantains is lowered into a basket lined with rice straw. It sits there for six or seven days, warming itself from within. Then the fruit is peeled and laid out “on a booth close to the sea shore”, turned to the sun all day, heaped in the evening under dry plantain leaves and a mat, and smeared with clarified butter. The next morning the same again, and the next, for seven days. At the end there is a dark, soft, chewy fruit that keeps for months and travels well: the “well-known Bassein dried-plantain”, made from the rajeli variety, and made, the government compilers of 1882 were careful to say, “only in the villages of Agashi, Vagholi, Vatar, Bolinj, Koprad, Nala, Umrale, Rajodi, and Murdes, all in Bassein” [n48; n21; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 292].

That description is the most precise recipe in the whole official record of this coast, and it is worth pausing on who was doing the work. About eighty-five families of Samvedi Brahmans, fifteen of Pachkalshis and about seventy-five of “Native Christians”, most of them converted Samvedis and Pachkalshis themselves, dried plantains at Agashi, Vatar and Koprad [n21; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 395; n34]. Hindu and Christian households, one origin and two faiths, laying the same fruit on the same sea-booths under the same sun. Agashi alone turned out about 160 tons a year, worth some Rs 27,000 [n21; n48; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 292]. Its dried plantains were, in the Gazetteer’s words, “the best in the district” [n48; Gazetteer XIV p. 2].

Today the same fruit is called sukeli, and in the market at Vasai a small packet wrapped in banana leaf can still be found by those who know to ask, though one local site says it has largely disappeared from the stalls [n50 §5]. Between the booth by the sea and the leaf-wrapped packet lies the whole story of how this coast has eaten: what grew here from the start, what came across the sea and over the ghats, what each community put on its plate and what it refused, what it drank, and how it feasted.

This chapter tells that story in time order, as far as the evidence allows. Then it walks through today’s kitchens, community by community, and ends where a visitor can eat and buy. Throughout, one honesty rule holds. Some of what follows rests on primary documents: the Periplus of the Erythraean Sea, the Bombay Presidency Gazetteer of 1882 for Thana, Grierson and Enthoven. Some rests on journalism and cookbooks. Some rests only on what communities say of themselves, passed down in kitchens and on community websites. Each claim says which. Where there is nothing at all, the chapter says so.

The image behind this book is a rice pot: a single vessel in which many grains, from many fields, cook together and become one meal. On this coast, that is not only a metaphor. It is lunch.

1. The oldest table

Rice, fish and the palm

What did the people of ancient Sopara eat? The honest answer begins with what we do not have. No archaeobotanical study of Sopara, no charred grain or fish bone published from its excavations, no list of rice or millet or coconut remains, has turned up in the research behind this book [n50 §2]. Bhagwanlal Indraji dug the great stupa in 1882 and found relics, not kitchens [n50 §2]. So the oldest table has to be laid from indirect evidence: the trade texts, the shape of the land, and what the first detailed records, much later, show people eating in ways that look very old.

The land itself sets the menu. Chapter after chapter of this book has described the coast as a strip of alluvial soil about three miles broad, between salt marsh and creek on one side and the rising ground toward the hills on the other, watered by shallow wells, ponds and, in the rainy season, by the monsoon itself [n21; Gazetteer XIII-2 p. 679]. In 1880-81, of about 36,541 tilled acres of government land in Bassein, rice covered 29,687 acres; ragi (nachni) 1,846; the hill millet vari 338 [n21; Gazetteer XIII-2 p. 680]. These are nineteenth-century figures, but nothing in them depends on the nineteenth century. Paddy in the wet flats, millets on the poorer upland soil, fish from creek and sea, coconut and palmyra on the sandy ridges: this is the oldest pattern the evidence lets us see, and every later layer is laid on top of it.

The 1882 Gazetteer describes one telling ladder of rice. The Agris ate nagle, “coarse rice”, every day, and “on holidays finer rice and perhaps a fowl” [n50 §1; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 116]. The Pachkalshis lived “on coarse and fine rice, rice bread, pulse, vegetables, and fish” [n50 §1; Gazetteer XIII-1 pp. 129-130]. The Bhandaris ate “rice, rice bread, and rice broth, ambil” [n50 §1; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 150]. Rice boiled, rice ground into bread, rice thinned into broth: the grain of this book’s title was the grain of daily life, and the difference between ordinary days and holidays was often only the difference between coarse and fine.

Fish was the other half of almost every plate that was not Brahman or Vani. “Bombils and mandils are the fish chiefly consumed by the agricultural classes,” the Gazetteer said, and farmers carried dried fish inland to swap it for grain [n50 §1; Gazetteer XIII-1 pp. 55-59]. The palm gave sap, drink, oil and shade. The Bhandaris, “often seen with a hollow gourd full of palm-juice on their head” and a tapping knife at the waist, had “palm-juice drawing and distilling” as their hereditary calling [n50 §1; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 150].

None of that is ancient evidence. But when a later record shows rice, millet, fish and palm sap at the base of every community’s diet, and when the land, the monsoon and the sea have not changed their basic gifts, it is reasonable (an inference, not a finding) to think the people of Shurparaka ate something like it two thousand years ago [n50 §2, §3 inferences].

What the Periplus saw moving

The one primary text that speaks of food on this part of the coast in the ancient period is the Periplus of the Erythraean Sea, a Greek merchant’s guide written about 40-60 CE. It does not describe a Sopara meal. It describes cargoes.

In its fourteenth section, the author says ships from “Ariaca and Barygaza” carry to the market-towns of the African coast “wheat, rice, clarified butter, sesame oil, cotton cloth… and honey from the reed called sacchari” [n50 §2; Periplus §14, Schoff 1912]. Ariaca is the author’s name for the stretch of western India that included the north Konkan; Barygaza is Bharuch on the Narmada. In section 41, describing the Gujarat coast just north of here, he calls the land “yielding wheat and rice and sesame oil and clarified butter, cotton” [n50 §2; Periplus §41]. In section 49, among the imports into Barygaza, he lists “wine, Italian preferred, also Laodicean and Arabian” [n50 §2; Periplus §49]. And in section 52 he names the market-towns south of Barygaza “in order”: “Suppara, and the city of Calliena” [n50 §2; Periplus §52].

So the merchant’s eye saw wheat and rice, ghee and sesame oil, and the cane sugar he called “honey from the reed” flowing out of this region across the Arabian Sea, and wine flowing in at Bharuch. “Honey from the reed called sacchari” is a lovely phrase to hold beside the 1882 Gazetteer’s report that about 750,000 sugarcanes went out of Bassein every year to Bombay, Surat and Broach, and that molasses was boiled in Bassein from February to June by Pachkalshis, Malis, Christians and Samvedi Brahmans [n21; n48; Gazetteer XIII-1 pp. 291, 390]. Eighteen centuries separate the two texts. Both see sweetness leaving this coast by boat.

But the Periplus lists no food export for Suppara itself [n50 §2]. It is tempting to write that ships at Sopara loaded rice and ghee; no source says so. The careful reading is that rice, ghee and sesame oil were the staples of the wider Gujarat-Konkan hinterland, and so plausibly part of what Sopara’s traders handled [n50 §2 inference]. Likewise, the wine drunk by merchants at Barygaza tells us that some foreign traders on this coast kept a drinking culture of their own; nothing ties a cup of Italian wine to a Sopara table [n50 §2 inference].

Monks, merchants and a silence

Sopara was, for centuries, a Buddhist centre: a stupa, a stream of monks, merchant donors, guesthouses near the trade hubs [n50 §2; Outlook Traveller]. A monastic seat implies alms food, and an ethic around the monasteries that leaned away from killing animals. It would be easy to imagine monks with begging bowls walking the lanes at dawn, receiving rice from the households of rich traders. That picture is not invented out of nothing; it is what Buddhist monasticism looked like across India. But the research behind this book found no text about what the monks of Sopara ate, and no Jain text on dietary practice at Sopara either [n50 §2 gaps]. The Kalpa Sutra tradition linking Jains to Sopara was searched for food detail and gave none [n50 §2 gaps]. This chapter therefore states the gap plainly: we know that Buddhists and, by tradition, Jains were here; we do not know what was on their plates.

The medieval centuries are almost as quiet. Ibn Battuta, in the fourteenth century, wrote memorably about coconut, about betel (“the offering of betel is much more important… than the gift of silver or gold”), and about a royal feast of round breads, roast meat, samosas and chicken on rice; but a secondary source notes that neither he nor Marco Polo mentions any Konkan port, and nothing he wrote can be tied to Sopara or Thana [n50 §2; Live History India, secondary]. The Arab geographers Al-Masudi, Al-Idrisi and Al-Biruni were searched without result for Sopara or Thana food [n50 §2 gaps]. Garcia de Orta, the Portuguese physician who published his Colloquies in Goa in 1563, wrote chapters on coconut, mango and betel; whether he ever wrote about Bassein’s food could not be found [n50 §2].

What carries across these silent centuries, visible again when the record resumes, is a set of habits that look older than any regime: the trade of dried fish inland for grain, the drawing and distilling of palm sap, the betel leaf in the garden and the betel nut in the hand [n50 §2]. They are the oldest table we can reconstruct: rice in the pot, fish beside it, a palm overhead, a leaf to chew at the end.

2. What arrived, and from where

The Columbian cargo

In 1534 the Portuguese took Bassein, and for two centuries the “Court of the North” was ruled from the fort at the mouth of the creek. Earlier chapters have told the politics, the conversions and the siege. The kitchen story of those two centuries is quieter, but in the long run it changed what every community on this coast eats, Hindu, Christian and Muslim alike.

The Portuguese were the carriers of what historians call the Columbian exchange to western India. Secondary writers credit them with bringing potato, tomato, tapioca, groundnut, corn, papaya, pineapple, guava, avocado, rajma, cashew, chikoo, capsicum and chilli [n50 §3; Live History India, “Pav from the Nau”]. Imagine a Konkan meal without chilli, and most of the modern table disappears: the red Koli masala, the dark bottle masala, the green chutney, the misal of the station canteen.

Three cautions belong beside that list. First, it is a popular synthesis about Portuguese India as a whole; the article names Vasai only in a list of settlements, and no source found says which crop reached Bassein in which decade [n50 §3 gaps]. Second, some of the credits are contested. One writer says at least three kinds of chilli were already growing in Goa about thirty years after Vasco da Gama, one of them called “Pernambucco Pepper”, which suggests a Brazilian route; another attributes the tomato to the Spanish, not the Portuguese; and papaya is said by another to have reached India through the Philippines and Malacca [n50 §3; Slurrp; The Wire, from search summaries]. Third, one single and weak source credits the Portuguese with the grafting that improved Konkan mangoes [n50 §3]. This chapter reports these as claims about Portuguese India, likely true in outline for Bassein, unproven in detail.

What the land itself suggests is a simple division. The garden crops the 1882 Gazetteer describes, plantain, betel, sugarcane, coconut, and the rice and fish of the plains, were here before 1534. Chilli, potato, tomato and papaya were post-1500 arrivals [n50 §3 inference]. The garden of Bassein that the British would later admire was an old garden with new plants grafted into it.

Bread, toddy and the oven

The Portuguese word pão gave Bombay its pav. One secondary account, citing the food historian Lizzie Collingham, says that the Portuguese, lacking yeast, fermented their dough with toddy, the palm sap the Bhandaris drew [n50 §3; Live History India]. Other writers tell the later history differently: Goan bakers selling pao commercially in Bombay, and Iranian bakeries later keeping the tradition alive with Goan workers [n50 §3; Goanet list post, weak]. The two stories do not contradict each other; they are about different centuries. But neither is about Bassein in particular. No Bassein-specific source on Portuguese ovens or bakeries in the fort was found [n50 §3 gaps].

What is documented is the bread’s later life. By 1882 the Christian wedding guest in Thana had “a piece of shop-made bread” set beside him, though the Gazetteer adds that guests “seldom eat any but home-made leavened and unleavened bread and sweetmeats” [n50 §1; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 214]. And the most famous Christian bread of this coast, the fugia, a small puffed round of fried dough, was historically fermented with toddy, then with khamir (dry yeast), and now sometimes with a modern fruit salt [n50 §4a; Goya Journal]. Its name, the Goya Journal reports, comes from the Marathi fugga, a balloon; Chef Michael Swamy links its origin to the yeast bread brought by Spanish and Portuguese [n50 §4a; Goya Journal]. The toddy-leavened fugia sits neatly beside the toddy-leavened Portuguese pão and the palm-spirit culture of the coast; that fit is suggestive, but no source dates the first fugia [n50 §3 inference].

Vinegar and the pig

The second great Portuguese inheritance is a technique: cooking meat, above all pork, in vinegar and spices so that it keeps. Vindaloo takes its name from the Portuguese vinho de alho, wine and garlic; secondary writers say it was introduced by Portuguese sailors around 1510, and that Franciscans distilled vinegar from coconut toddy [n50 §3; Live History India]. That article places all of this in Goa. On the Bassein side, the East Indian vindaloo and sorpotel are made instead with sugarcane vinegar, according to Chef Freny Fernandes, who cooks the Vasai Christmas table [n50 §3; Slurrp, A Vasai Christmas]. Sugarcane vinegar on a coast that sent three-quarters of a million canes a year to market is not a coincidence of taste; it is the local garden answering an imported recipe.

By 1882 the pork-and-vinegar dish was established enough to be the poor family’s wedding standby. “The poorer classes,” the Gazetteer said of Thana Christians, “almost always have two excellent dishes of cold pork, vinegar, and spices that remain fresh during the whole festivities which last for several days” [n50 §1; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 214]. The Gazetteer does not use the word sorpotel. But a cold pork dish made with vinegar and spices to keep through several days of a wedding is, in substance, what the community today calls sorpotel, vindaloo or indel [n50 §3 inference].

Food as a weapon

Food also carried the pain of conversion. The Gazetteer of 1882, citing the Oriental Christian Spectator of June 1859, records a missionary-era complaint about “the Portuguese thrusting biscuit and pork into the mouths of unwilling multitudes” [n50 §1, §3; n34; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. ~197]. It also records that “according to the story of the Uraps and other classes their forefathers were forced to become Christians by having pieces of flesh thrown into their wells” [n34; Gazetteer XIII]. This is the well-pollution tradition that survives in several forms on the coast.

Two things must be said together. The story is real as a tradition: it was being told in the 1850s and written down in 1882 as the account of the Urap Agris and others [n34]. And it is not found in any of the Jesuit or Franciscan letters read for this book, which record other mechanisms of conversion: gifts, feasts, land, rice to eat until the first harvest, oxen and seed, processions with flutes and garlands followed by a meal [n34]. One Jesuit described a new Christian village at Salsette given “rice to eat until their first harvest” [n34]. Food, in other words, appears on both sides of the conversion record: as bribe and welcome in the missionaries’ letters, and as violation in the converts’ descendants’ memory. The Kupari chapters of this book (K2) set out the evidence on each side in full. Here it is enough to notice that on this coast, what one ate, and with whom, was never just a matter of taste.

Bottle masala: a Kupari and East Indian invention

The single most distinctive thing in a Kupari or East Indian kitchen is a bottle. It is dark green or amber, often an old beer bottle, and it holds a ground spice blend of twenty to thirty ingredients, sometimes up to sixty [n13; n50 §3; Zee Zest; Homegrown]. The bottle’s colour keeps out the sun; the bottle gives the blend its name [n13; Zee Zest]. Every family’s mix is different [n50 §3; Zee Zest]. One Vasai producer calls it “mutton masala” too [n50 §4a; East Indian Memory Co.].

The making of it was a communal ritual. “In East Indian neighbourhoods like Vasai (Bassein),” the Zee Zest writer records, “it was common to find groups of women (called masalewalis) going from house to house to do the roasting and grounding” [n13; Zee Zest]. The chillies dried in the sun, the coriander and cumin and turmeric and a long list of others roasted one by one, the whole pounded and sieved and bottled to last the year: this is how a family’s flavour was made and remembered.

Where did it come from? Writers describe East Indian cooking as a blend of Maharashtrian, Portuguese and British influence [n50 §3; Asian Age 2016]; another says it was “heavily shaped by the Portuguese” [n50 §3; Slurrp]; a third gives Portuguese, Marathi and Goan in equal measure [n50 §3]. What is agreed is a difference of technique: East Indian cooks use a dry, stored, ground masala, where Goan cooks grind a wet masala fresh [n50 §3; Zee Zest; Homegrown]. The result is that East Indian sorpotel is brown and semi-dry and less sour than its Goan cousin [n50 §3]. Whether the bottle masala is a Portuguese-era invention or an older local practice of storing ground spice that Christians kept and elaborated could not be resolved from the sources [n50 §4 gaps]. The chillies in it, at least, are post-1500.

Gujarati merchants and the vegetarian table

The Portuguese were not the only arrivals who changed the table. From the north came Gujarati traders, and with them a strict vegetarianism and a love of butter. The Gazetteer of 1882 describes the Gujarati and Lad Vanis of Thana as “vegetarians, living on rice, millet and wheat, pulse, vegetables, butter, and sugar. They are great eaters and use much butter… no intoxicating drinks” [n50 §1; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 112]. The Porwad Vanis of Bassein, who had settled there about two hundred years before 1882, “never eat flesh. Their daily food is rice, rice and wheat bread, vegetables, pulse, butter, and milk”, in two meals, at noon and between seven and eight at night [n50 §1, §3; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 113]. The Lad Vanis from Cambay settled around Sopara about 1760 as traders [n48; Gazetteer XIV p. 316]. The Vanis of Papdi bought Bassein’s betel nuts wholesale and sorted them into six grades; “Bassein is famous for its phulbardi betelnuts” [n21; Gazetteer XIII-1 pp. 298-300].

Jains were counted separately, as Shravaks, 1,823 across the district [n50 §1]. Their diet in this period is not separately described in the notes; the Vani lines are the nearest record.

With the Gujarati merchants came wheat bread in a rice country. The Gazetteer’s Bassein port figures for 1881-82 show it: Bassein exported husked rice, molasses, coconuts and plantains to Bombay and Gujarat, and imported wheat, pulse, ghee, timber, cloth, oil and oil-cake [n21; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 358]. Wheat and ghee came in; rice and sugar went out. It is very nearly the Periplus list run backwards.

The Company’s cup of tea

The British left their name on the East Indians (adopted from the East India Company) [n50 §3; Slurrp], and the 1882 Gazetteer catches a few British habits arriving at the top of local society. Rich Konkani Muslims drank tea “with bread and eggs” at seven in the morning [n50 §1, §3; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 218]. Well-to-do Christians used “brandy and European wines” [n50 §1; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 204]; their wedding dinners were “laid and served in European fashion with many dishes and European wines” [n50 §1; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 214]. Beyond these lines, no source was found on British-period introductions to Vasai’s food: no record of when tea reached the ordinary household, or biscuits the village shop [n50 §3 gaps]. The community’s own record came much later. In 1981 the Bombay East Indian Association published the East Indian Cookery Book, now out of print [n50 §3; Homegrown].

British rule did leave one mark on drink that the Warli sources remember: the Company and Crown taxed palm trees and set up central distilleries [n50 §4f; Sahapedia]. The Gazetteer records the same pressure from the other side: since 1877 a palm-tree cess pushed many Bhandaris out of tapping and into farming [n50 §1; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 150].

The railway and the migrant plate

The railway, opened through the coast in the 1860s, connected Bassein’s gardens to Bombay’s appetite, and later brought new eaters. Marwari Vanis had come to Thana “almost entirely since the British conquest”, through Gujarat and Bombay, and “since the railway has been opened, they have come in great numbers” [n35; Enthoven/Gazetteer]. Over the twentieth century, and especially since the 1990s, Vasai-Virar became a city: 693,350 people in 2001, 1,343,402 in 2011, with Gujarati, Hindi and other languages alongside Marathi [n50 §3; Wikipedia: Vasai-Virar]. The migrants from Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, the south and Rajasthan brought their own food. And here the record fails almost completely: no retrievable source describes the migrant cuisines of Nallasopara or Virar except as entries in restaurant directories [n50 §3 gaps, §6 gaps]. That gap returns at the end of this chapter.

3. The table of 1882, caste by caste

A government menu

In 1882 the Government of Bombay published the Thana volumes of its Gazetteer, edited by James M. Campbell. Its Population chapter does something no earlier source had done for this coast: for almost every caste and community it gives a line on “daily food”, a line on feasts and what they cost, and a line on drink. Read together, these lines are a menu of the whole district on an ordinary day, and on a holiday, written by outsiders with a census-taker’s eye [n50 §1].

A caution belongs at the start. The Gazetteer survives for us in a digital text produced by optical character recognition, and in that text the caste headings are often lost or garbled. The page numbers given below are the printed page numbers as detected from the running heads, and can be out by one page either way [n50 §1]. Two lines in particular, describing Brahman groups, cannot be firmly attributed to a named caste from the OCR, and are reported below with that doubt attached [n50 §1]. Anyone quoting these lines on screen should check them against the page images [n50 §1 gaps]. With that said, the picture is vivid.

The Brahmans: rice, pulse, buttermilk

The Samvedi Brahmans, 2,563 people “found only in Bassein and Mahim”, were described as “clean, neat, sober, thrifty and orderly”, husbandmen and gardeners living in one-storeyed houses of wood planking and tile. “Their daily food is rice pulse and vegetables” [n50 §1; n08; Gazetteer XIII-1 pp. 81-82]. No fish, no meat is mentioned. Their rites show how food marked the stages of life: at a death, on the third day after the ashes were gathered, food on a leaf plate was given to the village Mhar; on the eleventh day rice balls were offered; a married but childless man of the caste (vanzkuli) was taken out of the village, paid two rupees and fed rice, amti and vadas, and feasted again on the thirteenth [n08; Gazetteer XIII-1]. Around 1750, the Gazetteer adds elsewhere, a Brahman annachhatra, an alms-house giving food, stood at Nirmal, and the Nirmal fair in November sold dried plantains and sweets [n50 §4b; n40 via n50; Gazetteer XIV p. 292].

Of the other Brahmans, the OCR gives three food lines whose caste names are uncertain. One group (p. 74) were “vegetarians, refuse garlic and onions, and drink no spirituous liquor. Their daily food is rice, pulse, and buttermilk”, and their feasts cost two to four annas a head. A Gujarati-style group (p. 80) ate “wheat bread, split pulse… onions and garlic are forbidden”. A third group, of Deshasth or Karhade type (p. 89), “eat fish, and the flesh of goats and sheep, but deem fowls unclean”, and did so, the Gazetteer says, “stealthily”, so as to appear properly Brahman-like [n50 §1]. Which jati each line belongs to cannot be fixed from the surviving text, and this chapter uses only the Samvedi line by name [n50 §1].

The Palshe Brahmans, the old coastal priesthood, carried an insult that is itself about food. Rivals derived “Palshe” from pala-ashi, “flesh-eater”; the Gazetteer itself called that etymology “probably the work of their rivals the Deccan and Chitpavan Brahmans”, who had displaced them as priests after the Maratha conquest of 1739-40 [n08; n35; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 81 fn]. On this coast, an accusation about diet could be a weapon in a priestly turf war.

The Pachkalshis and Vadvals: rice bread and a caste feast

The Pachkalshis, 15,367 strong, known variously as Sutars, Malis, Vadvals, Chaukalshis, Somvanshi Kshatris and Pathares, were the gardeners of the coast [n41; n08]. The Gazetteer gave them a full menu: they “live on coarse and fine rice, rice bread, pulse, vegetables, and fish. Their holiday dinners of mutton, chickens, pulse bread, and liquor, cost from 4½d. to 6d. (3-4 as.) a head, and their caste feasts from 1s. to 1s. 6d.” [n50 §1; n41; Gazetteer XIII-1 pp. 129-130]. And it caught the women at their trade: “The wives of husbandmen and gardeners help their husbands by selling vegetables, butter, and milk” [n41; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 130]. A Vadval wife with a basket of greens and a pot of butter is one of the first named food sellers in the record of this coast.

They also had their own word for oil-cake manure. Christians called it phod, the others khap [n48; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 291]: a small sign that farming communities who shared fields still kept distinct vocabularies.

Agris: salt, toddy and the evening drink

The Agris were salt makers (Mithagris), toddy drawers (Jasagris), drummers (Dholagris) and rice farmers [n08]. “Their staple food is nagle, or coarse rice, and fish, and on holidays finer rice and perhaps a fowl.” Their feasts “consist of pulse cakes, mutton, and liquor” [n50 §1; Gazetteer XIII-1 pp. 116-117]. And the Gazetteer added a sentence that has the tone of its time and its class: “They are excessively fond of liquor, all the men and women in a village being often drunk after sunset” [n50 §1; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 116]. The judgement is the official’s. The fact underneath it is that on the salt flats of Naigaon and Juchandra, after a day at the brine pans, the evening drink was part of life. The Urap Agris, Christian Agris of Bassein who returned to Hinduism in 1820 and 1828, were the community whose memory preserved the “flesh in the wells” story [n08; n34].

Bhandaris: the palm-juice drawers

The Bhandaris numbered 13,088 in the district [n50 §1]. “They eat fish, the flesh of sheep, goats, tortoises, and fowls, and drink liquor. Their every day food is rice, rice bread, and rice broth, ambil.” Holidays brought mutton and liquor [n50 §1; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 150]. Their calling was drink itself: “palm-juice drawing and distilling”, the gourd of juice on the head, the tapping knife [n50 §1]. Since 1877 a cess on palm trees had pushed many of them into farming [n50 §1].

Bhois and Son Kolis: dried fish every day

The fishing castes’ food lines are thinner than one would wish, because the OCR loses the Koli heading in the fishers’ section [n50 §1 gaps]. The Bhois, fishers, ate “rice and split pulse, dried fish, and occasionally mutton. They drink spirituous liquor”, and a caste dinner cost two annas a head [n50 §1; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 146]. Of the Son Kolis, about 8,000 “chiefly along the coast south of the Vaitarna”, the Gazetteer recorded fish taboos: few eat the porpoise (gada), crocodile, kend, whale (devmasa) and some others; all other fish, and fowl, goats and sheep, they eat. “Daily food: rice, nachni bread, pulse and fish.” They were “much given to drink”, hardworking, hospitable and honest [n08; Gazetteer XIII-1 pp. 148-149]. The detail of the whale and the porpoise is precious: a fishing people’s ethics written down in a few words, the creatures of the sea they would not eat.

Kunbis: three meals and the moha flower

The Kunbis, the great cultivator caste of the district (in which the 1882 compilers also grouped the Pachkalshis, Chaukalshis, Somvanshis and Malis [n41]), got the longest food line of all. “Besides grain, pulse, vegetables, fruit, garlic, onions, pepper, assafoetida, coriander, turmeric, tamarind, oil, curds and butter, they eat fish, fowls, sheep and wild hog.” Dried fish was “a daily article of diet”. On Holi and Gauri (Gavri) and at weddings they ate meat, “cut in small pieces and fried in oil or butter with assafoetida, garlic, onions and hot spices, eaten with rice bread, or pulse cakes, vadas”. Their everyday grains were nagli, vari and harik (kodo), rice only occasionally. They ate three meals a day. Their liquor was “chiefly palm juice either fermented or distilled, but in many cases moha spirits” [n50 §1; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 126]. Moha is mahua, the flower of the forest that would be distilled in Warli backyards a century later [n50 §4f]. The Kunbis also hunted: sambar, chital, porcupine, hare, mouse-deer and wild boar [n50 §1; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 47].

The forest peoples: Thakurs, Warlis, Kathkaris

East of the railway, in the hills toward Tungareshwar, the forest peoples ate from the forest. The Thakurs lived on “such coarse grain as vari and nachni, wild vegetables, and roots”, spent two to eight rupees a year on spices, salt and dried fish, and were “much more sober than Varlis and Kathkaris” but “drink freely on grand occasions” [n50 §1; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 178]. They and the Kolis gathered myrobalan (hirda) from October to January [n50 §1; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 30].

The Warlis “eat rice and other grains, and all kinds of meat except beef, bison, and nilgai. They are fond of fowls.” They ate “land crabs of several kinds, the roots of the wild plantain and those of another wild plant which is very unwholesome unless boiled, tender bamboo sprouts”, the bamboo shoot “for some months before the rains set in”. The Gazetteer’s judgement, again its own, was “Drink is their great bane” [n50 §1; Gazetteer XIII-1 pp. 184-185].

The Kathkaris, named for their craft of making catechu (kath), a product the Gazetteer notes they “never eat”, ate “every sort of flesh, except the cow and the brown-faced monkey”. Their everyday food was “nachni and field rats, squirrels, porcupines, lizards, snakes, monkeys… wild pig, doves, partridges”, about a pound of nachni and vari a day; they spent about fifteen rupees a year on liquor. Dhor Kathkaris ate beef; Son Kathkaris did not [n50 §1; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 160].

These lines were written by men who found the forest diet strange. Read today, they record something else: an expert knowledge of which roots must be boiled, which shoots are good before the rains, which crabs are edible. The modern Warli and Adivasi kitchens described later in this chapter are the inheritors of that knowledge.

The Vanis and the trading castes: butter and two meals

The Gujarati and Lad Vanis and the Porwads have been met already: vegetarian, wheat and rice and millet, butter and milk and sugar, no intoxicating drink, two meals a day [n50 §1; Gazetteer XIII-1 pp. 112-113]. One further trading caste, of Cutch origin and Solanki-Rajput descent by the Gazetteer’s account, with four divisions (Chevali, Panjabi, Sorathia, Kachhi), ate “rice, pulse, and vegetables, and, in private, fish and flesh”, and used “onions and garlic” much in their cookery [n50 §1; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 108]. The OCR heading for this caste is lost, and its identity is uncertain [n50 §1]. As with the Deshasth-type Brahmans who ate fish “stealthily”, the phrase “in private” is a window onto how public diet and private diet could differ when caste status was at stake.

The Parsis appear in the Gazetteer chiefly as “Gujarat Parsi liquor-contractors and Government servants, found all over the district” [n35; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 65]. Their own food line was not captured [n50 §1 gaps]. But their trade sat right at the junction between the toddy tappers and the government’s excise.

The Christians of Bassein: the whole family eats together

And so to the Christians, the community that includes the Kuparis’ great-grandparents. The Gazetteer’s line is one of the richest in the chapter.

“Except some of the richer families who have three meals a day… the bulk of the Thana Christians eat only twice… Unlike Hindus the whole of the family, men, women, and children eat at the same time.” They “live on rice, nagli, vari, pulse, vegetables, mutton, beef, pork, fowls, and fish, and drink liquor. On festive occasions they make rice cakes and eat them with mutton, potatoes, and plantains. They generally drink palm spirits called phul, with from three to five per cent of alcohol. Occasionally some drink and offer their guests bevda, double-distilled palm-juice. The well-to-do use brandy and European wines” [n50 §1; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 204].

There is a great deal in that paragraph. The family at one meal, all together, was something the Gazetteer marked as a difference from Hindu households, where men often ate first. Potatoes, a Portuguese-era crop, had become festival food. Plantains, the coast’s own fruit, were served with mutton. Rice cakes, the ancestors of vade and apas, were the festive bread. And the drink had a ladder: phul, the once-distilled palm spirit; bevda, double-distilled; brandy and wine for the rich [n50 §1 inference].

One phrase needs a careful reading. The Gazetteer lists beef among Thana Christian foods in 1882. A modern article states that “Samvedi and Vadval Christians do not eat beef” [n50 §4a; Free Press Journal]. The two are not necessarily in conflict. The Gazetteer is describing Thana Christians in general, a body that included converted Kolis, Kunbis and Bhandaris; the modern statement is about the Samvedi and Vadval Christians specifically, communities descended from Brahman and gardener castes, among whom an older taboo may have persisted after conversion [n50 §4 inference]. A sociological summary of the Vadvals likewise records them as non-vegetarian, avoiding beef, and rearing pigs [n41; Sahapedia]. But that is an inference from two differently scoped sources, not a documented history of when or why the taboo held. It is marked here as such.

The Gazetteer also caught the Christian life-cycle at table. At a baptism, friends were offered “wine and sweetmeats”; poorer families held “a feast of country liquor, dates, gram, and molasses”. The mother returned to her husband’s house carrying “sweet rice-flour balls, cocoanuts, boiled gram” [n50 §1; Gazetteer XIII-1 pp. 210-211]. There was a thanksgiving at the rice harvest [n50 §7; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. ~211].

And there was the wedding. Among the well-to-do, the wedding dinner was “laid and served in European fashion with many dishes and European wines. The poorer classes … almost always have two excellent dishes of cold pork, vinegar, and spices that remain fresh during the whole festivities which last for several days. A piece of shop-made bread is set beside each guest, but they seldom eat any but home-made leavened and unleavened bread and sweetmeats.” The poorest made do with “a dish of pork or a dish of dried prawns” with liquor [n50 §1; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 214].

For a Kupari reader, this is an extraordinary passage. “Festivities which last for several days” is the multi-day Kupari wedding, with its Saudio and Aayez customs that community sources describe [n50 §4a]. “Two excellent dishes of cold pork, vinegar, and spices that remain fresh” is a description, by an outsider who probably never cooked it, of a dish that sounds very like what Kupari families call indel: meat meant to keep for several days [n50 §3 inference; §4a]. The shop bread set out for show and the home bread actually eaten is a whole social history in one sentence: the bakery loaf as a token of the modern, the home-made fugia and vade as the real food. The European wines of the rich, the dried prawns of the poor: the Christian community of 1882 was not one class but many.

Elsewhere the same Gazetteer gives the Christians of Bassein their most famous compliment: “The most hardworking and skilful husbandmen are the Christians of Bassein”, who grew sugarcane and plantains and “turned the light sandy country about Bassein into an evergreen garden” [n21; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 300]. The Christians were not only eaters of the garden; they were among its chief makers.

The Konkani Muslims: never a meal without fish

The Muslim communities had their own varied table. The Konkani Muslims ate “rice, both boiled and made into bread, pulse, vegetables, fish, and mutton”, and “Konkanis are specially fond of fish and never let a meal pass without eating it, either fresh or dry.” Nearly all well-to-do townsmen ate mutton daily, and even the poorest tried to have mutton at Ramzan and Bakri Id. Beef was eaten but “seldom offered for sale”. The rich drank tea with bread and eggs at seven in the morning. Memons and Bohoras ate rice, wheat bread, pulse, vegetables, mutton and fish; Deccani Muslims, millet bread, pulse, fish and chillies [n50 §1; Gazetteer XIII-1 pp. 217-218]. The Muslim Naitas were among the traders of the Sopara garden belt [n48; Gazetteer XIV p. 316].

The dried-fish trade: bombil and sukhat

Behind almost every non-vegetarian line in the Gazetteer stands one commodity: dried fish. The Gazetteer’s fisheries section is a small economic history in itself. The bag net, the dol, was worked in divisions named for their catch: “the bombil mar, the vagti mar and the mandil mar”. Large fish, singhali, kajra and pomfret, were caught in the mouth of the net. Dried fish was graded and priced: the best bombil fetched six to twelve rupees for a bundle of 4,000; then came mandil and dhoma, sode, small and large sukhat (the word today usually written sukat or sukka), and vagti [n50 §1; Gazetteer XIII-1 pp. 55-59].

The trade ran in two directions. Farmers carried dried fish inland and swapped it for grain [n50 §1]. And dried and salted fish came in from far away: kas and surmai from Muscat, Sur, Makran and Gwadar, bombil from Diu. The trade was largely in the hands of one Abba Kachhi of Bhiwandi [n50 §1; Gazetteer XIII-1 pp. 55-59]. So the bombil on a Kunbi’s plate in the hills in 1882 might have been caught off Arnala, or off Diu; and the dried kingfish in a merchant’s store might have crossed the Arabian Sea from Oman, along one of the oldest sea-roads in this book.

The fishing was organised: an 1851 survey counted 22 salt-water and 18 fresh-water fisheries in Bassein [n48; Gazetteer XIII-1 fn]. On Saturdays at Virar, east of the station, “on the west bank of a large pond under big banyan and mango trees”, a large market was held, “thronged by Kolis and Varlis, selling fish, garden stuff, and cloth” [n48; Gazetteer XIV pp. 382-383]. The Koli with fish and the Warli with forest produce, meeting by a pond under the banyans: that market is one of the best food scenes in the whole 1882 record.

4. Bananas and sukeli

The nine plantains of Bassein

If rice is the grain of this book, the plantain is its fruit. “Sugarcane… is grown all over the district, especially in Bassein where sugarcane and plantains are the chief watered crops,” the Gazetteer said [n48; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 291]. And it named the varieties: “Nine kinds of Plantains are grown in Bassein, basrai, mutheli, tambdi, rajeli, lokhandi, sonkeli, bankeli, karanjeli, and narsingi” [n21; n48; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 291].

A footnote, contributed by the civil servant K. J. Ebden, gives the yearly sales of each, though the OCR digits are shaky and should be read as orders of magnitude rather than exact counts [n21; n48]. Green basrai dominated, about 220,000 bunches a year. Red tambdi came next, about 50,000 bunches, and the most expensive at about a rupee and a half a bunch. Round-ended mutheli sold about 12,000 bunches. Rajeli, karanjeli and narsingi about 5,000 each; bankeli, a wild species, about 2,000; lokhandi about 1,000; and bright yellow sonkeli only about 200 [n21; n48; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 292 fn]. In total, something like 300,000 bunches a year left the gardens [n21 inference].

The Gazetteer recorded how they were grown: in “light and sandy” soil, about 1,000 basrai plants to the acre but only 550 tambdi; manured first with oil-cake and then with fish, for “fish manure is cheaper, wants less water, and gives a better return than any other manure”, though it bred worms; tambdi fruiting in the tenth month, the others in the eighth [n21; n48; Gazetteer XIII-1 pp. 291-292]. Fish feeding the bananas that fed the people: the sea reached even into the orchards.

And nothing went to waste. The flower spike, kelphul, was sold as a vegetable at fourteen annas a dozen. The leaves served as plates, at four to eight annas a hundred. The wild bankeli, sun-dried and powdered, was “nourishing food for the sick and for women after childbirth” [n48; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 292]. The banana-leaf plate, the banana-flower curry and the banana-flour invalid food are all here in 1882.

The gardens themselves were a landscape. The Gazetteer’s description of the “garden or agar of Sopara”, Bolinj, Koprad and Umbrala in the north, Nirmal and Vagholi in the west, Gass in the south, Sopara and Mardesh in the centre, is one of the loveliest passages in the whole official record: “four or five feet below the level of the fields, shady lanes… wind among mango and plantain orchards, fields of rice and sugarcane”; “Till the month of March the island is full of water. Runnels from garden wells cross or flow alongside of the lanes… round ponds and long winding lakes, whose banks are fringed by high pile-supported Persian wheels” [n48; Gazetteer XIV p. 315]. The chief products of Sopara and its neighbourhood, it said, were “molasses, rice, plantains, and betel-leaves”; molasses, rice and betel went north by sea from the landings at Gass, Bolinj and Vagholi to Surat, Broach, Jambusar, Dholera, Bhavnagar and Gogha, or by rail “chiefly from the Virar station” to Ankleshwar, Nadiad, Viramgam and Patan, while “large quantities of plantains and betel-leaves are sent south to Bombay” [n48; n21; Gazetteer XIV p. 316].

The dried plantain trade of 1882

Among the nine varieties, rajeli had a special destiny. “The well-known Bassein dried-plantains are the fruit of the rajeli variety” [n21; n48; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 292]. The method that opens this chapter, straw basket, sea-booth, sun, leaves, mat, ghee, seven days, was practised in only nine villages: Agashi, Vagholi, Vatar, Bolinj, Koprad, Nala, Umrale, Rajodi and Murdes [n48]. Those villages lie almost exactly along the Virar-side coastal belt, at the Vaitarna end of the garden strip [n48 inference]. Dried plantain was, in effect, the export speciality of the villages that would later become greater Virar.

The trade was big. Agashi’s yield alone was “estimated at 160 tons (3000 Bengal mans) worth about £2700 (Rs. 27,000)” [n48; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 292]. Agashi itself, with 6,823 people in 1881, was a working port, its sea exports averaging about Rs 3.18 lakh a year in the five years to 1879, with “very good fishing boats and coasting craft” built there [n48; Gazetteer XIV pp. 1-2]. The drying families, again, were Hindu and Christian side by side: about 85 Samvedi Brahman households, 15 Pachkalshi and about 75 “Native Christian”, in a season from October to January [n21; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 395].

That figure deserves a separate moment for the Kupari reader. Seventy-five Christian families at Agashi, Vatar and Koprad, “originally Samvedis and Pachkalshis” [n34], laying out plantains on the same booths as eighty-five Hindu Samvedi families: the two halves of one community, divided by the conversion of the sixteenth century, still doing the same work in the same season by the same sea three centuries later. Whatever separated them at church and temple, the sukeli was shared.

The varieties today

The names have shifted. A travel writer around 2017 listed “rajeli, tambeli, mutheli and velchi” and wrote that “dried banana is a speciality of Vasai” [n50 §5; U.A. Satish]. Food bloggers celebrate the tiny velchi (cardamom) banana and the rajaali (royal) banana as “a very famous variety from Vasai or Bassein” [n13; Ruchik Randhap]. A Marathi agricultural page says the harisal variety is widely grown in the Vasai area [n50 §5; Vikaspedia, title only]. A 2025 study of Fusarium wilt took its samples in Vasai from Safed Velchi, Rajeli, Bunkel and Bhurkel [n50 §5; Springer, from a summary]. Some of these names match the 1882 list (rajeli, mutheli; “tambeli” is plausibly the old tambdi; “bunkel” perhaps bankeli); others, like velchi, do not appear in 1882 at all. No taxonomic source reconciles the old names with the new; they may be synonyms, spellings or genuinely different plants [n50 §5 inference]. One correction from the notes is worth repeating: basrai, the dominant variety of 1882, is today documented as the Jalgaon banana, under the Jalgaon GI of 2016, and no modern source ties it to Vasai [n13].

No Vasai banana has a GI tag, though Goa’s Myndoli banana received one in 2021 [n50 §5; n13].

The 1998 Thane banana study

The one modern economic snapshot comes from a survey of 1998, cited from the farmers’ journal Shetakari. It found about 911 hectares of banana in Thane district, mainly in Vasai and Palghar tehsils. Safed velchi made up about 76 per cent of per-farm production; lal velchi and bhurkel the rest. Bananas fetched about Rs 350 a quintal locally and Rs 500 in Mumbai. About 41 per cent went from farmer to contractor, about 45 per cent to commission agents and about 16 per cent to co-operatives (the shares sum to about 102 per cent, as published) [n50 §5; IndiaAgroNet]. By the end of the twentieth century, in other words, the small velchi had displaced the big basrai as the banana of Vasai, and middlemen carried most of the crop to Bombay.

The vegetable porters and the decline of the gardens

The gardens fed the city in person. Until the early 1980s, villages of the Vasai-Virar sub-region supplied vegetables to Mumbai’s suburbs; “paddy was widely grown, betel leaf was exported to Pakistan, and velchi kela plantains sold at a premium” [n17; Vikalp Sangam 2019]. The “Vasaiwale kawadis”, farmers in half-pants with a kawad (a shoulder pole slung with two baskets), came in on the four o’clock train to VT and sold at Bora Bazaar and Dadar Kabutarkhana to the cry of “baaju-baaju, Vasaiwale aale” (“make way, the Vasai people are here”) [n08; n50 §4c; India Food Network 2015]. Their baskets held garlic chives, coriander, kantola (teasel gourd), kelphul, field beans, ghevda, toor, light-green okra, brinjal, colocasia leaves for alu wadi, round bottle gourds for dudhi halwa [n50 §4c; n08]. The Vasai food blogger Apolina Fos, whose family farmed there, told the India Food Network that only a few of her cousins still farm [n50 §4c].

The causes of the decline are told in the economic chapters of this book: the 1988 de-reservation of green land, the real-estate boom from the mid-1990s, silted and polluted irrigation ponds [n17]. Mogra, betel and velchi gave way to sonchafa in the flower gardens [n13; The Better India]; betel leaf lost its Pakistani market as relations worsened [n08]. Curiously, the Gazetteer of 1882 already mentions sonchafa (Michelia champaca) planted among Bassein’s coconut palms, because “their strong perfume drives off the bees” [n48; Gazetteer XIII-1 pp. 295-297].

Sukeli today: rare, dear and worth asking for

And the dried plantain? In November 2012 a blogger bought seven or eight dried bananas wrapped in banana leaves, about a quarter-kilo, for about Rs 60 in Vasai’s main market, and described them as “preserved Bananas, which is sort of soft dry fruit like Anjir” [n50 §5; EkPravas, 1 Dec 2012]. A local community site reports that sukeli has largely disappeared from local markets [n50 §5; eVasai, search summary]. Today the name appears more on packaged products from elsewhere, “a traditional Indian sun-dried banana delicacy”, than on stalls in Agashi [n13; n51; Hunarsetu]. No source ties a named Vasai seller to sukeli; directory searches turn up Vasai banana-chip makers and wholesalers instead [n51]. One East Indian home kitchen near Giriz serves a honey-drizzled sukeli as dessert [n50 §4a; Homegrown].

No source describes how sukeli is made in Vasai today, whether the ghee-smearing and straw-basket ripening of 1882 survive, or how many tons are still dried [n50 §5 gaps]. What can be said is that the product line runs unbroken in the record from the Gazetteer to the 2012 market and to the dessert plate: at least 140 years of documented history, and certainly more before that [n50 §5 inference]. A family in Agashi or Vatar that still dries rajeli on a sunny roof in December is carrying on a craft the Government of Bombay thought worth describing step by step.

5. Today’s kitchens, community by community

The 1882 Gazetteer gave every caste a line. Modern sources are much less even. The East Indian and Kupari kitchen has cookbooks, food journalism, chefs and home-dining businesses. The Koli kitchen is documented mostly through Mumbai Koli sources. The Warli kitchen appears in writing about Palghar generally. And for the Agri, Bhandari, Hindu Samvedi, Hindu Vadval, Muslim, Jain, Gujarati and migrant kitchens of Vasai, Virar and Nallasopara, almost nothing citeable exists [n50 §4]. Searches in Marathi (आगरी जेवण, कोळी जेवण, कुपारी पदार्थ, बाटली मसाला) returned no newspaper articles on these subjects [n50 §4 gaps]. This tour therefore gives each community what the evidence supports, and says plainly where the evidence runs out. An empty paragraph is not a judgement on a cuisine; it is a measure of how little has been written down.

The Kupari and East Indian kitchen

The Kuparis, the Samvedi Christians of north Vasai, belong to the wider community now called East Indian, which also includes the Vadval Christians of south Vasai, Koli Christians and the Christians of Salsette, Bombay and Uttan [n50 §4a; n41]. Their cooking is the best documented on the coast, and much of what is written is about East Indians in Mumbai rather than Vasai in particular. Where a source is Vasai-specific, this section says so.

The bottle and the masalewali. The bottle masala described earlier remains the heart of the kitchen. In Vasai, the Zee Zest writer records, “masalewalis” went house to house to roast and grind it [n13; Zee Zest]. Today it is also bought: the Vasai business East Indian Memory Co., run by Regina Pereira, sells a bottle masala from a family recipe refined over decades, alongside fish masala and other blends [n50 §4a; n51; East Indian Memory Co., search summary]. The Jio Institute has built a digital exhibit on bottle masala [n13]. A Vasai blog lists bottle-masala chicken among the region’s signature dishes [n50 §4a; All About Vasai-Virar, low reliability].

Sorpotel, vindaloo, indel. East Indian sorpotel, cooked with bottle masala and sugarcane vinegar, is brown, semi-dry and less tangy than the Goan dish [n50 §3, §4a; Slurrp; Zee Zest]. Vindaloo uses the same sugarcane vinegar in Vasai [n50 §3; Slurrp, Chef Freny Fernandes]. Indyal or indel, especially pork indyal, is a Christmas dish on the Vasai table [n50 §4a; Slurrp]. A community-wiki-derived list describes indel as meat or fish meant to keep for several days [n50 §4a]: the same principle as the 1882 wedding pork that “remained fresh during the whole festivities”. Pork temperad also appears on East Indian festive lists [n50 §4a; Slurrp].

The breads. No community on this coast has so many breads. A Slurrp guide to the East Indian community names five [n50 §4a]: - bhokache orias, semi-sweet and doughnut-like, of lentil flour and jaggery, the wedding bread, eaten with sorpotel or prawn lonvas; - fugias, the puffed fried rounds; - chitaps, a lacy crepe of rice and coconut milk; - apas, from a rice dough called khoi, historically of red (raate) rice, a breakfast with chicken khudi or shrimp curry; - pangi, rice flour, jaggery and milk cooked on a banana leaf in ghee, with prawn pickle or balchao.

That guide does not mention Vasai. The fugia, though, has a well-documented Vasai life. The Goya Journal’s account names Regina Pereira, 64, of Vasai; describes the batter being blessed and marigold flowers added for weddings; and records that fugias are sold mostly by home cooks to pre-order and at church stalls, rather than in bakeries [n50 §4a, §6; Goya Journal]. The leavening has moved from toddy to dry yeast to fruit salt [n50 §4a].

The everyday table. East Indian everyday dishes listed by food writers include fish curry with dried mango, kardi aamba (prawns with fresh mango), varan, khuddi curry, usals, cabbage foogath, lonvas, tope, and dried Bombay duck (bombil) chutney with rice-flour roti [n50 §4a; Slurrp, not Vasai-specific]. Bombil threads through it all.

The wedding table. The East Indian wedding is a food event of several days. Slurrp lists variyas, fugias, khuddi, vindaloo, sorpotel, pork temperad, Arroz Fugath (wedding rice) and the wedding pickle, and describes the “umbracha paani” ritual on the day before, with coloured fried rice papris; weddings of seven days [n50 §4a; Slurrp]. The Asian Age, describing an East Indian feast in Mumbai in 2016, lists duck moile, chicken curry, fugia, fish kujit, prawn lonvas, wedding pulao, mutton khudi curry, sorpotel, foogaths, oxtail soup, boiled tongue, mutton chops, meatball curry and beef croquettes [n50 §4a; Asian Age]. Those are Mumbai-wide menus, and the beef items would not appear on Samvedi or Vadval Christian tables if the modern taboo reported for them holds [n50 §4a; Free Press Journal]. A paid “East Indian wedding delicacies” experience in Vasai was announced in 2024-25; its details could not be retrieved [n50 §6; n13; Shaadinews].

Christmas in Vasai. The most Vasai-specific account of the festive table comes from Chef Freny Fernandes, writing for Slurrp in 2025 [n50 §4a]. The Vasai Christmas, she says, has roast chicken stuffed with liver, gizzard, croutons and peas, rubbed with bottle masala; sorpotel; vindaloo; fugias; pork indyal; suckling pig; and beef tongue roast. The sweets are fruit cream, guava cheese, coconut rava cake and nevri, which she derives from the Maharashtrian karanji. There is a spiced wine like mulled wine. Families go crib-hopping and carolling in the East Indian tongue, and come home from midnight Mass to eat. And, she notes, food, dialect and spices differ between Vasai villages [n50 §4a; Slurrp 2025]. Other Mumbai East Indian voices add kalkals, milk cream, marzipan, coconut sweets, rose de coque, nankhatais, date rolls and homemade wine; Chef Michael Swamy remembers panettone, duck moile, eggs and ham for breakfast, and eggnog with brandy at midnight Mass; and Sequencia Kharvi describes neighbourhood women making navris together with rolling pins and cutters, though many families now buy them ready-made [n50 §4a; Free Press Journal]. Kimaad, a sweet port-like wine, is drunk at Christmas with the toast “Sukhala” [n50 §4a; Slurrp]; the same word is recorded as the East Indian or “Vasai Marathi” for “cheers” [n13; Free Press Journal 2024].

All Souls and the feasts of the saints. On All Souls Day, 2 November, East Indian families make atola, a rice pudding [n50 §4a, §7; Slurrp]. At Killa cha Sann, the feast of St Gonsalo Garcia at the Church of the Holy Name inside Vasai Fort, held on Sunday 11 January 2026, families camp the evening before and cook moile and stew, with meats and navalkol (kohlrabi) [n51; Free Press Journal 2026]. Devotees at the Gonsalo Garcia feasts cook East Indian dishes [n50 §6; Free Press Journal], though no menu has been published. The parish feasts that follow through the year, St Gonsalo Garcia at Gass in February, Holy Cross at Nirmal in September, St Francis Xavier at Giriz in December, are each a family meal as much as a church event [n51], but their food has not been documented separately.

The Kupari dishes the community remembers

Beyond the shared East Indian table, there is a set of dish names that Kupari families describe as their own, and the reader should know exactly where they come from. They come from a dish list on mirror copies of a community-written Wikipedia page; the live page, when checked, held only an introduction and no cuisine section; and no primary or reputable published source confirming them was found [n50 §4a, §4 gaps]. They are therefore offered here as community tradition, unverified in print, and the best possible thing a Kupari family can do with this chapter is correct or confirm them from their own kitchens.

The list names: - fish gravies, “especially Bombil”, the bombil that the 1882 Gazetteer called the fish of the agricultural classes; - dhapti, a steamed bread made from mixed flours and vegetables; - hingoli, a pita-like bread with a sweet filling, deep-fried; - indel, meat or fish prepared to keep for several days; - vade or talnayo rotyo, a fried bread of mixed flours [n50 §4a].

Earlier notes for this book carry a slightly longer list from community sources: indel, vade, dhapti, hingoli, fugias, sanna, dodol, fuddi, vindaloo, and sorpotel made with sugarcane vinegar [n50 §0]. The wedding customs Saudio and Aayez appear on the same pages [n50 §4a].

Three of these deserve a thought. Vade is the same word the Gazetteer used in 1882 for the Kunbis’ “pulse cakes, vadas” eaten with festival meat, and for the vadas fed to a childless Samvedi man at a funeral [n50 §1 inference; n08]. Indel has its plain ancestor in the 1882 Christian wedding pork. Dhapti, a bread of mixed flours, fits the Gazetteer’s picture of Christians eating rice, nagli and vari, three grains, and festive “rice cakes” [n50 §1]. None of these links is proof. Each is a thread that runs from a dish name in a Kupari kitchen back to a sentence in an 1882 book. A grandmother who still makes hingoli is, in this sense, a primary source.

The Kupari table at its best can be glimpsed at the home-dining kitchens of Vasai described in section 6 below: potato chops, fugias and honey-drizzled sukeli near Giriz Talao [n50 §4a]. A novel serialised online imagines a Vasai heritage lunch of fugias, potato chops, chicken indyal, prawn sambari and sukeli [n50 §4a; Sokaaji Saga]; it is fiction and is mentioned here only for its mood, not as evidence.

Vadval Christians and Koli Christians

The Vadval Christians of south Vasai share the East Indian table, and community writers say their speech, dress and songs differ slightly from the Kuparis’ of the north [n13; Vasaiker]. A sociological summary describes the Vadvals as non-vegetarian, avoiding beef, and rearing pigs [n41; Sahapedia]. The Free Press Journal reports that “Samvedi and Vadval Christians do not eat beef”, and that Koli Christians lived in “Kolivadas” [n50 §4a; Free Press Journal]. Beyond these lines, no source describes a specifically Vadval Christian or Koli Christian dish in Vasai.

The Koli kitchen

The Kolis of Arnala, Navapur, Rajodi, Pachubandar, Killa Bandar and the Koliwadas of Vasai, Hindu and Christian both [n50 §4d; n51], cook from the sea. The best sources are about Mumbai Kolis and Koli cuisine in general, and should be read that way [n50 §4d].

The Koli masala, called bhukni, commonly has about eighteen spices, in green (coriander-based) and red versions; the red, one source says, is “popular in the Vasai region” [n50 §4d; Slurrp; Jio Institute, from summaries]. Sukka bombil batata, dried bombil with potato, is made in most Koli households. Bombil is dried on bamboo racks and taken down at about four in the afternoon. Bombil, mandeli (golden anchovy) and kolambi (prawn) curries are eaten with tandalachi bhakri, rice-flour roti [n50 §4d]. The India Food Network’s Vasai vegetable recipes include kelphul bhaaji made with “Koli masala” [n50 §4c], a sign that the Koli spice blend crosses community lines.

At Arnala, a blogger in November 2012 saw “huge dry areas for fishes on both side” of the road to the beach [n50 §4d; EkPravas]. That is the nearest thing in the sources to a description of the drying grounds of north Vasai; the bombil racks of Arnala and Pachubandar have otherwise gone unrecorded [n50 §4 gaps; n13]. The continuity with 1882 is direct: the bombil mar net-division, the bundles of 4,000 dried bombil, and the sukhat named in the Gazetteer are the ancestors of the racks along the Arnala road [n50 §1].

The Kolis’ festival is Narali Purnima, the full moon of Shravan, when boats are painted and garlanded and coconuts offered to the sea. The festival foods are narali bhaat (coconut and jaggery rice), narali karanji and coconut ladoos [n50 §4d; n51; Lokmat Times]. The coverage found is for Mumbai, Uran, Karanja and Kambe in Bhiwandi, not Vasai or Arnala, and so the Vasai details are inference: the Koli villages of this coast follow the coast-wide custom [n51]. The Agri communities are said to offer coconuts to the sea at the same festival [n50 §4d]. The Versova Koli Seafood Festival, started in 2006 with over a hundred Koli women and more than forty-five stalls, is in Mumbai, not here [n50 §4d].

The Warli and Adivasi kitchen

The Warli, Kathkari and Thakur communities of Vasai’s eastern hills and of Palghar beyond have a cuisine that modern writers are only beginning to describe, and almost all of it is written about Palghar generally rather than Vasai taluka [n50 §4f, §4 gaps].

Today’s Palghar Adivasi kitchen, as travel writers record it, has bhakri of rice or nachni; suran curry, wild yam in coconut milk; pithla; toor dal with moringa; and bamboo-shoot sabzi with a paste, eaten with nachni bhakri [n50 §4f; Verve]. Warlis at Aarey eat soft bamboo shoots, river shrimp, loth leaves, the soft-shell river crab chimbori and mushrooms [n50 §4f; Outlook Traveller]. At Vikramgad a single forest lunch was described as “close to 40 recipes made with diverse wild ingredients” [n50 §4f]. Palghar district holds a wild vegetable festival for beneficiaries from Jawhar, Mokhada, Vikramgad and Wada [n50 §4f; Free Press Journal], and its Tribal Millet Roti appears on a list of the district’s living heritage [n50 §4f].

Set beside the Gazetteer’s 1882 lines, the continuity is striking: “tender bamboo sprouts… for some months before the rains”, land crabs, wild roots, nachni and vari [n50 §1]. The forest kitchen has lasted. The Kathkari, Thakur and Vasai-east Warli kitchens of today, specifically, have not been documented [n50 §4 gaps].

The Samvedi Hindu kitchen

The Hindu Samvedi Brahmans of Nirmal, Agashi, Umrale and the other garden villages were, in 1882, people of “rice pulse and vegetables” [n50 §1]. Enthoven, in 1922, says only that they “chiefly grow garden crops” [n50 §4b; Enthoven vol I]. They dried plantains alongside their Christian cousins and made molasses [n21]. Their temples, their alms-house at Nirmal and the November Nirmal fair, with its sweets and dried plantains, are in the record [n50 §4b].

What a Samvedi Brahman household cooks today, for its festivals or its daily meal, has no citeable source at all [n50 §4 gaps; n13]. This is perhaps the most surprising gap in the chapter, given how central the Samvedis are to the history of this coast and to the Kuparis’ own origins. It is also among the easiest to fill, by asking.

The Vadval and Pachkalshi Hindu kitchen

The Hindu Vadvals and Pachkalshis were the gardeners behind the Dadar trade, and the best modern window into their food is the India Food Network’s 2015 piece on the disappearing “Vasaiwale kawadis” [n50 §4c]. Its recipes include kelphul bhaaji (banana-flower vegetable) with Koli masala, and shikaran, cardamom milk with banana, offered as naivedya to the gods, with Vasai’s sweet yellow bananas chosen for both [n50 §4c, §5]. Colocasia leaves went into alu wadi, round bottle gourds into dudhi halwa [n50 §4c]. A blogger’s recipe for rajali banana bhaaji, bananas in coconut, is described as a GSB family dish, not a Vasai one [n50 §4c; n13]. Beyond these, no modern source describes Hindu Vadval festival food or a Vadval wedding feast [n50 §4 gaps].

The Agri and Bhandari kitchen

For the Agris, the salt and toddy people of Naigaon, Juchandra and the creek villages, and for the Bhandaris, the palm tappers, the record stops in 1882 [n50 §4e]. Enthoven’s volume yielded no usable food text for either [n50 §4e]. No modern source in English or Marathi describes Agri food in Vasai-Virar; the well-known kombdi vade of Raigad is attested, but not as an Agri dish [n50 §4e]. No source describes present-day Bhandari toddy tapping in Vasai [n08; n17]. These are communities with long food traditions, salt, toddy, rice and fish, and an entirely unwritten modern kitchen.

The Muslim kitchen

In 1882 the Konkani Muslims never let a meal pass without fish, fresh or dry, and even the poorest tried for mutton at Ramzan and Bakri Id [n50 §1]. No current source describes Eid or Ramzan food in Vasai or Nallasopara [n50 §4g, §7 gaps]. Commercial directories list Mughlai food and biryani, for example at the Sagar Family Restaurant on Station Road, Nilemore, Nallasopara [n50 §4g]; that is a listing, not a history.

The Jain and Gujarati kitchen

The Porwad and Lad Vanis “never eat flesh”; the Gujarati Vanis used “much butter” [n50 §1]. Today Virar is roughly half Marathi-speaking, with much of the remainder Gujarati and Catholic; undated figures give Gujarati speakers 28.30 per cent and Hindi 15.31 per cent [n50 §3; Wikipedia: Virar]. Agashi has a Jain temple, the Chalpeth derasar [n51]. Yet no modern source on Jain or Gujarati food in Vasai-Virar was found [n50 §4g]. The vegetarian kitchens of the merchant families, which shaped the town markets for two centuries, wait to be written about.

The everyday Marathi plate and the migrant kitchens

A Vasai-Virar blog describes the Maharashtrian household and street table as pav bhaji, misal pav, bhakri, pithla and varan bhaat, and coastal seafood cooked with coconut, spices and tamarind [n50 §4a, §4g; All About Vasai-Virar, low reliability]. The pav of the pav bhaji is the Portuguese pão; the chilli in the misal is from the Americas; the rice of the varan bhaat is the oldest grain on the coast. Even the cheapest station breakfast carries every layer of this chapter.

The kitchens of the people who have come to Nallasopara and Virar since the 1990s from Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, south India, Rajasthan and elsewhere, now a large share of the population, have no retrievable source beyond restaurant listings [n50 §3 gaps, §6 gaps]. The story of how a litti or a dosa found its place at a Nallasopara corner is part of this coast’s food history, and it is entirely unrecorded.

Festival foods across the year

Gathering what the sources document, the festival table of the coast looks like this: - Christmas (Kupari and East Indian): roast stuffed chicken, sorpotel, vindaloo, fugias, pork indyal, suckling pig, guava cheese, kalkals, nevri, fruit cream, coconut rava cake, kimaad and spiced wine [n50 §4a]. - Killa cha Sann and the Gonsalo Garcia feasts (January-February): moile and stew cooked at camps in and around the fort [n51]. - Weddings (East Indian): bhokache orias, sorpotel, wedding rice, wedding pickle, umbracha paani papris [n50 §4a]; in 1882, cold pork in vinegar and spices, and dried prawns for the poorest [n50 §1]. - All Souls Day (2 November): atola [n50 §7]. - Narali Purnima (August, Koli and Agri): narali bhaat, narali karanji, coconut ladoos [n50 §4d]. - Holi and Gauri (Kunbi, 1882): meat fried with asafoetida, garlic, onions and hot spices, with rice bread or vadas [n50 §1, §7]. - Ramzan and Bakri Id (Muslim, 1882): mutton, even for the poorest [n50 §1]. - The Nirmal fair (November, 1882): sweets and dried plantains [n50 §7]. - Baptism and the rice harvest (Christian, 1882): wine and sweetmeats, or country liquor, dates, gram and molasses; sweet rice-flour balls, coconuts and boiled gram; a harvest thanksgiving [n50 §1, §7].

Ganeshotsav modaks and Diwali sweets are eaten here as across Maharashtra, but no Vasai-specific source records them; nor were Holi, Easter, Eid or Diwali foods for Vasai-Virar found in any citeable source [n50 §7 gaps].

Drinks: toddy, mahua, kokum, solkadhi

Toddy is the oldest documented drink of the coast. In 1882 the Bhandaris and the Jasagri Agris drew it; the Christians drank phul, once-distilled palm spirit of three to five per cent, and offered guests bevda, double-distilled; the Kunbis drank palm juice “either fermented or distilled” [n50 §1]. Today, palms still ring Vasai Fort, and a 2022 blog describes toddy tapping around its “crown of palms” [n13; n51; Mumbai Paused]. The Warli sources explain the chemistry: neera, fresh sap, turns to tadi in about two hours, up to four per cent alcohol [n50 §4f; Sahapedia]. No licensed visitor-facing toddy venue was found [n51].

Mahua (the 1882 moha) is the forest drink. Warli women brew it in backyard three-pot stills; the trees bloom from March to May, and the flowers are gathered at about four in the morning and dried for the year. Liquor is offered in life-cycle rituals. The PESA Act of 1996 allows households up to five litres for domestic use [n50 §4f; Sahapedia]. Maharashtra revised the Mumbai Mahua Flower Rules of 1950 so that collecting and selling the flowers is lawful, though not the spirit [n51]. Virar police regularly raid illegal country-liquor stills, for example in the Barafpada forest of Virar East in June 2026 [n51; Free Press Journal]. Mahua cannot be recommended as a visitor experience.

Kimaad and spiced wine are the Kupari and East Indian festive drinks, toasted with “Sukhala” [n50 §4a].

Kokum and solkadhi, the pink coconut-milk-and-kokum digestive of the Konkan, are Konkan-wide drinks; no source specific to Vasai was found [n50 §7 gaps]. A restaurant at Bhabola Naka is named “Kokum” and serves seafood and Konkani food [n51].

Tender coconut water is how the East Indian home kitchen near Giriz welcomes its guests [n50 §4a].

Next door: Dahanu’s chikoo

One fruit from just up the coast belongs in this chapter. The chikoo (sapota) is among the crops the Portuguese are credited with bringing to India [n50 §3]. In Palghar district it became a major orchard crop: the Gholvad chikoo of Dahanu has a GI tag, covers about 5,000 hectares, and had 147 authorised GI farmers among about 5,000 growers when its first consignment was exported to the United Kingdom in May 2021 [n50 §4f; PIB 2021; Deccan Herald]. One article gives the GI year as 2016, unconfirmed [n50 §4f]. Vasai’s bananas have no GI; its northern neighbour’s chikoos do. That contrast is one argument for why documenting the rajeli and the sukeli matters.

6. Where to eat and buy today

The honest guide to eating in Vasai, Virar and Nallasopara starts with a warning. Most of what is distinctive about the coast’s food is cooked at home, at church feasts and by home cooks taking pre-orders, not in restaurants [n50 §6 inference; n51]. Most named restaurants come from aggregator listings of uncertain date and quality. What follows separates the documented from the merely listed.

The old Vasai bakeries

A book excerpt published by ThePrint on 31 January 2026, on Goan and Iranian bakers in Mumbai, names three old Vasai bakeries: Keith Bakery, Mikhail Bakers and Caterers, and Martina Super Bakery. They make bread, biscuits, chicken and mutton puffs, mava cakes and coconut cakes. The same excerpt notes that “Vasai has a large East Indian community”, and quotes the historian Fleur D’Souza on the East Indians as “rice-growing and eating farmers living in Salcette and Vasai” until the twentieth century [n50 §6; ThePrint 2026]. A Tosita Bakery appears on a dining listing without detail [n50 §6].

The 1882 Gazetteer’s “piece of shop-made bread set beside each guest” at Christian weddings [n50 §1] is the distant ancestor of these bakeries’ trade. Fugias, as noted, are mostly not sold in bakeries but by home cooks and at church stalls [n50 §6; Goya Journal]. No named Vasai bakery or seller of kulkuls, dodol or marzipan was found [n51].

East Indian home dining at Giriz

The most authentic way to eat the Kupari and East Indian table as a visitor is by arrangement in a home.

Regina and Bernard, opposite Giriz Talao in Vasai, serve home-cooked East Indian meals with an optional village and culture walk: potato chops, fugias and sukeli, a four-course meal over three to four hours, tender coconut water to welcome guests and honey-drizzled sukeli to finish [n50 §4a; n51; Homegrown; My Little Adventure listing, from a search summary]. The Homegrown guide describes them as a Vasai couple “keeping alive a dying cuisine” [n13].

East Indian Memory Co. (Regina Pereira) sells bottle masala, fish masala and other blends and hosts traditional East Indian lunches at a home in Vasai, booked by email [n50 §4a; n51; search summary only]. Whether these two listings describe the same household is not clear from the sources, and readers should check directly.

The same Homegrown guide lists other East Indian home chefs and bakers, Helen Barreto and Swirty’s Bakery and East Indian Food, without Vasai addresses, and East Indian stalls in Kalina, Manori, Gorai and Uttan on the Mumbai side [n50 §6; n51]. The best place to eat East Indian food in quantity, in season, is a parish feast, above all Killa cha Sann in January.

Fish thalis at Navapur and Pachubandar

For the sea, the fishing villages are the place. Listings name Beach Treats at Navapur Beach, Virar (about Rs 400 for two), and Farmhouse Sea N Sand at Pachubandar (mixed reviews) [n51; District; EazyDiner]. A Homegrown guide to fish thalis names Machli Lunch Home in Vasai, with a thali under about Rs 240 at the time of writing (dated) [n51]. Kokum, at Bhabola Naka, serves seafood and Konkani food, rated 3.9 from about 1,000 reviews [n51]. The heritage trails in this book suggest a fish-thali lunch at Pachubandar after the churches of Papdy, Sandor, Remedi and Giriz, and lunch at Navapur Beach after Agashi and Arnala [n51]. None of these has been verified by a visit; menus, prices and hours should be checked [n51]. The best-known Koli-cuisine restaurant in the region, Mi Hi Koli, is in Thane and Mulund, not here [n51].

No source names the Koli fish markets of Pachubandar or Arnala with hours or auction times [n50 §6 gaps; n51]. Boats land mostly early in the morning (an inference) [n51]. Grilled fish at Vasai market is mentioned in aggregator guides [n50 §6].

Aggregator picks (unverified)

The following come from aggregator and blog guides and have not been checked: Kunj Vihar Restaurant and Sweets on Vasai Road West, known for a “Jumbo Vada Pav” popular for over two decades; Shree Laxmi Market, Vasai East, for misal pav; sea-facing restaurants called Sea and Sand and Kinara; and Agashe Bhujing Centre in Virar, for Maharashtrian snacks [n50 §6; Mumbai Orbit, Travelkhana, Sygic]. Sagar Family Restaurant, Station Road, Nilemore, Nallasopara, lists Mughlai food and biryani [n50 §4g]. Treat all of these as leads, not recommendations.

Where to buy

Sukeli, rajeli bananas and bottle masala are best bought directly from growers and home cooks, or at the village market, rather than from any shop; none of them could be pinned to a named Vasai shop on the evidence found [n51]. East Indian Memory Co. sells bottled masala [n51]. Sukeli under that name is sold online by producers outside Vasai [n13; n51]. Vasai banana-chip makers appear in trade directories [n51].

There is no documented Vasai food festival. The Vasai Kala Krida Mahotsav, a sports and arts event whose 36th edition runs from 26 to 31 December with 85 competitions and some 60,000 contestants, does not, in the sources, include food stalls [n50 §6]. Vasai Fort itself has no food stalls inside [n51].

Closing: filling the pot

Line up the evidence of this chapter and a pattern appears. The Periplus saw rice, ghee, sesame oil and cane sugar moving through the region two thousand years ago. The Portuguese brought chilli, potato and the vinegar-pot. The Gujarati merchants brought wheat bread and butter. The British brought tea at seven and brandy for the rich. The railway brought the kawadis to Dadar and, later, millions of new neighbours. And through all of it, the coast kept its oldest things: rice in three grades, bombil on the rack, palm sap in a gourd, plantains on a sea-booth smeared with ghee.

The 1882 Gazetteer, for all its prejudices, did something remarkable: it wrote down a food line for every caste, from the Samvedi Brahman’s rice and pulse to the Kathkari’s field rats and nachni. No source since has done the same for this coast. Today, one community’s kitchen is well recorded, and much of that record is about Mumbai rather than Vasai. For the Samvedi Hindus, the Hindu Vadvals, the Agris, the Bhandaris, the Muslims, the Jains and Gujaratis, the Warlis of Vasai east and the migrants of Nallasopara, the modern record is almost blank. Even for the Kuparis, the dishes they name as most their own, indel, hingoli, dhapti, vade, rest on a community-written page that cannot now be verified.

That is a gap families can fill, and quickly, because the sources are alive. Some practical ways, for any household in any community:

  • Write down one recipe, exactly as it is made, with the family’s own names for ingredients and steps, and the name and village of the person who taught it. Note what has changed: toddy to yeast to fruit salt in a fugia is history.
  • Record a grandparent cooking, on a phone, in the family’s own dialect (Samvedi, Vadvali, Koli, Agri, East Indian Marathi). The words matter as much as the measures; the dialects are declining [n13].
  • Photograph the bottle masala being made, with the list of spices and the proportions if the family is willing, and the date.
  • Keep the festival menus: what was cooked for a wedding, a Christmas, a Narali Purnima, an Eid, a Diwali, a Gauri, a death anniversary, and who cooked it.
  • Ask about the old things: how sukeli was dried, whether ghee was still smeared, what the rajeli looked like, where the bombil racks stood, who tapped the toddy, what the Saturday market at Virar sold.
  • Share them with parish archives, community associations, village libraries or a public digital archive, so the record outlives the cook.

The book’s governing image is a rice pot, and the rice pot is the right image to end on. Every grain in it came from a different field and a different hand. Some grains are well described in old government books; some are remembered only in one family’s kitchen. A full account of every grain at the table is a promise this chapter cannot yet keep. The families of Vasai, Virar and Nallasopara can.
