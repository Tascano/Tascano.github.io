A doorstep in Bolinj

In 1881 or 1882 the men compiling the Thana volumes of the Gazetteer of the Bombay Presidency walked the lanes north of Sopara looking for old stones. At Bolinj, which they believed “was formerly a large town and probably had rich temples”, they found one. It was a slab about two feet two inches long, carved with Devanagari letters, and it was serving as the doorstep of a house. The house belonged to a Christian. The surveyors wrote his name the way the village said it, “Juju Manu Lop”, and then translated it for their readers: “that is Joseph Manuel Lopez”. [n40; GzXIV pp. 341–342] [n39; GzXIV p. 342]

Half a mile to the south-west, in the hamlet of Koprad, they found a second inscribed stone, this one of the fifteenth century, lying near the house of the village headman. They recorded his name too: “Bhiku Patil, a Samvedi Brahman”. [n40; GzXIV p. 342] The stone itself, dated Shaka 1386 (AD 1464), named Mahim, Thana, Koprad and “Bimbstan”, the old name of the country the Marathi chronicles connect with Raja Bimb. [n03; GzXIV Koprad entry; Gazetteer XIII Part II p. 441 n.4]

Those two names, set down a few lines apart in a colonial survey, hold most of what this chapter is about. One man carried a Portuguese Christian name, Joseph Manuel Lopez, which his neighbours had worn down in their own speech to “Juju Manu Lop”. The other carried a Marathi office title, Patil, headman, and belonged to the Hindu Samvedi Brahmans, the farming community from which, the same Gazetteer says, many of the Christians of this garden country had come. [n34; GzXIV p. 315; GzXIII-1 p. 395] They lived within a short walk of each other, in villages that grew the same plantains and betel, on land that had passed through the hands of Shilahara kings, Gujarat sultans, Portuguese fidalgos, Jesuit procurators, Maratha officers and British collectors. Neither of them, as far as any record shows, was ever asked to tell his own story.

This chapter is in three movements. The first is about names: how the Christians of the Vasai villages came to carry Portuguese surnames, what the one surviving Portuguese land register of the last years before 1739 shows about who held the land, what some of the names mean, which names stayed Marathi, and what it would take to trace one family’s line. The second is a portrait, village by village, of every place the sources tie to the Kuparis, the Samvedi Christians of north Vasai, and of the neighbouring Vadval villages, Christian and Hindu, to the south and east. The third is about voices: the people from these villages whose names reached print, the much larger number whose names did not, and how families today can make sure that the next historian of this coast has more to work with than a doorstep and a headman’s name.

A word about the evidence before we begin. The research behind this chapter searched the full text of fifteen primary and early sources for the word “Kupari” and its spellings, from the 1876 history of Gerson da Cunha and the 1882 Gazetteer to the Census reports of 1901, 1911 and 1931 and four volumes of the Jesuit letters. It found the word in none of them. [n34; headline finding] In the documents, the people this chapter is about appear as “Native Christians”, “Portuguese Christians”, “Firgi” and “Kinstav”. [n34] The first printed uses of “Kupari” that could be found are a community blog of 2007 and Wikipedia. [n34] That silence shapes everything that follows. When this chapter says “Kupari village”, it means a village that the community’s own lists name as Kupari, checked against what the older documents say about the people who lived there. Where the two disagree, or where only one speaks, the chapter says so.

The author of this book is a Kupari from Gass, and carries the surname Tuscano. That is a reason to be careful about family names, not to be generous with them.

I. Names

How a convert got a Portuguese name

No document from sixteenth- or seventeenth-century Bassein has been found that says, of a named Kupari family, “this is how they got their surname”. [n39 §1 takeaway] What the sources give instead is the mechanism, documented for the Portuguese world of western India as a whole, and a handful of Bassein-specific facts that fit it.

The mechanism was baptism. In Goa, by the account of modern historians quoted on the Wikipedia page on the Christianization of Goa, “the converts typically adopted the surnames of the Portuguese priest, governor, soldier or layman” who stood as godfather, and the old Konkani Hindu names were set aside at the font (Prabhu 1999, p. 133, as quoted). [n39; Wikipedia, Christianization of Goa] The Provincial Council of Goa in 1567 decreed that converts could no longer use their former Hindu names (de Sousa 2011, p. 69, as quoted). [n39] The same page gives examples: a Prabhu of Benaulim became Francisco Fernandes; a Kamat of Curtorim became Aleixo Menezes. [n39] And it gives the detail that matters most to anyone trying to trace a family today: “Since in many cases, family members were not necessarily baptized at the same time, this would lead to them having different surnames.” Two brothers, sons of a man called Pero Parras, became a Barbosa and a Rangel (do Carmo Costa 2003, p. 12, as quoted). [n39] These quotations reached the research through a secondary page; the books themselves were not opened, and they describe Goa, not Bassein. [n39 §1 gaps]

The Mangalorean Catholics, further south, tell the same story about themselves: their European surnames were “bestowed on their ancestors by the Portuguese who stood sponsors to them at the time of their baptism” (Farias 1999, p. 12, as quoted), although some families kept pre-conversion surnames such as Shett, Shenoy, Kamath and Padval. [n39; Wikipedia, Mangalorean Catholic name]

For Bombay, Salsette and Bassein together, the sociologist Rita Rodricks, writing for Sahapedia, says converts “were encouraged, or in some cases compelled, to take Portuguese names”, that Marathi speakers “modified the Portuguese names to suit their language”, and that as a result families with the same surname cluster across villages and gaothans, while caste was retained after conversion. [n39; Sahapedia] Her article cites no sources in the text that could be seen, so it is an informed synthesis rather than a record. [n34 §1]

The Bassein-specific evidence is thinner but real.

First, the Jesuit letters show how baptisms in Bassein were staged, and who stood beside the converts. Father Francisco Cabral, writing from Bassein on 5 December 1562, described catechumens kept at the Jesuit college, men and women separately, taught each night by a lay brother who knew the language, and then baptised in procession “with the captain and the whole city”, with garlands, psalms and flutes, and feasted afterwards. [n34; Documenta Indica V, doc. 90, p. 629] Da Cunha, summarising the Jesuit chronicle Oriente Conquistado in 1876, described the method of converting “a high-caste man, a Brahman, who was sure to be imitated by a number of his and other classes”, with a procession of the governor and nobility and “a sumptuous banquet”. [n34; da Cunha 1876 p. 234] When the captain of Bassein and the leading Portuguese of the city walked in a baptismal procession, they were available as godfathers; that is the setting in which a governor’s or a captain’s surname could pass to a convert. One named example survives at second hand: the Brahmin astrologer Parashuram Joshi, baptised “Henrique da Cunha” on 8 September 1565, whose baptism was followed by about 250 Hindus, more than fifty of them Brahmins. [n25 §1.8; Wikipedia, Bombay East Indians, citing Machado 1999] The source does not say where he was baptised, and whether his new surname came from the governor Nuno da Cunha, who had taken Bassein in 1534, is a guess, not a sourced claim. [n39 §1A]

Second, there is the landlord. The Portuguese of Bassein, Cabral wrote, “all have villages”, and at first they feared that making one villager Christian would empty the village, so Christianity “was little favoured”; after the Jesuits preached, “the inhabitants who before would riot if a vassal of their villages was made [Christian] now come to beg us to go and convert their villages”. [n34; Documenta Indica V, pp. 628–629] More than three and a half centuries later, the Census of 1931, describing the Bassein coast, gave the outcome in one sentence: “With the advent of the missionary a great deal of mass conversion took place, not unaided by competition among the owners of these estates, and in many villages every tenant in the village was baptized and given the surname of the owner of the village, e.g., Demonte, Pereira, Ferreira, etc.” [n34; Census 1931 Vol VIII Part I pp. 363–364] The census writer was describing the Christian fishing coast rather than the northern garden villages, and he was writing in 1931, not in 1580. But it is the only official statement that ties Bassein surnames to landholders, and it fits what Cabral saw. [n34 §3]

Third, the vocabulary itself. The 1882 Gazetteer records that a Christian child of the Thana district was carried to church for baptism between the eighth and fifteenth day “by its godfather, padrinho, and godmother, madrinha”, and that “the mother never goes to the christening”. [n34; GzXIII-1 pp. 210–211] In 1905 George Grierson’s Linguistic Survey of India found Portuguese words in the speech of the district’s Christians, among them padron, godfather, and madron, godmother. [n34; LSI VII, Kiristav section] Godparenthood was so central to these families’ lives that its Portuguese words entered their Marathi. The best-known explanation of the word “Kupari” itself, from Portuguese compadre, the godfather of one’s child, belongs to the same world; it is repeated widely but rests on forum posts and a dead dictionary link, not on any scholarly or period source. [n34 §2; Wikipedia, Kupari] A second, unsourced folk etymology, “those who drank from the well (kup)”, circulates as well; it is offensive to many, it is tied to a conversion legend that the 1882 Gazetteer recorded as a story of the Urap Agris and other classes, not of the Samvedis, and it is folklore, not evidence. [n34 §2; GzXIII-1 p. 201 n.2; Talk:Kupari]

Fourth, there is a nineteenth-century observer’s complaint. In a footnote about the Goan conspirators of 1787 known as the “Pintos”, da Cunha praised men who had not, “unlike modern Christians, sunk their time-honoured surnames to assume some commonplace Portuguese patronymic”. [n39; da Cunha 1876, footnote near pp. 116–117] He was writing as a Goan Brahmin in Bombay, contemptuous of the Bassein Christians, and the footnote is about Goa. But it shows that in 1876 an educated observer took it for granted that the Christians of this coast carried “commonplace” Portuguese surnames in place of older ones.

Finally, the 1882 Gazetteer says that “though Christian names were given to all alike, the Portuguese treatment of converts of good birth was very different from” that of lower-class converts: men of rank were admitted to Portuguese society, married Europeans and received grants of land, while those of low birth “were left in a state little removed from servitude”. [n34; GzXIII-1 p. 202] A Portuguese surname, in other words, was universal; it did not by itself make anyone Portuguese, or free.

Put together, the documented ways a Bassein convert family could have come by its surname are these: from a godfather at baptism, whether priest, captain, official or layman; from the Portuguese holder of the village in which the family were tenants; and, in later generations, by marriage, adoption or the drift of spelling. The sources show that all of these happened in the Portuguese north. For no Kupari family has a document yet been found that says which one happened to them. [n39 §1 takeaway; n44 §3]

The landholders’ book: the Tombo de Baçaim, 1727–1730

If a Kupari family took its surname from the holder of its village, the obvious question is: who held the villages? For most of the Portuguese period the answer is lost. For its last years, one remarkable document survives.

Between 1727 and 1730, twelve years before the Marathas took Bassein, the Portuguese Crown had its officers in Baçaim compile a tombo, a register of the lands it leased out, with the holders, the terms and the rents. The register opened with payments dated 12 June 1727, received by the feitor and alcaide-mor of Baçaim, André Ribeiro Coutinho, the same royal factor whom da Cunha shows being sent to report on the fort in 1728. [n44 §2; Tombo introduction] [n39 §1C; da Cunha 1876 c. p. 75] The historians André Teixeira and Silvana Pires published the register’s table of foreiros, the lease-holders, in 2007, with the folio numbers of the original, in the open-access journal Anais de História de Além-Mar. [n39 §1B; Teixeira and Pires 2007, pp. 325–363] The tombo lists 362 titles in all: 36 in the caçabé (the home district) of Baçaim, 28 in that of Agaçaim, and 4 in Sopará, besides the northern aldeias and the praganas of the Taná province. [n40 §2; Tombo pp. 338–339]

The holders are not converts. They are Portuguese fidalgos, widows of fidalgos (the title “D.” for Dona marks a lady), and the Jesuits. [n39 §1B] They held their villages either “in lives” (em vidas, for a set number of lives, often three) or em enfiteuse, a hereditary lease. [n40; n44 §2] Their rents were reckoned in pardaus. Matched by sound and by context to modern villages, the entries for the Kupari and Vadval belts read like this; the matching is the research’s own and is an inference, while the folio numbers are the tombo’s: [n40 §2; Tombo table from p. 345]

  • “Gris”, that is Giriz: D. Margarida Teles de Meneses, in three lives, 166 pardaus 4 larins (fl. 23), the first title in the Baçaim caçabé.
  • “Sandor”: Jorge da Silva Coelho, in enfiteuse, 435 pardaus (fl. 29v).
  • “Bolinja e terra Belvar”, Bolinj: D. Maria Henriques Coelho da Silva, in lives, 450 pardaus (fl. 54v).
  • “Vatar Rajoly”: Manuel Varjão da Fonseca, in lives, 700 pardaus, the highest rent among the pacarias (fl. 45v).
  • “Mardes Sirgoly”: Lucas de Almeida de Andrade, in enfiteuse, 180 pardaus (fl. 47v).
  • “Vagoly”, Wagholi: Manuel de Abreu de Lima (fl. 41v); and “Vastal Vagoly”: Fernão da Silveira e Meneses, 60 pardaus (fl. 45).
  • “Cholna Roba”, Chulne: the Jesuit Procurador Geral, 130 pardaus (fl. 49).
  • “Agaçaim”, Agashi: D. Domingas Pereira de Azevedo (fl. 42), who also held the coloria of Agaçaim and the “Ilha das Vacas”, the island of Arnala (fl. 41), and the rent on the wild palms of Agaçaim (fl. 44v).
  • “Nala Magir”, Nala: Miguel Francisco Mira de Vasconcelos (fl. 43), with an extra horta, Matarvary, held by Gonçalo Carneiro (fl. 40).
  • “Umella”, Umela: Francisco Pereira de Sousa, 157 pardaus (fl. 26v).
  • “Boroly Mulgão”, perhaps Mulgaon: Fernão de Brito de Melo, 333 pardaus 1 larim 79 réis (fl. 24v); he also held “Quirauly e Mafarea” (fl. 31).
  • “Virar Naringuy”, Virar: D. Pedro de Meneses, 2,000 pardaus, the highest rent among the aldeias (fl. 121).
  • “Palle”, Pali: D. Maria Correia de Macedo (fl. 172).
  • “Maroy” and “Naucer e Cascarida”: the Jesuits (fl. 27, 37v).

One more entry stands out because the holder is not Portuguese. In the pacaria of Gris, a horta “Vitoba das palmeiras bravas”, an orchard of wild palms, is entered under an Indian name, “Jasaman Naique” (fl. 53). [n40 B9; Tombo table] A native landholder named in a register of fidalgos is rare; it is a reminder that local people with Marathi names and titles were still on the land in 1727, alongside the Portuguese who held it from the Crown.

The researcher counted how often surnames occur in the extracted text of the tombo, notes included and spelling variants not merged, so the figures are approximate: Pereira 74 lines, Melo 56, Meneses 35, Coutinho 22, Almeida 20, Brito 13, Carvalho 9, Rodrigues 9, Dias 8, Miranda 6, Cunha 6, Lopes 5, Vaz 5, Fernandes 4, Gonçalves 4, Correia 3, Nunes 3, Toscano 3, Sequeira 2, Pinto 2, Machado 1, Furtado 1. [n39 §1B] Many holders carried compound names, such as Fernão de Brito de Melo, Pedro de Meneses or Jorge Pereira Coutinho, so the same great houses recur across Bassein, Thana and Salsette. [n39] Some names common in Vasai today are not in the tombo at all: Mascarenhas, Colaço, Remédios, Rumão or Romão, and Dabre. [n39]

Set beside the surnames of Vasai’s Christian villages today, that list is striking. Pereira, Melo (D’Mello), Meneses (Menezes), Coutinho, Almeida, Brito (D’Britto), Rodrigues, Dias, Lopes, Vaz, Fernandes, Gonçalves (Gonsalves), Correia, Sequeira, Pinto, Machado and Furtado are all names that Vasai Christians carry. [n39 §1D; Wikipedia, Goan Catholic names and surnames] The tombo’s holders are exactly the “owners of the village” of the 1931 Census sentence. A tenant family on the Almeida land at Mardes, the Pereira land at Agashi, the Brito de Melo land at Mulgaon or the Meneses land at Giriz could have taken that name. [n39 §1 inferences]

But three cautions apply, and they are not formalities.

The first is time. The tombo describes the holders of 1727–30. The great conversions of the Bassein villages happened, by the best modern account, between about 1535 and 1620, and above all between about 1570 and 1620. [n34 §3; Mendiratta 2017 p. 211] A family baptised in 1580 would have taken the name of a godfather or landholder of 1580, not of 1727. Villages changed hands over 150 years; the tombo’s holders are the last generation, not the first.

The second is that the same surnames were everywhere. They are Goan, Mangalorean and Bombay East Indian as well as Vasai names. [n39 §1 inferences] A Pereira in Agashi is not proof of a link to D. Domingas Pereira de Azevedo, any more than a Pereira in Goa is.

The third is that, in villages the Jesuits held, such as “Cholna Roba” and “Maroy”, the likely sponsors were priests, not lay landlords; and names absent from the tombo, such as Mascarenhas, Colaço, Remedios and Dabre, may have arrived by later migration from Goa or from the Bombay mission, or by baptism with clergy rather than landholders, or, in Dabre’s case, may be a Marathi name that survived. All of these are possibilities, not findings. [n39 §1 inferences]

So the tombo gives Vasai Christians something precious, a list of real people, with folio numbers, who held their villages’ land just before 1739, and whose surnames are the surnames of the villages today. It does not give anyone a pedigree.

What the names mean

Most Portuguese surnames began as descriptions of a place, an occupation or an ancestor, and a few of the commonest Vasai names have meanings that reference works record. None of these meanings is specific to Vasai; they are the meanings the names had in Portugal.

  • Pereira: “pear tree”, a topographic or habitational name from Portugal and Galicia, found in western India through Portuguese colonists. [n39 §1D; Geneanet; MyHeritage]
  • Machado: from the Portuguese word for “hatchet”, a person who made or used hatchets. [n39 §1D; Behind the Name]
  • Furtado: described in an American Name Society article, seen only in a search fragment, as a synonym of a word meaning foundling or waif, spread because it matched the name of the Hurtado de Mendoza family. [n39 §1D]
  • Mascarenhas: a noble house whose family history is traced from João de Mascarenhas, first Marquis of Fronteira; a noble-house history rather than an etymology. In da Cunha, Dom Francisco Mascarenhas commands at the siege of Chaul in 1570–71, and Pedro Mascarenhas is a viceroy. [n39 §1D] It was Pedro de Mascarenhas who, as viceroy in March 1555, ordered measures against the “gentios” of the Bassein territory. [n34 §3; Mendiratta p. 210]
  • Toscano: “someone from Tuscany”, Italian in origin; in Portuguese toscano is also the ordinary word for “Tuscan”. [n39 §1D; n44 §1]
  • Romão (Rumao in the Vasai spelling): from Latin Romanus, and from medieval Portuguese villages named Romão, by a weak source; whether Vasai’s Rumao is the same name is an inference that fits the Marathi reshaping of Portuguese names. [n39 §1D]

For the rest of the common Vasai names, Rodrigues, Gonsalves, D’Souza, D’Mello, Correia, Lopes, Almeida, Coutinho, Dias, Menezes, Nunes, Pinto, Sequeira, Vaz, Carvalho, Colaço, Remedios, Miranda, D’Britto and Fernandes, the research found no citable page that gives a meaning. [n39 §1 gaps] The standard work for them is José Pedro Machado’s Dicionário Onomástico Etimológico da Língua Portuguesa, which a family wanting to know what its name meant in Portugal should consult. [n39 §1 gaps] This chapter does not guess.

The names that stayed Marathi

Not every name in these villages is Portuguese, and the exceptions are some of the most interesting.

Dabre is the clearest case. It is one of the best-known Christian surnames of north Vasai, carried by the first Bishop of Vasai, Thomas Dabre, who was born at Bhuigaon in 1945. [n39 §1E; Catholic-Hierarchy] It is not a Portuguese word, and it does not occur in the 1727–30 tombo. [n39 §1B] The research found no etymology for it. A genealogy aggregator counts about 3,829 people named Dabre in India and gives the Marathi spelling डाबरे; a 2006 mirror of an old Wikipedia list of Maratha clan names includes “Dabre”, which is weak evidence. [n39 §1E; Forebears] It may be a Marathi family name that survived conversion, as the Mangalorean Shetts and Kamaths did; but that, the research says plainly, is a guess. [n39 §1 inferences] The origin of the name Dabre is, for now, unexplained.

Patil and Mhatre belong to the same world of Marathi village office. In 1882 the headman of Koprad was “Bhiku Patil, a Samvedi Brahman”, a Hindu. [n39 §1A; GzXIV p. 342] In AD 1150 a Shilahara grant of the village of Vattaraka, Vatar, was witnessed by “Risi Mhatara, headman of Vattéraka village, Naguji Mhatara, Ananta Nayaka, and Changdev Mhatara”. [n40 A13; GzXIV p. 385 n.1] At Gass in 1882 one inscribed stone stood before the house of “Degu Pobria Mhatara”, and another before that of a Samvedi Brahman, “Bab Naik by name”. [n40 A6; GzXIV p. 341] Mhatara is the old form of the surname Mhatre, which still occurs among Samvedi and Vadval families; that the twelfth-century Vatar headmen and the modern Mhatres are connected is an inference no source tests. [n40 A13 inferences] What the record does show is that Mhatara, Nayaka (Naik) and Patil were names of standing on this land for centuries before the Portuguese and after them.

Whether Samvedi Christian families themselves kept Marathi surnames such as Patil, Mhatre, Raut, Vartak or Joshi is not documented. The only source is a Wikipedia talk-page comment, which also notes that these names occur in other castes. [n39 §1E] The Hindu Pachkalshis’ surnames, which the 1882 Gazetteer gives as “Raut, Vartak, and Chodhri” (Chaudhari), belong to the Hindu Vadval and Pachkalshi families of the same villages. [n40 B11; GzXIII-1 p. 130]

The reverse movement is documented too. The Urap Agris of Salsette and Bassein, who returned to Hinduism in 1820 and 1828, kept the surnames of their Christian past. Enthoven’s Tribes and Castes of Bombay lists them as “Gomes, Soz [Souza], Fernan, Frutad [Furtado] and Minez [Menezes]”. [n34 §5; Enthoven vol I, Urap Agris] These are Portuguese names in Marathi mouths, worn down the way “Joseph Manuel Lopez” became “Juju Manu Lop”: Souza to Soz, Furtado to Frutad, Menezes to Minez. The Gazetteer of 1882 names Navapur, Umela, Juchandra, Achole, Virar and Kolwad as Bassein villages with Urap Agris. [n40 §1; GzXIII-1 pp. 116–117 and n.1] The Gomes of the Urap list is worth noticing for another reason: one of the Kupari hamlets of Nirmal parish is called Gomes Ali, “Gomes lane”, and the surname Gomes is therefore attested among Bassein converts by 1882, though no source connects the hamlet’s name to any particular family. [n40 A5; GzXIII-1 p. 116]

The lesson of the Marathi names and of the worn-down Portuguese ones is the same. Names on this coast were not fixed labels. They were given at baptism, kept through reconversion, reshaped by speech, and spelled by whoever was holding the pen.

Toscano and Tuscano: the author’s own name

Every family in these villages could tell a version of this section about its own surname. This one is told about the author’s, because it shows both how far the documents can take a family and where they stop.

The surname Toscano is an Italian-derived name meaning “from Tuscany”. Italian surname dictionaries derive it from the ethnonym and find it most frequent in Campania and Calabria, with “Tuscano” as a southern Italian variant spelling. [n44 §1; British Surnames; surnamesinitaly.com] In Portuguese, toscano is simply the ordinary adjective and noun for “Tuscan”, so the name was at home in the Portuguese language as well as in Italy. [n44 §1; kaikki.org] A commercial heraldry site claims that the Portuguese Toscano family was of Italian origin, with arms of a silver lion on a red field, citing the Armorial Português; no independent confirmation was found, and the claim should be treated as unproven. [n44 §1] Italian merchants, physicians and Jesuits were present in Portuguese Asia, so a Toscano in India could in principle have been of Italian stock; but no document ties any Toscano in India to Italy. [n44 §1 inferences]

In the whole of the Tombo de Baçaim, the name appears three times. [n44 §2; n39 §1B] All three concern one man, the licenciado André Toscano. In the section of the register for the pragana of Herá, a district of the Bassein province, the table of lease-holders has this line: “136v — André Toscano — aldeia Sircar — Em enfiteuse — 100 0 0 — 4,5 — 33 1 45”. [n44 §2] Read with the editors’ note that rents were paid in instalments, it appears to record that André Toscano held the village of Sircar on a hereditary lease for a yearly rent of 100 pardaus, with a first instalment of 33 pardaus, 1 larim and 45 réis; the reading of the unlabelled number columns is the researcher’s. [n44 §2] On the facing folio, the neighbouring entry is D. Maria Coutinho, holding the aldeia Sircanacer “in lives”. [n44 §2]

Two marginal notes, entered by the scribe João Pinheiro, add a date. On 7 May 1727, “ficava encabeçado nestas hortas o licenciado André Toscano em enfiteuse, por compra” (fl. 136): the licenciado André Toscano became the holder of these orchards on a hereditary lease, by purchase, on condition that within a year he have the rent re-valued, on pain of losing it to the royal treasury. The same note appears on a second orchard entry, whose earlier holder had been a woman, described as the wife of António de Amaral de Meneses. [n44 §2]

So in May 1727, twelve years before the fall of Bassein, a university-trained man named Toscano bought his way into land in the Province of the North. Licenciado meant a holder of a university licence, in law or in theology or arts; the tombo does not say whether he was a layman or a cleric-lawyer. [n44 §2] Because he acquired his land by purchase in 1727, he was a recent arrival among the landholders, not an old Bassein house. [n44 §2 inferences] Where “Sircar” lay has not been found; it is in the Herá block of the tombo and cannot be identified with any present-day Vasai village, and certainly not with Gass. [n44 §2 inferences] Who André Toscano was, where he was born, whether he was Goan or European, whether he had any link to the Jesuits or the parishes: nothing reached by the research says. [n44 §2 gaps] No other Toscano, whether captain, priest, notary or married settler, was found in the sources searched for Goa, Chaul, Daman or Bassein. [n44 §2 inferences]

Here the honest caveat has to be stated in full. André Toscano is a documentary anchor for the surname in Baçaim; he is not an ancestor. Nothing found links him, or any other Toscano, to any convert family, as godfather, landlord or anything else. [n44 §3] A man who bought land in 1727 is late for the great conversion waves of about 1535 to the early 1600s, so if he had any connection to a convert family, it would more plausibly be as a godfather at a later baptism than as the source of a name taken in the first generation. That too is an inference, explicitly not documented. [n44 §3 inferences] A Vasai Tuscano family could have taken its name from a godparent or priest called Toscano, from a Toscano landholder or employer, or by some later adoption; which applies to any given family cannot be recovered from the sources so far found. [n44 §3 inferences]

The spelling raises its own question. “Tuscano”, with a u, is the same word as Toscano. It matches the southern Italian variant, but in Vasai it is more likely to have come from English-era or Marathi-influenced spelling in nineteenth- or twentieth-century civil, school and church records, since o and u are close in Marathi-influenced speech. That is an inference; no document supports a date or a cause for the change. [n44 §4 inferences] Sahapedia’s observation that Marathi speakers reshaped Portuguese names is the general basis for such drift, and “Juju Manu Lop” and “Frutad” are documented examples of it. [n44 §4; n39 §1A; n34 §5]

Neither Toscano nor Tuscano appears on the Wikipedia list of Goan Catholic surnames, which also omits Lopes and Rumao, or in the article on Mangalorean Catholic names, which the researcher checked directly. [n44 §4, §6] A list’s silence is weak evidence, but it suggests that Tuscano is not a major Goan or Mangalorean surname, and that in Portuguese India Toscano was a minor name, borne by a handful of individuals such as André Toscano rather than by a great landholding house like the Meneses or the Coutinhos. [n44 §6 inferences] No source was found that lists the villages where Tuscano families live, or that assigns the surname to Kuparis, Vadvals, Kolis or Salsette Christians, and no Tuscano or Toscano with a verified Vasai or East Indian connection was found among clergy, writers, artists, sportspeople or officials in public sources. [n44 §4, §5] The author’s own family is from Gass, a Kupari village; but the documents do not yet say how, or when, a Tuscano came to be there.

The honest sentence, then, is this: the surname Toscano means “Tuscan”; a licenciado André Toscano appears in the Tombo de Baçaim in 1727 as the new holder, by purchase, of the aldeia Sircar and some orchards in the Bassein province; this shows the name was present among Baçaim’s landholders twelve years before the fall of 1739; but no document found links him to any convert family, and how Vasai’s Tuscano families acquired the name remains unknown. [n44, suggested wording]

What would settle a family’s line

Every family that wants to know where its name came from is in the same position as the Tuscanos. The general mechanism is known; the particular case is not. The documents that could settle a particular case exist, or existed, in a few known kinds of place.

The parish registers. These are the real evidence. A Catholic baptismal entry names the child, the parents and the godparents, the padrinho and madrinha whom the 1882 Gazetteer describes carrying the child to church. [n34 §5; GzXIII-1 p. 210] If a run of registers survives for a Vasai parish back to the Portuguese period, the moment a family’s surname first appears, and the name of the godfather or landholder standing beside it, could be found. The research found no parish register of the Vasai villages online. [n39 §1 gaps; n34 §1 gaps] Some registers will have been lost: the Maratha conquest of 1739 destroyed most of the churches outside the fort, and at Nirmal, according to the plaque on the bell tower, the cemetery served as the church from 1739 to 1856. [n40 A1; EI Nirmal] Others, especially after 1739, when priests from Goa and the native clergy under a Vicar General at Kurla took over, may survive in parish safes, in the archives of the Diocese of Vasai, or in Goa. [n34 §6; Mendiratta pp. 216–218] The first practical step for any family is a polite request to its own parish priest to see what the parish holds, and how far back it goes.

Elsie Baptista’s book. The East Indians: Catholic Community of Bombay, Salsette and Bassein (1967) is the standard reference on the community, cited for the five groups into which it divides the East Indians, among them the Samavedi Christians “commonly called Kuparis”. It is not online, and the research could not check what it says about surnames, villages or families. [n34 §1 gaps, §7; n39 §1 gaps] It belongs on the reading list of anyone writing a family history, in a library copy.

The Tombo and the thesis. The Tombo de Baçaim itself, of which only the table of holders has been published, lies in the Portuguese archives; André Teixeira’s thesis Baçaim e o seu território (1534–1665) would cover the earlier period, but the repository holding it could not be reached. [n39 §1 gaps; n34 §3 gaps] The Torre do Tombo’s online catalogue, digitarq, could not be searched full-text by the research; a name search there, in the India and Overseas Council series, is the next step for any surname, Toscano included. [n44 §2 gaps]

The Jesuit letters and chronicles. Documenta Indica, the edited Jesuit letters, gives baptisms, procession and method, but its volumes were searched only for Baçaim passages, and a name index search for particular surnames has not been done. [n44 §1 gaps; n34 §10] Franciscan, Dominican and Augustinian chronicles, which would cover Nirmal, Agashi, Nandakhal, Remedy, Merces and Pali, were not reached at all. [n40 overall gaps]

A Portuguese onomastic dictionary, José Pedro Machado’s, for what each name meant in Portugal. [n39 §1 gaps]

And the families themselves, which is the subject of the last part of this chapter.

Until those records are read, the truthful statement for any Vasai Christian surname is the one this chapter has tried to make for Tuscano: what the name means; whether a person of that name appears in the Baçaim record, and when; and the plain admission that no document yet links that person to the family.

II. Villages

Two belts and a list

Before describing the villages one by one, it is worth being clear about how the list was made, because no document makes it for us.

No primary source lists the Kupari villages or the Vadval villages. The Gazetteer of 1882 lists occupations and a few named places; the censuses never counted Kuparis; the parish directories list parishes, not communities. [n40 §1 gaps] What exists is three kinds of evidence, each partial.

The first is the community’s own list. Two community writers, Sachin Mendes in 2007 and Rajan D’Mello in 2009, name thirteen Kupari places: Nirmal, Agashi, Nandakhal, Gass, Bhuigaon, Umraala (Umrale), Naanbhat, Shrilaiwadi, Bolinj, Mardes, Wagholi, Gomes Ali and Rajodi. [n39 §2; n40 §1; vasaikupari blog; D’Mello blog] D’Mello’s post cites no sources and dates the community’s origin to the “15th century”, which contradicts its own 1534 treaty date. [n40 §1] A parish table preserved in a mirror of an older version of Wikipedia’s “Kupari” article adds Vatar (Our Lady of Nazareth, a centre under Nandakhal), Jyoti (Our Lady of Light, split from Agashi), Shirlaiwadi (Our Lady of Lourdes, from Agashi), Gomes Ali (Holy Trinity, from Nirmal) and Gass (St Gonsalo Garcia, from Nirmal). [n40 §1; Bharatpedia mirror]

The second is the 1882 Gazetteer, which never uses the word Kupari but describes the same country. It calls the “richly wooded well-watered and highly tilled tract, including Bolinj, Koprad and Umbrala in the north, Nirmal and Vagholi in the west, Gass in the south, and Sopara and Mardesh in the centre”, “the garden or agar of Sopara”, and says that “the husbandmen of Sopara are Christians, many of them converted Samvedi Bráhmans”. [n40 §1; GzXIV pp. 314–315] [n11; Gaz. 1882 p. 314] And in its chapter on crafts it says that dried plantains were made “only in the villages of Agáshi, Vagholi, Vatár, Bolinj, Koprád, Nala, Umrále, Rajodi, and Murdes all in Bassein”. [n40 §1; GzXIII-1 p. 292] That single sentence, written by a British official with no interest in community lists, independently names nine of the thirteen villages on the community’s list, or their immediate neighbours. [n40 §1]

The third is a scatter of statements about the Vadvals. The Gazetteer says that “the traders, Christian cartmen chiefly of Chulna, Mánikpur, Gokhivra, Sándor, and Barhánpur in Bassein, are known as Vadvals or gardeners”. [n40 §1; GzXIII-1 p. 97] Wikipedia describes Sandor as “predominantly Vadval”; a community photograph captions a “Vadval East Indian house in Giriz”. [n40 §1] And at Agashi, Vatar and Koprad the Gazetteer records “about eighty-five families of Sámvedi Bráhmans, fifteen families of Páchkalshis, and about seventy-five families of Native Christians originally Sámvedis and Páchkalshis”. [n40 §1; GzXIII-1 p. 395]

From these, two belts emerge. The Kupari villages are the northern and western garden belt: Nirmal, Agashi, Nandakhal and the villages around them, the 1882 agar of Agashi and Sopara. The Vadval Christian villages are the southern Bassein parishes around the old fort: Sandor, Papdi, Remedy, Merces, Pali, Manickpur, Chulne, Gokhivare and their neighbours. Hindu Pachkalshi and Vadval families live inside the northern belt too, at Agashi, Vatar and Koprad, and in Virar and Umela. [n40 §1 takeaway] The line between them was never a wall; at Agashi, the one village where the 1882 record counts both, Kupari and Hindu Pachkalshi families lived side by side, and the Gazetteer’s “Native Christians originally Sámvedis and Páchkalshis” suggests that in 1882 observers did not draw the line between Samvedi and Vadval Christians sharply either. [n40 A2 inferences; n34 §1]

Some names that are sometimes put on such lists do not belong there. Anjur, whose Naik family received a patilship and inam from the Peshwa after 1739, is in Bhiwandi taluka, not Vasai. [n40 §1, B12; GzXIII-1 p. 130 n.1] The Census 2011 village “Kopri”, with 10,180 people, is a Virar-side village and not the Kupari hamlet of Koprad. [n40 §1] Navapur, Holi, Tarkhad and Khochivade have no Kupari or Vadval evidence in any source reached. [n40 §1]

All these villages share one documented backbone. They lay in a Gujarat Sultanate port and ship-building country taken by the Portuguese between 1530 and 1534; Bassein district passed into Portuguese possession on 23 December 1534. [n40 §2; da Cunha p. 157] The first parish churches outside the fort were built between about 1557 and 1606. One list, from a local history website, gives “Nirmal (1557), Remedi (1557), Sandor (1566), Agashi (1568), Nandakhal (1573), Papdi (1574), Pali (1595), Manickpur (1606), and Merces (1606)”; other sources give different dates for almost every one, and each entry below sets out the conflict. [n40 §2; vasaiker.com] The Marathas besieged Bassein from 17 February 1739, and the surrender treaty was signed on 16 May 1739; most churches outside the fort were destroyed in the campaign. [n40 §2; da Cunha p. 148] Under the Marathas and then the British, these villages were the farming hinterland of Bombay. In 1876 da Cunha counted 12,400 native Christians in the Bassein parishes: Palli 800, Espirito Santo (Nandakhal) 2,000, Papari 1,200, Nossa Senhora das Merces 700, Monte Calvario 1,500, Manikpura 900, Nossa Senhora dos Remedios 3,000, Agasi 900, and Sam Thome 1,000. [n40 §2; da Cunha p. 161] The Gazetteer adds Nirmal’s 2,184. [n40 §2; GzXIV p. 293] And on 3 July 2009 most of them were absorbed into the new Vasai-Virar City Municipal Corporation, formed from four municipal councils and 53 villages; a Wikipedia-derived page gives 2010, a conflict. [n40 §2; VVCMC] Before that, the 2008 heritage survey had listed them as gram panchayats: Agashe, Arnala, Vatar, Satphale, Kofrad, Bolinj, Umbrale, Nale-Rajodi, Vagholi, Madres, Navale, Ghas, Nirmal, Bhuigaon Khurd and Budruk, Girij, Kaular Khurd and Budruk, Saloli, Sandor, Mulgaon, Umele, Naigaon, Juchandra, Gokhivare and others. [n40 §2; CRIT Vol I] Because the corporation’s whole population, 1,222,390 in 2011, was counted as one urban unit, almost none of these villages has its own figure in the 2011 Census. [n40 §2; census2011.co.in]

For most villages, then, there is no “state in 2026” from any village-level source. Where the entries below say nothing about the present, it is because nothing was found, not because nothing is happening.

The portraits follow the order of the belt, starting with the mother parish of the north.

Nirmal: the stainless place

Nirmal is the religious centre of the northern belt for Hindus and Christians alike, and its name is a story in itself.

The Gazetteer of 1882 gives two derivations. The first is the temple’s own: the Nirmal Mahatmya tells that a demon, Vimal, harassed Brahman seers near the Vaitarna; Parashuram cut off his hands and feet, which grew back; with Shiva’s help Parashuram overcame him, pardoned him, and set up the linga Vimaleshvar where he fell, and “from Vimal it came to be called Nirmal or stainless”. [n40 A1; GzXIV p. 292 n.2] The second came from a British civil servant, Mr A. Cumine, who proposed that the name was a Sanskrit rendering of Dravidian nir, water, and mal, hill: “the sea hill”, which the Gazetteer called “a true description of the place which is a wooded mound rising from bare flats once under water”. [n40 A1; GzXIV p. 292 n.2] Da Cunha in 1876 gave “Vimala … now called Nirmala, or stainless, since it was purified by Parasurama”. [n40 A1; da Cunha p. 126] The first is sacred tradition, the second a colonial guess; neither is proven.

The place is old. The Skanda Purana calls it “the best of holy pools, the seat of perfection”, and in front of a house about 500 yards north of the chief temple stood a dressed stone with letters “which seem part of a Sanskrit inscription of the seventh century”, the earliest datable thing at Nirmal. [n40 A1; GzXIV pp. 292–293] Tradition makes Nirmal the burial place of one of the great Shankaracharyas; the Gazetteer, noting that the great Shankaracharya died in Kashmir, suggested that the Nirmal one “may have been one of his religious descendants”. [n40 A1; GzXIV p. 292 n.3] A 2008 heritage survey repeats a temple tradition that dates samadhis here to the fifth and fourth centuries BC; no historian supports those dates. [n40 A1; CRIT Vol III asset 82] Hindu Samvedis regard Adi Shankaracharya as their chief teacher, and the same survey records a tradition linking the Samvedis to temple musicians here, a name that became “Samadhi” and “Shamedi”. [n25; n08] [n40 A1; CRIT Vol III asset 82a] These are traditions, told by the community and the temple, and are reported here as such.

The Christian story of Nirmal begins with destruction. The Portuguese, both the Gazetteer and da Cunha say, “had pulled down the temples and destroyed the ling”; da Cunha says “the Inquisition pulled down the temple”. [n40 A1; GzXIV p. 293; da Cunha pp. 126–127] The church of Santa Cruz, Holy Cross, was built near the old holy place. Its date is disputed by up to three decades: a community blog and a parish directory say 1557 or 1558; the marble plaque on the bell tower says “This Church was built by the Portuguese in 1580”; the heritage register says “1586; rebuilt 1856” in one place and “built by the Franciscan missionaries in 1580” in another. None is a contemporary document. [n40 A1] The plaque, in its Marathi form “Santa Cruza”, goes on: “Destroyed during Portuguese-Maratha war in 1739. Rebuilt by the Parishioners in 1856, at the cost of Rs 20,000 and physical labour. From 1739 to 1856 the present Cemetery was pro tempore their Church.” [n40 A1; EI Nirmal] For 117 years, if the plaque is right, the Christians of Nirmal worshipped in their graveyard.

The Hindu story of the same years runs the other way. “On the fall of Bassein (1739) Nirmal was purified, prints of the feet of Shri Dattatraya took the place of the ling, and a reservoir was built”, and stones the Portuguese had built into churches “were probably used to raise the temples of the older gods”. [n40 A1; GzXIV p. 293] About 1750 Shankarji Keshav, the Maratha Sarsubhedar of Bassein, built eight temples at Nirmal, endowed with yearly grants that the British still paid in 1882: the Shankaracharya Svami (Rs 421), Mahavishnu (Rs 118), Vimaleshvar Mahadev (Rs 100), Bhuteshvar (Rs 60), Ganpati, Durga and Hanuman together (Rs 35), Siddheshvar (Rs 25), a Brahman alms-house (Rs 454) and a Bairagi alms-house (Rs 148). [n40 A1; GzXIV pp. 292–293] The 2008 survey’s “early 17th century … patron: Peshwa” cannot be right as stated, and a 2019 blog that credits Chimaji Appa contradicts the Gazetteer’s Shankarji Keshav. [n40 A1] The same stones, it seems, moved from temple to church and back again as rulers changed. [n11 Q4 inferences]

By 1882 Nirmal held both stories in one landscape. The Holy Cross church, rebuilt “by the parishioners”, measured 96 by 28 by 26 feet, had a congregation of 2,184, a vicar paid a British grant of Rs 14-8 a month, and a private school whose teacher played the violin in church. [n40 A1; GzXIV p. 293] The Gazetteer gives the rebuilding cost as about Rs 24,000, against the plaque’s Rs 20,000. [n40 A1] On the hill, the Shankaracharya fair on Kartik Vadya 11, in November, lasted a week and drew 6,000 to 7,000 pilgrims, “Hindus, Musalmans, Christians, and a few Parsis”, trading brass and copper vessels, dried plantains, sweetmeats, cloth and cattle worth about Rs 10,000. [n40 A1; GzXIV p. 292] Below, the Vimal and Malai talavs surrounded the temple hill, and “from the Shankaracharya temple is a fine view across the great Nirmal lake to the sea”. [n40 A1; GzXIV p. 292 n.2] A flight of stone steps led down the west side of the hill to the village. [n39 §3]

Holy Cross is the mother parish of the Kupari west. From it were carved Gass (petition 1941, parish 1942), Bhuigaon (2 June 1946) and Gomes Ali (5 June 2004), and, according to the mirror table, Mardes and Wagholi. [n40 A1; EI pages; Bharatpedia] Nirmal’s priest also served Nandakhal’s worshippers before Nandakhal had a church of its own. [n38 §5; Wikipedia, Nandakhal] The feast of the Holy Cross is kept on 14 September. [n38 §5; UCA News directory] In 1979 the vicar of Nirmal was Fr Edwin Colaco, later Bishop of Amravati. [n40 A1; EI Nirmal] Holy Cross High School, Nirmal, educated boys from the surrounding villages; a Gass boy who studied there became Bishop of Poona in 2026. [n42 §6]

Nirmal is no longer a separate census village; before 2009 it was a gram panchayat, with Navale and Vagholi as neighbours, and the Nirmal Bazaar of 1957 lies in Vagholi gram panchayat. [n40 A1; CRIT Vol I] The temple hill is reached from “Nirmal naka”, and the November jatra still runs. [n40 A1] Beyond that, no 2026 source describes the village.

Agashi: the shipyard village

Agashi is the best-documented village of the northern belt, and the one where the documents speak most clearly about who lived there.

The name has no recorded etymology. The Portuguese wrote it Agaçaim, and a 1570 source “Agazim”; da Cunha used Agasi and Aganasi. [n40 A2] The oldest dated object associated with it is a stone found at Agashi, later moved to the Collector’s garden at Thana, dated Shaka 1072 (AD 1150), in the reign of the Shilahara king Haripaldev, which granted a village in Shurparaka; the village it granted, though, was not Agashi but Vattaraka, the modern Vatar, and its witnesses were Vatar’s headmen. [n40 A2; GzXIV p. 385 and n.1]

The Gazetteer calls Agashi “probably an old timber trade and ship-building centre”. About 1530 it built ships “as good as Portuguese ships, able to make the voyage to Europe” (the printed text says “1580”, probably a slip). The Portuguese burned the town and 300 boats; the chronicler Faria y Souza adds a second destruction in March 1531. [n40 A2; GzXIV pp. 1–2] Then the Franciscans came. In 1535 Fr Antonio do Porto founded an orphanage here for forty boys, Nossa Senhora da Luz, Our Lady of Light; da Cunha says that “Agasi was the first place of this district visited by Franciscan missionaries”. [n40 A2; da Cunha p. 159] In April 1540 a Gujarat army under the general “Bramaluc”, retreating after being repelled at Bassein by Captain Lourenço de Tavora, came through Agashi; by Portuguese accounts five people who had stayed in the orphanage refused to become Muslims and were burnt, a “batch of Christian martyrs … consisting entirely of native converts”. [n40 A2; da Cunha pp. 159–160] The community retells this as the story of the first Indian martyrs. [n25 §1.3; EI Agashi] In 1554 a great mosque became the commandant’s residence; in 1570 “Agazim” was a port trading with Gujarat; a hurricane struck in 1618; and in 1634 Mughal raiders from Gujarat destroyed “a handsome Dominican monastery”. [n40 A2; GzXIV pp. 1–2; da Cunha p. 160]

The Lisbon archive record of St Gonsalo Garcia, the first Indian-born saint, describes him as “natural de Agaçaim”, a native of Agashi. [n25 §1.1; DNA 2014] He was the son of a Portuguese soldier and a local mother, schooled by the Jesuits at Fort Bassein, and crucified at Nagasaki on 5 February 1597. [n25 §1.1] His story is told in full elsewhere in this book; here it is enough that the oldest archival birthplace of Vasai’s saint is the shipyard village of the north.

The 1727–30 tombo shows Agashi held by a lady, D. Domingas Pereira de Azevedo, who also held the rights over Arnala island and the wild palms. [n40 A2; Tombo fl. 41, 42, 44v] Twelve years later the Marathas took it, and they used it as a base. Shankarji Keshav Phadke built the large temple of Bhavani Shankar here, with a yearly grant of Rs 50, beside a holy bathing place believed to cure skin diseases, and a Hanuman temple (Rs 8); smaller Vishnu and Ganpati temples stood near them. [n40 A2; GzXIV p. 2] The Gazetteer’s date of “AD 1691 (Shak 1613)” for the Bhavani Shankar temple cannot fit a commander active from 1737; the heritage survey labels the temples “18th century, patron Peshwa”; the conflict is unresolved. [n40 A2; n25 §2] A Jain temple of Parasnath was built about 1820 by “Motilal a Vani of Bombay”. [n40 A2; GzXIV p. 2]

Under the Marathas the Christians stayed. On 16 December 1760 the French traveller Anquetil du Perron reached Agashi on the church’s feast day, “through the finest orchards in the world”, and found the lanes “full of people going to church as freely as in a Christian country”; the priest was entertaining the “Canarin” priests who had helped at the service. [n40 A2; GzXIV p. 2; da Cunha p. 164] Two churches had once stood here, Our Lady of Light under the Franciscans and St James under the secular clergy; both were destroyed in 1739, and St James was rebuilt soon after, by the heritage survey’s date in 1760. [n40 A2; EI Agashi; CRIT Vol III asset 44] In 1837 Agashi was “the chief town on Bassein island with a large Christian and Hindu population”, trading grain, garden produce and salt to Gujarat and Bombay, and building “very good fishing boats and coasting craft”. [n40 A2; GzXIV p. 2]

The census of 1881 gives the clearest picture of any village in this chapter: 6,823 people, of whom 5,168 were Hindus, 1,500 Christians and 155 Muslims. [n40 A2; GzXIV p. 2] Agashi traded plantains and betel leaf with Bombay, “its dried plantains being the best in the district”; sea exports averaged Rs 3,18,470 a year and imports Rs 85,650 in the five years to 1879. [n40 A2; GzXIV p. 2] The church then standing, “built after the destruction of Nossa Senhora da Luz”, measured 75 by 22 by 16 feet; its vicar was paid Rs 14-8 a month by the British government, and its school of seventeen pupils learned Portuguese reading, arithmetic, singing and music, with a master who played the violin in church. [n40 A2; GzXIV p. 2] At Agashi, Vatar and Koprad together, the eighty-five Samvedi, fifteen Pachkalshi and seventy-five Native Christian families dried about 160 tons of plantains a year, worth about Rs 27,000. [n40 A2; GzXIII-1 pp. 292, 395]

The parish of St James was extended by Fr Jovito V. Dias in 1900 and again in 1975. Its schools followed: the English primary school “Holy Martyrs” in 1938, named for the orphans of 1540, St James Marathi School in 1968 and St James High School in 1977. [n40 A2; EI Agashi] The parish’s seven villages are Mangala, Jyoti, Kumbar Wada, Bhandar Wada, Cross Naka, Dhobi Wada and Shirlai; Jyoti, under the old title of Our Lady of Light, and Shirlai, or Shirlaiwadi, under Our Lady of Lourdes (listed in 1987), later became parishes of their own. [n40 A2] Fr Ismail Da Costa, an assistant at Agashi, built a hut chapel for the Koli fishermen of Arnala in 1919, which became St Peter’s church. [n40 A2, C1]

The heritage survey’s “Agashe Talav Precinct” gathers the Hindu town around the tank: the Bhawani Shankar, Hanuman, Vishnu and Ram temples; Khanolkar House, a Peshwa guest house; Phadkewada, built by Subhedar Phadke and “lost to high-rise construction” by 2019; a public library founded in 1914 by Seth Shah Prabhudas Bhagvandas, in a building of 1939; and a Zilla Parishad school of 1886. [n40 A2; CRIT Vol I items 43–46] A blog adds the Garde family’s Ram Mandir of 1885 and Chalpeth Road, lined with Maratha-era houses. [n40 A2] The same blog says that many structures 100 to 150 years old were lost to urbanisation in the decade and a half before 2019. [n40 A2] Agashi was a gram panchayat until 2009; it has no 2011 census figure of its own, and no 2026 source describes it.

Nandakhal: the church the bees defended

Nandakhal’s name has no recorded meaning, and its church, the Holy Spirit, has more than one date. But it has the best parish record of any Kupari village, because the parish wrote its own history and that history found its way, translated, onto Wikipedia.

The church, the community pages say, was built “about 1573” by the Franciscans, “primarily for the convenience of those parishioners of Holy Cross who lived closer to Nandakhal”. [n40 A3; EI Nandkhal] The heritage survey agrees on the Franciscans and on 1573, and adds what makes Nandakhal unusual: “Though the Franciscans had to give up the parish at the time of the Maratha invasion in 1739, the church escaped destruction.” [n40 A3; CRIT Vol III asset 60b] Da Cunha in 1876 called “Espirito Santo” “the oldest among the rural churches, and the only one standing in its primitive state, i.e. whose restoration has not interfered with its former shape”, and counted 2,000 Christians in its parish. [n40 A3; da Cunha p. 161] (He also listed it among churches under the Jesuits, which does not fit the Franciscan record; his list may mix groups.) [n40 A3] It is described as the only church dedicated to the Holy Ghost in the diocese. [n40 A3; n09]

Why did it survive 1739? The parish has a story. Maratha soldiers who stormed the church, it says, retreated when they were attacked by a swarm of bees from the surrounding woods. [n40 A3; EI Nandkhal] That is a tradition, and it is reported here as one; no Maratha or Portuguese source records it.

There is a documentary puzzle here too. The Gazetteer’s list of nine Bassein churches in 1881 includes a Holy Spirit church, but places it at Koprad, not Nandakhal; and the heritage survey records that the Nandakhal church stands in Kofrad (Koprad) gram panchayat, on Nandakhal Road. [n34 §3; GzXIII-1 p. 206 n.3] [n40 A3; CRIT Vol I item 60] The likeliest reading is that the Gazetteer’s “Koprad” church and today’s Nandakhal church are the same building, named for different parts of one cluster of hamlets; but that has not been confirmed. [n34 §3]

The parish history, as translated on Wikipedia, gives the rest. Franciscan priests were first appointed in 1585; after 1739 priests came from Goa. In 1907 the Collector granted forest timber free for repairs; in 1908 a big bell was donated. In 1922 Mgr Francesco de Silva cleared the forest, planted coconuts and in 1923 started an English school, which closed for lack of pupils; the parish history quotes the reason given, “we get bread and butter from farming and not by studying”. Marathi schools followed from 1935 and St Joseph’s school in 1939. [n40 A3; Wikipedia, Holy Spirit Church Nandakhal] On 5 January 1943 Rev. Frank Lobo was ordained, the first local priest “after 370 years”. [n40 A3; n25 §1.12] The Queen of the Apostles sisters came in 1952 (the text also says 1935). Mother Teresa visited on 6 April 1986. [n40 A3] The church was rebuilt and extended in stages: in 1907–10 with government timber, four metal pillars and an upper floor; a front extension in 1920; a dividing wall removed in 1973; a new sacristy and residence in 1978–83, blessed by Cardinal Simon Pimenta on 27 November 1983; and a 425th anniversary in 1996–97. It has twin towers, gilded altars, a Pentecost painting, a lakeside statue of Christ the King from the 1960s, a statue of Mary from 2006, and a statue of Pope John Paul II given in 2011, a reminder of the Pope’s visit to Vasai in February 1986. [n09; n40 A3]

Nandakhal is the mother of the eastern Kupari parishes, as Nirmal is of the western ones. Umrale became independent in 1970, Nanbhat on 1 June 1975; Bolinj was made a centre in 1988 and a parish on 10 June 2001; a centre opened at Jeladi in 1990; the Rajodi church was inaugurated on 1 May 1999. [n40 A3; n09]

In December 2016 the parish counted 2,150 families and 7,339 people in 29 hamlet groups. The largest were Ghosali (767 people), Nandan (585), Persav (552), Kolbhag (485) and Jeladi (401); others included Rumao Ali (369), Ranebhat (330) and Vatar-Barodi (187). [n40 A3] One of those hamlet names, Rumao Ali, “Rumao lane”, carries a Portuguese surname in Vasai spelling, like Gomes Ali in Nirmal; no source explains either. Several others end in bhat, the Marathi word for paddy land, as in Unganbhat, Ranebhat, Torbhat, Sakharbhat, Medhe-Borbhat and Banbhat. [n40 A10] By the parish’s own count, Nandakhal “has sent 68 priests and many nuns”. [n40 A3] Those figures come from the parish and are unaudited.

The open space in front of the church is, in the heritage survey’s words, “the chief public place in the area”. [n40 A3; CRIT Vol I item 60] Farming dominated until the late 1960s and 1970s, by community accounts. [n40 A3] Fr Francis D’Britto, the Marathi writer and green-belt campaigner, was from Jeladi in this parish, and was buried from Nandakhal in July 2024 before a crowd of about 3,000. [n40 A3; n38 §6] He belongs to the third part of this chapter.

Bhuigaon: wells, crosses and a bishop

Bhuigaon lies about two kilometres south-west of Nirmal, toward the sea. Its name is Marathi and probably means “land village”, from bhui, earth; no source gives an etymology, so that is an inference. [n40 A4] It appears first in print in 1876, when da Cunha described the Arnala and Vaitarna side of the island as “divided into several villages, at one of which, by name Bhoegaum, the sea enters, runs through that of Vagholi, and debouches at that of Vatari”. [n40 A4; da Cunha p. 159] He was describing a creek, and Bhuigaon was where the sea came in. The 1882 Gazetteer has no entry for it, and no Bhuigaon title can be matched in the 1727–30 tombo. [n40 A4]

The ground of Bhuigaon holds older things than its name. On 1 January 1956 N. A. Gore, librarian of the Bombay Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society, found at Bhuigaon a fragment of the ninth rock edict of the emperor Ashoka, of the third century BC; it was taken to the Prince of Wales Museum in Bombay and published by D. C. Sircar in Epigraphia Indica. [n25 §4; Sircar, EI 32] (A corpus listing places the fragment under edict VIII instead; the disagreement is unresolved.) [n25]

Administratively Bhuigaon is two villages, Bhuigaon Khurd and Bhuigaon Budruk, small and large, the usual Maratha pairing, each a gram panchayat before 2009. The heritage survey lists the Kumbhar, Khurd, Bhola, Gorlai and Dongri talavs, and a “Church - Champavati Mandir Road”, a Hindu temple on the same road as the church. [n40 A4; CRIT Vol I items 19–20, 83–87]

The parish story is told in the community calendar. “Out of the thousand Catholics of the village only a handful attended services at Nirmal,” it says, because the walk was long. Fr, later Mgr, Raymond Mendez began catechism classes; a simple church was built in 1946 on land given by the poor villagers, with money from the Archbishop of Bombay and the parishes of Bandra; and on 2 June 1946 Bhuigaon became the second village, after Gass, to be separated from Holy Cross, Nirmal, with Fr Acuico Dourado as its first parish priest. A single-storeyed structure begun in 1948 took seven years; Fr Nazareth Garbu completed the present church in 1984. [n40 A4; EI Bhuigaon] The church is dedicated to the Holy Family.

The most vivid description of Bhuigaon is a blogger’s, from a walk in April 2011. It found about forty large irrigation wells, bhowkal, kept by the women of the community, watering vegetables, hybrid bananas and flowers and holding monsoon water for paddy. In the western quarter stood the Madikar Cross, which the blogger called “the local equivalent of the Gaondevi”, the village goddess, marking the territory of the extended Marti family; crosses were fixed on front doors; the dialect was Kadodi, “different from the dialect of the neighbouring Vadval community”; and the community’s correspondence and magazine were in Marathi. [n40 A4; n07 §2; Mumbai Paused 2011] The blogger also recorded a family tradition of descent from entertainers from Orissa; that is an unverified oral claim, and other research treats it as a late community legend. [n40 A4; n34 §1] The blogger forecast that the wells would be eaten by sprawl within ten years; that was a forecast, not a finding, and no later source says whether it came true. [n40 A4] Fifteen years after that walk, a family in Bhuigaon could tell a historian more about its wells than any document can.

Bhuigaon’s most famous son is Thomas Dabre, born there on 23 October 1945, ordained priest on 31 October 1971, auxiliary bishop of Bombay from 1990, the first Bishop of Vasai from 22 May 1998 (installed at Papdi on 15 August 1998), and Bishop of Poona from 2009 to 2023. [n40 A4; GCatholic; Catholic-Hierarchy] Bhuigaon beach is today a developing tourist spot. [n40 A4] No 2026 source describes the village.

Gomes Ali, Chowkala, Vanda, Rambai and Darseng

Gomes Ali is the newest Kupari parish, in the north-west of Nirmal’s old territory. Its name pairs the Marathi ali, lane, with the Portuguese surname Gomes, which looks like a hamlet named for a landholding family; no source says so. [n40 A5] The surname Gomes is attested among Bassein converts by 1882, in the Urap Agri list, but that proves nothing about this lane. [n40 A5; GzXIII-1 p. 116]

The parish record is short and precise. Gomes Ali, Chowkala, Vanda, Rambai and Darseng formed “a cluster of five villages” within Nirmal parish. Mass for the section was first offered on 2 September 1979 by Fr Edwin Colaco, “then Vicar of Nirmal, now Bishop of Amravati”, in the primary local board school at Chowkala. Don Bosco sisters came on 31 January 1992. In 2001 a committee of 72 “servant-leaders” was formed; Bishop Thomas Dabre blessed the foundation stone on 2 December 2001; the church and parochial house were blessed on Trinity Sunday, 25 May 2002; and on 5 June 2004 the parish of the Most Holy Trinity was established, with Fr Johnson Menezes as its first parish priest. [n40 A5, A1; EI Nirmal] Beyond these dates, nothing about the five villages, not their old names, their origins or their populations, was found. [n40 A5 gaps]

Gass

Gass, the southern Kupari village of the Sopara agar, and the author’s birthplace, has its own chapter, the next in this book, Chapter 26. Only the facts the neighbouring villages depend on are set down here. In 1882 the Gazetteer called it “the village of Gass, which, according to the people of the place, is the site of old Sopara”; boats of ten to twelve tons still reached its landing, about a mile from Sopara, and it shipped molasses, rice and betel to Surat; in its field Sonarbhat lay a female figure, perhaps Ambika, the remains of a Jain-looking temple, a Muslim saint’s tomb bordered with old bricks, and basalt blocks carved with letters of about the second century BC, one at the house of Degu Pobria Mhatara and one at that of the Samvedi Brahman Bab Naik. [n40 A6; GzXIV pp. 315–316, 341] The village petitioned for its own parish in 1941; the parish of St Gonsalo Garcia was formed from Nirmal in 1942, with Fr Thomas as its first priest, and its church was blessed by Cardinal Gracias on 8 March 1962. [n40 A6; EI Ghass] It has no separate 2011 census figure; the Virar-side Chandansar-Gass-Kopri gram panchayat group is a different place, and the tombo’s “Gris” is Giriz, not Gass. [n40 A6] Its people, its parish, its saint’s feast, its bishop and its fight in 2025 against a dumping ground are told in Chapter 26.

Wagholi: the creek village

Wagholi, written Vagholi in 1882 and not to be confused with the Wagholi near Pune, has no recorded etymology. [n40 A7] It was a creek village. Da Cunha in 1876 traced the sea-channel that entered at Bhuigaon, ran through Vagholi and came out at Vatar; the 1882 Gazetteer notes that Anquetil du Perron in 1760 “mentions Vagholi with a creek of its own”. [n40 A7; da Cunha p. 159; GzXIV p. 322]

In the tombo of 1727–30 the village appears twice: “Vagoly”, held by Manuel de Abreu de Lima, and “Vastal Vagoly”, held by Fernão da Silveira e Meneses for 60 pardaus. [n40 A7; Tombo fl. 41v, 45] In 1882 Vagholi was one of the three landings, with Gass and Bolinj, from which molasses, rice and betel went by sea to Surat, Broach, Jambusar and Gogha; small boats came “from the west as near as Vagholi about two miles” from Sopara. [n40 A7; GzXIV pp. 315–316] An inscribed stone of fourteen lines, all illegible, was taken from a well in Vagholi, about a mile west of Sopara, to the Collector’s garden at Thana. [n40 A7; GzXIV pp. 342, 419] And Vagholi is on the 1882 list of plantain-drying villages. [n40 A7; GzXIII-1 p. 292]

The mirror table gives a parish of Our Lady of Velankanni for Wagholi and Mardes, split from Nirmal, without a date; the same table gives the same dedication to Rajodi, so at least one entry is wrong. [n40 A7] The heritage survey records the Bhandale Talav off the Nirmal-Aghase road and the Nirmal Bazaar of 1957 on the Nirmal-Kalamb road in Vagholi gram panchayat. [n40 A7; CRIT Vol I items 68–69] No named person, parish founding date or population was found. [n40 A7 gaps]

Mardes: the edict village

Mardes stood, in 1882, “in the centre” of the Sopara agar, with Sopara itself. [n40 A8; GzXIV p. 314] The Gazetteer spells it Mardesh, and in its plantain list “Murdes”; the 2008 survey writes Madres. [n40 A8] In 1727–30 the tombo shows “Mardes Sirgoly” held in enfiteuse by Lucas de Almeida de Andrade, for 180 pardaus. [n40 A8; Tombo fl. 47v] Of all the tombo’s holders, his is the Almeida name in the Kupari heartland, the same surname that a Gass family would carry to the bishop’s chair of Poona three centuries later; whether the two are connected, no document says.

Mardes is the village of the Ashoka stupa. The heritage survey records the third-century-BC stupa, with the ninth edict, at “Madres”, and the Madres Talav, where “local Catholic communities gather around the cross for prayers and ritual processions” at Christmas and the feast of the Nativity. [n40 A8; CRIT Vol I items 70–71; Vol III asset 71] (The survey’s placing of the ninth edict here sits uneasily with the record of its discovery at Bhuigaon in 1956; the Sopara finds of 1882, including part of an Ashokan edict, are told in earlier chapters.) [n40 A8; GzXIV p. 386] [n25 §4] A Christian cross beside a tank, a few hundred yards from an emperor’s stupa, is as good a picture of this coast as any.

The parish is shared with Wagholi in the mirror table. [n40 A8] No date, named person or population was found.

Umrale: the pilgrim centre

Umrale is the north-western village of the Sopara agar, named with Bolinj and Koprad “in the north” in 1882, and on the plantain list of the same year; the Gazetteer spells it Umbrala and Umrale. [n40 A9; GzXIV p. 314; GzXIII-1 p. 292] The community lists it as Umraala. It does not appear in the tombo under any spelling the research could match. [n40 A9]

Its parish history comes through Nandakhal’s. Mass was said at Umrale and Nanbhat from 1949 under Mgr Mendes; a prayer house was founded at Umrale and Nanbhat by Mgr Vaz in a year the text gives as “1935” but which it contradicts elsewhere; and Umrale became an independent parish, St Joseph’s, in 1970. [n40 A9; Wikipedia, Holy Spirit Church Nandakhal] On 7 June 2026 the Diocese of Vasai designated St Joseph’s Church, Umralla, a pilgrim centre for the Year of St Francis, declared by Pope Leo XIV for the 800th anniversary of the saint’s death, with a plenary indulgence. [n38 §5; Catholic Connect] That makes Umrale one of the few Kupari villages with a dated event in 2026.

The heritage survey lists two tanks, the Kudhvat Talav on the Umbrale-Kofrad road and the Umbrale Talav on the Umbrale-Karmala road, where Ganesh idols are immersed at the Hindu festival. [n40 A9; CRIT Vol I items 65–66] As at Madres, the tanks of a Kupari village are shared by both faiths.

Nanbhat: the paddy hamlets

Nanbhat, or Naanbhat, is a farming hamlet group on the Nandakhal side. Its name may join nan to bhat, the Marathi word for paddy land that recurs through the Nandakhal hamlets; that is an inference. [n40 A10] Mass came from Nandakhal from 1949, and “on 1 June 1975, due to the efforts of Monsignor Philip Tavares, the Nanbhat parish became independent”, under the patronage of St Paul. [n40 A10; Wikipedia, Holy Spirit Church Nandakhal; Bharatpedia] The Gazetteer, the tombo and the heritage survey do not mention it. It has no census figure, and no 2026 source. Nanbhat is a village whose history, outside a single sentence in a parish chronicle, exists only in the memory of its families.

Bolinj: the town that was

Bolinj is where this chapter began, at the doorstep of Joseph Manuel Lopez. In 1882 it was the northern limit of navigation on the backwater the Gazetteer called the Bolinj creek. [n39 §3; GzXIV pp. 314–315]

The tombo gives it a fidalga landlady in 1727–30: “pacaria Bolinja e terra Belvar”, leased for lives to D. Maria Henriques Coelho da Silva for 450 pardaus. [n40 A11; Tombo fl. 54v] At almost the same moment, in 1728, a Portuguese report warned that the creek between Sopara and Bolinj had to be dug “as deep as the height of a man”, or it would silt up level with the fields. [n40 A11; GzXIV p. 322] It was not dug, or not enough. Teak rafts had once floated down to the Sopara landing, but by 1882 “for many years boats have been unable to pass beyond Bolinj”. [n40 A11; GzXIV p. 338] Bolinj remained one of the three landings, with Gass and Vagholi, for the molasses, rice and betel of the Sopara gardens, about two miles from the old landing at Dedar. [n40 A11; GzXIV pp. 315–316]

“Bolinj was formerly a large town and probably had rich temples,” the Gazetteer said, and the evidence was in the village’s houses: the Devanagari slab serving as the Lopez doorstep, and carved stones said to lie in the Devhara pond. [n40 A11; GzXIV pp. 341–342] The metalled Virar-Agashi road brought traffic “about a mile to Bolinj”, but “between Bolinj and Sopara the road is a narrow low lane passable for wheels only in fair weather”. [n40 A11; GzXIV p. 316] Bolinj was a plantain-drying village, and is listed, with Bassein, Nela, Sopara, Agashi, Kaman, Manikpur, Virar, Bhatane and Kharbhav, among the trade centres of the sub-division. [n40 A11, A15; GzXIII-1 p. 292, OCR p. 300]

The heritage survey records a Zilla Parishad school of 1879, among the oldest in the belt; the seventeenth-century Bolinj Talav, used by devotees visiting the temple; the Manukarnika Devi Talav with its new temple; and the Karmale Soneri Talav with a small dargah. [n40 A11; CRIT Vol I items 61–64] A Hindu goddess, a Muslim shrine and a Christian parish share one village.

That parish is young. Bolinj was made a centre of Nandakhal parish on 10 May 1988 and separated as the parish of Christ the King on 10 June 2001, by the parish history; a directory dates the church to 2000. [n40 A11] Bolinj sits inside the municipal corporation, next to the Virar-side Chandansar-Gass-Kopri group, and has no 2011 figure and no 2026 source of its own. [n40 A11]

Koprad: the headman’s hamlet

Koprad, also written Kofrad, is a hamlet “half a mile south-west of Bolinj”. [n40 A12; GzXIV p. 342] Its fifteenth-century stone, dated 1464, named Mahim, Thana, Koprad and “Bimbstan”, together with Muslim names; it is one of two inscriptions, with a Deonar stone of 1368, that show a real place called Bimbasthan existed, whatever the truth of the legends of Raja Bimb. [n03; GzXIV Koprad entry; Gazetteer XIII Part II p. 441 n.4] In 1882 the stone lay near the house of the headman, “Bhiku Patil, a Samvedi Brahman”. [n40 A12; GzXIV p. 342]

Koprad is therefore the place where the record shows most clearly how mixed these villages were. Its headman was a Hindu Samvedi Brahman; its plantain-drying families, by the Gazetteer’s count for Agashi, Vatar and Koprad together, were Hindu Samvedis, Hindu Pachkalshis and Christians who were “originally Samvedis and Pachkalshis”. [n40 A12; GzXIII-1 p. 395] And by 1881 a Holy Spirit church was listed “at Koprad”, which is probably today’s Nandakhal church, standing in Kofrad gram panchayat. [n34 §3; n40 A3] The heritage survey lists “Agashe, Papdy, Kofrad” as the sites of Portuguese churches, orphanages and rectories built outside the fort from the mid-sixteenth to the seventeenth century. [n40 A12; CRIT Vol I chronicle p. 7] Koprad should not be confused with Kopri, the Census 2011 village of 10,180 people on the Virar side; Koprad itself has no census figure. [n40 A12]

Vatar: the oldest name

Vatar has the oldest datable mention of any village in this chapter. The Agashi stone of Shaka 1072, AD 1150, from the reign of the Shilahara king Haripaldev, grants “the village Vattéraka in Shurparaka”, and names as witnesses “Risi Mhatara, headman of Vattéraka village, Naguji Mhatara, Ananta Nayaka, and Changdev Mhatara”. The Gazetteer’s footnote identifies the place: “Vattaraka is the modern Vatargaon, two miles north-west of Sopara. Du Perron (1760) notices it as Outar.” [n40 A13; GzXIV p. 385 and n.1] Here, nearly four hundred years before the Portuguese, are the headmen of a Kupari village, with names, Mhatara and Nayaka, that the land still knows.

Da Cunha in 1876 named “Vatari” as the village where the Bhuigaon creek came out to sea. [n40 A13; da Cunha p. 159] In 1727–30 the tombo shows “pacaria Vatar Rajoly” held “in lives” by Manuel Varjão da Fonseca for 700 pardaus a year, the highest rent among the pacarias of the register: the most valuable garden land of the last Portuguese years. [n40 A13; Tombo fl. 45v] In 1882 Vatar was one of the plantain-drying villages, and one of the three villages, with Agashi and Koprad, whose Samvedi, Pachkalshi and Christian families dried the district’s best plantains. [n40 A13; GzXIII-1 pp. 292, 395]

Today Vatar’s hamlets are part of Nandakhal parish, with a community centre of Our Lady of Nazareth under the Nandakhal vicar. In December 2016 Vatar-Barodi had 53 families and 187 people, Jeladi 108 families and 401 people, and Adlay 37 families. [n40 A13] The heritage survey lists the Vatar gram panchayat’s Don Talav, two tanks on the Arnala-Nirmal road, and the Bhongale Talav off the Jaladi-Bhongale road. [n40 A13; CRIT Vol I items 55–56]

Rajodi: the beach village

Rajodi, the north-western coastal village of the belt, is in the Nale-Rajodi gram panchayat of 2008. [n40 A14] It is on the 1882 plantain list. [n40 A14; GzXIII-1 p. 292] The tombo has three entries with the name “Rajoly”, one joined to Vatar, one “Rajoly e Caraly” held by Lourenço Dias in enfiteuse for 90 pardaus, and one “aldeia Rajoly Marelá” held by António de Lisboa for 240 pardaus; which, if any, is this Rajodi, rather than Rajavali to the east, cannot be fixed. [n40 A14; Tombo fl. 45v, 37, 149]

Its church came late: “On 1 May 1999 Rajodi church was inaugurated,” the Nandakhal history says. [n40 A14] At the Nale Talav on the Arnala-Vasai road the gram panchayat found ghats submerged below the water level. [n40 A14; CRIT Vol I item 67] Tourism listings describe Rajodi beach as a secluded family beach between Kalamb and Arnala. [n40 A14; n39 §3] That is all; no person, story or population was found.

Sopara and Nala

Sopara is the ancient port whose story fills the early chapters of this book, and its village in 1882 is the clearest single description of the community mix of the Kupari belt. “Still a rich country town with a crowded weekly market and a population of about 1700,” the Gazetteer said; “the husbandmen of Sopara are Christians, many of them converted Samvedi Brahmans; Musalmans many of them Naitas … and Hindus, chiefly Samvedi Brahmans and Bhandaris”. Gujarat Vanis of the Lad division had come from Cambay about 1760, and the town traded molasses, rice, plantains and betel leaf. [n40 A15; GzXIV pp. 314–316] Da Cunha in 1876 called it “once a famous bandar, but now almost abandoned”. [n40 A15; da Cunha p. 157]

Its Christian past is older than the Portuguese. About 1321 the Dominican friar Jordanus found Nestorian Christians and a church of St Thomas at Sopara, baptised many and gave communion to about ninety; the bodies of the four friars martyred at Thana that year were buried in that church. [n40 A15; GzXIV p. 322] [n25 §1.9–1.10] Under the Portuguese, a report of 1728 describes Sopara as a fortified post with four wooden stockades. [n40 A15; GzXIV p. 322] The tombo lists only four titles in Sopará. [n40 §2] Today’s landmarks, in the heritage survey, are the Burhanpur Chowk market, named after a nineteenth-century Mr Burhanpur Harris, the Rehmatullah Alleya dargah of 1555 (rebuilt), the Chakreshwar Talav, the Sopara Zilla Parishad school, the talavs of the Sopara-Gass road, and the dargah of Hazrat Diwan Shah. [n40 A15; CRIT Vol I items 8–11] A local helper of the 1882 survey, “Mr Ramdas Kasidas Modi, a native of Sopara”, is one of the few Sopara men of the period whose name survives. [n42; GzXIV p. 314 fn.]

Nala, a mile north of Sopara, was thought by earlier research to have no 1882 trace. In fact it has several. “Nala” is on the Gazetteer’s plantain-drying list; it is one of four Bassein villages, with Agashi, Sopara and Navghar, where hundis, bills of exchange, could be cashed up to £1,000; and it is probably the “Nela” among Bassein’s ten trade centres. [n40 A15; GzXIII-1 p. 292; OCR pp. 300, 304] An inscribed stone “brought from Nila about a mile north of Sopara” names the Yadava king Ramchandradev, of AD 1280–1298 by the Gazetteer’s reading, and the name Shurparak twice; it was kept in the rest-house at the Manikpur railway station. [n40 A15, B6; GzXIV p. 387] In 1727–30 the tombo shows “pacaria Nala Magir” held by Miguel Francisco Mira de Vasconcelos, with an orchard, Matarvary, held by Gonçalo Carneiro. [n40 A15; Tombo fl. 43, 40] How Nala and Sopara together became “Nallasopara”, the name of the railway station and now of the city, is told in an earlier chapter; the exact year remains unresolved. [n40 A15; n31] Today both lie inside the municipal corporation, with no separate village figure.

The Vadval villages of the south

South of Nirmal and Gass, toward the old fort, the parishes change character. These are the Vadval Christian villages, the people the 1882 Gazetteer called “Christian cartmen chiefly of Chulna, Mánikpur, Gokhivra, Sándor, and Barhánpur”, and their neighbours. [n40 B; GzXIII-1 p. 97] They were founded by all four of the great orders, the Jesuits, Franciscans, Dominicans and Augustinians, between 1566 and 1606, round the walls of Bassein. [n40 Part B reading note] Evidence that any given village is Vadval is thin and often indirect, because no gazetteer or parish page lists villages by community; each entry says what the evidence is. [n40 Part B reading note] The origin traditions of the Vadvals, from Paithan and Raja Bimb to Gujarat and the “five pots” of the name Pachkalshi, belong to other chapters.

The Gazetteer’s portrait of the Vadval cartmen is one of the most vivid passages it has on any Bassein people. “The Vadvals are the best axemen in the district; their carts are larger and their cattle stronger” than elsewhere. They bought fallen timber from the government in Vada and cut green wood in Jawhar, and travelled at night in gangs of five to thirty carts, earning at least twelve annas a day for a cart, a man, a boy and the cattle. [n40 B7; GzXIII-1 pp. 97–98] Elsewhere the same volume ranks the Vadvals “among the most skilful cultivators in the district”. [n34 §5; GzXIII-1 p. 205]

Sandor: the mother church of the south

Sandor is the clearest Christian Vadval village. It is on the Gazetteer’s list of cart villages, and Wikipedia says that “the Sandoris are predominantly of the Vadval sub-ethnic group and speak the Vadvali dialect”, though that page is tagged for more citations. [n40 B1; GzXIII-1 p. 97; Wikipedia, Sandor] Its name has no recorded meaning. In the tombo it is “pacaria Sandor”, held in enfiteuse by Jorge da Silva Coelho for 435 pardaus; the tombo’s “Chandory/Sandory” and “Chandary/Sandary” are different places and must not be merged with it. [n40 B1; Tombo fl. 29v]

The church of St Thomas the Apostle is called “the Mother Church of all the churches of South Bassein”, and the heritage survey calls it “the oldest church building that presently exists outside the Vasai Fort”. [n40 B1; EI Sandor; CRIT Vol III asset 103] Its dates are a tangle. The heritage survey gives 1571: “In 1571 the Mohamedans besieged Bassein and the chapel was destroyed. A church was built on the spot of the chapel with the first Mass being celebrated on the feast of St Thomas. This Jesuit church and parish came under the care of the Secular Clergy when the Jesuits had to quit Bassein.” The parish page says “built by the Jesuit fathers in 1556”, under a heading dated 1566, and says the church escaped destruction by the Marathas in 1739, was enlarged in 1889, and has three altars “from one of the churches in Bassein Fort”. Wikipedia, from the parish’s tradition, says that after conversions in 1564 a noblewoman, “Donna Irina”, built a chapel of Our Lady of Help, where the first Mass was said on 11 November 1566; that it was renamed for St Thomas when his picture came from the fort; that Gujarat forces destroyed it in 1571 and it was rebuilt in 1573; that Arabs from Gujarat damaged it in 1679 and “Kakaji” in 1690; and that Fr Pedro de Almeida SJ, “Pai dos Cristãos”, had charge of Sandor and Papdi in 1574–76. [n40 B1] The years 1556, 1564, 1565, 1566, 1571 and 1573 cannot all be right; the simplest reconciliation is a chapel of 1566 that became the church of St Thomas between 1566 and 1573, but that is an inference. [n40 B1 inferences] A Sandor tradition says that the boy Gonsalo Garcia was present at the 1566 foundation; it is a tradition only. [n40 B1]

In 1882 the church, three miles north of Bassein, had a congregation of 1,725; its side walls were sixteenth-century, its chapel of 1838 and its frontispiece of 1858. [n40 B1; GzXIV p. 37] From Sandor, by the parish’s account, were carved Remedy, Papdi, Pali, Merces and Porim (Manickpur), between 1574 and 1606, and later Giriz (1917–18) and Chulne (1963). [n40 B1] The heritage survey lists inside Sandor a Dev Talav and a Hanuman Mandir, both of the Maratha eighteenth century, a Relba Talav, and a nineteenth-century gram panchayat office in an old Zilla Parishad school. [n40 B1; CRIT Vol I items 99–103] Hindu shrines inside a Christian village, again. The Cardinal Gracias Memorial Hospital at Bangli, Sandor, with 147 beds, is a Catholic hospital. [n38 §6] Sandor had 8,336 people and 78 per cent literacy in 2001, by Wikipedia’s figure; it has no separate 2011 figure. Its feast falls on 9 to 15 December. [n40 B1]

Papdi: from Brahman quarter to cathedral

Papdi is the village whose character changed most visibly in the record. In 1876 da Cunha described “Papari, a village about a cannon-shot from Bajipura [Bassein], chiefly inhabited by Brahmans of Chitpawan, Karada, and Deshastha divisions, as well as Palshas, Sonars, and other inferior castes of the Hindus”, yielding Rs 1,80,630 in revenue. [n40 B2; da Cunha p. 157] That was the Maratha-era Papdi of the Peshwa’s officers and priests. [n40 B2 inferences] Yet in 1882 the Gazetteer recorded the Christian church of Our Lady of Grace there, two miles north-east of Bassein, with a congregation of 1,294, rebuilt in 1865 for Rs 18,000, 102 feet long, with a parish school and a violin master. [n40 B2; GzXIV p. 37]

The parish’s own account, repeated in the heritage survey, says it was “the second parish to be founded in South Bassein outside the Fort, the first being St Thomas, Sandor”; it took in people from Davly (Remedy), part of Sandor and “Maferegua”, who “were poor farmers and mainly survived on manual labour”, about 1,388 parishioners at first. The Jesuits served it from 1574 until the Maratha conquest; “unlike other churches during the invasion, this church was not destroyed”; and from 1839 it had a vicar of its own. [n40 B2; EI Papdy; CRIT sheet 40] (The parish page gives “1789” for the invasion, an error for 1739.) [n40 B2]

The heritage survey adds a striking sentence: “Although Papdy was known as a famous shipbuilding centre, the present fabric of Papdy shows palimpsests from the 18th century when it was given as a jagir to the community which transported goods, supplies and food to the Maratha army.” It lists the Papdy Talav, a Ram Mandir rebuilt in the early twentieth century whose original marked Maratha dominance, the Bondali and Taam talavs, the Govardhan Vidyalaya, the Mohanlal Vakil bungalow, the Shah Wada of the 1850s and the Thomas Baptista School. [n40 B2; CRIT Vol II assets 36–42] The survey does not name the carrier community; that it was the cart-owning Vadvals of the Gazetteer is an inference, and no source names Vadvals at Papdi at all. [n40 B2 gaps]

On 22 May 1998 the Diocese of Vasai was created, and on 15 August 1998 Bishop Thomas Dabre was installed at Our Lady of Grace, which became its cathedral. [n40 B2; GCatholic] The diocese opened the Jubilee of 2025 there on 29 December 2024. [n38 §5] In 2025 the cathedral’s community-led restoration won a UNESCO Asia-Pacific Award of Merit. [n40 B2; Outlook Traveller] Bishop Ivan Pereira, listed by Wikipedia as a bishop in Jammu and Kashmir, is named there as a son of Papdy; that listing has not been individually verified. [n27]

Remedy: the shrine village

Remedy takes its name from its church, Nossa Senhora dos Remédios, Our Lady of Remedies. The older village name is Davly in the Papdi parish page and “Dhauli” in the Gazetteer, where it is a survey village of the Bassein municipality of 1864. [n40 B3; GzXIV pp. 36–37; EI Papdy] The church was founded by the Dominicans; the heritage survey says in 1577, “the second to be separated from the mother parish of St Thomas, Sandor”, while a local list gives 1557 and the Jesuit historian Hull gives “Douli (N.S. dos Remedios) built 1583, rebuilt 1839”. [n40 B3; CRIT Vol II asset 22] [n38 §5; Hull I p. 5] Da Cunha found it in Dominican charge and “still in a fair state of preservation”, with 3,000 Christians in 1876, the largest congregation in his table. [n40 B3; da Cunha pp. 161, 163] The Gazetteer of 1882 gives a church two miles north of Bassein, 108 by 33 by 23 feet, built in 1821 for Rs 18,600, of which Rs 18,000 came by subscription and Rs 600 from government, with a congregation of 3,238. [n40 B3; GzXIV p. 37] The old door of a Vasai fort church is said to be kept here. [n40 B3]

Remedy’s Holy Market precinct, on the Remedy-Par Naka road, draws vendors from Sandor, Kaular Budruk and other villages, who pay Rs 5 a day; Patil Wada of the 1850s is, in the heritage survey’s words, a typical house of the landowning farming community; and Matkari Wada, of 1868, was the home of the advocate Raja Ram Keshab Matkari (1920–63). [n40 B3; CRIT Vol I items 21–25] The creation of the Diocese of Vasai was announced at the Shrine of Our Lady of Remedy on 28 June 1998; the shrine has a minor seminary, and Bishop Thomas D’Souza was its rector from 2013 to 2019. [n40 B3; n38 §5] Archbishop Felix Machado, Bishop of Vasai from 2009 to 2024, was born at Remedy on 6 June 1948. [n40 B3; GCatholic] There is no direct evidence that Remedy is a Vadval village; it is a parish of the Christian south, and that is all the sources allow. [n40 B3]

Merces: the village named after a church

Merces is a village that took its name from its church, Nossa Senhora das Mercês, Our Lady of Mercy; the village was earlier Kaular, and its church stands in Kaular Khurd gram panchayat. [n40 B4; EI Merces; CRIT Vol I items 22–23] Kaular appears in the Gazetteer among Bassein’s village fairs of under a thousand people. [n40 B4; GzXIII-1 p. 385]

The parish page places it “between the villages of Sandor and Remedy and three kilometres northwest of the fort”, among “predominately fisher folk (kolis)” who had “preferred to attend religious services at Remedy”. Jesuit reports of 1669 and the researches of Fr Conti point to a foundation in 1606; the Augustinians served it until 1739, when the church was destroyed and the priest fled; the parish then came under the vicar of Remedy; the church was rebuilt in 1856; and its first secular vicar, Fr Sebastião José Florêncio de Faria, came on 17 January 1862. [n40 B4; EI Merces] The heritage survey, citing Fr Philip Vaz’s Catholic Directory, agrees on the Augustinians from 1606 and the rebuilding of 1856. [n40 B4; CRIT Vol III asset 92] Da Cunha disagrees: he says the church was “built by Dom Fr. Aleixo de Menezes, the Archbishop who fought the interesting sect of the Syro-Chaldeans”, and puts it under the Franciscans. [n40 B4; da Cunha p. 161] The conflict is unresolved.

In 1882 the church, about two miles north of the mamlatdar’s office, had a congregation of 974, had been built by private subscription, and its vicar was paid Rs 17 a month by the Portuguese government, nearly a century and a half after the Portuguese had lost Bassein. [n40 B4; GzXIV p. 37] The heritage survey lists the church and the Motha Talav on Augustine Kulas Road. [n40 B4; CRIT items 91–92] Merces’ place among the Vadval villages is indirect; the parish page itself speaks of Kolis. [n40 B4]

Pali and Mulgaon

Pali, historically “Palle”, lies about three miles east of Bassein on the Papdy-Naigaon Station road, in Vadavali gram panchayat. [n40 B5; CRIT Vol III asset 107] Its church of the Mother of God, A Madre de Deus, was, in the heritage survey’s words, “built in 1585, which is recorded in the Franciscan Chronicler”; the Franciscans left in 1739, and it “was however rebuilt between 1840-44 at a new site 100 feet west of the original site”. [n40 B5] A local list gives 1595. [n40 B5] Da Cunha counted 800 Christians in 1876 and said the church “has long been in a dilapidated condition”; the Gazetteer of 1882 gives a congregation of 900, a church built in 1840 for Rs 15,000, and a parish school of thirty pupils. [n40 B5; da Cunha pp. 161–162; GzXIV p. 37] The tombo shows “aldeia Palle” held by D. Maria Correia de Macedo. [n40 B5; Tombo fl. 172]

Pali is also a village of Muslim monuments. The heritage survey records the Pali Dargah of the sixteenth century, with a step well, dome, minarets and stone screens, and the Pali Masjid of the eighteenth century, which mixes Islamic, Portuguese and wada features, and notes that “before the Portuguese domination” there was “significant Muslim population due to the traders and merchants who had settled here”. [n40 B5; CRIT Vol I items 107–109] The “Pali fiesta” is named among Kupari festivals in a community blog, which suggests that the Kuparis of the north came south for it. [n40 B5; n07 §4] (The Census 2011 village “Pali”, 792 people, sits in the north-east rural run and may be a different place.) [n40 B5]

Mulgaon, a survey village of the 1864 Bassein municipality, became a parish in 1945 when it was separated from Palle under the patronage of St Mary Magdalene, with Fr Ayres Fernandes as first parish priest. Its church was consecrated in 1966, and it celebrated its golden jubilee on 12 November 1995. Its parish history keeps one personal story: Fr Clement de Sa died on 13 November 1963 while giving Communion. [n40 B5; EI Mulgaon] The tombo’s “Boroly Mulgão”, held by Fernão de Brito de Melo, may be this village, but the identification is uncertain. [n40 B10]

Manickpur: Porim of the five pacarias

Manickpur was not always called Manickpur. “The Church of St Michael of Porim (or Purim) was named after the village in which it was located. The name of this village was changed to Manickpur in the beginning of the 20th century,” the parish page says. [n40 B6; EI Manikpur] The Gazetteer of 1882, however, already uses “Manikpur” for the Bassein Road railway station and the “Manikpur creek”, and da Cunha in 1876 writes “Purim or Manikpura”, so the new name is older than 1900. [n40 B6; GzXIV pp. 314, 323; da Cunha pp. 157, 161]

The parish story is one of the fullest in the south. “Till 1606 the Catholics of Porim were parishioners of St Thomas, Sandor. It was only at the insistence of the Jesuit Provincial that the Archbishop of Goa, Dom Aleixo de Menezes, decided to make Porim an independent parish.” When the first mud-and-thatch church proved too small, the Jesuits at the fort built a new one “on the spacious grounds in the centre of the five pacarias of Wananapor, Devamany, Porim, Navgaon and Ullmemany”, with jurisdiction over Gocavarem (Gokhivare), Calmona, Balliam, Sattolli, Achola and Rajaoli. It “was the first to be demolished by the conquering Maratha army in 1739”. A new church was built on the same site, “presumably in 1783”, the date on an old cross found by Fr Conti. The Jesuits left, the secular clergy served the parish, and in March 1949 it was returned to the Jesuits. [n40 B6; EI Manikpur] The heritage survey says a new church was “immediately built on the foundations of the old”; the Gazetteer records a church of St Michael the Archangel, four miles north-east of Bassein, congregation 800, rebuilt in 1851 for Rs 15,000; da Cunha counted 900 Christians and noted that “the old one, on the same spot, belonged to the Jesuits”. The dates for the second church conflict. [n40 B6] A tradition that Dona Ines Francisca gave land for a chapel to the Jesuits in 1572 comes from a search summary of a placeholder website and should not be relied on. [n40 B6]

Manikpur is one of the Gazetteer’s Vadval cart villages, and one of Bassein’s trade centres in 1882. [n40 B6; GzXIII-1 p. 97] Wikipedia lists its groups as Kunbi, Kumbhar, Vadval and Koli. [n40 B6] At the railway station a double-storeyed rest-house held two inscribed stones, one from Nala. [n40 B6; GzXIV p. 387] The heritage survey lists the church precinct and three talavs on the Chulne-Manikpur road: Father Benedict, Nazareth and Umeleman. [n40 B6; CRIT Vol II items 17–20] Navghar-Manikpur was a municipal council until the merger of 2009. [n40 B6]

Chulne: the cart village that became a bishop’s birthplace

Chulne is the first name on the Gazetteer’s list of Christian Vadval cartmen, “Chulna”. [n40 B7; GzXIII-1 p. 97] In 1727–30 the tombo shows “pacaria Cholna Roba” held by the Jesuit Procurador Geral for 130 pardaus: a Jesuit village, where the likely sponsors of converts were priests. [n40 B7; Tombo fl. 49] [n39 §1 inferences] In 1882 the survey party passed through “Chulna” on the way back from Sopara to Manikpur, two miles west of the station. [n40 B7; GzXIV p. 341]

The parish page describes “the suburban village of Chulne whose inhabitants are practically all Catholics”, about three kilometres from Vasai Road station. Sunday Mass began there on 9 September 1951 under Fr Philip Tavares; the foundation stone was laid on 22 April 1961; the parish of Our Lady of Fatima was established on 1 June 1963; and the church was blessed on 17 May 1964. [n40 B7; EI Chulne] The heritage survey lists the Chulne, Khargoda and Nazareth talavs. [n40 B7; CRIT Vol I items 15–17]

Chulne has given the Church two bishops. Thomas Francis D’Souza, born at Chulne on 23 March 1970, ordained in 1998, became Bishop of Vasai on 9 November 2024 and was consecrated on 15 December 2024. [n40 B7; GCatholic] Wikipedia’s list of notable people of the city also names Archbishop Elias Gonsalves of Nagpur as a son of Chulne; that entry has not been individually verified. [n27]

Gokhivare: the vow against cholera

Gokhivare, “Gokhivra” on the Gazetteer’s list of Vadval cartmen and “Gocavarem” in the old jurisdiction of St Michael’s, lies east of Vasai Road. [n40 B8; GzXIII-1 p. 97; EI Manikpur] A report of 1728 records a bridge built across the creek at “Gokirva” by General Louis de Mello Pereira, and by 1882 the Baroda railway had a five-mile feeder “from Gokirva to Papdi”. [n40 B8; GzXIV p. 322; GzXIII-1 p. 319]

Its parish was born of a promise. “This parish was founded as a result of a promise made to St Roque by the people of Gokhivare. In 1914 the village was severely affected by an epidemic of cholera.” Two hundred villagers built a chapel, forty feet by eighteen, to which Archbishop Lima added a sanctuary; the Dean of South Bassein blessed it on 30 June 1930; and in 1943 it became an independent parish, with Fr Nubiola SJ as its first priest. The Jesuits have served it ever since, except in 1968–71, and the church was extended in 1990. [n40 B8; EI Gokhivare] The heritage survey lists the Gokhivare Talav near an Adivasi hamlet. [n40 B8; CRIT item 122] St Roque, invoked against plague, was the saint a village struck by cholera would choose; the vow, the chapel and the parish are a small history of how Vadval Christians met the epidemics of the early twentieth century.

Giriz: the boundary village

Giriz lies between Vasai and Nirmal, and is the village where the Kupari and Vadval belts meet. The evidence points to the Vadval side. [n40 B9] It was part of Sandor parish “ever since the latter was established”, according to the heritage survey, and the parish page says it had belonged to “the vast parish of St Thomas, Sandor and Our Lady of Mercy, Merces”; a community photograph caption describes a “Vadval East Indian house in Giriz”; and none of the Kupari lists includes it. [n40 B9]

In 1727–30 Giriz was the first title in the Baçaim caçabé: “pacaria Gris”, leased in three lives by D. Margarida Teles de Meneses for 166 pardaus 4 larins. Inside it lay a plot, “terra Oratem”, held by João Cabral, and an orchard of wild palms, “Vitoba”, held by “Jasaman Naique”, the native landholder already noticed. [n40 B9; Tombo fl. 23, 52v, 53]

The church came by the villagers’ own effort. The inhabitants petitioned the Archbishop of Damaun; the foundation stone was laid on 14 February 1917; and the church was finished a year later, built, the parish says, “without foreign aid” and by “donations from the parishioners”. Giriz became an independent parish under Fr Augusto D’Souza, its first parish priest. Its golden jubilee in 1968, under Fr Francis Gratian Monteiro, brought the Marshal Pereira Community Hall; a porch was added in 1968, an extension in 1977 under Fr Dominic Pereira with German and diocesan aid, and new spires in May 1981. Its feast, of St Francis Xavier, is on 3 December. [n40 B9; CRIT Vol III asset 90; EI Giriz; Wikipedia] The heritage survey lists the sixteenth-century Girij Talav, with ghats, and the twentieth-century gram panchayat office on the Vasai-Nirmal road. [n40 B9; CRIT items 88–90] Bishop Malcolm Sequeira of Amravati, born in 1961, is named as a son of Giriz. [n38 §6; n27]

Naigaon, Umela, Juchandra, Achole and Saloli

To the south-east, between the fort and the Naigaon creek, lie villages whose community make-up no source states directly. They sit inside the Sandor and Manickpur parish areas, and some were Vadval or Agri by place; but no attribution should be made without fieldwork. [n40 B10]

Saloli, with its hamlet Kharbhat, is served by the parish of Sandor; its sixteenth-century tank is used for Ganesh immersions and fish auctions, and a weak online source says Kharbhat’s people have followed Christianity since the St Thomas church was built nearby in 1566. [n40 B10; CRIT items 97–98]

Naigaon is where the Mother of God church of Pali stands, in Naigaon West; the tombo’s “Nagão”, in a group with “Sedacer, Choly, Calamba … e Varchory” held by Maria da Silva Moura Rolim, may be Naigaon, with Holi and Kalamb, but that is unverified. [n40 B10; Tombo fl. 56]

Umela was “pacaria Umella” in 1727–30, held by Francisco Pereira de Sousa for 157 pardaus. In 1882 it was a Bassein village of Urap Agris, the converts who returned to Hinduism in 1820 and 1828. A chapel of the Immaculate Conception was built there, under Manickpur, in 1974. And the 1935 Marathi edition of the Anjur bakhar names a Somvanshi Kshatriya Chaudhari family of Umela, Kuker alias Kuk Chaudhari, and Lakham Chaudhari, among the local helpers of the Marathas in the 1739 campaign. [n40 B10; Tombo fl. 26v; GzXIII-1 p. 117 n.1; n41 §6, §10] In one village, then, the record shows a Portuguese lease-holder, Christian converts who went back to Hinduism, Hindu Vadval families who sided with the Marathas, and a twentieth-century Catholic chapel.

Juchandra and Achole were Urap Agri villages in 1882. Achole had a Portuguese fort, “a mile and a half north of Manikpur”, and lay within St Michael’s old jurisdiction as “Achola”; Juchandra has a Zilla Parishad school of 1872 and the Gode and Gandhi talavs. [n40 B10; GzXIV pp. 323, 341; CRIT items 111–114]

Virar and the Hindu Pachkalshi belt

The Hindu Vadvals and Pachkalshis do not have a parish register, and so in the sources they appear as surnames and wards rather than as villages with founding dates. The Gazetteer gives their surnames as “Raut, Vartak, and Chodhri”. [n40 B11; GzXIII-1 p. 130] They are recorded inside the Kupari belt itself, as the fifteen Pachkalshi plantain-drying families of Agashi, Vatar and Koprad in 1882. [n40 B11; GzXIII-1 p. 395] And their strongest presence in the sources is at Virar.

Virar in 1882 was “a rich village well-wooded and with many patches of sugarcane”, about seven miles north of Bassein and thirty-eight from Bombay, with a railway station whose passenger traffic rose from 48,294 in 1878 to 83,176 in 1880, a metalled road four miles west to Agashi, and a Saturday market “thronged by Kolis and Varlis”. Fifty yards south of the station stood a ruined Portuguese church and well, and a mile south “a notable Portuguese tower or fortified mansion”. On the Jivdhan hill, a mile east, were ruined fortifications and the shrine of a goddess, with a legend of a Mhar herdsman. [n40 B11; GzXIV pp. 116, 382] In 1727–30 “aldeia Virar Naringuy” was held by D. Pedro de Meneses for 2,000 pardaus, the highest rent of any aldeia in the register. [n40 B11; Tombo fl. 121] The heritage survey lists Virar’s tanks and wards, among them Naringi, Dongarpada, Vartakwada, Totale, Manvelpada and Ranele. [n40 B11; CRIT Vol I] “Vartakwada”, the Vartak ward, carries a Pachkalshi surname; that it was a Pachkalshi quarter is an inference no source confirms. [n40 B11 inferences]

The Pachkalshi role in 1739 is documented in three government texts. Enthoven wrote that they “rendered material help to the Marathas during the siege of Bassein and the acquisition of adjoining country in 1739-40”, and that “to the siege of Bassein and other events of the great Maratha-Portuguese struggle are traced back the patelships of many families of the Vadval division”. [n41 §6; Enthoven III p. 161] The Bombay City Gazetteer names two Panchkalshis, Bugaji and Gangaji Naik, granted the village of Anjur in inam with the title of Naik Sarpatel; the 1882 Thana Gazetteer gives the year as “1743”, almost certainly a slip for 1739. [n41 §6] Anjur is in Bhiwandi, not Vasai. [n40 B12] The 1935 bakhar edition lists Raut helpers from the Vasai campaign, Dhaga, Gan, Laxman and Mayaji Raut, beside the Chaudharis of Umela. [n40 B11; n41 §10] The Gazetteer’s Nirmal and Agashi temples, it should be said, are the work of the Maratha officer Shankarji Keshav, not Pachkalshi foundations. [n40 B11]

The best-known Hindu Pachkalshi name of modern Vasai is Vartak. Hari Govindrao “Bhausaheb” Vartak (1914–1998) was Congress MLA for Bassein from 1962 for three terms, a state cabinet minister and a Padma Shri; his family is linked to Vartak College and the Vidyavardhini institutions. [n26 §2; n16] Tarabai Vartak (1926–2008), born at Bordi and married into the family in 1944, was India’s first woman Zilla Parishad president (Thane, 1972) and Vasai’s MLA in 1980–85. [n26 §2] Wikipedia’s Panchkalshi page also names Hitendra Thakur of Virar among members of the community. [n40 B11] Virar is today the northern third of the city, and no village-level source describes its old wards. [n40 B11]

Arnala, at the mouth of the Vaitarna, is neither a Kupari nor a Vadval village; it is a Koli fishing village, with 15,331 people in 2011, plus 2,566 on the fort island, and its parish of St Peter grew from the hut chapel Fr Ismail Da Costa of Agashi built in 1919, blessed as a church in 1931 with a gift of Rs 3,000 from a Hindu community association; “Kolis consider Fr Da Costa as the true Apostle of Arnala”. [n40 C1; EI Arnala] It belongs to the story of these villages because its island was leased with Agashi in 1727–30, and because its parishioners first worshipped at Agashi.

That is the belt as the sources show it. Most of it is drawn from four kinds of record: a colonial gazetteer, a Portuguese rent roll, a heritage survey made in a hurry in 2008, and parish histories written by the parishes. In none of them do the farmers of these villages speak for themselves.

III. Voices

The names that reached the record

If the people of these villages are gathered from all the documents this book has used, in the order in which they appear, the list is short, and it says a great deal about who wrote history on this coast and who did not.

The first are the headmen of Vatar in AD 1150, Risi Mhatara, Naguji Mhatara, Ananta Nayaka and Changdev Mhatara, witnesses to a king’s grant of their own village. [n40 A13; GzXIV p. 385 n.1] They appear because a king’s scribe needed witnesses. They were not Christians; there were no Christians here then but the Nestorians of Sopara. But they are the earliest named people of a village on the Kupari list, and their surname is still heard in these villages.

Next, in 1540, come the five people burnt in the orphanage at Agashi, “a batch of Christian martyrs … consisting entirely of native converts”. [n40 A2; da Cunha pp. 159–160] They are not named. The Portuguese chroniclers who recorded the death of native converts did not record what they were called.

Then comes the saint. St Gonsalo Garcia, “natural de Agaçaim” in the Lisbon record, born in 1556 or 1557 to a Portuguese soldier and a local “Canarim” mother, schooled by the Jesuits in the fort, catechist and interpreter in Japan, a Franciscan lay brother, crucified at Nagasaki on 5 February 1597 and canonised on 8 June 1862, is the first person from this coast whose life was written down in full. [n25 §1.1] He is the patron of the Diocese of Vasai, and his feast is kept at the ruined church of the Holy Name in the fort, “Killa cha Sann”, at Erangal, and at the church that bears his name in Gass. [n25 §1.1; n38 §5] Was he a Kupari? The honest answer is that the question cannot be asked of him. The word did not exist; his father was Portuguese; and the claims of particular villages to his birth, Gass among them, are tradition only. [n25 §1.1] The record ties him to Agashi. The whole Christian coast claims him, and with good reason, but no village can prove he was its own.

In 1727, in the tombo, comes “Jasaman Naique”, holder of an orchard of wild palms in Giriz, the one Indian name among the fidalgos. [n40 B9] In 1760 come the “Canarin” priests whom Anquetil du Perron saw being entertained by the vicar of Agashi after the feast; they are not named either. [n40 A2; da Cunha p. 164] After 1739 the native clergy under a Vicar General at Kurla “kept the bulk of the people from forsaking Christianity”, in the Gazetteer’s words; the research found none of their names. [n25 §1.14; n34 §6]

In 1882 the Gazetteer gives the first ordinary villagers by name: Joseph Manuel Lopez of Bolinj, with his doorstep; Bhiku Patil, the Samvedi Brahman headman of Koprad; Degu Pobria Mhatara of Gass, whose family washed clothes on a stone carved two thousand years before; Bab Naik, a Samvedi Brahman of Gass; and Ramdas Kasidas Modi, “a native of Sopara”, who helped the survey. [n25 §2; n11 Q4; n40 A6, A11, A12] Of these five, only one, Lopez, is certainly Christian. All of them appear because of the stones in front of their houses, not because anyone asked what they knew.

The same Gazetteer describes the people of these villages at length, their dress, their weddings, their funerals, their food, their violin-playing schoolmasters, and their “proposal [that] comes from the boy’s side” in Bassein. [n34 §5; GzXIII-1 p. 211 n.1] But it describes them from outside. Its sources were district officers and earlier reports, some of which, it admits, spoke of Christians “in terms of contempt”. [n34 §6; GzXIII-1 p. 203]

The twentieth century adds the clergy. Mgr Raymond Mendez, who began catechism at Bhuigaon; Fr Acuico Dourado, its first parish priest; Fr Thomas, first parish priest of Gass; Fr Augusto D’Souza at Giriz; Fr Clement de Sa, who died at the altar rail in Mulgaon in 1963; Fr Ismail Da Costa, “the true Apostle of Arnala”; Mgr Francesco de Silva, who cleared the Nandakhal forest and planted coconuts. [n40 A4, A6, B5, B9, C1, A3] The parish histories do not say where most of them came from, and none of them is identified as a Kupari.

The first priest the record calls a son of a Kupari village is Fr Frank Lobo of Nandakhal, ordained on 5 January 1943, “the first local priest after 370 years”, in the parish’s own words. [n40 A3; n25 §1.12] That he was a Kupari is a reasonable inference from his village; no source says so in words.

After him come the bishops. Here the chapter must be most careful, because the community naturally claims them, and the sources do not quite say what the community says.

Thomas Dabre, born at Bhuigaon on 23 October 1945, ordained in 1971, auxiliary bishop of Bombay in charge of Vasai from 1990, first Bishop of Vasai from 1998 and Bishop of Poona from 2009 to 2023. [n40 A4; n38 §6; GCatholic; Catholic-Hierarchy] His birthplace is documented, and Bhuigaon is on every Kupari list. But no source found says that he is a Kupari; that is an inference from his birthplace and his Marathi surname, and his Wikipedia biography could not be checked. [n34 §8; n38 §6 gaps] Kupari identity: probable, unconfirmed.

Simon Almeida, born at Gass on 4 November 1960, schooled at St Gonsalo Garcia Primary School in Gass and Holy Cross High School in Nirmal, who entered the Prakash Bhavan minor seminary in Pune in 1978, was ordained priest for Poona on 1 April 1990, appointed Bishop of Poona by Pope Leo XIV on 1 November 2025, and ordained and installed on 17 January 2026, with the motto “To make all things new in Christ”. [n42 §6; Wikipedia, Simon Almeida; Diocese of Poona] His brother, Fr Peter Almeida, was parish priest of Gass in August 2023. [n42 §5] Two sources now give Gass as his birthplace, Wikipedia spelling it “Ghas” and the Poona diocese “Gass, Bhatori, Vasai”; an earlier search found only one local source and called it unconfirmed. [n42 §6; n38 §6] Gass is a Kupari village; but again, no source found states his community. Kupari identity: probable, unconfirmed. His story is told more fully in Chapter 26.

Sr Nandita Pereira of Nandakhal, listed by Wikipedia as Superior General of the Missionary Sisters of the Queen of the Apostles, in Vienna. [n27] The entry is from a list of notable people of the city, not individually verified, and her community is not stated. Kupari identity: unconfirmed.

Fr Francis D’Britto, the most widely known person from these villages in the last half-century, was from Jeladi, a hamlet of Nandakhal parish. [n40 A3] A Franciscan priest, he edited the diocesan Marathi monthly Suvarta from 1983 to 2007; spent some thirteen to fifteen years on the Subodh Bible, a Marathi translation with introductions and about two hundred illustrations, which won the state translation award in 2013 and the Sahitya Akademi award in 2014, reported as the first Bible translation to win a national award; received the Dnyanoba-Tukaram Puraskar in 2015; and on 22 September 2019 was elected president of the 93rd Akhil Bharatiya Marathi Sahitya Sammelan, held at Osmanabad in January 2020, described as the first Christian to hold that office. [n38 §6; n27; n40 A3] He founded and led the Harit Vasai Sanrakshan Samiti, the movement to protect Vasai’s villages, farms, forests and fishing grounds from builder-driven urbanisation, and called the draft regional plan “builder-driven”. [n38 §6; n11 Q3; n07 §7] He died on 25 July 2024, aged 81 or 82 (sources give his birth year as 1942 or 1943), and about 3,000 people came to his funeral at Nandakhal. [n38 §6; n26] The sources describe him as a son of Vasai, of Jeladi and of Nandakhal; none of those read calls him a Kupari. Kupari identity: probable from his village, unconfirmed.

From the Vadval and southern parishes come Archbishop Felix Machado of Remedy (born 1948; under-secretary of the Pontifical Council for Interreligious Dialogue 1999–2008, Bishop of Nashik, Bishop of Vasai 2009–2024); Bishop Thomas D’Souza of Chulne (born 1970; Bishop of Vasai from 2024); and, from Wikipedia’s unverified list, Archbishop Elias Gonsalves of Chulne (Nagpur), Bishop Malcolm Sequeira of Giriz (Amravati) and Bishop Ivan Pereira of Papdy (Jammu and Kashmir). [n38 §6; n40 B3, B7; n27] None of these sources names their community either; the villages are Vadval or southern parishes, and that is all that can be said. A coast that in 1920 lamented “the paucity of vocations to the priesthood among the community” has, a century later, given the Church a diocese’s worth of bishops. [n34 §7; Mendiratta p. 236]

Beyond the Church, the Hindu Pachkalshi villages produced Vasai’s first long political dynasty in the Vartaks, and Virar the Thakur family that held the Vasai seat for most of the years from 1990 to 2024. [n26 §1–2] Their stories belong to the chapters on the Republic.

And at the very end of the list, in the last twenty years, come the first people from these villages who wrote about the Kuparis as Kuparis. Sachin Mendes, on a blog in 2007, estimated the community at “about 40,000 to 45,000” and listed its villages. [n34 §8; vasaikupari blog] Rajan D’Mello, in 2009, wrote down its life-cycle words, Baltim for baptism, Komsar for confession, Cominyao for first communion, Hamdao, Lagin and Varad for the stages of a wedding, Saudio for the celebration after it, Yentar for a funeral, Saatvoh for the seventh-day Mass, Pirsao for the candlelit Lenten procession, Paya bharni for the laying of a house’s foundation and Bejimat for its blessing, and its foods: vade, dhapti, hingoli, fogey, dodal, fuddi, sanna, indel. [n07 §4; D’Mello blog] Neither post cites sources, and both repeat errors, such as dating the community to the fifteenth century. [n34 §9] But they are the first written Kupari voices this research could find. In 2024 three native speakers named Dabre, Dabre and Pereira published “Suman”, a Kadodi-Marathi-English dictionary of 949 entries and 942 sentence triples, the first scholarly resource for the Kupari dialect. [n34 §4; ACL Anthology 2024] A 2026 article on the red cap and the red lugda said both had “largely disappeared from everyday life”. [n34 §8; All About Vasai Virar 2026]

The silence

Set side by side, those lists show the shape of the silence.

For four centuries the people of these villages were described by others: by Jesuits who wrote candidly that “those who come to conversion are commonly the poorest and most needy” and that “we set them the snares of human things”; [n34 §3; Documenta Indica IV pp. 100–101] by a Goan Brahmin scholar who wrote that “there is no descendant of any high-class native amongst them”; [n34 §1; da Cunha pp. 250–251] by a British official in 1819 who found that they “form a sort of separate caste in the middle of the community”; [n34 §6; Mendiratta p. 220] by reports of 1832 and 1838 that called the Christians of Salsette “in the most lamentable state of superstitious degradation” and “poor, ignorant and drunken”; [n34 §6; GzXIII-1 p. 204] by a census writer in 1931 who thought the Christians of the Bassein coast “more unorthodox than is desirable”. [n34 §6; Census 1931 pp. 363–364] Some of these writers were sympathetic and some contemptuous; none of them was one of the people being described.

The word by which the community knows itself appears in none of the documents. [n34, headline] When the Bombay East Indian Association was founded in 1887 by about thirty “leaders of the community”, it was a Bombay meeting, and the research found no Kupari voice in its records, or anywhere else, between 1887 and 1930. [n34 §7] The Bombay East Indian newspaper and the Association’s souvenirs were not found online. [n34 §7 gaps]

There is one sentence in the whole record that sounds like a Kupari speaking, and it is unattributed. When the English school that Mgr de Silva opened at Nandakhal in 1923 closed for lack of pupils, the parish history recorded the reason the villagers gave: “we get bread and butter from farming and not by studying”. [n40 A3; Wikipedia, Holy Spirit Church Nandakhal] Fifty years later the same villages were sending their children into engineering, medicine, banking and the professions, and abroad to the Gulf, Europe, North America and Australia. [n34 §8; n07 §4] That turn from the field to the office is one of the great changes in Kupari life, and nobody in the record says, in their own words, how it felt.

The songs are going the same way. A local website describes Nandakhal folk songs, “also called ‘Kupari songs’”, whose themes are the bride leaving her father’s house, women fetching fodder, the young groom’s first shave, and a daughter returning home after the rice planting; and it says they “are rarely heard as lifestyles have changed”. [n07 §4; vasaiker.com, “Music of Vasai”] No text of any of them was found in print. The Vadvals are recording their own oral songs because they fear their dialect may disappear; no comparable Kupari project with a name and a date was found. [n41 §8; n34 §8 gaps] No Kupari organisation with a verifiable name and founding date, no anthropological study of the Kuparis as such, and no count of Kupari priests and nuns were found either. [n34 §1, §8 gaps]

The community’s own estimate of its size, between 40,000 and 60,000 people, comes from blogs; the only hard numbers are for all Christians in Vasai taluka (about 110,800, or 8.25 per cent, in the 2011 census, by a Wikipedia table) and all Catholics in the diocese (117,026 at the end of 2022). [n34 §8] A people of tens of thousands, with four and a half centuries of continuous Christian history, a dialect of its own, its own villages, its own songs and its own words for the stages of a life, has left almost no record of itself in its own voice. That is not because it had nothing to say.

An invitation, and a practical guide

The gaps this chapter has listed are not permanent. Most of them can be closed by the families of the villages themselves, with nothing more than time, patience and a phone. What follows is a practical guide, written for any Kupari or Vadval family, and for anyone in these villages who wants the next history of this coast to be written partly in their own words. It is advice, not history; the history it serves is the one above.

Start with the oldest people, and start now. Every entry in this chapter that ends “no source” is a question that someone’s grandmother can answer. Sit with the eldest members of the family, one at a time, in a quiet room. Ask permission to record, and say what the recording is for. A phone’s voice recorder is enough; hold it still, close to the speaker, away from fans and traffic. Record in whatever language they speak most easily, Kadodi, Vadvali, Marathi or English, and do not correct them. Say the date, the place and the speaker’s name at the start of each recording.

Ask the questions the documents cannot answer. For example: - What was the family’s house called, and who built it? Which hamlet, which ali? Who lived on either side? - Where did the surname come from, in the family’s telling? Is there a story of a godfather, a priest or a landlord? Which parts of the story did they hear from whom? - Did anyone in the family carry a different surname, or a Marathi name, or a nickname for the house? - What did the family grow: plantains, betel, sugarcane, paddy, vegetables, flowers? Did they dry plantains? Who took the produce to market, by what road, cart or boat, and to where? - How were the wells, the bhowkal, dug and kept, and who kept them? - What was the parish like before it had its own church? Who walked to Nirmal, Agashi or Nandakhal, and how long did it take? Who helped build the church, and with what? - What happened in the cholera and plague years, the floods, the famines? What did people vow, and to whom? - What words, songs, proverbs, prayers and games do they remember? What was sung at a Hamdao, a Lagin, a Varad, a Saudio? What was done for a Yentar, a Saatvoh, a Pirsao? - What did people wear, the red cap, the red or checked lugda, and when did they stop? - Who first went to college, to Bombay, to the Gulf, to England, America or Australia, and why?

Sing the songs onto the record. If anyone still knows a Kupari song, the Nandakhal songs about the bride, the fodder, the first shave and the return after planting, or any other, record the whole song, not a fragment, and then record the singer talking about it: where they learned it, from whom, and when it was sung. Then write the words down in Devanagari and in Roman letters, with a translation, and keep the writing with the recording. A song that is written down and recorded with its singer’s name is a historical source; a song that is only remembered is one funeral away from being lost.

Find the parish entries. Ask the parish priest, politely and in person, what registers the parish holds and how far back they go. If the registers survive, ask whether you may consult the entries for your own family, and photograph them with permission. For each baptism, note the date, the child’s name, the parents’ names, the godparents’ names, the priest’s name and the village. The godparents’ names are the ones that matter for the surname question: they are where a Portuguese name, or a change of name, may first appear. Work backwards a generation at a time. Be careful with the personal details of living people, and do not publish them without consent.

Look after the photographs and papers. Scan old photographs at a high resolution. Write the names, the place and the approximate date on a separate sheet or in the file name, never on the photograph itself. Do the same for letters, land papers, wedding invitations, school certificates, parish magazines and anything written in the community’s own Marathi. Keep one copy in the family and one somewhere else.

Report the stones. This chapter began with an inscribed stone used as a doorstep, and the Gazetteer found others built into plinths, lying in ponds and serving as washing stones. [n40 A11; n42] There may be more in the villages still. If a family has a carved or lettered stone, photograph it in good light from several sides, with a measuring tape beside it, and leave it where it is.

Mark what is tradition. When the family’s story says something the documents do not, write it down as the family’s story, with the name of the person who told it. Do not try to make it fit the documents. A tradition recorded honestly, with its source, is evidence; a tradition dressed up as fact misleads the people who come after.

Share it. A copy for the parish, a copy for the family, and a copy for any community or diocesan archive willing to keep it safely. The author of this book would be glad to hear from any family willing to share what it finds, so that later editions can tell more of this story in the community’s own voice.

The pull of one village

This chapter has had to do what the documents do: look at the Kupari and Vadval country from a distance, village by village, a sentence or two at a time. One village deserves more than that, because it is the author’s own, and because looking closely at one place shows how much can still be recovered when someone takes the trouble. It is the village whose people told the surveyors of 1882 that it was “the site of old Sopara”, where a two-thousand-year-old name served as a washing stone, which petitioned for its own church in 1941, and whose son became a bishop in 2026. It is Gass, and its story is the next chapter.
