In this chapter. Village by village: the parishes round the fort, the Kupari villages of the south, the shore, and the east of the railway.

The violin masters

In 1882 every Catholic parish around Bassein paid a music master. The Gazetteer’s compilers noticed him wherever they went. At Papdi the parish school of 56 pupils had “a violin master”. At Merces there was another. At Nirmal the teacher of the private school “played the violin in church”. At Agashi the master taught seventeen children Portuguese reading, arithmetic, singing and music, and played the violin at mass. [n11; n40; n34; Gaz XIV pp. 2, 37, 293] “Each church”, the Gazetteer wrote of the Christians of the district, “pays one or two music masters who… play on the violin.” [n34; Gaz XIII-I p. 207]

The violins sounded in a landscape the same volume described with unusual care. It was sugarcane under coconut and brab palms; mango, tamarind, banyan, pipal and karanj trees; sunken lanes “four or five feet below the level of the fields” between high hedgerows; old footways of basalt blocks; runnels from the garden wells; round ponds and winding lakes “fringed by high pile-supported Persian wheels”; and tiled houses with wooden walls on high masonry plinths, often built from older carved stones. [n11; Gaz XIV p. 315] Through that country ran the parishes of the Portuguese centuries, and between them the temples the Marathas had rebuilt after 1739, the salt pans of the creek, the fishing hamlets of the shore, and, east of the railway, the rice flats and the forested slopes of Tungar hill, where the Varlis lived.

This chapter walks through that country village by village. It covers the villages of the south and west of Vasai taluka around the old town of Bassein, whose fort and town are the subject of Chapter 17: the Christian parishes round the fort (Papdi, Sandor, Remedy, Merces, Giriz, Pali and Mulgaon, Manickpur, Chulne, Gokhivare); the Kupari villages of the south-west (Nirmal, Bhuigaon, Gomes Ali, and short notes on Gass and Nandakhal, which have their own chapters); the shore and creek villages (Holi, Kalamb, Rangaon, Tarkhad, Naigaon, Panju, Umela, Juchandra, Sasunavghar); and the villages east of the railway and in the foothills of Tungareshwar (Achole, Waliv, Sativali, Kaman, Bapane, Chandip, Pelhar, Parol, Majivali, Bhatane, Tivri, Chinchoti). For each it asks the same questions: when is it first mentioned; who lives there, and since when; what are its church, temples and landmarks; how did its people earn a living, and how has that changed; what stories it keeps; what the census says; and how it stands now. Where the record says nothing, the entry says so plainly.

How to read the villages

Four things should be said before the walk begins.

First, the communities sort themselves roughly by ground. The Christian villages immediately around the fort, the southern parishes the Portuguese called South Bassein, are mainly Vadval: Christian descendants of the Pachkalshi gardeners. The Gazetteer of 1882 names “Christian cartmen chiefly of Chulna, Mánikpur, Gokhivra, Sándor, and Barhánpur in Bassein” as “Vadvals or gardeners”. [n40; n41; Gaz XIII-I p. 97] The Christian villages of the north-west, the garden belt of Sopara and Agashi with Nirmal at its heart, are mainly Kupari: Samvedi Christians, “many of them converted Sámvedi Bráhmans” in the Gazetteer’s words. [n40; Gaz XIV p. 315] The shore and creek have Kolis, Christian and Hindu, and Agris on the salt pans and rice land. The eastern hills have the Varlis and other Adivasi peoples. And through all of them run the Hindu Samvedi Brahmans, Pachkalshis, Bhandaris, the Brahman families the Marathas brought after 1739, the Gujarati Vanis of the market villages, and the Muslims of the old trading coast. [n40; n08; n11] These are tendencies, not borders. Giriz, between the two Christian belts, has been claimed by both, and on the evidence belongs to the Vadval side. [n40] Many villages are mixed.

Second, most of these villages have no recent census figure of their own. When the Vasai-Virar City Municipal Corporation was formed on 3 July 2009, it took in most of the garden villages, and the 2011 census counted it as one urban unit of 1,222,390 people. Only about fifty villages of Vasai taluka, mostly on the north-west coast and in the east, were counted separately as rural. [n11; n12; n40] So Papdi, Sandor, Remedy, Merces, Nirmal, Bhuigaon, Giriz, Holi, Chulne, Gokhivare, Naigaon, Juchandra, Kaman, Pelhar, Sasunavghar and many others have no 2011 village figure. The last village-level count is usually the 2001 census, which the notes did not read except for Sandor and Gokhivare. [n11; n12; n40]

Third, the founding dates of the parish churches are contested. Most “founded in 15xx” dates come from parish tradition, community websites, a list on a local history site and a heritage survey of 2008, and they disagree by years or decades; the Gazetteer gives the dates of nineteenth-century rebuildings. [n40; n09; n11] Each entry sets out the conflicting dates and their sources rather than choosing one.

Fourth, much of what follows depends on one professional but hurried source: the survey of heritage buildings and precincts of the Vasai-Virar sub-region made in 2008 by the Collective Research Initiatives Trust (CRIT) for the Mumbai Metropolitan Region Heritage Conservation Society. CRIT listed every gram panchayat of the plantation zone and their ponds, temples, churches and old houses, and its list is the nearest thing to a register of these villages’ landmarks. But it was compiled quickly from parish, council and secondary sources, and some of its dates are wrong. [n40] It is cited as “CRIT” with its volume and item numbers.

The Kuparis, the author’s community, receive in this chapter what the book’s plan asks for everywhere: in each village where they are present, a distinct and fuller passage of their own, after the account of all that village’s peoples. Where the notes record no Kuparis in a village, the entry says that too.

Part One: The parishes round the fort

The Christian villages that ring the fort are the oldest parishes outside its walls. A Jesuit account, as reported on Wikipedia, says that Remedy, Papdi, Pali, Merces and Porim (Manickpur) were all carved out of the mother parish of Sandor between 1574 and 1606. [n11; n40] In 1876 Gerson da Cunha counted 12,400 native Christians in the Bassein parishes: Palli 800, Espirito Santo (Nandakhal) 2,000, Papari (Papdi) 1,200, Nossa Senhora das Merces 700, Monte Calvario 1,500, Manikpura 900, Nossa Senhora dos Remedios 3,000, Agasi 900 and Sam Thome (Sandor) 1,000. [n40; da Cunha p. 161] (Monte Calvario cannot be placed from the notes.) Six years later the Gazetteer gave the six parishes nearest the town, with their congregations in 1881: Our Lady of Mercy at Merces (974), Our Lady of Remedies at Dhauli (3,238), St Michael at Manickpur (800), St Thomas at Sandor (1,725), Our Lady at Pali (900) and Our Lady of Grace at Papdi (1,294). [n32; Gaz XIV pp. 36–37]

Papdi (Papdy, Papari)

First mention and names. The notes give no Portuguese-era document naming Papdi itself, though the parish is traditionally dated to 1574. Its first secular description is da Cunha’s of 1876, as “Papari, a village about a cannon-shot from Bajipura”, the Maratha name for Bassein. [n40; da Cunha p. 157] A parish history says its first parishioners came from “the village of Davly (Remedy), part of Sandor and Maferegua”; Maferegua cannot be identified from the notes. [n40; EI Papdy]

The parish. Our Lady of Grace, Nossa Senhora da Graça, was, by the parish’s own account, “the second parish to be founded in South Bassein outside the Fort, the first being St Thomas, Sandor”. Its people “were poor farmers and mainly survived on manual labour”; there were about 1,388 parishioners at first. The Jesuits looked after it until the Maratha conquest, and “unlike other churches during the invasion, this church was not destroyed”. It got a vicar of its own in 1839. [n40; EI Papdy; CRIT sheet 40] The heritage survey agrees: built 1574 by the Jesuit missionaries, it “escaped destruction during the Maratha occupation”. [n40; CRIT] Da Cunha names Nossa Senhora da Graça as one of three Bassein parishes the Jesuits founded. [n37; da Cunha p. 234] The Gazetteer of 1882 gives the church standing then: about two miles north-east of Bassein, about 102 feet long, rebuilt in 1865 for Rs 18,000 raised by subscription, with a congregation of 1,294 and a government-aided parish school of 56 pupils with a violin master. [n11; n40; n37; Gaz XIV p. 37]

On 15 August 1998, when Bishop Thomas Dabre was installed as the first Bishop of Vasai, Our Lady of Grace became the cathedral of the new diocese. [n11; n40; GCatholic] On 29 December 2024 the diocese opened the Jubilee Year 2025 there. [n38] In 2025 the parish community’s own restoration of the church won a UNESCO Asia-Pacific Award of Merit for heritage conservation. [n11; n40; Outlook Traveller; FPJ]

Hindu Papdi. Papdi has a second history that the parish account leaves out. In 1876 da Cunha described it as “chiefly inhabited by Brahmans of Chitpawan, Karada, and Deshastha divisions, as well as Palshas, Sonars, and other inferior castes of the Hindus”. [n32; n40; da Cunha p. 157] These were the Brahman families who came with the Maratha conquest of 1739, settled within reach of the new governor at Bajipur, and displaced the old Palshe priesthood of the coast from its first place. [n08; Gaz XIII-I p. 81 n.] The heritage survey puts it in its own terms: “Although Papdy was known as a famous shipbuilding centre, the present fabric of Papdy shows palimpsests from the 18th century when it was given as a jagir to the community which transported goods, supplies and food to the Maratha army.” [n40; CRIT Vol II] The survey does not name that community. One note behind this book suggests the Vadval carters of the Gazetteer, and that is an inference, not a record. [n40] The shipbuilding claim, too, rests on CRIT alone.

The Vanis of Papdi. The Gujarati merchants of Papdi ran the betel-nut trade of the whole garden belt. The areca palms of the Bassein gardens were grown partly by Christians, “Malis converted by the Portuguese”, and partly by Chavkalshi and Pachkalshi Hindus, and their nuts were sold wholesale “to the Vanis of Papdi, about two miles from Bassein”. The Vanis sorted them into six classes, phulbardi, tambdi, chikni, lavangchuri, pandhri and dagdi or kapkadi, and sold them at Rs 21 to Rs 36 for a maund of eighty pounds, to Bombay, Poona and Gujarat. “Bassein is famous for its phulbardi betelnuts.” [n21; n41; Gaz XIII-I pp. 298–300] Lad Vani families, who had come from Cambay about 1760, lived at Papdi as well as Nala and Agashi. [n05; n37]

Landmarks. The heritage survey’s Papdy Talav precinct lists the Papdy Talav; the Ram Mandir, rebuilt in the early twentieth century, whose original “marked Maratha dominance” from 1739 to 1818; the Bondali Talav; Govardhan Vidyalaya; the Mohanlal Vakil Bungalow; Shah Wada, of the 1850s; the Thomas Baptista School; and the Taam Talav. [n40; CRIT Vol I items 36–42; Vol II] It is a precinct of Hindu temples and Christian schools, Brahman and merchant houses, around the ponds that watered the gardens. The Baroda railway had a five-mile feeder road “from Gokirva to Papdi”, linking it to the line east of the creek. [n40; Gaz XIII-I p. 319] Part of the gardens “between Bassein and Pápdi” lay inside the municipality founded in 1864. [n32; Gaz XIV p. 36]

Livelihoods. Gardens, betel nut and the Vanis’ trade in the nineteenth century; the parish’s “poor farmers” in its early centuries; today, a suburb of Vasai West, with its cathedral, schools and market. [n40; n21]

Census and today. Papdi has no separate 2011 census figure; it lies inside the old Vasai municipal area and the corporation. [n40] The cathedral is active, and its restoration is recent and celebrated. The notes hold no village-level data for 2026.

Kuparis at Papdi. No source the notes reached records Kupari families at Papdi. It is a parish of the Vadval south, and its Kupari connection is the cathedral itself: the seat, since 1998, of the diocese whose first bishop was born at Bhuigaon, a village on the community’s own list of Kupari villages. [n40; n34]

Sandor (Sandore)

First mention. Sandor has the oldest dated record of any village round the fort. A stone found at the Relbav pond carries a Shilahara grant of AD 1255 (Shaka 1177), by a king titled Konkan chakravarti, giving the astrologer Kheidev “a garden in the part called Nivayi within the limits of Sandor village”; it names a headquarters station, Nagapur, and a high road. A second inscribed stone at Sandor is dated Hijra 966, AD 1558, and mentions “Nayak Laro, lord of the western ocean”. [n37; Gaz XIV p. 402] So Sandor was a gardened, granted village under the Shilaharas, three centuries before the Portuguese, and in 1558 someone there set up a stone in the Islamic calendar. Who Nayak Laro was, the notes do not say.

Names. “Sandore” in the Gazetteer; “pacaria Sandor” in the Tombo de Baçaim of 1727–30, leased in perpetuity (em enfiteuse) to Jorge da Silva Coelho for 435 pardaus a year. The same register has an “aldeia Chandory/Sandory”, which seems to be a different, northern place. [n40; Tombo fl. 29v, 111v]

The mother church. St Thomas the Apostle at Sandor calls itself “the Mother Church of all the churches of South Bassein”, and it is the oldest church building outside the fort that still stands, by the heritage survey’s judgement. Its dates are tangled. The parish page says it was built by the Jesuits “in 1556” (probably a misprint) and heads it 1566. Wikipedia, translating the parish tradition, says that after the conversions of 1564 a Portuguese noblewoman, “Donna Irina”, built a chapel of Our Lady of Help, whose first mass was said on 11 November 1566; a picture of St Thomas brought from the fort gave the chapel its new name; Gujarat forces destroyed it in 1571 and it was rebuilt in 1573; Arabs from Gujarat damaged it in 1679 and “Kakaji” in 1690. CRIT dates the church to 1571: “In 1571 the Mohamedans besieged Bassein and the chapel was destroyed. A church was built on the spot of the chapel with the first Mass being celebrated on the feast of St Thomas.” [n40; n11; EI Sandor; CRIT Vol III asset 103; Wikipedia: Sandor] A Jesuit, Father Pedro de Almeida, served as Pai dos Cristãos, the official protector of converts, over Sandor and Papdi from 1574 to 1576. [n40] The church escaped destruction in 1739. It was enlarged in 1889, and “the three altars in the church are from one of the churches in Bassein Fort”. [n40; EI Sandor] The Gazetteer of 1882 found the side walls of the sixteenth century, a chapel of 1838 and a front of 1858, three miles north of Bassein, with a congregation of 1,725. [n11; n40; Gaz XIV p. 37]

A Sandor tradition holds that St Gonsalo Garcia was present as a boy at the founding of the chapel. It is tradition only. [n11; n40]

From Sandor came Remedy, Papdi, Pali, Merces and Porim (Manickpur) between 1574 and 1606, and later Giriz (1917–18) and Chulne (1963). [n40] Its feast is kept from 9 to 15 December. A St Vincent de Paul conference was founded there in 1971. [n40]

The people. Sandor is the clearest Christian Vadval village in the record. The Gazetteer of 1882 lists it among the villages of the Vadval cartmen. [n40; Gaz XIII-I p. 97] Wikipedia, in a passage tagged as needing citations, says “the Sandoris are predominantly of the Vadval sub-ethnic group and speak the Vadvali dialect”. [n40; n11] The Vadvals of Sandor were the axemen and carters the Gazetteer admired, carrying timber from the Vada forests in convoys of five to thirty carts by night, and the gardeners of the evergreen belt. [n41; Gaz XIII-I pp. 97–98] Grierson recorded their dialect in 1905: a Marathi dialect “most intimately connected with the usual Marathi of the Central and Northern Konkan”, with Gujarati words and Bhil-like sound changes, in which char (“four”) becomes sar and sona (“gold”) becomes hona. [n41; LSI VII pp. 145–146]

Inside this Christian village stand Hindu shrines. The heritage survey lists, in Sandor gram panchayat, the Dev Talav and a Hanuman Mandir, both of the eighteenth century and so of the Maratha period; the Relba Talav, where the 1255 grant was found; a nineteenth-century grampanchayat office in a former Zilla Parishad school; and the church. [n40; CRIT Vol I items 99–103] Sandor was one of the three survey villages taken into the Bassein municipality of 1864. [n11; n32; Gaz XIV p. 36]

Saloli and Kharbhat. Near Sandor, the village of Saloli is served today by Sandor parish. Its Barmala and Saloli ponds are listed by CRIT, the latter dated to the sixteenth century and used, the survey says, for Ganesh immersions and for fish auctions: a Christian village’s pond serving both a Hindu festival and the fish trade. [n40; CRIT items 97–98] A hamlet of Saloli, Kharbhat, says of itself on a community wiki that its people have followed Christianity since the church was built in 1566. [n40; Everybodywiki] That is a community claim.

Census and today. Wikipedia gives Sandor a population of 8,336 in 2001, with 78 per cent literacy, as a census town; in 2011 it lay inside the corporation and has no separate figure. [n11; n40] Sandor was among the places that grew fastest after 1988: the TIFR study says Vasai, Virar, Sopara and Sandor grew by 290 per cent between 1981 and 1991. [n05] The notes hold no 2026 village data.

Kuparis at Sandor. No source records Kupari families at Sandor. It is a Vadval village, and the community’s Kupari lists do not include it. [n40]

Remedy (Remedi, Dhauli, Davly, “Douli”)

Names. The village was Dhauli to the Gazetteer, Davly to the Papdi parish history, and “Douli” to Hull, the historian of the Bombay mission; it is called Remedy, or Remedi, after its church of Nossa Senhora dos Remédios, Our Lady of Remedies. [n40; n38; Gaz XIV pp. 36–37; Hull I p. 5] Dhauli was one of the three survey villages of the Bassein municipality of 1864. [n32]

The church. The founding date is disputed. A local history site and a Goanet post give 1557; the heritage survey says “the Dominicans founded the church outside the Fort in 1577. The parish was the second to be separated from the mother parish of St Thomas, Sandor”; GCatholic gives 1577 for the shrine; Hull gives “built 1583, rebuilt 1839”. [n40; n09; n38; CRIT Vol II asset 22; Hull I p. 5] Da Cunha in 1876 found Nossa Senhora dos Remedios in the charge of the Dominicans, “still in a fair state of preservation”, with 3,000 Christians. [n40; da Cunha pp. 161, 163] The Gazetteer of 1882 described the church then standing: two miles north of Bassein, 108 by 33 by 23 feet, built in 1821 for Rs 18,600 (Rs 18,000 raised by subscription and Rs 600 from the government), with a congregation of 3,238, the largest of the six parishes round the town. The Dhauli parish school had about 15 pupils. [n11; n32; n40; Gaz XIV p. 37] It was extended in 1974–75. [n40]

Remedy is today a diocesan shrine with a minor seminary. The creation of the Diocese of Vasai was announced at the Shrine of Our Lady of Remedy on 28 June 1998. [n40; n38] Bishop Thomas D’Souza was its rector from 2013 to 2019, before he became bishop. [n38] The diocese’s women’s sodalities, 7,000 to 8,000 strong, belong to “The Federation of Our Lady of Remedy”. [n09; n05] Near the church stands the “Milagres Cross”, called miraculous, where devotees are often seen praying. [n09] A tradition says the old door of a church in Vasai Fort is kept at the Remedy church; no parish source confirms it. [n09; n40]

The healing tank. Remedy’s oldest story is a shared one. Da Cunha recorded that near the church of Our Lady of Remedies, on the road between Bassein and Agashi, there was a healing tank to which Hindus had made pilgrimage; the Inquisition “had put a stop to all pilgrimage” there “under very severe penalties”; and by his own day, 1876, Hindus held a jatra there again. [n37; da Cunha p. 163] The Gazetteer adds that at one famous pool between Bassein and Agashi the Franciscans converted the pool, built a chapel to Our Lady of Healing, and “the pilgrimage and cure-working continued”. [n37; Gaz XIII-II p. 461 n.] Whether these are the same pool is not stated, but both describe a Hindu healing water absorbed into a Christian shrine and then, after the Portuguese were gone, returned to Hindu use.

Landmarks. CRIT lists at Remedy the Holy Market precinct on the Remedy–Par Naka road, where vendors from Sandor, Kaular Budruk and neighbouring villages pay five rupees a day; Patil Wada, of the 1850s, “the typical house of the landowning agricultural community”; Matkari Wada, of 1868, home of the advocate Raja Ram Keshab Matkari (1920–63); and the R. P. Wagh School. [n40; CRIT Vol I items 21–25; Vol II] The Patil and Matkari houses are among the few named Hindu family houses of the southern villages that appear anywhere in the record.

People. No source names the Vadvals at Remedy directly; it is a parish of the Vadval south by its place and its mother church. [n40] The Patil and Matkari wadas show a landed Hindu presence beside the Christian parish.

Notable people. Archbishop Felix Anthony Machado was born at Remedy on 6 June 1948; he was Bishop of Vasai from 2009 to 2024, after service in the Vatican. [n40; n38; GCatholic]

Census and today. Inside the old Vasai municipal area and the corporation; no separate figure. The shrine is active. [n40]

Kuparis at Remedy. No Kupari presence is recorded.

Merces (Kaular Khurd)

Names. The village takes its name from its church, Nossa Senhora das Mercês, Our Lady of Mercy; before that it was Kaular, and the church stands in the gram panchayat of Kaular Khurd (“lesser Kaular”), beside Kaular Budruk (“greater Kaular”). The Gazetteer lists “Kaulér” among Bassein’s village fairs of under a thousand people. [n40; CRIT Vol I items 22–23; Gaz XIII-I p. 385]

The church. Merces lies “between the villages of Sandor and Remedy and three kilometres northwest of the fort”. Its people, the parish page says, were “predominately fisher folk (kolis)”, who at first preferred to attend services at Remedy. The founding date is disputed; Jesuit reports of 1669 and the research of Father Conti point to 1606. The Augustinians held it until 1739, when the church was destroyed and the priest fled, and the parish passed to the vicar of Remedy. It was rebuilt in 1856, and the first secular vicar, Father Sebastião José Florêncio de Faria, came on 17 January 1862. [n40; EI Merces; CRIT Vol III asset 92] Da Cunha, in conflict, says the church was built by Archbishop Aleixo de Menezes and was Franciscan. [n40; da Cunha p. 161] The Gazetteer of 1882 found it about two miles north of the mamlatdar’s office, built by private subscription, with a congregation of 974 and a violin master; its vicar, unlike the others, was paid Rs 17 a month by the Portuguese government, not the British. [n11; n40; Gaz XIV p. 37] That detail is a small reminder that the old Padroado, the Portuguese crown’s patronage of the church, still reached into this village a century and a half after the fall of Baçaim. The church is graded II B by CRIT, and near it lies the Motha Talav on Augustine Kulas Road. [n40; CRIT items 91–92]

People. Merces is the one parish round the fort whose page names its people as Kolis, Christian fisher-farmers, rather than Vadval gardeners. [n40] The Gazetteer’s “Uraps and other Christian Kolis in Bassein”, who “are professional fishermen”, are the people of villages like this. [n21; Gaz XIII-I p. 55]

Census and today. Inside the corporation; no separate figure. No 2026 source. [n40]

Kuparis at Merces. None recorded.

Giriz (Girij; the Tombo’s “Gris”)

First mention. Giriz is the first title in the caçabé of Baçaim in the Tombo of 1727–30: “pacaria Gris”, leased for three lives to Dona Margarida Teles de Meneses for 166 pardaus and 4 larins. The same register has a “terra Oratem que está a pacaria Gris”, held by João Cabral, and a “horta Vitoba das palmeiras bravas que está na pacaria Gris”, a garden of wild palms, held by a man with an Indian name, “Jasaman Naique”. [n40; Tombo fl. 23, 52v, 53] A named local landholder in a register of Portuguese fidalgos and Jesuits is rare, and Jasaman Naik of Giriz is one of the very few Indian names the Tombo gives.

The church. Giriz had no church of its own for 350 years. “Girij formed a part of St Thomas parish at Sandor ever since the latter was established,” the heritage survey records; the parish page says it belonged to “the vast parish of St Thomas, Sandor and Our Lady of Mercy, Merces”. Its people walked to distant churches, which was hard in the monsoon. They asked the Archbishop of Damaun for a church; the foundation stone of St Francis Xavier’s was laid on 14 February 1917, and the villagers built it in about a year, “without foreign aid”, by their own donations. Father Augusto D’Souza supervised the work and became the first parish priest. [n40; n11; n09; CRIT Vol III asset 90; EI Giriz; Wikipedia] The golden jubilee of 1968 added a porch and the Marshal Pereira Community Hall under Father Francis Gratian Monteiro; an extension and parish house followed in 1977, with German and diocesan help, under Father Dominic Pereira; the twin spires were replaced in May 1981. The feast is on 3 December, the feast of St Francis Xavier. [n40; n09]

Landmarks. CRIT lists the Girij Talav, which it dates to the sixteenth century, with stone ghats, and a twentieth-century grampanchayat office on the Vasai–Nirmal road. [n40; CRIT items 88–90]

People. Giriz is a boundary village between the Vadval south and the Kupari north-west, and some lists have put it with the Kuparis. The evidence points the other way: it belonged to Sandor parish from the beginning, and a community photograph of 2021 is captioned “Vadval East Indian house in Giriz, Vasai”. [n40; East Indian Memory Co.] No source calls Giriz Kupari, and the community’s own Kupari lists omit it. [n40]

Census and today. No separate figure; inside the corporation. The parish is active. [n40]

Kuparis at Giriz. The record does not show a Kupari community at Giriz. On the evidence it is a Vadval village with an independent parish founded by its own people in 1917.

Pali (Palle, Palli) and Vadavali

Names and first mention. Pali appears in the Tombo of 1727–30 as “aldeia Palle”, held by Dona Maria Correia de Macedo. [n40; Tombo fl. 172] The church stands in the gram panchayat of Vadavali, on the Papdy–Naigaon station road. [n40; CRIT Vol III asset 107] Care is needed with the name. The census of 2011 has a rural village called Pali, with 193 houses and 792 people, 204 of them of Scheduled Tribes, and a literacy rate of 88.77 per cent; but its census code sits in a run of villages that seems to lie on the north-east or south-west fringe, and it may be a different Pali from the parish village. [n11; n40] One note places the Pali church in Naigaon West. [n40]

The church. “Also known historically as the Palle Church,” the heritage survey says, the church of the Mother of God (A Madre de Deus) was “built in 1585, which is recorded in the Franciscan Chronicler. The Franciscans had to leave the church when this region was captured by the Marathas in 1739. It was however rebuilt between 1840–44 at a new site 100 feet west of the original site.” [n40; CRIT Vol III asset 107] A local list gives 1595. [n40; n09] Da Cunha, in 1876, counted 800 Christians at Palli and noted that their church “appears… has long been in a dilapidated condition”. [n40; da Cunha pp. 161–162] The Gazetteer of 1882 found the new church, “Our Blessed Lady the Mother of God”, about three miles east of Bassein, built in 1840 for Rs 15,000, with a congregation of 900 and a parish school of about 30 pupils. [n11; n32; Gaz XIV p. 37] A community blog lists a “Pali Fiesta” among the festivals of the Kupari and Samvedi Christians; it is not clear whether that refers to this Pali. [n11; n40]

The Muslim shrines. Pali has the most important Muslim monuments of the southern villages. CRIT lists the Pali Dargah, of the sixteenth century, with a step well, a dome, minarets and stone lattices, and the Pali Masjid, of the eighteenth century, whose building mixes Islamic, Portuguese and wada (Maratha mansion) features. “Before the Portuguese domination”, the survey says, the area had “a significant Muslim population due to the traders and merchants who had settled here.” [n40; CRIT Vol I items 107–109; Vol III] A sixteenth-century dargah in a village with a sixteenth-century Franciscan church is one of the clearest signs on the ground that the Portuguese period did not erase the Muslim presence of the old Gujarat port.

Mulgaon. The daughter parish of Mulgaon, St Mary Magdalene, was separated from Pali in 1945, with Father Ayres Fernandes as its first priest; its church was consecrated in 1966 and kept its golden jubilee on 12 November 1995. Its parish history remembers Father Clement de Sa, who died on 13 November 1963 while giving communion. [n40; EI Mulgaon] Mulgaon was one of the three survey villages of the 1864 Bassein municipality, with Dhauli and Sandor. [n40; n32; Gaz XIV p. 36] The Tombo’s “pacaria Boroly Mulgão”, held by Fernão de Brito de Melo for 333 pardaus, may be it, but the match is unsure. [n40; Tombo fl. 24v] CRIT lists the Mulgaon Talav. [n40]

Kirawali. Near by, the gram panchayat of Kirawali (Kiravali) appears in CRIT’s list of plantation-zone villages, and a Catholic blog of 2015 records a Furtado family cross at “Kirawali-Chobare”. [n40; n09] The Tombo’s “Quirauly e Mafarea”, held by Fernão de Brito de Melo, may be Kirawali; the match is the notes’ inference. [n39] Nothing more is recorded.

People. No source names the community of Pali’s Christians directly; it was a Bassein Christian farming parish of the south, and is counted with the Vadval villages by place. [n40] Pali also had, by CRIT’s account, Muslim families from before the Portuguese.

Census and today. See the caution above on the census “Pali”. The parish is active; no 2026 village data.

Kuparis at Pali. None recorded, unless the “Pali Fiesta” of the community blog is this village’s feast, which the notes cannot confirm.

Manickpur (Manikpur, Manikpura; Portuguese Porim or Purim)

Names. “The Church of St Michael of Porim (or Purim) was named after the village in which it was located,” the parish history says. “The name of this village was changed to Manickpur in the beginning of the 20th century.” [n40; EI Manikpur] The record contradicts the date: da Cunha in 1876 writes “Purim or Manikpura”, and the Gazetteer of 1882 already uses “Manikpur” for the railway station, the creek and the village. [n40; da Cunha pp. 157, 161; Gaz XIV pp. 314, 323, 402] The new name is at least nineteenth-century.

The church. Until 1606 the Catholics of Porim belonged to Sandor. At the insistence of the Jesuit Provincial, the Archbishop of Goa, Dom Aleixo de Menezes, made it a parish. The first mud-and-thatch church was too small, and the Jesuits of the fort built a new one “on the spacious grounds in the centre of the five pacarias of Wananapor, Devamany, Porim, Navgaon and Ullmemany”, with jurisdiction over “Gocavarem (Gokhivare), Calmona, Balliam, Sattolli (Sativali), Achola (Achole) and Rajaoli (Rajavali)”. It was “the first to be demolished by the conquering Maratha army in 1739”. [n40; EI Manikpur] Three dates are given for its successor: “immediately” after 1739 (CRIT), “presumably in 1783”, the date on an old cross Father Conti found (the parish page), and 1851, when the Gazetteer says the church of St Michael the Archangel, four miles north-east of Bassein, was rebuilt for Rs 15,000; it had a congregation of 800 in 1881. [n40; n11; CRIT Vol II asset 19a; Gaz XIV p. 37] Da Cunha counted 900 Christians and placed the church “close to the railway station at Purim or Manikpura. The old one, on the same spot, belonged to the Jesuits.” [n40; da Cunha p. 161] After the Jesuits left, the parish was run by secular clergy until March 1949, when it was returned to the Jesuits. [n40] A tradition that a Dona Ines Francisca gave land for a chapel to the Jesuits in 1572 comes from a website that was down when it was checked, and should not be relied on. [n40]

Burhanpur. About 500 yards south-west of the station, by one note (800 by another), lay a place called Puri, “said to be Burhanpur”, which a Maratha chronicle of the siege of 1738 names. The Gazetteer considered whether it might be the Shilahara capital Puri, and found “no signs of old remains”. [n11; n37; Gaz XIV p. 402] The Gazetteer’s list of Vadval cart villages includes “Barhánpur in Bassein”, which is very probably this place. [n40; Gaz XIII-I p. 97]

People. Manickpur is one of the five Vadval cart villages the Gazetteer names. [n40; Gaz XIII-I p. 97] A thin Wikipedia passage lists its groups as Kunbi, Kumbhar, Vadval and Koli. [n40]

The station. From 28 November 1864 Manickpur was the site of Bassein Road station, today’s Vasai Road, which Chapter 17 describes. A double-storeyed rest-house at the station held inscribed stones brought from Nandui and Nila; an inscribed stone four feet ten inches long was found on the way in 1881. [n40; n12; Gaz XIV p. 387] The Gazetteer’s visitors started their tour of Sopara from the Manikpur bungalow. [n12; n11] Below the station, the Dublas of Gujarat worked the salt pans in the fair season. [n21]

Landmarks. CRIT’s precinct round St Michael’s lists the Father Benedict Talav, the Nazareth Talav and the Umeleman Talav on the Chulne–Manikpur road. [n40; CRIT Vol II items 17–20]

Census and today. Navghar-Manikpur was a municipal council until 2009, when it merged into the corporation; there is no separate 2011 figure. [n12; n40] The parish is Jesuit.

Kuparis at Manickpur. None recorded.

Chulne (Chulna; the Tombo’s “Cholna Roba”)

First mention. The Tombo of 1727–30 lists “pacaria Cholna Roba”, held by the Jesuit procurator-general for 130 pardaus. [n40; Tombo fl. 49] In 1882 the Gazetteer’s visitors returned from Sopara “via Chulna village”, about two miles west of Manikpur station. [n11; n40; Gaz XIV p. 341]

People. Chulne is the first of the five Vadval cart villages the Gazetteer names: “Christian cartmen chiefly of Chulna, Mánikpur, Gokhivra, Sándor, and Barhánpur”. [n40; Gaz XIII-I p. 97] A century later the parish history describes it as “the suburban village of Chulne whose inhabitants are practically all Catholics”, about three kilometres from Vasai Road station. [n40; EI Chulne] A Jesuit-held village in 1730 and almost wholly Catholic in the late twentieth century: Chulne is one of the places where the notes’ inference that Jesuit priests, rather than lay landlords, were the likely baptismal sponsors seems most fitting, though no document proves it. [n39]

The church. Chulne had no church until the 1950s. Sunday mass began on 9 September 1951 under Father Philip Tavares; the foundation stone was laid on 22 April 1961; the parish of Our Lady of Fatima was canonically established on 1 June 1963; the church was blessed on 17 May 1964. [n40; EI Chulne]

Landmarks. CRIT lists the Chulne Talav, the Khargoda Talav and the Nazareth Talav. [n40; CRIT Vol I items 15–17]

Notable people. Bishop Thomas Francis D’Souza was born at Chulne on 23 March 1970, ordained in 1998, rector of the Remedy shrine from 2013 to 2019, and Bishop of Vasai from 9 November 2024 (consecrated 15 December 2024). [n40; n38; GCatholic] GCatholic lists Chulne as a bishop’s birthplace. [n11]

Census and today. Inside the corporation; no separate figure. The parish is active. [n40]

Kuparis at Chulne. None recorded; Chulne is a Vadval village.

Gokhivare (Gokirva, Gokhivra; Portuguese Gocavarem)

First mention. In 1728 a Portuguese report noted that General Louis de Mello Pereira had built a bridge across the creek at “Gokirva”, which it complained was unprotected. [n12; n40; Gaz XIV p. 322] The village was in the jurisdiction of St Michael’s, Manickpur, as “Gocavarem”. [n40]

The road to Tungar. In the 1870s Gokhivare was the gateway to the hill. The government road ran from the station to Gokirva, “bridged, drained, embanked, and metalled, 1 mile 766 feet”, then two miles more to the foot of the hill and about five and a half miles to the top; the stretch from Gokirva to the summit was built in 1869–71 for about Rs 8,555. At Gokirva, the Gazetteer says, “is a shrine of Mahadev, a pond, and the remains of an old Portuguese stockade”. On a hillock at the base of the hill stood a ruined Portuguese tower, “probably built as a defence against the attacks of the Jawhar chief and his Kolis, who were very formidable to the Portuguese, ‘leaping like monkeys from tree to tree’”. [n12; n10; Gaz XIV pp. 366–367] The Baroda railway’s five-mile feeder road ran “from Gokirva to Papdi”. [n40; Gaz XIII-I p. 319]

People. Gokhivare is one of the five Vadval cart villages. [n40; Gaz XIII-I p. 97] CRIT notes that its pond lies near an adivasi pada, a hamlet of Adivasi families. [n40; CRIT item 122]

The church. The parish of St Roque was born of a vow. “In 1914 the village was severely affected by an epidemic of cholera,” the parish history says, and the people promised a church to St Roque, the saint invoked against plague. Two hundred villagers built a chapel of 40 by 18 feet; Archbishop Lima added a sanctuary; the Dean of South Bassein blessed it on 30 June 1930. It became a parish in 1943, with the Jesuit Father Nubiola as first priest, and has been Jesuit since, except from 1968 to 1971; it was extended in 1990. [n40; EI Gokhivare]

Census and industry. Gokhivare was a census town in 2001 with 19,772 people, and now lies inside the corporation. [n12; n40] Its eastern fringe belongs to the industrial belt of Vasai East: the Nova Industrial Estate in Gokhivare was registered under the real estate law on 24 January 2023, one of many small industrial estates of galas, units for sale or rent, between Gokhivare, Sativali and Waliv. [n12] Chapter 31 describes Gokhivare’s northern edge.

Kuparis at Gokhivare. None recorded.

Part Two: The Kupari villages of the south-west

North-west of the fort, beyond Giriz, the land rises a little into the garden country of the old Sopara agar: “a richly wooded well-watered and highly tilled tract”, the Gazetteer called it, “including Bolinj, Koprad and Umbrala in the north, Nirmal and Vagholi in the west, Gas in the south, and Sopara and Mardesh in the centre”. “The husbandmen of Sopara are Christians, many of them converted Sámvedi Bráhmans.” [n11; n40; Gaz XIV pp. 314–315] This is the country of the Kuparis. The community’s own list names thirteen places: Nirmal, Agashi, Nandakhal, Gass, Bhuigaon, Umrale, Nanbhat, Shirlaiwadi, Bolinj, Mardes, Wagholi, Gomes Ali and Rajodi. [n40; n39] The northern ones, from Agashi to Rajodi, lie on the Virar side and are described in Chapter 31. This part takes the southern ones, those nearest Vasai.

Nirmal (Nirmala; Portuguese parish Santa Cruz)

The name and the legend. “Nirmal or the stainless,” the Gazetteer explains, and tells the legend from the Nirmal Mahatmya. A demon, Vimal, harassed the Brahman seers “who lived near the sacred Vaitarni”. Parshuram cut off his hands and feet, which grew back; with Shiva’s help he overcame Vimal, pardoned him, and set up the linga Vimaleshvar where he fell, and “from Vimal it came to be called Nirmal or stainless”. [n11; n40; Gaz XIV p. 292 n.] Da Cunha gives a version: “Vimala… now called Nirmala, or stainless, since it was purified by Parasurama”. [n40; da Cunha p. 126] The Gazetteer also records a drier etymology, from Mr A. Cumine of the Civil Service: a Sanskrit rendering of Dravidian nir (water) and mal (hill), “the sea hill”, “a true description of the place which is a wooded mound rising from bare flats once under water”. [n40; Gaz XIV p. 292 n.] The Skanda Purana, the Gazetteer says, calls Nirmal “the best of holy pools, the seat of perfection”. [n11; n40]

First datable trace. In front of a house about 500 yards north of the chief temple stands a dressed stone with letters “which seem part of a Sanskrit inscription of the seventh century”. [n11; n40; Gaz XIV p. 293] It is the oldest datable object in Nirmal, and it means the hill was a place of some importance long before the Portuguese. A blog of 2011 adds that remains of an urban settlement lie buried under Nirmal’s fields; that is a blogger’s report. [n11; n40]

The temple destroyed and rebuilt. “The Portuguese had pulled down the temples and destroyed the ling,” the Gazetteer says. “On the fall of Bassein (1739) Nirmal was purified, prints of the feet of Shri Dattatraya took the place of the ling, and a reservoir was built.” The stones the Portuguese had built into churches “were probably used to raise the temples of the older gods”. Da Cunha says it plainly: “the Inquisition pulled down the temple”. [n11; n40; n37; Gaz XIV p. 293; da Cunha pp. 126–127] Eight temples were then built at Nirmal “all about 1750 by Shankarji Keshav, Sarsubhedar of Bassein”, the first Maratha governor: the Shankaracharya Svami temple (with a yearly government grant of Rs 421), Mahavishnu (Rs 118), Vimaleshvar Mahadev (Rs 100) and Bhuteshvar (Rs 60) west of the lake; Ganpati, Durga and Hanuman (Rs 35 together) east of it; and Siddheshvar (Rs 25) to the north, supported by Shankarji Keshav’s descendants. There were also grants to a Brahman annachhatra, a free kitchen (Rs 454), and a Bairagi alms-house (Rs 148). [n11; n40; Gaz XIV pp. 292–293] A blog of 2019 credits Chimaji Appa with the rebuilding and says the caretakers today are a Gujarati Brahmin family; the Gazetteer’s attribution to Shankarji Keshav is the better one. [n11; n40] The heritage survey dates the Shankaracharya temple to the “early 17th century” with a Peshwa as patron, which cannot be right, since the Peshwas ruled in the eighteenth. [n40]

The Shankaracharya and the fair. Tradition makes Nirmal the burial place of one of the great Shankaracharyas. The Gazetteer, noting that the great Shankara died in Kashmir, suggested the Nirmal one “may have been one of his religious descendants”. [n40; Gaz XIV p. 292 n.] The heritage survey repeats a temple tradition of samadhis of a fifth and seventh Shankaracharya in the fifth and fourth centuries BC; no historian supports those dates. [n40] On Kartik Vadya 11, in November, a fair began that lasted a week and drew 6,000 to 7,000 pilgrims, “Hindus, Musalmans, Christians, and a few Parsis”, from Thana, Gujarat, Bombay, the Deccan and the southern Konkan; brass and copper vessels, dried plantains, sweets, cloth and cattle worth about Rs 10,000 changed hands. [n11; n40; Gaz XIV p. 292] It was the region’s greatest pilgrimage in 1882, and its crowd of every faith is the best nineteenth-century evidence in this book of shared devotion on this coast. [n10] Today the samadhi temple stands on a fortress-like hillock reached from Nirmal naka, with shrines to Shiva, Parvati and Shitala Mata in the main hall. [n11] “From the Shankaracharya temple is a fine view across the great Nirmal lake to the sea.” [n39] The November jatra still runs. [n40]

The church. Holy Cross, Santa Cruz, is the mother parish of the Kupari south. Its founding date is given as 1557 (the community blog, a local history list), 1558 (a parish directory), 1580 (a marble plaque on the bell tower: “This Church was built by the Portuguese in 1580”) and 1586 (the heritage survey register). [n40; n09; n11] The plaque continues: “Destroyed during Portuguese-Maratha war in 1739. Rebuilt by the Parishioners in 1856, at the cost of Rs 20,000 and physical labour. From 1739 to 1856 the present Cemetery was pro tempore their Church.” [n40; EI Nirmal] For 117 years, then, the Christians of Nirmal said mass in their graveyard. The Gazetteer of 1882 gives the cost of the rebuilding as about Rs 24,000, the church 96 by 28 by 26 feet, a congregation of 2,184, a vicar paid Rs 14-8 a month by the British government, and a private school whose teacher played the violin in church. [n11; n40; Gaz XIV p. 293] The church has a stepped approach and an unusual wooden mansard roof, held with tie beams and no trusses. [n40; CRIT Vol III asset 81] Its feast is on 14 September, the Exaltation of the Holy Cross. [n38] Bishop Edwin Colaco, later Bishop of Amravati, was vicar of Nirmal in 1979. [n40]

From Nirmal parish came Gass (1942), Bhuigaon (1946) and Gomes Ali (2004), and, by a Wikipedia table, Mardes and Wagholi. [n40]

Landmarks. The temple hill is surrounded by the Vimal Talav, with the Vimaleshvar temple, and the Malai Talav; the great Nirmal lake lies below. Nirmal Bazaar, founded in 1957, is in Vagholi gram panchayat. [n40; CRIT Vol I items 69, 82]

Communities. Nirmal is the one village in this book where the Hindu and Christian halves of the Samvedi people stand around the same hill. The Samvedi Brahmans’ own tradition makes Nirmaleshwar on the Nirmal hillock their community deity, and Adi Shankaracharya at Nirmal their chief teacher; their villages are those “around the Nirmal sarovar”. That tradition rests on a single, poorly sourced page. [n08; Wikipedia: Samvedi] The heritage survey records a tradition that the temple’s musicians, the “Shamaneedresh”, gave the Samvedis their colloquial name “Shamedi”. [n40] Bhandaris lived here too, and the alms-houses served Brahmans and Bairagis. [n40]

The Kuparis of Nirmal. Nirmal is the heart of the Kupari world. The Gazetteer’s inference that Portuguese church stones went into the temples of 1750, and da Cunha’s statement that the Inquisition pulled down the temple, together mean that Holy Cross was built at an existing Hindu holy place; the conversion of Nirmal was a conversion of the Samvedi Brahman families around a living shrine. [n40] What made them convert is the subject of Chapter 34; the record for Nirmal itself has no conversion document. [n40] What the record does show is what came after. When the church fell in 1739 and the temple rose again on the hill, the Christian Samvedis kept their faith in their cemetery for more than a century, and rebuilt their church in 1856 with their own money and labour. [n40] In 1882 their parish of 2,184 was the third largest in the taluka. The Kupari dialect, Kadodi, which a Bhuigaon family in 2011 said differed from the Vadval speech of the southern villages, and the customs the Gazetteer recorded for Christian and Hindu Samvedis alike (the red cap, the rites of the fifth night after birth) belong to this hill and its villages. [n34; n08] Nirmal was the mother of the Kupari parishes of the south: Gass, Bhuigaon, Gomes Ali. Its November fair, to which the Christians of 1882 came with the Hindus, is still held below the church. [n40]

Census and today. No separate 2011 figure; Nirmal was a gram panchayat until 2009 and is now in the corporation. [n40]

Bhuigaon (Bhuigaon Khurd and Bhuigaon Budruk)

First mention. Da Cunha in 1876 described the creek system of the Arnala–Vaitarna side as “divided into several villages, at one of which, by name Bhoegaum, the sea enters, runs through that of Vagholi, and debouches at that of Vatari”. It is the earliest printed mention the notes found; the Gazetteer of 1882 has no Bhuigaon entry. [n40; da Cunha p. 159] The name is probably Marathi bhui, earth, “the land village”, but no source gives it. [n40] There are two gram panchayats, Bhuigaon Khurd and Bhuigaon Budruk, the usual Maratha pairing of lesser and greater. [n40; CRIT items 19–20]

Landscape. CRIT lists the Kumbhar, Khurd, Bhola, Gorlai and Dongri ponds, and notes a “Church – Champavati Mandir Road”, a Hindu temple on the same road as the church. [n40; CRIT items 83–87] A blogger who walked the village in April 2011 found about forty large irrigation wells, bhowkal, kept by the women of the village, watering vegetables, hybrid bananas and flowers and holding monsoon water for paddy. [n11; n40; Mumbai Paused] Bhuigaon beach, west of the village, is a developing tourist spot. [n40]

The church. “Located 2 km southwest of the village of Nirmal, this was the second village after Gass to be separated from the parent parish,” the parish history says. Of its thousand Catholics only a handful went to services at Nirmal. Father, later Monsignor, Raymond Mendez began catechism classes; a simple church was built in 1946 on land given by the poor villagers, with money from the Archbishop of Bombay and the parishes of Bandra. Bhuigaon became a parish on 2 June 1946, with Father Acuico Dourado as its first priest. A single-storeyed church took seven years to build from 1948, and Father Nazareth Garbu completed the present church in 1984. Its dedication is the Holy Family. [n40; n11; EI Bhuigaon]

The Kuparis of Bhuigaon. Bhuigaon is one of the thirteen villages on the community’s own Kupari list. [n40] The 2011 walk gives the fullest portrait of Kupari village life in any source the notes reached, and it should be read as one blogger’s report of what villagers told him. The Samvedi Christians of Bhuigaon called themselves “Samvedi”, after the Sama Veda. They spoke Kadodi, or Kupari, which they said was “different from the dialect of the neighbouring Vadval community”. The extended Marti family were the village’s “territorial protectors”. A stainless-steel cross in the western quarter, the Madikar Cross, served as “the local equivalent of the Gaondevi”, the village goddess, marking the Marti family’s ground, and the houses carried crosses at the front door. The women kept the wells. A family tradition traced its descent to entertainers from Orissa; the blogger did not verify it, and one note treats it as a late community legend, though it echoes the Samvedi Brahmans’ own tradition of an origin in Odisha. [n11; n40; n34; n08] The blogger predicted that urban sprawl would swallow the wells within ten years; whether it has, no later source records. [n40]

Bhuigaon’s son of note is Thomas Dabre, born there on 23 October 1945, ordained priest in 1971, auxiliary bishop of Bombay in 1990, first Bishop of Vasai from 1998 (installed at Papdi on 15 August 1998), and Bishop of Poona from 2009 to 2023. [n40; n39; Catholic-Hierarchy] No source states his community; the link to the Kuparis is through his birthplace. [n34] His surname, Dabre, is a Marathi one, which the notes could not explain. [n39]

Census and today. No separate 2011 figure; inside the corporation. [n40] No 2026 source.

Gomes Ali, with Chowkala, Vanda, Rambai and Darseng

The parish. Gomes Ali is the newest Kupari parish. The five hamlets of Gomes Ali, Chowkala, Vanda, Rambai and Darseng formed a section of Nirmal parish. Mass was first said for them on 2 September 1979 by Father Edwin Colaco, then vicar of Nirmal, in the primary local board school at Chowkala. Don Bosco Sisters came on 31 January 1992; a committee of 72 “servant-leaders” was formed in 2001; Bishop Thomas Dabre blessed the foundation stone on 2 December 2001; the church and parish house were blessed on Trinity Sunday, 25 May 2002; and the parish of the Most Holy Trinity was established on 5 June 2004, with Father Johnson Menezes as first priest. [n40; EI Nirmal]

The name. Ali is Marathi for a lane, and Gomes a Portuguese surname; the name looks like a hamlet named for a landholding family, but no source says so. The surname Gomes is attested among Bassein converts: it is one of the Christian names the Urap Agris kept after their return to Hinduism in 1820 and 1828. [n40; n39; Gaz XIII-I p. 116]

The Kuparis of Gomes Ali. Gomes Ali is on the community’s Kupari list, and its parish history is a Kupari story of the late twentieth century: five hamlets, too far from their mother church, organising themselves into a parish over twenty-five years, from a mass in a school room in 1979 to a church of their own in 2002. Nothing older is recorded. [n40]

Gass (Ghas, Gas)

Gass has its own chapter, K7, and is only summarised here. In 1882 boats of ten or twelve tons came up the creek to the Gas landing, about a mile from Sopara, and Gas shipped molasses, rice and betel leaf to Surat. The people of Gas told the Gazetteer that their village was “the site of old Sopara”. In the field called Sonarbhat, “gold field”, lay a broken female figure, perhaps Ambika, the remains of what was said to be a Jain temple of Parasnath, and the tomb of a Muslim saint edged with very large old bricks; by the lake stood the foundations of an eleventh- or twelfth-century temple. In front of the house of Degu Pobria Mhatara a six-sided basalt block, used for washing clothes and grinding curry powder, carried a word in letters of about 200 BC, “Badhuya”, “of Badhu”; at the house of the Samvedi Brahman Bab Naik another block named “Kalavad the Kod” and “Ugradeva”. [n11; n40; Gaz XIV pp. 315–316, 341] Gass is a Kupari village. It petitioned for a parish in 1941, and the parish of St Gonsalo Garcia was formed from Nirmal in 1942, with Father Thomas as first priest; its church was blessed on 8 March 1962 by Cardinal Gracias, and it keeps a week-long feast of the saint in February. [n40; n09; EI Ghass] See Chapter 26.

Nandakhal (Espirito Santo)

Nandakhal lies north of Nirmal, on the Virar side, and Chapter 31 tells its story. In brief: its church of the Holy Spirit, said to have been built by the Franciscans about 1573 “for the convenience of those parishioners of Holy Cross who lived closer to Nandakhal”, is the only church in the diocese dedicated to the Holy Spirit, and it escaped destruction in 1739; a parish legend says Maratha soldiers who stormed it retreated before a swarm of bees. Da Cunha called it the oldest of the rural churches and “the only one standing in its primitive state”. In December 2016 it had 2,150 families and 7,339 people in 29 hamlets, and it had sent 68 priests. It is the mother of Umrale, Nanbhat, Bolinj and Rajodi, and the home parish of Father Francis D’Britto of Jeladi. [n40; n09; da Cunha p. 161] It is the largest Kupari parish.

Wagholi and Mardes

Two more Kupari villages of the old Sopara garden lie west and north of Gass, on the Nallasopara side. Wagholi (Vagholi) was a landing on its own creek, noticed by Anquetil du Perron in 1760; in 1882 small boats came “from the west as near as Vagholi”, and it shipped molasses, rice and betel to Surat. An inscribed stone of fourteen illegible lines from a Vagholi well went to the Collector’s garden at Thana. The Tombo splits it into two leases, “Vagoly” and “Vastal Vagoly”. [n11; n40; Gaz XIV pp. 315–316, 322, 342] Mardes (Mardesh, Madres), “in the centre” of the Sopara garden, is the village of the Ashokan stupa of the third century BC; at the Madres Talav, CRIT says, local Catholics gather around the cross at Christmas. Its Tombo lease was “Mardes Sirgoly”, held by Lucas de Almeida de Andrade. [n40; Gaz XIV p. 314] Both were among the nine villages that dried the famous Bassein plantains in 1882. [n40; Gaz XIII-I p. 292] A Wikipedia table gives them a joint parish of Our Lady of Velankanni, split from Nirmal; its date and dedication are unverified. [n40] Beyond the plantain and parish records, the notes hold nothing on either village’s Kuparis.

Part Three: The shore and the creek

West and south of the garden belt lie the beach villages, and along the north bank of the Vasai creek the villages of the salt pans and the fishing harbours. These are the places the record serves worst. For several of them the only facts are a census line and a heritage-survey pond.

Holi

Holi is a living village of Vasai West, but in the written record it is almost nothing. The 1882 Gazetteer mentions Holi only as the festival. [n40] A comment on a Wikipedia talk page says Holi is a residential area of Bhandaris, the palm-tappers; that is a weak source. [n40] The Tombo of 1727–30 has a lease in the Taná group, “Sedacer, Choly, Calamba, Nagão e Varchory”, which one note suggests might include Holi (Choly), Kalamb (Calamba) and Naigaon (Nagão); the identification is unverified. [n40] No church, temple, census figure or story for Holi is recorded in the notes, and no Kupari or Vadval presence. [n40; n11]

If Holi was indeed a Bhandari settlement, its people belong to the community the Gazetteer drew so vividly: tappers who sang while they climbed, wore scarlet waistcoats at work, carried heavy tapping knives on their left thighs, worshipped Cheda with goats and fowl, and turned to farming and labour when the palm-tree cess rose in 1877. Among them were the Kirpals, “once Christians”, who, by the census of 1911, went back to Hinduism soon after 1739. [n08; n34; Gaz XIII-I pp. 149–150] That is context, not a record of Holi itself.

Kalamb

Kalamb is a beach village on the coast north-west of Nirmal. In 2011 it had 1,169 houses and 5,329 people, of whom 604 were of Scheduled Tribes and 46 of Scheduled Castes, with a literacy rate of 87.20 per cent and 2,636 workers. [n11; n40] It is not the same as Kalambhon, an inland village on the Tansa with a Shilahara inscription. [n11] Tourism listings name its beach among Vasai’s main beaches with Rangaon, Suruchi, Rajodi and Arnala. [n11] Its history is not recorded beyond that; the Tombo’s “Calamba” may be it. [n40] Kalamb is described with the Virar villages in Chapter 31.

Rangaon

Rangaon lies on the coast west of Vasai town. In 2011 it had 875 houses and 4,166 people, 399 of Scheduled Tribes and 269 of Scheduled Castes, with 85.19 per cent literacy and 2,080 workers. [n11; n40] The heritage survey, without a source, lists Rangaon with Agashi and Papdi as “chief ship-building centres” before the Portuguese. It records the Rangaon Talav, an eighteenth-century Hanuman Mandir, the Devali Talav and a Zilla Parishad school founded in 1917, in the gram panchayat of Kaular Budruk. [n40; CRIT Vol I p. 6, items 93–96] Its beach is one of the listed beaches of Vasai. [n11] No Marathi or English source the research reached says anything of its fishing, its families or its festivals. [n08; n20] No Kupari presence is recorded.

Tarkhad and Khochivade

Two small coastal villages have census rows and nothing else. Tarkhad had 283 houses and 1,094 people in 2011, 24 of Scheduled Tribes, and a literacy rate of 94.76 per cent, the highest of the rural villages of this part of the coast. Khochivade had 476 houses and 2,219 people, 452 of Scheduled Tribes, with 89.46 per cent literacy. [n11; n40] Neither appears in the Gazetteer, the Tombo, the heritage survey or any parish page the research reached. [n40] Their community make-up is not recorded.

The beaches: Vasai beach and Suruchi

The Palghar district website describes “Vasai Beach” at the mouth of the Ulhas on the southern border, calling it a “Mini Goa” of clean soft sand and few crowds, and Suruchi beach, which takes its name from its suru, casuarina, trees, about 7.6 kilometres from Vasai Road station. [n39; n11] No history of either is recorded.

Naigaon

Place and name. Naigaon is the southernmost settlement of the taluka, on the north bank of the Vasai creek opposite Ghodbandar, which the Gazetteer of 1882 called “a small village and port in Salsette”. [n12] Naigaon station, on the Western line between Bhayandar and Vasai Road, was not in the station list of 1882; it opened later, in a year the notes could not find. [n28; n12] The Tombo’s “Nagão” may be Naigaon; unverified. [n40] A Shilahara grant found at Sandor names a “head-quarter station Nagapur”, but the Gazetteer thought that meant Nagaon in Alibag, and no connection with Naigaon should be claimed. [n37]

Communities. Naigaon’s make-up is not documented in the sources reached; it is described informally as a fishing and salt-pan village of Kolis and East Indian Christians, which the notes could not confirm. [n12] Wikipedia names Naigaon as one of the places where the Vadvali (Phudagi) dialect is spoken. [n08; n41] The Marathi daily Sakal in January 2023 listed Naigaon, with Arnala and Pachu Bandar, among the main fishing harbours of Palghar district. [n20] CRIT lists a Naigaon gram panchayat and Naigaon Bazaar. [n40]

Church. A church of the Mother of God at Naigaon is listed in a directory; one note identifies it with the Pali church, which stands on the Papdy–Naigaon station road. [n11; n40] The chapel of the Immaculate Conception at Umela, “Umela (Naigaon)”, is described below.

The creek crossings. The creek here has been bridged three times. The railway crossed by Panju island from 1864 (Chapter 17). The first road bridge on the Mumbai–Ahmedabad highway was built in 1968 (one weak source says 1928); as it weakened, the National Highways Authority began a second four-lane bridge alongside in 2018, at over Rs 247 crore, and its 918-metre span opened on 27 March 2023. [n12; n28] A double-decker bridge has been proposed. [n12]

Stories. In the 1940s the freedom fighter Augustine Koli of Uttan (1921–2022) worked underground at Dahisar, Borivali and on Panju island off Naigaon. [n16; n25] In 2020 the first suspected COVID-19 death within the corporation’s limits was a 42-year-old resident of Naigaon. [n29]

Census and today. Naigaon East and West have their own postal codes but no separate 2011 census figure; they are inside the corporation. [n12] No Kupari presence is recorded.

Panju

Panju is an island in the Vasai creek, the island that split the railway bridges of 1864 into a southern crossing of 69 spans and a northern one of 25. Pile-driving trials for the new bridges were held there on 11 September 1923. [n05; n28] In 2011 it was a rural village of 1,358 people. [n12] It was one of Augustine Koli’s places of underground work. [n16] Nothing else about its people is recorded.

Umela (Umele)

First mention. The Tombo of 1727–30 lists “pacaria Umella”, held by Francisco Pereira de Sousa for 157 pardaus. [n40; Tombo fl. 26v] St Michael’s parish at Manickpur once had among its five pacarias one called “Ullmemany”, which may be related; CRIT lists an “Umeleman Talav” near the Manickpur church. [n40]

Communities. Umela’s record shows three peoples. In 1882 it was one of the Bassein villages (“Umelmal”) with Urap Agris, the Christian Agris who went back to Hinduism in 1820 and 1828 with the help of Palshe Brahmans, paying Rs 1,200, and who kept only surnames like Gomes, Soz, Fernan, Frutad and Minez as signs of their Christian past. [n40; n37; Gaz XIII-I pp. 116–117] The Gazetteer gives the cause as the cholera years, when “some of the Christian Kolis, finding that they suffered as much as their Hindu neighbours, took to propitiating the goddess of cholera”; another writer, Vaupell, blamed an attempt “to extend the priestly prerogative”. [n37; Gaz XIII-I p. 203] Umela also had Somvanshi Kshatriya families: the 1935 edition of the Sashtichi Bakhar names the Chaudharis of Umele, Kuker alias Kuk Chaudhari and Lakham Chaudhari, among those who helped the Marathas in the Vasai campaign of 1739. [n40; n41; Naik 1935] And it has Christians: a chapel of the Immaculate Conception was built at Umela in 1974 under Manickpur parish. [n40] CRIT lists the Umele Talav and Umele among the gram panchayats of the plantation zone. [n40]

Census and today. No separate figure; inside the corporation. [n40] No Kupari presence is recorded.

Juchandra

Juchandra lies east of Naigaon, on the edge of the salt flats and the hills. In 1882 it was another Urap Agri village. [n40; Gaz XIII-I p. 117 n.] CRIT records a Zilla Parishad school founded in 1872, one of the older village schools of the taluka, and the Gode and Gandhi ponds. [n40; CRIT items 111–114] Juchandra is a station on the Central Railway’s Vasai Road–Diva line, whose opening date the notes do not give. [n12] Kharbav, on the road beyond toward Bhiwandi, is in Bhiwandi taluka; a creek bridge between Kasarvadavli and Kharbhav was contracted in 2024. [n12] The Gazetteer’s list of Bassein trade centres in 1882 included “Kharbhav”. [n40] Nothing more is recorded of Juchandra’s people, and no Kupari presence.

Sasunavghar (Sassu Navghar)

The port. “About six miles east of Bassein”, the Gazetteer wrote in 1882, Sasunavghar “is a small port”. In the five years to 1878–79 its exports averaged Rs 46,610 a year and its imports Rs 10,070, though exports swung from Rs 1,180 in 1874–75 to Rs 1,08,270 in 1876–77. [n12; n11; Gaz XIV p. 305] It may be the “Navghar” of the Gazetteer’s three Bassein ports, Agashi, Bassein and Navghar, though that identification is not made in the notes; Navghar is also the name of the Manickpur municipal council. [n21; n12]

The paper mill. Sasunavghar has the most curious industrial story in this chapter. “A paper-mill was started here in 1871 by Messrs. Johnson and Littlewood.” Its buildings, its English machinery and a dam across the nearby stream cost Rs 80,000. It began working in 1877 and made paper from grass and rice straw, “but none was ever sold although many dealers had approved of the samples”. The manager died in an accident in 1880 and the mill closed. “The project failed through want of funds to buy new machinery”; it needed another boiler to make a ton of paper a day. [n12; Gaz XIV] Littlewood is the name of the family that leased the interior of Vasai Fort from 1860 for its sugar works (Chapter 17); the notes do not say whether the paper-maker was the same man. Whether anything of the mill survives was not checked. [n12]

Today. CRIT lists Sasunavghar among the gram panchayats of the plantation zone. [n40] It has no separate 2011 figure. Its people are not described in any source; no Kupari presence is recorded.

Part Four: East of the railway and the foothills of Tungar

East of the railway the land changes. The Gazetteer’s visitors of 1882 crossed “a salt waste that flooded and became impassable in the rains” between Manikpur and Sopara, and beyond the line lay rice flats, then the long trap ridge of Tungar, “capped with a layer of iron-clay or laterite”, with magnificent forests on its north and east and bamboo forest on its gentler south and west. [n12; Gaz XIV pp. 366–367] Its people in 1882 were “Kolis and Varlis”. [n10; Gaz XIV pp. 367–368] The Bassein sub-division had 6,099 Varlis in 1872, of the Nihiri section that belonged to south Mahim, Bassein, Jawhar and Vada; “their love for the forests is so great that… they cannot be induced to go so far from their woods”. [n08; Gaz XIII-I pp. 182–183] Today this is the industrial and dormitory belt of Vasai East, pressing against a wildlife sanctuary.

Achole and Rajavali

The visitor of 1882 was sent in the afternoon to “Achola, a mile and a half to the north of Manikpur”, to see “its Portuguese fort”; the road to Tungar “passes between the villages of Achola and Rajavli to Gokirva”. [n12; n40; Gaz XIV pp. 323, 341] Both were in St Michael’s old jurisdiction as “Achola” and “Rajaoli”. [n40] In 1882 Achole was one of the Urap Agri villages. [n40; Gaz XIII-I p. 117 n.] About a mile north of Achole, on the west face of Rakshi Dongar, or Tulinj hill, the Gazetteer admired a basalt dyke whose dark rock “glistens with mica, and when struck rings like an anvil”. [n12] Achole is now part of Nallasopara East, where Achole Road was flooded for five days in 2018. [n29] Rajavali appears in CRIT’s list of gram panchayats. [n40] The Tombo’s “Rajoly” leases may include Rajavali or Rajodi; which is which cannot be fixed. [n40]

Waliv and Sativali

Sativali. In 1882 the path up Tungar “begins to rise” at Satavli, “passing through the Varli hamlet of Dhondvira to the temple of Shri-Tungareshvar, about five miles from the foot of the hill”. [n12; n10; Gaz XIV p. 367] It was “Sattolli” in Manickpur’s Jesuit jurisdiction. [n40]

Waliv. Waliv, with Sativali, is today a dense cluster of small industrial estates: Tara, Samruddhi, Pride on Sativali Main Road, Pinnacle, Pushpraj, Anmol, mostly listed as galas for sale or rent. [n12] When the industry came is not recorded; the 1970s to 1990s is a guess the notes do not confirm. [n12] Palghar district counts one MIDC estate, three cooperative industrial colonies, 5,757 registered small industries and 427 large and medium ones. [n12] In July 2026 a man’s body was found in Waluv pond during the floods. [n29] Both villages appear in CRIT’s gram panchayat list. [n40] The people of these villages before industry, and the workers who came with it, are not described in any source reached.

Kaman and Kamandurg

Kaman. Kaman was one of the ten Bassein trade centres the Gazetteer listed in 1882, with Bassein, Nela, Sopara, Agashi, Bolinj, Manikpur, Virar, Bhatane and Kharbhav. [n40; Gaz XIII-I] Today it has a station, Kaman Road, on the Vasai Road–Diva line, and a gram panchayat in CRIT’s list. [n12; n40] An environmental-clearance summary of 2017 exists for a “Kaman Project”, which the research did not open. [n12] Activists estimate at least 20,000 unauthorised constructions between Kaman and Virar East, on the edge of the sanctuary. [n08; HT 2024] The shrines of Kaman, likely village-goddess or Waghoba shrines of the Koli–Varli belt, are documented only, if at all, in Marathi or oral sources. [n10]

Kamandurg. Above Kaman rises “the finely rounded peak of Kamandurg”, at the south end of the level line of Tungar, as the Gazetteer saw it from Sopara and from Kanheri. [n12] It is about 2,100 feet high, a three-hour trek from Devkundi village, with rock-cut steps and five rock cisterns but no surviving walls. [n12; n39] Trekking sites, low in reliability, say it was built to watch the Ulhas trade, is named in the Mahikavati chronicle under King Bhimsen, was taken by the Portuguese from the Marathas on 12 September 1685 and abandoned for want of water, and was in Maratha hands again in 1737–39. [n12] The Gazetteer of 1882 named Jivdhan and Kamandurg as the two inland forts of the Bassein sub-division. [n12] Kaman point, halfway along the Tungar ridge, “a commanding eminence” over the Bassein river, is a different place. [n12]

Bapane and Chandip

For Bapane and Chandip the notes found nothing: no first mention, no community, no landmark, no census figure. They are missing from the 2011 rural village list, probably because they fall inside the corporation. [n12] Their history is a gap.

Pelhar

“Along the Tansa valley Pelar and Mandvi were once places of importance,” the Gazetteer recorded from W. W. Loch, in a footnote that also mentions the reservoir and old bricks at Bhatane. [n12; Gaz XIV, Sopara fn.] The Tansa valley route between the Vaitarna and the Ulhas was, in the Gazetteer’s view, one of the reasons Sopara became a trade centre; so Pelhar may once have stood on Sopara’s road inland. That rests on a single footnote. [n12] Today Pelhar is known for its dam, whose old scheme supplied about 14 million litres a day to the towns, beside Usgaon’s 24.5 and, since 2023, the Surya scheme’s much larger supply. [n12] The Pollution Control Board has an action plan for the polluted stretch of the Pelhar river. [n12] Western Railway has blamed flooding on the tracks partly on overflow from the Pelhar dam. [n06] Pelhar appears in CRIT’s list of gram panchayats. [n40] No source describes its people or shrines; the village lies in the Koli–Varli belt of the Gazetteer. [n10]

Tungareshwar and its foothills: Parol, Majivali, Bhatane, Tivri, Chinchoti

The hill and its god. Tungar, about 2,200 feet high and about ten miles from the sea, is “perhaps the Dunga of Ptolemy”, the Gazetteer suggested. Its mahatmya says Parashurama drove the demons led by Vimal into the sea; Vimal alone settled on Tungar, won Shiva’s favour by penance, and received immortality, “a holy pool and a lingam”. [n12; Gaz XIV p. 366 n.] It is the same Vimal whose defeat gave Nirmal its name. The temple, four square buildings in a small valley, was “rebuilt about a hundred and thirty years ago [c. 1750] by Shankarji Keshav”, the Peshwa granted it about three square miles of land, and the British continued the grant. Stones carved with the sun, the moon and human figures lay in the nearby stream. [n12; n10; Gaz XIV p. 367] Jain cells on the Vaitarna point were “supposed to have been cut about… 1234”. [n10] Today the temple complex, about three or four kilometres from the sanctuary gate, includes Kal Bhairav, Jagmata and Khodiyar Mata shrines, a Ram Kund and the math of Balyogi Sadanand Maharaj; a bhandara is held at Mahashivratri and on the Mondays of Shravan. [n10] In an NGT case of 2024 the forest department said the Balyogi ashram had encroached on sanctuary land; a Supreme Court order of 2019 had already led to the demolition of an illegal ashram structure. [n08; n39]

The hill station that never was. In 1869 the summit was surveyed for a hill station, “fifty-three house sites were marked off”, and about thirteen miles of foot and bridle paths were cleared; Mr T. C. Hope built a road “on a gradient better than the Matheran ascent”. Tungar, the Gazetteer hoped, might serve “as a sanitarium for poor families, who cannot avail themselves of the expensive accommodation of Matheran; and for the large colony of Europeans employed by the Baroda and Peninsula Railway Companies”. Its viewpoints were named Vaitarna point, Kaman point, Bellevue and Panorama. Nothing was built. [n12; Gaz XIV pp. 367–368]

The sanctuary. Tungareshwar Wildlife Sanctuary was notified on 24 October 2003 (another source says November), covering 85.7 square kilometres, a corridor between Sanjay Gandhi National Park and Tansa. It holds about 600 plant species, more than 250 bird species and 150 butterflies; a camera-trap study found five leopards. [n12; n39] Warli families live on its slopes. [n10; n08]

Parol and Majivali. “A village on the east base of Tungar hill, about eight miles east of Virar station”, Parol in 1882 had the remains of two old Hindu temples, and neighbouring Majavli (the census’s Majivali) two more, none in worship. The Parol temples were named after pools in the stream: Mota Tankya, the Big Pool, and Lahan Tankya, the Little Pool. The Big Pool temple had a Ganpati on its lintel and a tree rooted in its roof that “threatens to ruin it”. The larger Majavli temple kept only its plinth, a fragment of a lotus ceiling, a goddess flanked by elephants whose trunks arched over her head, and pillar capitals with “indecent carvings”; the stones of the other had been built into “a Portuguese granary or store-house”. [n12; Gaz XIV] In 2011 Parol had 2,153 people and Majivali 1,194. [n12]

Bhatane. “At Bhatana, a landing station to the north of Tungar, are a reservoir of dressed stones and quantities of old bricks.” [n12] Bhatane was a Bassein trade centre in 1882. [n40] It had 4,786 people in 2011. [n12]

Tivri and Chinchoti. Tivri (Tivari), a rural village, had 388 houses and 1,597 people in 2011, with 83.94 per cent literacy. [n12; n40] Near it, the monsoon waterfall at Chinchoti, about fourteen kilometres from Vasai station in the Tungareshwar forest, draws picnic crowds and takes lives: one death and more than a hundred stranded in heavy rain in 2018; three deaths in twenty-four hours in July 2023; two college students from Goregaon drowned in July 2025 despite restrictions, and an eighteen-year-old in another case. The authorities have closed the falls from time to time. [n12; n39] The Chinchoti–Anjur Phata road is the road approach to Kaman. [n12]

Communities of the foothills. The Adivasis of these slopes, Varlis, Katkaris and Thakurs, are described in Chapter 31. Their recent history here is of pressure: on the forest from building, and on the people from the old debt bondage the Gazetteer already glimpsed in 1882, when “Varlis who had pledged their land” worked others’ fields in the rains. [n08] The Shramjeevi Sanghatana, founded in 1982 by Vivek Pandit, who began his work in Dahisar village in Vasai, has led the struggle against bonded labour, especially among Katkaris; Pandit was independent MLA for Vasai from 2009 to 2014. [n08] A proposed water dam for the city would submerge eleven Adivasi hamlets. [n08] No source the notes reached names the individual padas of Kaman, Pelhar, Bhatane or Chandip. [n08] No Kupari presence is recorded in the foothill villages.

The villages at work, 1535–2026

The entries above give each village’s work where the record names it. Taken together, the sources show how the whole belt earned its living over five centuries, and the story applies to nearly every village in this chapter.

Under the Portuguese

The first list of the villages’ work is a tax list. In 1535 the Portuguese collected the first half-year’s revenue of the Bassein district, 158,475 fedeas, from taxes on coconut oil, opium, cotton, toddy, vegetables, fish, sugarcane and betel, and on the trades of butchers, dyers, Koli fishermen and shepherds. [n36; da Cunha pp. 158–159] The land itself belonged to the Crown and was granted out. By the Tombo of 1727–30 the villages of this chapter were leased to Portuguese fidalgos, widows and the Jesuits: Giriz to Dona Margarida Teles de Meneses, Sandor to Jorge da Silva Coelho, Pali to Dona Maria Correia de Macedo, Umela to Francisco Pereira de Sousa, Chulne to the Jesuits, Mulgaon perhaps to Fernão de Brito de Melo. [n40; n39] The grants were nominally for three lives, and “no right in the land was conceded to the husbandmen or tenants”. [n37; Gaz XIII-II p. 460] The people who actually tilled the gardens, Christian and Hindu, appear in the register only once by name, in Jasaman Naik’s garden of wild palms at Giriz. [n40] The toddy rents of Baçaim, the urracas, were held by Francisco Pereira Pinto; the coloria, the fishing or boat rents of Agashi and Arnala island, by Dona Domingas Pereira de Azevedo. [n39] Even the palm-tappers and fishermen worked under a fidalgo’s lease.

After 1739: the garden belt

The Marathas offered rent-free land to bring Hindu settlers back, and the gardens recovered. [n32; Gaz XIV p. 34] The British survey of 1858–62 settled the Bassein villages in four classes, with garden land assessed at up to Rs 8 an acre, far above rice land; the revenue officer Williamson had cut garden rates in 1836–37, finding gardens abandoned through over-assessment and lack of a market. [n21; n28; Gaz XIII-II pp. 609–611] By 1880–81 the sub-division had 29,687 acres of rice, 1,188 acres of sugarcane and 1,297 acres of fruit, vegetables and other garden crops, watered from 191 ponds and 2,624 wells. [n21; Gaz XIII-II pp. 678–680] The ponds of the heritage survey and the wells of Bhuigaon are the remains of that system.

The crops of 1882 were sugarcane, about 750,000 canes a year sent to Bombay, Surat and Broach, manured with oil-cake from Gujarat; raw sugar and molasses, made from February to June “by Pachkalshis, Malis, Native Christians and Samvedi Brahmans”; betel nut, sorted by the Vanis of Papdi; coconuts; and plantains, of nine varieties, of which basrai was the most sold and rajeli the one dried into the famous Bassein dried plantains. [n21; Gaz XIII-I pp. 291–300, 390] The drying was done only in nine villages of the Kupari belt, among them Vagholi, Mardes, Umrale, Rajodi and Nala, and at Agashi, Vatar and Koprad by “about eighty-five families of Samvedi Brahmans, fifteen families of Pachkalshis, and about seventy-five families of Native Christians originally Samvedis and Pachkalshis”. [n21; n40; Gaz XIII-I pp. 292, 395] So in 1882 Hindu and Christian Samvedis, the latter the Kuparis’ forebears, did the same work side by side in the same villages. The Vadvals of the southern villages, meanwhile, carted timber from the forests of Vada and Jawhar. [n41] The creek landings of Gass, Vagholi and Bolinj shipped molasses, rice and betel to Surat, Broach, Jambusar and Gogha, until the silting creek and the railway turned the trade to the stations. [n11; n37]

The twentieth century: kawadis, betel and bananas

The railway carried the gardens’ produce to Bombay. Within living memory, the Vasai farmer-vendors, the kawadis, called “Vasaiwale mama” and “maushi”, came in on the 4 pm train to Victoria Terminus with shoulder-poles of garlic chives, kantola, banana flower, beans, okra and colocasia leaves, and Vasai’s sweet yellow bananas, used for offerings and shikran, and sold them at Bora Bazaar and Dadar to the cry “baaju-baaju Vasaiwale aale”. By 2015 a food writer found that they had “disappeared”: their children had taken office jobs, contractors sold to supermarkets, and vegetables came from Nashik and Pune. [n05; n08; India Food Network 2015] About thirty-five years before 2023, the farmers grew betel leaf, exported to Pakistan until relations soured, and velchi, miniature bananas, until blight struck them and tissue-culture bananas replaced them. [n11; 101Reporters 2023] The Kupari community’s own accounts say farming dominated Kupari life until the late 1960s and early 1970s. [n34; n11]

Flowers and the end of the fields

The last crop of the garden belt is flowers. Mogra came first, then sonchafa, which Moreshwar Bhoir of Arnala introduced in the late 1980s from a nursery at Kudal. In 2023 about 500 flower-growing families in 25 villages of the corporation area, within about eighteen kilometres from the Arnala coast to Vasai, sent sonchafa to the Dadar market at Rs 150 for a box of a hundred flowers, rising to Rs 700 at Ganesh Chaturthi; the growers still recharged their fields from the old bawkhal ponds. [n11; 101Reporters 2023] In August 1988 the state turned the green belt over to housing, and growth after that came almost wholly from people commuting to Mumbai. [n05; n29] In the 1990s, builders’ groundwater extraction raised salinity and damaged farm produce. [n29] The fields of Sandor, Papdi, Remedy and Merces became suburbs first; the gardens of Nirmal, Bhuigaon and Gass, protected for a time by the Harit Vasai movement and the rule limiting building to a strip along the railway, held out longer. [n29; n05] The salt pans of the creek have their own fight: a proposed growth centre in the regional plan of 2016–2036 covers 1,560 acres that were salt pans until 2016, and about 63,000 local people objected; Father Francis D’Britto called the draft “builder-driven”. [n11; ICSF]

The fishing villages

The fishing villages of the shore, Naigaon, Pachu Bandar, the Koliwada of the fort, followed a different curve. A few decades ago, an ICSF report says, Vasai’s fishers caught many kinds of fish with their traditional dol bag-nets; now Vasai “has become largely a one-fish town”, with purse-seine boats the most immediate threat, creek pollution choking the breeding grounds, and educated young people leaving. [n08] A 2017 state resolution bans LED lights, purse seining and gill-netting by mechanised boats in certain waters; traditional fishers have demanded an inquiry into illegal fishing. [n08] In 2024 the Koliwadas from Cuffe Parade to Zai, Pachu Bandar among them, shut down in protest against the Vadhvan port planned up the coast. [n20] Village-level figures for boats, catch and fishers do not exist in the notes for any village in this chapter. [n21; n08]

Shared ground

Three things recur through these villages, across every community.

The first is the cross at the crossroads. Crosses stand “in almost every gaothan of Vasai”, and at each one’s annual feast the villagers gather to say the rosary. [n09] The Milagres Cross at Remedy, the Madikar Cross at Bhuigaon that stands in for a village goddess, the Furtado family cross at Kirawali, the cross at Koliwada’s St Peter’s church: each marks a village or a family the way a gaondevi shrine marks a Hindu one. [n09; n11]

The second is the pond. Nearly every village in CRIT’s list is entered by its talav: the Relba pond at Sandor where a Shilahara grant was found, the Saloli pond used for Ganesh immersions and fish auctions, the Girij pond with its sixteenth-century steps, the Vimal and Malai ponds around Nirmal’s hill, the Big and Little Pools at Parol. [n40; n12] The garden belt was a landscape of water, and its ponds served every faith.

The third is the reused stone. Temple stones went into Portuguese churches and granaries; church stones, the Gazetteer thought, went back into the temples of 1750; a Devanagari inscription became a doorstep in a Christian house at Bolinj; a basalt block with letters of 200 BC became a curry-grinding stone at Gass. [n11; n40; n12] The villages of Vasai are built, quite literally, out of each other’s past.

What the record does not hold

  • Village census figures after 2001 for every village inside the corporation: Papdi, Sandor, Remedy, Merces, Giriz, Mulgaon, Manickpur, Chulne, Gokhivare, Nirmal, Bhuigaon, Gomes Ali, Holi, Naigaon, Umela, Juchandra, Sasunavghar, Kaman, Pelhar, Bapane, Chandip, Waliv, Sativali, Achole. [n11; n12; n40]
  • Holi, Bapane and Chandip: no history at all. [n40; n12]
  • Tarkhad, Khochivade, Kalamb and Rangaon: census rows and, for Rangaon, a heritage-survey list; no community account, no fishing history, no festivals. [n40; n08]
  • Naigaon’s origin and community make-up, and the opening date of its station. [n12; n28]
  • The Vadval identity of each southern parish, which is stated directly only for Sandor, Chulne, Manickpur, Gokhivare and Burhanpur, and inferred for the rest. [n40]
  • Contemporary documents for the founding of the Portuguese parishes. Every date before 1800 here rests on parish tradition, a heritage survey or a later list. [n40]
  • The individual Adivasi padas of the Tungar foothills, and Warli, Katkari and Thakur village histories. [n08]
  • Kupari life in the southern villages after the 1970s, beyond parish milestones and one blogger’s walk through Bhuigaon. [n34; n40]
  • Any village-level source for 2026. [n40]

From the villages to the city

In 1882 the Gazetteer’s visitor could walk from Manikpur station to Gas, Sopara and the stone dam, and back through Chulna, in an afternoon, through gardens, ponds and sunken lanes, past churches with violin masters and temples with government grants. [n11] A century and a half later most of that route lies inside a city of more than a million people, and the villages survive as names on bus stops, parishes on a diocesan list and ponds choked, a newspaper reported on 15 November 2026, with garbage and water hyacinth. [n06] Yet the parish feasts are still kept, the Nirmal fair still meets below the church, the crosses still stand at the crossroads, and the people of Sandor, Nirmal, Bhuigaon and Chulne still know which village they come from. The next part of the book turns from places to the people who made them.
