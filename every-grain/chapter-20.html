Carts in the night

In the cold season of the early 1880s, on the forest roads that came down from Jawhar and Vada towards the coast, gangs of bullock carts travelled by night. There were five carts in a gang, sometimes thirty. They carried timber: wood the drivers had bought as fallen trees at the Government’s forest sales in Vada, and green wood they had cut in the forests of Jawhar. Their destination was the timber marts of Bassein, which received, the Gazetteer said, the best timber of the whole district. A cart, with its driver, a boy and the pair of bullocks, earned at least twelve annas a day. [n41; n40; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 97–98]

The officers who compiled the Gazetteer of the Bombay Presidency for Thana, published in 1882, wrote down who these carters were. “The traders, Christian cartmen chiefly of Chulna, Manikpur, Gokhivra, Sandor, and Barhanpur in Bassein, are known as Vadvals or gardeners.” They were, the same passage said, “the best axemen in the district, and their carts are larger and their cattle stronger than those found in other sub-divisions.” [n54; n41; Gaz XIII-1 p. 97] Every one of the five villages named is in the south of the Vasai of today: Chulne and Manickpur round Vasai Road station, Gokhivare to the east of it, Sandor north of the old fort, and Barhanpur, which the Gazetteer elsewhere calls Puri, a few hundred yards from the station. [n54; Gaz XIV p. 402]

That sentence holds most of what this chapter is about. The carters were Christians, the descendants of converts of the Portuguese century. They were called by a Hindu caste name, Vadval, which their Hindu cousins still used. They were gardeners at home and carters on the road. Their carts linked the forests of the Adivasi hills to the marts of a port that had once been the capital of the Portuguese north. And the village roads they drove on, from Gokhivare to Manikpur and on to Papdi and Sandor, still run through the parishes, temples, Koli quarters, salt pans and housing estates of Vasai.

This chapter tells all the communities of Vasai town and the southern villages, roughly in the order in which they reached this ground, as far as the record lets that order be known. It begins with the forest people of the eastern hills, then the fishing people of the creek and the shore, then the Brahmin and gardening communities whose traditions place their coming in the age of the Shilaharas and of King Bimb, and the Christians who were made from several of them in the sixteenth century. It goes on to the priests who came with the Marathas in 1739, the salt-makers, palm-tappers and rice farmers, the Muslims, Parsis, Jains and Gujarati traders who came by sea, and last the hundreds of thousands who came by train after 1988. For each it asks the same questions: where they came from and when, and how sure we can be; which villages they lived in; what work they did; what they spoke; what they worshipped, and whether and why some of them changed their faith; what customs the record holds; and how they live now. The second part of the chapter walks through the sacred places and the festivals that belong to the south of the taluka.

How much can be known

The deepest source for nearly every community here is the same as for Virar and Nallasopara: the 1882 Gazetteer, Volume XIII (Thana, Parts I and II) and Volume XIV (Places of Interest), compiled under James M. Campbell from officers’ and informants’ reports and quoting the census of 1872. [n54; n08] For this chapter the Gazetteer was re-read at its passages on southern Bassein, and it yielded things the earlier chapters of this book did not carry: the size of each southern parish’s congregation in 1881, a Kunbi caste found nowhere but Bassein, an 1837 report on a wholly Christian petty division round Manikpur, and the Vani families of Papdi. [n54] Behind the Gazetteer stand R. E. Enthoven’s The Tribes and Castes of Bombay (1920–22), largely compiled from the communities’ own informants and so best read as what each community said of itself; George Grierson’s Linguistic Survey of India, Volume VII (1905); Gerson da Cunha’s Notes on the History and Antiquities of Chaul and Bassein (1876); and the Jesuit historian Ernest Hull’s Bombay Mission-History (1927), which reprints the church lists of the Goa Annuario of 1901. [n54; n35; n41] After them the record thins to parish histories, a heritage survey of 2008 by the Collective Research Initiatives Trust (CRIT), community websites, newspapers and Wikipedia. Where a claim rests on a weaker source, this chapter says so; where the record is empty, it says that too.

Several chapters already cover parts of this ground. Chapter 17 tells Vasai town and the fort; Chapter 21 walks through the villages of Vasai one by one; Chapters 18 and A4 tell Vasai since 1947 and its land, water and livelihood; Chapters 33 to K7 tell the Kuparis from their origins to today, and Chapter 40 tells how each community’s language formed; Chapter 31 tells the same communities on the Virar side. This chapter points to those chapters rather than repeating them, and adds what they leave out: the people of the south, community by community, and their customs. The Kuparis, whose southern villages round Nirmal and Bhuigaon lie inside Vasai West, get a separate and fuller section of their own, as the plan of this book asks; but the other communities are told at the same depth, and the order is the order of arrival, not of favour.

The frame: two counts, 130 years apart

Two sets of numbers frame everything that follows.

The first is a church count. In its entry on Bassein, the 1882 Gazetteer described “six modern Catholic churches in and near Bassein” and gave the congregation of each in 1881: Our Blessed Lady of Mercy at Merces, 974; Our Blessed Lady of Remedies at Dhauli (Remedi), 3,238; St Michael the Archangel at Manikpur, 800; St Thomas the Apostle at Sandore, 1,725; Our Blessed Lady the Mother of God at Pali, 900; and Our Blessed Lady of Grace at Papdi, 1,294. [n54; Gaz XIV pp. 36–37] Added together, those six congregations come to 8,931 people. It is the only near-complete head count of the southern Christian belt before the twentieth century, and it shows Remedi as by far the largest parish, with about 36 per cent of the six. [n54] In 1872 the whole Bassein sub-division, Virar’s side included, had 13,126 Christians. [n34; Gaz XIII-1 p. 71]

The second set is the census of 2011. Vasai taluka then had 1,343,402 people, of whom 1,230,140 were counted as urban and only 113,262 as rural. Scheduled Tribes numbered 98,298 (7.32 per cent) and Scheduled Castes 53,533 (3.98 per cent). Hindus were 78.05 per cent, Muslims 8.45, Christians 8.25, Buddhists 2.53 and Jains 1.75. Marathi was the first language of 49.97 per cent, Hindi of 24.78, Gujarati of 8.60, Bhojpuri 2.92, Urdu 2.52, Marwari 1.64, Malayalam 1.41, Bengali 1.28, Konkani 1.25 and Kannada 0.93. [n54; citypopulation.de; Wikipedia: Vasai taluka] Inside the municipal corporation alone, Marathi fell below half, to 46.49 per cent, while Hindi and Gujarati together reached about 36. [n54; Wikipedia: Vasai-Virar] Between those two sets of numbers lies the whole history of this chapter: a Marathi-speaking coast of gardens, parishes and fishing hamlets that became, in a generation, one of the most mixed cities in India.

No census since 1931 has counted castes, and no census has ever counted Kuparis or Vadvals. Village-by-village religion and caste tables for 2011 could not be opened. [n54; n41] So for every community below, the present is told from parish counts, websites and newspapers, and the reader should take those numbers as what they are.

Part One: The Peoples

I. The people of the hills: Warlis, Katkaris and Thakurs of Kaman, Pelhar and Tungareshwar

Before the record

The Adivasi peoples of the eastern hills have the strongest claim to have been on this ground first, and the weakest record of when they came. The best colonial source says plainly that nobody knows: of the Varlis, as the colonial record spells Warli, Enthoven wrote in 1922 that “regarding their origin nothing is known.” The Katkaris were thought “probably of Bhil origin”, believed to have come from the north out of the Surat country; the Thakurs carried a tradition that Gujarat fugitives took refuge among them in the reign of Mahmud Begada (1459–1511). Grierson found all three speaking Marathi laid over an older, Bhil-like speech. [n35; Enthoven II pp. 171–173; III pp. 376–377, 445–447] Chapter 40 tells how those languages formed, and Chapter 31 tells these peoples on the Virar side. What follows is what the record holds for the south: Tungar, Kamandurg, Kaman, Pelhar and the villages at their feet.

The forest villages of 1882

The Gazetteer’s account of the district’s forests draws the map. East of the Tungar range, it said, the forests “are extensive, the chief being on the slopes of Tungar and Kamandurg, in the Pelhar, Kaman, Chichoti, Poman and Paya villages in the south-east, and in the villages of Nagla and Sasunavghar.” [n54; Gaz XIII-1 p. 33] Those are the villages of the south-eastern taluka: Kaman and Pelhar at the foot of the ridge, Chinchoti (the Gazetteer’s Chichoti) under Tungar, Sasunavghar on the creek. When the forest people of the district gathered in hunting parties to burn and drive the forests, the Gazetteer recorded, they marched “to Asheri in Mahim, and Takmak and Tungar in Bassein.” [n54; Gaz XIII-1 c. p. 188] The two hill forts of the Bassein sub-division were Jivdhan above Virar and Kamandurg above Kaman; the Gazetteer’s general account says such forts were built by Musalmans or Portuguese between 1300 and 1600, repaired by Shivaji, and taken by the British in 1774 or handed over in 1817. [n54; Gaz XIII-1 p. 98] On the Tungar ridge a spot called “Kaman point” looked out over the Bassein river and the fort. [n54; n10; Gaz XIV pp. 367–368]

Who lived on those slopes the Gazetteer said in a few words: the people of Tungar hill were “Kolis and Varlis”, and the path up to the Tungareshwar temple passed the Varli hamlet of Dhondvira. In the stream bed near the temple lay stones carved with the sun, the moon and human figures. [n10; Gaz XIV pp. 367–368]

The Varlis of Bassein

The 1872 census counted 70,015 Varlis in the Thana district: 45,330 in Dahanu, 6,804 in Mahim, 6,099 in Bassein, 4,277 in Shahapur, 2,142 in Vada, 815 in Bhiwandi and 300 in Salsette, of which the Gazetteer added, “there are now no Varlis in Salsette.” [n54; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 182–184] The Bassein figure, which includes the Virar side, was about 8.7 per cent of the district’s Varlis. [n54] Of the three Varli sections, Murdes, Davars and Nihiris, which did not intermarry, the Nihiris “belong to South Mahim, Bassein, Jawhar and Vada”; the Varlis of the Vasai hills were therefore, in both the Gazetteer’s and Enthoven’s accounts, Nihiris. [n54; n08; Gaz XIII-1 p. 183; Enthoven III]

The Gazetteer’s description of them is half observation and half an officer’s judgement. They always spoke Marathi, except those in the far north, who spoke Gujarati, but they clipped their words, kot jás for kothe jātos, “where are you going?”, and said nāngne for “to see”. They were “innocent and harmless, but immoderately fond of liquor”; they depended on the forest; and their “love for the forests is so great that … they cannot be induced to go so far from their woods.” [n54; n08; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 182–184] It also recorded, in passing, the first written sign on this coast of the debt that would define Adivasi life for the next century and a half: Varlis who had pledged their land to moneylenders worked it for others in the rains. [n08; Gaz XIII-1 p. 183]

Thakurs and Katkaris

The Thakurs numbered 55,674 in the district. The Gazetteer divided them into Ka-Thakurs and Ma-Thakurs and called them “small squat”, “truthful, honest, teachable, harmless”, and “more thrifty and more sober than either Varlis or Kathkaris”. Their hamlets stood “always on a level spot” near the forest. They spoke Marathi “with a long drawl”, tilled the uplands with the hand hoe for nachni and rice, gathered firewood and wild roots, and buried their dead. [n54; Gaz XIII-1 p. 178; Enthoven III]

The Katkaris, in the same record, were the poorest of the forest peoples. The Gazetteer called them “drunken, given to thieving”, in the language of its age. They boiled catechu from the heartwood of the khair tree and bartered the whole product at the village shop for beads and cloth; some settled Son Katkaris in Bhiwandi and Karjat cleaned rice, and the Dhor Katkaris would not eat with them. [n54; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 154, 160, 167] The Gazetteer gave no Katkari figure for Bassein. Enthoven recorded a Katkari division called Varap, made up of people who had returned from Christianity or Islam; where those conversions had happened, he did not say. [n35; Enthoven II pp. 171–173]

The shrines of the hill people

No named Waghoba shrine, sacred grove or Adivasi pada (hamlet) of Kaman, Pelhar, Chinchoti or Poman appears in any source reached for this book. [n54; n10] What can be said comes from a regional study. In 2021 Nair, Patil, Surve, Andheria, Linnell and Athreya published a survey of 150 shrines of Waghoba, the tiger or leopard god, across Mumbai Suburban, Thane and Palghar, and found at least one in every Warli village they visited; Vasai is named among the Warli areas surveyed. A typical shrine shows a feline under a sun and moon, carved in stone or teak and smeared with vermilion, often at the entrance to the village; the festival is Waghbaras, on the first day of Diwali, a two-day rite led by a shaman with offerings of coconut, toddy, chickens and goats. [n10; Frontiers in Conservation Science 2021] The sun-and-moon stones the Gazetteer saw in the stream below Tungareshwar match that iconography, but no source makes the identification, and this chapter does not either. [n10]

The CRIT heritage survey of 2008 recorded a Gokhivare pond “near an adivasi pada”, the one place in that survey where an Adivasi hamlet is marked inside the Vadval villages. [n40; CRIT item 122] It gives no name or community.

Bondage, the movement and the forest

No document in the notes records the conversion of the Adivasis of the Vasai hills to any faith. Their modern history is a history of labour, land and forest.

In 1982 Vivek Pandit, born in Dahanu in 1957, and his wife Vidyullata founded the Shramjeevi Sanghatana, which became the main Adivasi movement in the district against bonded labour, above all among the Katkaris. Before founding it, the couple had first worked in Dahisar, a village of Vasai taluka. Pandit won the International Anti-Slavery Award in 1999 and sat as independent MLA for Vasai from 2009 to 2014, with Shiv Sena support. [n08; Wikipedia: Vivek Raghunath Pandit; n52] In 2026 the Sanghatana, led by its president Rambhau Varna, staged silent fasts for the Katkaris, who face bonded labour, child trafficking and the collapse of schooling under seasonal migration to brick kilns; at one Palghar kiln Katkari families refused bayana, the advance that binds a family for a season, and the owner signed an affidavit acknowledging it illegal under the 1976 Act. [n08; Free Press Journal 2026; Newsdrum] The line from the Varlis “who had pledged their land” in 1882 to the bayana of the 2020s runs through the same economy.

The forests the Gazetteer listed have shrunk to a sanctuary. The Tungareshwar Wildlife Sanctuary, about 85 square kilometres east of Vasai and Virar, notified in 2003, is a corridor between the Sanjay Gandhi National Park and Tansa. Activists estimate at least 20,000 illegal constructions between Kaman and Virar East. [n08; HT via PressReader, 28 Apr 2024] On Tungar hill itself the Balyogi Sadanand Maharaj ashram, dating from 1977, was ordered demolished by the Supreme Court in 2019, which on review spared its temple, samadhis and three rooms. [n10] A proposed water dam for Vasai-Virar would submerge eleven Adivasi hamlets. [n08; India Together] None of these sources names which hamlets, or which peoples live in them.

What the numbers do and do not say

In 2011 the taluka returned 98,298 members of Scheduled Tribes, 7.32 per cent of its people. [n54; citypopulation.de] Set against 6,099 Varlis in Bassein in 1872, and with Thakurs and Katkaris not tabulated by taluka in that year, the modern figure reflects both natural growth and a wide stream of Adivasi migrants into the urban area; the census does not say how many belong to the old forest hamlets of Kaman and Pelhar. [n54] The oldest people of southern Vasai therefore appear in the written record three times: in 1882, as the people of a forest and a hill path; in the 1980s, as the people a labour movement was founded to free; and in the 2020s, as the people whose hamlets lie in the way of dams and buildings. Everything between is silence.

II. The people of the shore: the Kolis of Killa Bandar, Pachubandar, Naigaon and the Koliwada

Three kinds of fishermen

The 1882 Gazetteer treated the fishermen of Bassein as three overlapping groups. “The Karadi Kolis in Panvel, the Machhis and Mangelas, also called Divars, in Dahanu, the Uraps and other Christian Kolis in Bassein, and the Thalkars in Salsette, are professional fishermen, but the mass of the sea-fishing population are Son Kolis.” In June and July, when the boats could not go to sea, some took to tillage and most made new ropes, nets and sails. [n54; Gaz XIII-1 p. 55] So the fishing people of the south were Son Kolis, the Hindu sea-fishers; Christian Kolis, among whom the Gazetteer counted the “Uraps”, a word it used for people who had gone back to Hinduism; and, further north, the Mangelas and Vaitis of the Vaitarna mouth, who belong to Chapter 31.

Fishing was licensed and taxed. Before Act XIX of 1844, Kolis, Mangelas and Vaitis paid a poll-tax called dāy-dene, one rupee four annas, for the right to fish. In 1851 the district had 129 salt-water fisheries, twenty-two of them in Bassein, and 101 fresh-water fisheries, eighteen in Bassein; the fresh-water fisheries of Kalyan and Bassein together paid about £13 a year. [n54; Gaz XIII-1 p. 54 n. 2] Those forty Bassein fisheries, salt and fresh, are the only count in the notes of the fishing grounds of this sub-division before the twentieth century.

The Son Kolis

The Gazetteer estimated 8,000 Son Kolis in 1872, “found chiefly along the coast south of the Vaitarna”, which is to say the whole Virar and Vasai shore. It thought them “probably a tribe of Kolis who have mixed with foreign settlers from beyond the sea”. [n54; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 148–149] Their customs are told fully in Chapter 31 and need only an outline here: short, sturdy men with top-knots and moustaches; a Marathi with “so curious an accent” that it was hard to follow; refusal of porpoise, crocodile, whale and a few other fish; a tribal head, the Vagh Patil at Alibag in Kolaba, “whence the Thana Son Kolis say they originally came”; family gods, Khanderao, Bhavani, Bhairav, Babdev, Vir, Kalkai, Cheda and Marubai, kept in the houses of older men; and the wedding rite in which “the bangles intended for the right hand are consecrated and thrown into the sea, the ocean being invoked to take care of the husband.” [n54; n08; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 148–149] Enthoven’s informants placed the Kolis’ learning of Khandoba of Jejuri in the time of the Peshwas, about two centuries before 1920, and dated a migration of hill Kolis to the coast about four hundred years before; his author doubted the second tradition himself. [n35; Enthoven II pp. 259–260]

How the Kolis spoke matters for the south in particular, because their speech is woven into the other dialects of Vasai. Enthoven called the Son Koli home tongue “a patois of Marathi gradually obsolescent under the grinding of the Local Board school and the newspaper”. [n35; Enthoven II pp. 259–260] A modern summary says the dialect of the Vasai Kolis “resembles” Vadvali, the speech of the Vadval gardeners, “closely, with a heavier accent”. [n08; Marathi-Konkani languages (search summary)] And the Bombay City Gazetteer of 1909 said that the home dialect of a Panchkalshi woman of the farming class outside Bombay was “hardly distinguishable from the dialect of a Koli fish-wife, who uses kain for where, mijim for by me, and gehlum for I had gone.” [n41; Bombay City Gazetteer I p. 239] An 1838 paper to the Bombay Geographical Society had already called the common speech of the oldest coastal peoples a “Colee bhasha”, strongest among the Kolis. [n41; Trans. Bom. Geog. Soc. I pp. 128–131] On this coast the fishermen’s speech was not on the margin; it was close to the root.

The Christian Kolis

The Christian Kolis of Bassein were, in the Gazetteer’s words, its professional fishermen. Among the castes from which the Christians of Bassein and Salsette came, “the bulk of them are Bhandaris, Kolis and Kunbis”. [n54; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 55, 200] The record shows how these Christian fishermen kept their own identity inside the Church. In matters of marriage, the Gazetteer said, the lower classes kept to their old caste distinctions: Kolis, Bhandaris, Bhois, Kumbhars, Nhavis and Dhobis formed separate Christian castes, and when Salsette had no suitable bride, they looked for brides as far as Chaul and Daman. [n54; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 200–201] Christian weddings were held between eight and ten in the morning, “except Kolis”, who married in the afternoon with native music. [n34; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 211–214] “Kolis alone have a headman or council” among the Christians. [n34; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 207–210] And among the head-dresses of Christian men, “fishermen wear red broadcloth caps”, the same cap the Son Kolis wore. [n34; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 204–205] The Gazetteer also recorded that a Christa Purana, a life of Christ which it dated to 1659, was “still well known and much read by the people … in the home dialect of the Thana Son or Sea Kolis”. [n34; Gaz XIII-1 p. 207 n. 1]

The census of 1931, writing of the Christian fishing coast round Bassein, gave the clearest official account of how this community got its Portuguese surnames. “The Portuguese parcelled out the countryside among certain of their countrymen … With the advent of the missionary a great deal of mass conversion took place, not unaided by competition among the owners of these estates, and in many villages every tenant in the village was baptized and given the surname of the owner of the village, e.g., Demonte, Pereira, Ferreira, etc.” The same report said the priests admitted that “the Indian Christian population of Bassein and the coastal area around Bombay is more unorthodox than is desirable”, but added: “There is no evidence that this community worships at Christian altars behind which are placed Hindu gods.” [n34; Census 1931 vol. VIII part I pp. 363–364] The report was describing the fishing coast, and its explanation of surnames, which applies to the whole Christian belt, is told for the other communities in Chapter 38.

Not every Christian Koli stayed Christian. In the cholera of 1818–20 and again in 1828, some Christian Kolis “took to propitiating the goddess of cholera, and either left or were driven from the Church”. [n49; Gaz XIII-1 p. 208] Those who left and took the top-knot became the Thalkars, “found in small numbers in Bassein, Thana and Bhiwandi”, descendants of Christian Kolis who in the cholera year sought the protection of Devi, Khandoba and Vithoba. [n49; Gaz XIII-1, Christians chapter] Others, the Gazetteer said, were expelled but did not become Hindus, and “still attend their parish churches at festivals”. [n49; Gaz XIII-1 p. 208 n. 3] The fishing coast of Vasai thus held, in the 1880s, Hindu Kolis, Christian Kolis, and Kolis who had been Christian and were Christian no longer, sharing the same boats, nets and sea.

Modern sources say little more. Wikipedia’s page on Koli Christians lists Bassein and Agashi among their places, gives a figure of 9,000 for 1989 from Narain, and notes that they share the shakarpuda engagement ritual with Hindu Kolis and often marry in church; it does not mention Naigaon, Pachubandar or Killa Bandar. [n54; n08; Wikipedia: Koli Christians] The parish history of Merces, the Augustinian parish between Sandor and Remedi, describes its village as “predominately fisher folk (kolis)”, who in earlier times preferred to attend services at Remedi. [n40; EI Merces]

The landings: Killa Bandar, Pachubandar, Naigaon and the Koliwada

The fishing quarters of southern Vasai are the places this book would most like to describe and can least. There is no source in the notes on Killa Bandar, the landing under the fort, as a Koli village; nothing on its boat-owning families; nothing on the Christian-Hindu division of Naigaon Koliwada; nothing on the jetty at Pachubandar. Searches in Marathi for the Koliwada’s history and for Narali Purnima at Killa Bandar returned only general pages on the fort and on Mumbai’s festivals. [n54; n08] What the record does hold is a handful of facts.

In 1882 Bassein “has a good landing place and a custom house”, and its five-year average trade was £51,414 of exports and £22,520 of imports. [n54; Gaz XIV p. 36] The Gausia Masjid at Koliwada, Vasai, began as a prayer shed; the foundation stone of its present two-storey building was laid in 1982, and it runs an Arabic school with free lodging for about fifty students. [n10; Palghar district: Religious Places] So the Koliwada quarter has a Muslim presence too.

The Catholic parish of the Koliwada is St Peter’s, Vasai West, with a wayside cross in its premises. [n09; Vasai Catholics blog 2015] On 28 June 2025 the Catholic news service Catholic Connect reported that the parish prayer group had run a four-day “Life in the Spirit” seminar for the parish’s fishing community, from 17 to 20 June 2025, at its mass centre in Pachubandar. About 2,100 people attended: more than 900 women and girls on the first two days and nearly 1,200 men on the last two. Bishop Thomas D’Souza presided, and the parish priest was Fr Francis Dabre. The seminar, the report said, is held every monsoon, when the fishermen are ashore repairing their boats and nets. [n54; Catholic Connect, 28 June 2025] It is the only dated trace of Pachubandar’s fishing people in any source reached, and it shows the old rhythm of the coast, the June and July of mending that the Gazetteer described in 1882, still setting the calendar of a parish in 2025. A Herald Goa headline, which could not be opened, says that fisher folk from Vasai camp at Old Goa for the feast of St Francis Xavier. [n54; Herald Goa (headline only)]

At Naigaon, the Syro-Malabar St Joseph’s centre began in a rented school classroom with the address “Naigaon-Koliwada, Naigaon (W)”, and a single directory lists a Walmikeshwar temple at Naigaon Koliwada. [n54; Kalyan diocese page (search summary)] The name recalls the Vaitis’ founder “Valhya Koli”, the robber who in legend became the poet Valmiki, but no source links the temple to that community. A Wikipedia summary once listed Pachu Bandar among Mangela Koli villages; the Mangela Kolis page itself, when opened, did not, and this chapter does not rely on it. [n54; n08]

The fishing year and its decline

The festivals of the fishing year are general to the coast, and no source describes them at Killa Bandar or Pachubandar specifically. Narali Purnima, the full moon of Shravan, marks the end of the monsoon closure and the start of the new season with coconut offerings to the sea; fishing communities in Mumbai, Thane and Palghar keep it. The Bhois of the district in 1882 kept “Narli Purnima … going to the shore, worship the sea”, with Gauri and Shimga as their other great days. Koli Holi, Shimga, can run fifteen days from the day after Mahashivratri, and in Vasai, as in Worli and Colaba, the Holi pyre is built of areca-nut branches. [n08; Jio Institute; News on AIR; FPJ (search summary); Gaz XIII-1 p. 146]

The economy behind those festivals is under strain. Studies summarised in the notes describe Vasai as having become “largely a one-fish town” after decades in which its fishermen caught many species in their traditional dol bag-nets; purse seines are the most immediate threat, creek pollution chokes the breeding grounds, and educated young people are leaving fishing. A 2017 Maharashtra government resolution banned LED lights, purse seining and gill netting by mechanised boats in certain waters, and traditional fishermen have demanded a special investigation into illegal purse-seine and LED fishing. [n08; ICSF; Outlook; FPJ] There are no figures in the notes for boats, landings, or the Hindu and Christian shares of the fishing families of any southern Vasai village. [n08]

III. The Samvedi Brahmins

The gardeners who were Brahmins

The Samvedi Brahmins were, in the 1882 record, a community found nowhere else in India. The 1872 census counted 2,563 of them, 1,323 men and 1,240 women, and the Gazetteer said they were “found only in Bassein and Mahim”. [n08; n49; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 74, 81–82] Enthoven’s whole entry on them, forty years later, was one line: “Samavedis are found only in the Thana district, where they chiefly grow garden crops.” [n35; Enthoven I p. 249] They were Brahmins by name and by the Veda they claimed, the Sama Veda, but by work they were husbandmen and gardeners, not priests, and that combination is the key to their place in Vasai.

The oldest documentary anchor for a Samaveda-following Brahmin presence on this coast is a land grant of 1026 in Salsette naming the Chhandoga shakha of the Sama Veda; it is told in Chapter 05 and does not name the Samvedis as a community. [n35] For the Samvedis’ own origin the record offers only tradition. Modern community pages say they came to Shurparaka from Utkala, Odisha, as temple musicians and singers, that their Vedic learning lapsed under Buddhism and was restored by Adi Shankaracharya at Nirmal, whom they regard as their chief teacher, and that under Raja Pratap Bimb they turned to growing sugarcane and the betel vine, nagvel, adding paddy in the late seventeenth century and flower gardens in the eighteenth as Bombay grew. [n08; Wikipedia: Samvedi; nirajpatilsblog 2008] The Wikipedia article that carries this is tagged for original research, rests on one reference and contradicts itself on dates. [n08] No scholarly source supports the Odisha story. Grierson’s reading of Samvedi speech in 1905, as “a mechanical mixture of Konkan Marathi and Gujarati”, points to a western contact zone, not Odisha; the earlier research for this book flags that as an inference, not a published finding. [n35; n34; LSI VII] The oldest written strata, the Gazetteer and the Bimbakhyan as reported in it, tie the Palshe Brahmins, not the Samvedis, to King Bimb. [n08] Chapter 33 tells the Samvedi origin debate in full.

What the 1882 record shows

The Gazetteer’s portrait is precise. The Samvedis “speak incorrect Marathi”, saying áde for kothe (where), hád for kā (why) and geltu for gelā hotā (had gone); they were “strong, tall, and fair, with regular features … clean, neat, sober, thrifty and orderly”; they lived in one-storeyed houses with walls of wooden planking and tiled roofs and ate rice, pulse and vegetables. The men wore short waistcloths, a shoulder cloth wrapped round the body and “red broadcloth caps like Telugu Brahmans”, and on high days white turbans, coats and silk-bordered waistcloths; the women wore the Maratha bodice and robe and gold ornaments with names of their own, mudaga khudi for the head, lavanga bali, mugdya and gáthe for the ears, putlyáchi mál and vajratik for the neck, tode, vále and phule for the feet. [n08; Gaz XIII-1 p. 82]

Their rites were recorded in detail. Sati was worshipped on the fifth day after a birth, and the child named on the twelfth with a caste dinner. In the thread ceremony, before the age of ten, the boy stood on a raised earthen seat behind a cloth that was pulled away as the drums beat; then he set out on a mock pilgrimage to Benares, and after about fifty paces his maternal uncle stopped him and promised him his daughter in marriage. Girls married before ten; a bride-price of Rs 200 to 1,000 went to the girl’s father; twenty or thirty earthen pots were worshipped the day before the wedding, rice-flour lamps were held over the elders’ heads in a bamboo basket, the groom rode to the bride’s house on horseback, and the couple garlanded each other when the cloth between them was drawn aside. Widows could not remarry. The dead were burned, except children under three, and on the third day food was given to the village Mhar. The Samvedis had no headman and settled disputes by majority vote; “they send their boys to school and are on the whole prosperous.” [n08; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 82–83] Chapter 31 tells these customs at more length for the Samvedis of Agashi, Vatar and Koprad.

The Samvedis of the south

The Gazetteer places the Samvedis it describes in the northern garden villages, at Agashi, Vatar and Koprad on the Virar side and among the husbandmen of Sopara. [n49; n34] In the south of the taluka, the record of Hindu Samvedis is thin. They are documented chiefly through the temple and fair at Nirmal, the lake sanctuary that the community’s tradition makes the seat of its deity, Nirmaleshwar, and of its teacher’s samadhi; the community page says Samvedi musicians play the sanai, choughada, mridanga, bheri and veena there and at the Sureshwar temple and the Bhavani Shankar temple at Agashi. [n54; n08; Wikipedia: Samvedi] The Gazetteer’s own account of Nirmal, which shows the whole temple group to be a public-funded creation of about 1750, is told in Part Two of this chapter. A heritage blog of 2019 on the Nirmal temple names the Brahmin families who served it after 1739 but never uses the word Samvedi. [n54] No village-by-village survey of Hindu Samvedis for Nirmal, Gomes Ali, Holi or Rangaon exists in any source reached. [n54]

The modern picture comes from one opinion essay. In 2019 Shrish Vaze wrote in Swarajya that the villages of Vasai remain “highly segregated socially and communally”, with Catholics, Vadvals and Samvedi Brahmins dominant, and named the Kuparis as “Samavedi Brahmins who converted”. [n54; Swarajya, 6 Mar 2019] It is a polemical piece, and its value here is as evidence that in the twenty-first century the Hindu Samvedis are still seen as one of the landed communities of the old villages. No academic study of Samvedi population or institutions today was found. [n08]

IV. The Kuparis of the southern parishes

Who they are

The Kuparis are the Samvedi Christians of the north-western garden belt, the community to which this book gives a whole Part, Chapters 33 to K7. Most of their villages, Agashi, Nandakhal, Bolinj, Umrale, Vatar and the rest, lie on the Virar side and in Nallasopara, and Chapters 31 and N4 tell them there. But the mother parish of the southern half of their country, Holy Cross at Nirmal, and three of its daughter parishes, Bhuigaon, Gomes Ali and (through the Nallasopara chapter) Gass, lie inside Vasai West, as do Wagholi and the boundary village of Giriz. This section tells the Kuparis of that southern half.

The best period evidence for who they were is a pair of sentences in the 1882 Gazetteer. At Sopara, “the husbandmen … are Christians, many of them converted Samvedi Brahmans; Musalmans …; and Hindus, chiefly Samvedi Brahmans and Bhandaris.” At Agashi, Vatar and Koprad, about seventy-five families of “Native Christians originally Samvedis and Pachkalshis” dried plantains beside about eighty-five Hindu Samvedi families. [n34; n54; Gaz XIV p. 315; XIII-1 p. 395] So the Kuparis were, at least in large part, a Christian branch of the same garden-owning stock as their Hindu Samvedi neighbours, which the Gazetteer saw living beside them in the same villages. The Gazetteer never used the word Kupari. A search of fifteen primary and early sources, from the 1876 da Cunha to the 1931 census, found no “Kupari”, “Kumpari” or “Cupari” in any of them; the Christians of Bassein appear there as “Native Christians”, “Portuguese Christians”, “Firgi” and “Kiristav”. The earliest attestations found are a community blog of 2007 and Wikipedia. [n34] The common derivation, from the Portuguese compadre, godfather or co-father, fits the Portuguese godparent words the Christians used in 1882, but no scholarly source states it. [n34]

The same Gazetteer is careful to say that this is part of the story, not all of it. The Christians of Bassein and Salsette were “chiefly converted Brahmans, Prabhus, Pachkalshis and Kolis”, but “the bulk of them are Bhandaris, Kolis and Kunbis”; and the hostile Goan scholar Gerson da Cunha insisted in 1876 that there was “no descendant of any high-class native” among Bassein’s Catholics, an opinion the modern historian Sidh Mendiratta reads as rooted in the quarrels between Goan and local Catholics. [n34; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 63, 200; da Cunha pp. 250–251; Mendiratta 2017 p. 234] The modern verdict, from Mendiratta and from Kenneth Ballhatchet as quoted by others, is that most converts on this coast were farmers, fishermen and toddy-tappers. [n34] The Kuparis’ claim to Samvedi descent has genuine 1880s support in the northern villages; it describes that community, not the whole Christian population of the coast.

The Gazetteer recorded one other tantalising line. In its chapter on the Christians, a footnote reads: “One recent report mentions a Christian village in Bassein that claims to be older than the Portuguese. No confirmation of this statement has been received.” [n54; n34; Gaz XIII-1 p. 200 n. 7] Which village made the claim, the Gazetteer did not say. The friar Jordanus had found Nestorian Christians and a church of St Thomas at Sopara about 1321, and Chapter 06 tells that story; but the Gazetteer itself concluded that “no trace seems to be left either of the Nestorian or of the Latin converts.” [n40; n34; Gaz XIV p. 322; XIII-1 p. 200]

How and why they became Christians

The conversion is told in Chapter 34, and only its outline is needed here. It took place mostly between 1535 and about 1600, first under the Franciscans and then the Jesuits. Mendiratta writes that between about 1570 and 1620 they converted “a majority of the population” of Salsette and of the Bassein and Agashi subdivisions, and that “whole villages were converted, normally following the conversion of the respective ‘matará’ or headman”. Christian converts who were farmers or fishermen received particular benefits, while those who did not convert had to keep their worship private. [n34; Mendiratta 2017 p. 211] A Jesuit, Gonçalo Rodrigues, wrote frankly from Bassein in 1558 that converts came “commonly the poorest and most needy … more drawn and moved by hope of some interest and remedy of life than by preaching, which they do not understand”, and that the missionaries set “the snares of human things” for “the rustics, farmers and those who live in woods and hedges”. [n34; Documenta Indica IV pp. 98–102] Another, Francisco Cabral, described in 1562 how the Portuguese landlords of Bassein, who had at first opposed conversion for fear of losing their tenants, came to beg the Jesuits to convert their villages. [n34; Documenta Indica V pp. 628–631] The Gazetteer’s own editor judged that “force may have been used in some cases, but the Portuguese accounts seem to show that persuasion and interest were the chief means of conversion.” [n34; Gaz XIII-1 p. 201 n. 2] Mendiratta adds that Goa’s harshest laws against Hindu worship were never enforced in the mainland subdivisions closest to these villages. [n34; Mendiratta p. 211]

Against that stands the memory of force. The Gazetteer recorded that “according to the story of the Uraps and other classes their forefathers were forced to become Christians by having pieces of flesh thrown into their wells”, a story it found in print in 1859. [n34; Gaz XIII-1 p. 201 n. 2] The story is attached in the record to the Uraps, not to the Samvedis. The Swarajya essay of 2019 repeats a well-poisoning tradition for the Kuparis and claims that missionaries made new Christians wear red caps and red saris; the 1882 Gazetteer records red broadcloth caps on Hindu Samvedi men too, so the red cap was a shared regional cap, not a convert’s mark. [n54; n34; Gaz XIII-1 p. 82] Both sides of the argument are in the record; neither has a document from a Kupari village.

Customs in the 1882 record

There is no period ethnography of the Kuparis by name. What there is, is the Gazetteer’s long account of the “Thana Christians”, which contains a few details specific to Bassein, read beside its account of the Hindu Samvedis. [n34]

The most concrete difference is a small one. “In Salsette the parents of the girl propose; in Bassein the proposal comes from the boy’s side.” [n54; n34; Gaz XIII-1 p. 210 n.] That Bassein custom matches the Hindu Samvedi practice of paying a bride-price to the girl’s father, and it is the kind of detail a village interview could still confirm. [n54] The Christians’ child was baptised between the eighth and fifteenth day, carried by its godfather, padrinho, and godmother, madrinha; “the mother never goes to the christening”; and on the third or sixth night the poorer families strewed gram at the door against the spirit Sathi, the same Sati whom the Hindu Samvedis worshipped on the fifth day. [n34; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 82, 210–211] The ornament names mugdya and gathe appear in both the Hindu Samvedi and the Christian lists. [n34; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 82, 205 n. 1] Christian houses were “substantial tile-roofed houses with walls of wooden planks, mud, or brick and stone”, much like the Samvedis’ plank-walled houses; and unlike Hindu families, the whole Christian family, men, women and children, ate together. [n34; Gaz XIII-1 p. 204]

Their religious life had its own Bassein colour. The Jesuits had begun passion plays about the middle of the sixteenth century; a society of penitents was founded in 1551, and Fr Melchior Nunes Barreto brought the practice to Bassein in 1552. In the 1880s the usual form was the priest telling the story of the Passion and then drawing a curtain on a tableau of images, ending with the crucifixion, “carried on with solemnity”. [n54; n34; Gaz XIII-1 p. 208] Each church paid one or two music masters who played the violin; the Gazetteer thought the Christians had “lost their old fondness for singing”, though an 1843 Lisbon source remembered the Thana choristers as famous singers. [n34; Gaz XIII-1 p. 207] First fruits of the rice harvest were blessed at church; and in outlying Bassein villages, the Gazetteer said, secret offerings to Ganpati and Gauri and vows to Shitladevi were “said occasionally” to continue, against the priests’ disapproval. [n34; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 207–210] Wealthy families who were village headmen and owners of rich garden land “often bring up one or more of their sons for the Church”, and a considerable number who knew English worked as clerks in Bombay. [n34; Gaz XIII-1 p. 205] The thread that runs from those sons to the bishops born in Bhuigaon and Gass is told below and in Chapter 25.

Nirmal: the mother parish

Nirmal is the religious centre of the Kupari south, and of the Hindu Samvedis too. On the hill stands the Shankaracharya’s samadhi temple; below it, the church of Holy Cross, Santa Cruz.

The church’s founding year is disputed by three decades. A community blog and a parish directory give 1557 or 1558; the marble plaque on the bell tower says “This Church was built by the Portuguese in 1580”; the CRIT register gives “1586; rebuilt 1856” and also “built by the Franciscan missionaries in 1580”. None is a contemporary document; the 1557 date probably comes from the same list that gives Remedi 1557. [n40] What happened next is better recorded. The plaque continues: “Destroyed during Portuguese-Maratha war in 1739. Rebuilt by the Parishioners in 1856, at the cost of Rs 20,000 and physical labour. From 1739 to 1856 the present Cemetery was pro tempore their Church.” [n40; EI Nirmal] For 117 years, then, the Christians of Nirmal worshipped in their graveyard. The Gazetteer of 1882 gives the 1856 rebuilding “by the parishioners … at a cost of about £2400 (Rs 24,000)”, a church 96 feet by 28 by 26, a congregation of 2,184, a vicar on a British grant of Rs 14-8 a month, and a private school whose teacher played the violin in church. [n40; n34; Gaz XIV p. 293] The CRIT survey noted its stepped approach and an unusual wooden mansard roof with tie beams and no trusses. [n40] The same year that the Portuguese church fell, 1739, the Marathas “purified” the Hindu sanctuary above it; the two stories, of a church in a cemetery and a temple rebuilt from public funds, are the same event told from either side. [n40; Gaz XIV p. 293]

Holy Cross was the mother of the southern Kupari parishes. Gass petitioned for its own church in 1941 and became a parish in 1942; Bhuigaon followed in 1946; Gomes Ali in 2004; and a Wikipedia-derived table says Wagholi and Mardes were also carved from Nirmal. [n40; EI Nirmal, Ghass, Bhuigaon; Bharatpedia “Kupari”] Bishop Edwin Colaco, later Bishop of Amravati, was Vicar of Nirmal in 1979. [n40]

Bhuigaon: the village of wells

Bhuigaon lies about two kilometres south-west of Nirmal. Its earliest printed mention is in da Cunha’s 1876 description of the creeks of the island, where “Bhoegaum” is the village at which the sea enters and runs through Vagholi to Vatar. [n40; da Cunha p. 159] The Gazetteer of 1882 has no entry for it. Today it is two former gram panchayats, Bhuigaon Khurd and Bhuigaon Budruk, the usual Maratha pairing of the small and the large village, with the Kumbhar, Khurd, Bhola, Gorlai and Dongri ponds and a road on which the church and the Champavati temple stand side by side. [n40; CRIT items 19–20, 83–87]

One of the oldest written stones of the whole coast came out of Bhuigaon. On 1 January 1956 N. A. Gore, Librarian of the Bombay Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society, found in the village a broken basalt slab holding a little over half of the ninth Rock Edict of the emperor Ashoka; D. C. Sircar copied it at the Prince of Wales Museum in 1957. [n02; Sircar, Epigraphia Indica 32] Another account says it was found by a boy of Bhuigaon. [n02; Shah 2020] Chapter 04 tells the edicts.

Bhuigaon’s parish history is a twentieth-century story of distance. “Out of the thousand Catholics of the village only a handful attended services at Nirmal.” Fr (later Mgr) Raymond Mendez began catechism classes; a simple church was built in 1946 on land given by the poor villagers, with money from the Archbishop of Bombay and the parishes of Bandra; on 2 June 1946 Bhuigaon became an independent parish under Fr Acuico Dourado. The simple single-storeyed church took seven years from 1948, and Fr Nazareth Garbu completed the present Holy Family church in 1984. [n40; EI Bhuigaon]

A blogger who walked the village in 2011 left the fullest modern description of any Kupari village in the south. About forty large irrigation wells, bhowkals, kept mostly by the women, watered vegetables, hybrid bananas and flowers and held monsoon water for the paddy. The Madikar Cross in the western quarter served as “the local equivalent of the Gaondevi”, the village goddess, and marked the territory of the extended Marti family; crosses stood on the front doors. The speech of the village was Kadodi, the Kupari dialect, which the people said differed from the Vadval dialect of their neighbours; one family told of descent from Orissa entertainers, an unverified oral claim. [n40; n08; Mumbai Paused, April 2011] The same writer noted that Vasai farmers specialised in betel leaf and the miniature velchi banana, and that betel leaf had been exported to Pakistan until relations soured. [n08] His forecast that sprawl would swallow the wells within ten years is a guess, and no later source reports on them.

Thomas Dabre was born in Bhuigaon on 23 October 1945, ordained priest on 31 October 1971, made auxiliary bishop of Bombay in 1990, and appointed the first Bishop of Vasai on 22 May 1998, installed at Papdi on 15 August that year; he was Bishop of Poona from 2009 to 2023. [n40; GCatholic; Catholic-Hierarchy] No source says he is a Kupari; that is an inference from his birthplace. [n34]

Gomes Ali and its cluster

In the north-west of Nirmal’s old parish lie five hamlets, Chowkala, Gomes Ali, Vanda, Rambai and Darseng, which the parish history calls “a cluster of five villages”. Mass was first said for them on 2 September 1979, by Fr Edwin Colaco, in the Primary Local Board School at Chowkala. Don Bosco Sisters came on 31 January 1992; a committee of seventy-two “servant-leaders” was formed in 2001; the foundation stone of a church was blessed on 2 December 2001 by Bishop Thomas Dabre; the church and parochial house were blessed on Trinity Sunday, 25 May 2002; and on 5 June 2004 the cluster became the Parish of the Most Holy Trinity, the newest Kupari parish, under Fr Johnson Menezes. [n40; EI Nirmal] The name Gomes Ali, ali meaning lane, looks like a hamlet named after a family; Gomes is attested as a Bassein convert surname in 1882, among the Urap Agris who had returned to Hinduism, but no source connects the two. [n40; Gaz XIII-1 p. 116]

Wagholi and the creek villages

Wagholi lies on the western creek between Nirmal and Vatar. The Portuguese register of crown estates, the Tombo de Baçaim of 1727–30, split it into two leases, “Vagoly”, held by Manuel de Abreu de Lima, and “Vastal Vagoly”, held by Fernão da Silveira e Meneses for sixty pardaus a year. [n40; Tombo fl. 41v, 45] The French traveller Anquetil du Perron mentioned it in 1760 with a creek of its own. In 1882 it was one of the three landings, with Gass and Bolinj, from which molasses, rice and betel leaf went by boat to Surat, Broach, Jambusar and Gogha, and one of the nine Kupari-belt villages that dried plantains. An inscribed stone from a well in Vagholi, fourteen lines and illegible, was taken to the Collector’s garden at Thana. [n40; Gaz XIV pp. 315–316, 342, 419; XIII-1 p. 292] The mirror table says Wagholi and Mardes form one parish dedicated to Our Lady of Velankanni; the same dedication is given to Rajodi in the same table, so at least one entry is wrong. [n40] A Shani temple stands at Wagholi. [n10; vasaivirar.org]

Holi and Rangaon, on the coast west of Giriz, appear on the Marathi Wikipedia list of Vasai’s villages with Bhuigaon, Gass and Giriz, but no source in the notes ties either to the Kuparis; a Wikipedia talk page calls Holi a Bhandari residential area, which is weak evidence. [n54; n40] Giriz, between Vasai and Nirmal, belongs by its parish history to the Vadval side, and is told there.

The dialect and its revival

The Kuparis’ speech, called Kadodi or simply Kupari, is described either as a dialect between Marathi and Konkani with Portuguese loanwords or as a divergent Konkani dialect with Marathi and Gujarati influence; it is oral, and written in Devanagari when written at all. [n08; Wikipedia: Kadodi language] The research for this book reads it as a Samvedi base with Portuguese church and kinship words, pay and may for father and mother, padron and madron for the godparents, which Grierson recorded in the Christians’ Marathi in 1905, followed by later levelling toward standard Marathi. [n34; LSI VII] In 2024 three scholars, Dabre, Dabre and Pereira, published “Suman”, a Kadodi–Marathi–English dictionary of 949 entries with 942 sentence triples from native speakers. [n34; ACL Anthology 2024] It is the first scholarly work on the dialect found for this book. Chapter 36 tells the language in full.

The Kuparis of the south today

There is no census figure for the Kuparis. Community blogs estimate 40,000 to 60,000 in all, and Ethnologue’s figure of about 60,000 Kadodi speakers is the same kind of estimate; both are uncited. [n34] The hard numbers are larger units: about 110,800 Christians of all kinds in the taluka in 2011, and 117,026 Catholics in the Diocese of Vasai in 2022, in forty-nine parishes and four missions. [n34; Wikipedia: Vasai taluka; GCatholic] The community blogs say farming dominated until the late 1960s and early 1970s, after which people moved into engineering, medicine, banking and information technology, and abroad to the United States, Europe, the Gulf and Australia. [n34] An article of 29 June 2026 on the red cap and the red lugda says both have “largely disappeared from everyday life” under urbanisation, education and migration, though they remain part of the community’s identity; it names no revival group or event. [n34; All About Vasai Virar, 29 Jun 2026] The legal identity under which a Kupari family gets a backward-class certificate is “East Indian”, listed at serial number 341 of Maharashtra’s state list of Other Backward Classes; the Kupari name appears in neither the state order nor the central proposal. [n34]

So in the south, the Kuparis are the people of Nirmal’s church and its daughters, of the wells of Bhuigaon and the five hamlets of Gomes Ali. They kept their villages, their dialect and their parishes through the destruction of 1739, a century of worship in a graveyard and a century of the Maratha and British state, and in the last fifty years they have watched the city reach the edge of their fields.

V. The Vadvals and the Pachkalshis

One people, many names

The Vadvals are the people of the carts in the opening of this chapter, and of the southern parishes round the fort. Their community has more names than any other on this coast, and the names are part of its history. In 1882 the Gazetteer counted 15,367 Pachkalshis in the district, “in strength along the coast”, and said they were “known as Sutars, Malis, Vadvals, Chaukalshis, Somvanshi Kshatris, and Pathares, all of whom except the Chaukalshis eat together and intermarry.” [n41; n08; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 129–130] Enthoven, compiling from the community’s own society, the S. K. Hitechhu Mandali, treated “Somvanshi Kshatriya Pathare” as the tribal name, Panchkalshi as the generic one, and Vadval as the name of a division “particularly found in the Bassein, Mahim and Dahanu talukas of the Thana district formerly known as the Feringana”, meaning “the managers of wadis or orchards, viz., of cocoa-nut and palm groves.” [n41; Enthoven III p. 160] Panchkalshi, from the five earthen pots, kalash, used at their weddings, and Chaukalshi, the four-pot group of Vasai and Palghar, are told in Chapter 31; the community’s claim to Kshatriya rank, its dispute with Brahmin writers and the documents it produced to defend its name are told there and in Chapter 40. [n41]

What matters most for southern Vasai is that the one stock was split by religion. The Hindu Vadvals and Pachkalshis are concentrated inland and to the north, in Virar, Agashi, Umele, Palghar and Dahanu. The Christian Vadvals are the people of Sandor, Manickpur, Chulne, Gokhivare and the parishes round them. The Gazetteer’s use of “Vadval” for Christian cartmen shows the word was a community name for Christians as well as for Hindus. [n41; n40]

Where they came from: tradition and evidence

Every version of the Vadval origin tells of a prince, Bimb or Bhima (Bhimdev), who founded a principality with its capital at Mahim, Mahikavati, and settled followers on the coast. Enthoven’s informants placed his coming at the end of the thirteenth century from Paithan on the Godavari; an alternative they also recorded brought him from Anhilvada Patan in Gujarat. The Bimbakhyan, as Edwardes summarised it in 1902, dated his arrival to Saka 1216, AD 1294, with nine families of Yajurvedi Brahmins and sixty-six other families, twenty-seven of them Somavanshi. [n41; Enthoven III pp. 160–161; Edwardes pp. 22–23] Edwardes himself warned that the dates were inaccurate and the statements “so very contradictory” that they could not be accepted without independent evidence. The Gazetteer doubted the Paithan story and argued for a settlement from Gujarat. [n41; Gaz XIII-1 p. 62] Grierson’s study of Vadval speech, with its Gujarati words and Bhil-like sound changes, supports a Gujarat background. [n41; LSI VII p. 145] The dates in play, AD 1025, 1138, 1194, 1294 and 1495, cannot be reconciled; the community website’s “about 1060 CE” has no source. [n41; n35] No source places the Vadvals’ origin in Magadha or Odisha. [n41]

The split of the Vadvals from the other Panchkalshis is dated by Enthoven’s informants to “some time in the fourteenth century”, and “reputed to be due to the admission of a Koli into the caste”, each side accusing the other. Their right to the Panchkalshi name rested on letters from the Shankaracharya in 1836 and 1852 and on “a resolution of the Palshikar Brahmans of the Bassein taluka in 1855”. [n41; Enthoven III pp. 161–162] That resolution, passed by the priests of Bassein itself, is the one document in the story that belongs squarely to southern Vasai; its text has not been found. Among the Vadvals, unlike the other divisions, the bridegroom’s parents approached the bride’s to ask for her horoscope, which matches the Bassein Christian custom of the proposal coming from the boy’s side. [n41; Enthoven III p. 163]

Conversion and its reasons

The community’s account of its conversion, as Enthoven recorded it, is short and hard: “Under the Portuguese … there was much persecution, and this is said to have forced many members of the caste to fly to the outlying parts of the Southern Konkan and join those who were already settled in the Ashtagar. Many were then converted to Roman Catholicism by force and their descendants may still be traced at Bassein and Salsette.” [n41; Enthoven III p. 161] The Bombay City Gazetteer of 1909 said the same of Panchkalshis on the island: others “were forcibly converted to Christianity and became the ancestors of some native Christian families now resident in Bombay, Bandora and Marole.” [n41; Bombay City Gazetteer I p. 238] These are a community’s memory and a colonial summary of it.

The 1882 Gazetteer, as already quoted, judged persuasion and interest to have been the chief means. It also recorded what the converts became. The Christians of Bassein, “originally Brahmans, Bhandaris, Chavkalshis, Pachkalshis, Kharpatils, and Kolis”, were “the most hardworking and skilful husbandmen … who grow sugarcane and plantains, and have turned the light sandy country about Bassein into an evergreen garden”. The lower classes of Christians “are husbandmen, some of them as the Vadvals among the most skilful cultivators in the district.” “Betel-nut growers are partly Christians, Malis converted by the Portuguese, and partly Hindus of the Chavkalshi or Pachkalshi class.” [n41; n49; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 205–206, 301–302] The Christian Vadvals of the south kept their caste, their crops and their old name. The same caste on the Hindu side, as the next pages show, earned its patilships by fighting against the Portuguese.

The carters and their roads

The Gazetteer’s cartmen passage is the clearest picture of Christian Vadval work in the nineteenth century, and its other chapters show the roads the carts used. A Baroda railway “feeder” road of five miles ran from Gokirva, today’s Gokhivare, to Papdi; a bridge at Manikpur lay “on the Gokirva-Papdi road”; and the Virar-Agashi road was metalled. [n54; Gaz XIII-1 c. pp. 319, 323] The five cart villages, Chulne, Manikpur, Gokhivare, Sandor and Barhanpur, are the ends of that road system: the carts came down from the forests to Gokhivare, crossed by Manikpur, and ran on to Papdi, Sandor and the Bassein marts. [n54] A land sale of 313 acres “in Divanman and Chulne” fetched £150, a sign that Chulne was then cultivated land, not a landing. [n54; Gaz XIII-1 c. p. 282] The Gazetteer’s walking tour of Sopara ended along the “stone dam” of basalt blocks south of Gass, “about a mile and a half of return road to Chulna village, and from Chulna village about two miles east to Manikpur”, which places Chulne on the old causeway between the Kupari garden villages and the Vadval parishes. [n54; Gaz XIV p. 341]

Barhanpur, the fifth cart village, the Gazetteer described separately: “Puri village lies about 500 yards south-west of Manikpur or Bassein Road station. Its full name is said to be Burhanpur, but it is generally called Puri, and it is mentioned as Puri in a Maratha chronicle of the siege of Bassein in 1738.” It found no old remains there. [n54; Gaz XIV p. 402] The Portuguese name of Manickpur itself was Porim or Purim. [n54; n40]

The CRIT survey of 2008 recorded one more trace of the carters in the fabric of Papdi: “Although Papdy was known as a famous shipbuilding centre, the present fabric of Papdy shows palimpsests from the 18th century when it was given as a jagir to the community which transported goods, supplies and food to the Maratha army.” [n40; CRIT Vol II] The survey does not name the community; the earlier research for this book infers that it was the cart-owning Vadvals, and that is an inference only. [n40] The Gazetteer recorded another carting caste in Bassein, the Hindu Baris, “husbandmen, cartmen and labourers” said to have come from Gujarat, who appear in Section VII; the Christian Vadvals were not the only carters of the south. [n54]

No source describes Vadval carts, bullock-cart racing or the timber trade of Chulne and Gokhivare today. [n54]

The petty division of Manikpur, 1837

In 1837 the British revenue officers looked at the Bassein petty division of Manikpur and recorded its people in a sentence: “The people were Christians, hardworking and skilful husbandmen. They were very highly assessed paying cesses besides a very heavy parcel or toka rate.” Rice had sold at about Rs 16 a khandi on the ten-year average to 1836. New rates per bigha of seven, six and five shillings were introduced, a sacrifice from £605 to £396, a cut of 34 per cent. In the same year the Bassein garden lands were found so heavily taxed that a large area had fallen out of tillage, and a reduction of nearly 100 per cent was sanctioned. [n54; Gaz XIII-2, land settlement, 1836–37] The report implies that by the 1830s the whole Manikpur division was Christian and arable. It also shows that the “evergreen garden” of the Gazetteer had, fifty years before, been half abandoned under the weight of the land tax.

The Vadval parishes

The Christian Vadval parishes are told as buildings in Part Two; here they are told as communities.

Sandor is the clearest Christian Vadval village: named among the Gazetteer’s cart villages, and described by its Wikipedia page as a place where “most Sandoris belong to the Vadval sub-ethnic group and speak Vadvali, which the government treats as a Marathi dialect”, a classification the page says is disputed. [n54; n40; Wikipedia: Sandor] Its church of St Thomas the Apostle is called the “Mother Church of all the churches of South Bassein”, the parent of Remedi, Papdi, Pali, Merces and Manickpur between 1574 and 1606, and later of Giriz and Chulne. [n40] Sandor had 8,336 people in 2001. It also serves the neighbouring village of Saloli, whose hamlet Kharbhat a weak source describes as Christian since the founding of St Thomas. [n54; n40] The Sandor parish started a St Vincent de Paul conference in 1971 under Msgr Philip Tavares, and its schools are the Carmelite Convent English High School, St Thomas High School and Notre Dame School. [n54] Inside this Christian village the CRIT survey recorded a Dev Talav and a Hanuman temple, both of the Maratha eighteenth century. [n40]

Manickpur, the Jesuits’ Porim, was a cart village in 1882, and Wikipedia lists its groups as Kunbi, Kumbhar, Vadval and Koli. [n40] Its Christians had gone to Sandor for the Eucharist until 1606, when the Archbishop of Goa, Dom Aleixo de Menezes, made Porim a parish at the Jesuits’ insistence; its church stood “in the centre of the five pacarias of Wananapor, Devamany, Porim, Navgaon and Ullmemany”, with a jurisdiction stretching to Gokhivare, Sativali, Achole and Rajavali. It was “the first to be demolished by the conquering Maratha army in 1739”. [n40; EI Manikpur] The same village appears in the Gazetteer’s list of places where Christian Agris had returned to Hinduism in 1820 and 1828, so in 1882 Manickpur held both Christian Vadvals and Hindu Urap Agris. [n54; Gaz XIII-1 p. 117 n. 1] The village was renamed from Porim to Manickpur, the parish says, early in the twentieth century, though the Gazetteer already used “Manikpur” in 1882. [n40] It returned to the Jesuits in March 1949, and today St Michael’s is the main Sunday Mass for Syro-Malabar Catholics in Vasai, in Marathi. [n40; n54]

Chulne, the Jesuit holding “Cholna Roba” in the 1727–30 Tombo, held by the Jesuits’ Procurador Geral at 130 pardaus, is described by its parish as “the suburban village of Chulne whose inhabitants are practically all Catholics”, about three kilometres from Vasai Road station. Sunday Mass began there on 9 September 1951 under Fr Philip Tavares; the foundation stone of a church was laid on 22 April 1961; the parish of Our Lady of Fatima was canonically established on 1 June 1963, and its church blessed on 17 May 1964. [n40; EI Chulne; Tombo fl. 49] Thomas Francis D’Souza, Bishop of Vasai since December 2024, was born at Chulne on 23 March 1970. [n40; GCatholic]

Gokhivare became a parish because of a vow. In 1914 cholera struck the village severely, and its people promised a church to St Roque, the plague saint. Two hundred villagers built a chapel forty feet by eighteen, to which Archbishop Lima added a sanctuary; it was blessed on 30 June 1930 by the Dean of South Bassein, and in 1943 Gokhivare became a parish under Fr Nubiola SJ. [n40; EI Gokhivare] On the old road from Gokhivare to Tungar the Gazetteer saw a shrine of Mahadev, a pond, the remains of a Portuguese stockade and a ruined tower built against Koli raids from Jawhar. [n54; n10; Gaz XIV p. 367] Cholera, which drove some Christian Kolis out of the Church in 1820 and some Agris back to Hinduism in 1828, here drove a Christian village to build a church a century later.

Papdi, Remedi, Merces, Pali and Giriz are the other parishes of the Christian south. No source names Vadvals at Papdi; its parish history describes its first people, from Davly (Remedi), part of Sandor and a place called Maferegua, as “poor farmers” who “mainly survived on manual labour”. [n40; EI Papdy] Merces was a parish of Koli fisher-farmers. [n40] Giriz, between Vasai and Nirmal, was part of Sandor and Merces parishes until its villagers built their own church in 1917–18, “without foreign aid”, and a photograph of 2021 is captioned “Vadval East Indian house in Giriz”; no source calls Giriz a Kupari village, and the Kupari lists omit it, so it is the boundary village between the two Christian worlds. [n40; East Indian Memory Co. 2021] For Remedi and Pali, the evidence of Vadval identity is indirect, from their place in the southern parish family. [n40]

The Hindu Pachkalshis of the south and the siege of 1739

On the Hindu side, the Pachkalshis took the Maratha part in the war that ended Portuguese rule. “They rendered material help to the Marathas during the siege of Bassein and the acquisition of adjoining country in 1739–40. In consequence of this help the Naik family of Anjore, among others, obtained the title of Sarpatil and received inam lands from the Peshwa’s Court. To the siege of Bassein and other events of the great Maratha-Portuguese struggle are traced back the patelships of many families of the Vadval division.” [n41; Enthoven III p. 161] The Bombay City Gazetteer named the brothers: “As late as 1739 AD two Panchkalshis, named Bugaji and Gangaji Naik, were granted the village of Anjur in inam with the title of Naik Sarpatel.” [n41; Bombay City Gazetteer I p. 238] Anjur is in Bhiwandi taluka, not Vasai. [n40] The 1882 Gazetteer’s footnote dates the help to “the siege of Bassein in 1743”, which is almost certainly a slip for 1739. [n41; Gaz XIII-1 p. 130 n. 1]

A Marathi study of 1935 by Gajanan Govind Naik, a Pathare Kshatriya writer of Anjur, gives the closest look at the southern villages in that war. It lists the helpers of the Maratha campaign by caste, and among the Somvanshi Kshatriyas it names the Chaudhari family of Umele, Kuker alias Kuk Chaudhari and Lakham Chaudhari, who served Gangaji Naik, and the Ambemoda family; among the Vasai helpers it names Rauts, Dhaga Raut, Gan Raut, Laxman Raut and Mayaji Raut. It also lists Brahmans, Pathare Prabhus, Bhandaris, Kolis, Christians and Agris among the helpers. [n41; n40; Naik, Sashtichi Bakhar (1935) pp. 15, 320 ff.] Umele, today’s Umela near Naigaon, thus held Hindu Somvanshi families in 1739; it held Urap Agris in 1882; and in 1974 a chapel of the Immaculate Conception was built there under Manickpur. [n40] The reward of 1739 was patilships and land, not a caste title; it underlies the landed position of the Vadval patil families. [n41]

Gods, weddings and dress

The Hindu Pachkalshis’ household gods in 1882 were an unusual mixture: “images of Cheda and other demon-gods in their houses placed along with brass and stone images of Ganpati, Shiv, and Krishna.” [n41; Gaz XIII-1 p. 130] Cheda was a spirit shared by Agri, Bhandari, Pachkalshi and Son Koli households, a single local god across four communities. [n08] Enthoven, forty years later, did not mention Cheda. He called the community both Shaiva and Vaishnava, devoted to Dattatraya, Ganpati and Vithoba, with Khandoba having “a few votaries”, household goddesses Mahalakshmi, Ekvira, Vajreshwari and Jogeshwari, and Palshikar Brahmans as priests; they believed in astrology and witchcraft “but cannot be described as demon-worshippers”. [n41; Enthoven III pp. 164–165] The poorer Vadvals, the Bombay City Gazetteer said, paid special veneration to Ekvira, to Mahalakshmi of Kolvan near Dahanu and to Vajreshwari near Bhiwandi, and kept the new moon of Chaitra as an important day. [n41; Bombay City Gazetteer I p. 240] Vajreshwari, the goddess whose temple the Maratha governor built after the victory of 1739, is thus a Vadval household goddess, and her temple is told in Part Two.

Their weddings carried no dowry “among any section of this caste”; the bride’s family gave a nose-ring, an ear-ring, five copper pots and a ring or two for the groom; and during the antarpat rite “a sword or dagger piercing the canopy above the heads of the bridal pair” was held up, which Enthoven called a survival of the old Kshatriya custom. Widows could remarry by pat at night under a mango tree. [n41; Enthoven III pp. 162–164] Vadval women at weddings wore “yellow saris of silk or cotton with embroidered borders”. [n41; Bombay City Gazetteer I p. 241] Vadval men in 1909 wore a short waistcloth and a bara bandi waistcoat folded double with twelve fastenings under the arms, and for a head-dress either the Koli cap or a large red turban; the women’s cloth reached the knee and was tucked up behind like a Koli woman’s, with a silver belt, kargota. [n41; Bombay City Gazetteer I pp. 240–241] Of the Christian Vadvals, an East Indian source relayed by Sahapedia says the women wore red saris and the men red caps, and that a village council fined those who shirked their duties at deaths and marriages. [n41; Sahapedia] The red turban of Hindu Vadval men in 1909 shows that red head-cloth was a Vadval trait on both sides of the religious line. [n41] A community tradition says that south Vasai’s music comes from the Vadvals, “more fun-loving” than the conservative Kuparis; it is a tradition, from a community source, and it is the kind of thing two neighbouring communities say of each other. [n41]

Vadvali

The Vadvals’ speech is the best independent evidence of where they came from. Grierson recorded it in 1905: “The Vadvals are one of the sub-tribes of the Pānchkalsī Kunbīs. They are returned to the number of 3,500 from Thana, where they are found in the coast talukas of Bassein, Mahim and Dahanu.” The dialect was “most intimately connected with the usual Marathi of the Central and Northern Konkan”, but with changes like those of the Bhil dialects of Chhota Udaipur: ch became s or h, so that Marathi chakar, chand and char, servant, moon and four, became sakar, sand and sar; and s and sh became an h “probably like the sound of ch in loch”, so that gold, field and hundred were hona, hat and hambhar. Some forms were Gujarati, not Marathi. [n41; LSI VII pp. 145–146] Grierson recorded the parable of the Prodigal Son in Vadvali, which begins eka don por hote, “one man two sons were”. He grouped Vadval, Phudgi and Samvedi as “three small dialects in Thana, which still have preserved many of the characteristic features of Gujarati Bhili”. [n41; LSI VII pp. 146–147] A study in the People’s Linguistic Survey of India (2017) gives further examples, hakar for sakhar (sugar), hanj for sanj (evening), samcha for chamcha (spoon), and says the community is recording and printing its oral folk songs because it fears the language could disappear. [n41; indiandistricts (relaying PLSI 17.2)]

Modern figures are thin: about 2,500 speakers of Phudagi-Vadvali by an Ethnologue count from 2000, in “Dahanu, Naigaon and Thane”; Glottolog lists the language as threatened. [n41; Wikipedia: Phudagi language; Glottolog] Sandor’s people speak it, by their Wikipedia page; the Diocese of Vasai is said to list Wadval among its working languages, which is unverified. [n41; n54] Whether Hindu and Christian Vadvals speak the same variety is not documented. [n41]

The Vadvals today

There is no reliable count. Enthoven’s informants returned 7,916 Vadvals in 1901; a Joshua Project profile of unknown method gives 47,000; Wikipedia’s Palghar page, citing a Maharashtra Times feature, calls the Vadvals the most numerous community in Palghar. [n41] None of these is a census. The Hindu community’s institutions are the Vadval Samaj, which runs directories, matrimonial and blood-donor services online, and the Somvanshi Kshatriya Samaj Sangh, which held its forty-second annual sports festival in recent years. [n41; vadvalsamaj.com]

Two Vartaks are the best-known names of twentieth-century Vasai. Hari Govindrao “Bhausaheb” Vartak (1914–1998) won the Bassein seat for the Congress in 1962, became a minister in Vasantrao Naik’s cabinet, received the Padma Shri in 1991, and founded the Vidyavardhini society in 1970, whose colleges opened at Vasai Road in 1971; Tarabai Vartak (1926–2008) was president of the Thane Zilla Parishad in 1972 and won Vasai for the Congress in 1980. [n41; n52] Wikipedia’s Pachkalshi page lists Bhausaheb Vartak, and also Hitendra Thakur, Vasai’s MLA for most of the years from 1990 to 2024, among community members; their own pages do not state their community, and the Vartak surname is shared with a Chitpavan clan, so this chapter does not assert it. [n41; n52]

VI. The priests: Palshe, Chitpavan, Deshastha and Gujarati Brahmins

The Palshes, the old priests of the coast

Before 1739 the Hindu priesthood of Bassein was Palshe. The Palshes, “or as they call themselves Vajasaneyi Brahmans”, numbered 9,311 in the 1872 census by the Gazetteer’s text (the district Brahman table prints 2,311, a discrepancy the record does not resolve), found in Panvel, Bassein, Mahim, Dahanu, Bhiwandi, Salsette and Kalyan. [n49; n08; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 74, 81] Their name probably came from Palsavli village in Kalyan, which the Bimbakhyan says King Bimb gave to his family priest; they were “generally believed to have come in 1297 from Mungi Paithan … with Bimb”, though the Gazetteer thought they more likely came from Gujarat, since they followed the White, or Gujarat, Yajurveda. [n08; n35; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 61–62, 81] They were priests, physicians and astrologers, and they held “the privilege of fixing the auspicious time for marriage and thread ceremonies for all classes in Salsette, Bassein, Mahim and Dahanu”. [n08; Gaz XIII-1 p. 81] In Bassein they were the priests of the Pachkalshis and the Agris, and they married the Mangelas at sunset. [n54; n08; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 80, 147]

The Palshes of Bassein appear three times in this chapter at turning points. It was a Palshe, Ramchandra Baba Joshi, who in 1820 readmitted a group of Christian Agris of Bassein to Hinduism, for a fee of Rs 1,200, and was temporarily put out of caste for it; and another Palshe of Bassein, Vithal Hari Naik Vaidya, who readmitted a second group on 12 November 1828. [n54; n08; Gaz XIII-1 p. 117] It was the Palshikar Brahmans of the Bassein taluka who, in 1855, passed the resolution supporting the Vadvals’ right to the Panchkalshi name. [n41; Enthoven III p. 162] And it was the Palshes who lost most when the Marathas came.

1740: the newcomers

“Of Maratha power”, the Gazetteer wrote of Bassein, “the chief relics are priestly Brahmans of the Konkanasth and Deshasth classes.” After the conquest, Chitpavan and Deshastha Brahmins “settled as priests, and to a great extent ousted the Palshis and other older classes of priests”. [n35; Gaz XIII-1 p. 63] The insulting folk etymology of the Palshes’ name as palā-āshi, flesh-eater, was in the Gazetteer’s view “probably the work of their rivals the Deccan and Chitpávan Bráhmans at whose hands the Palshes have suffered much since the Maratha conquest of western Thana (1740).” [n08; Gaz XIII-1 p. 81 n.]

The Gazetteer gave one family’s story as an example. Two Kudale Brahmans migrated to the north Konkan about 1728; one settled at Thana and was made a Sardar; the other, Yeshwantrao, settled in Bassein, and his son became the Atrekar, a clerk (karkun), of Bassein fort. [n35; Gaz XIII-1 p. 63 n. 2] The Pandharpeshas, a privileged class of landholding Brahmans and Prabhus, were in the Gazetteer’s words relics of Maratha rule. [n35]

Where these newcomers lived is shown by one village. In 1876 da Cunha described Papari, today’s Papdi, as “a village about a cannon-shot from Bajipura [Bassein], chiefly inhabited by Brahmans of Chitpawan, Karada, and Deshastha divisions, as well as Palshas, Sonars, and other inferior castes of the Hindus”, with an annual revenue of Rs 1,80,630. [n40; da Cunha p. 157] Bajipura was the name the Marathas gave Bassein after 1739. [n40] Papdi had by then become the seat of the Peshwa’s Brahman officers and priests; and the same Papdi was, by 1882, a Christian parish of 1,294 people with a church newly rebuilt. The Hindu temple that marked Maratha dominance there, the Ram Mandir of Papdy, was rebuilt in the early twentieth century. [n40; CRIT Vol II] The Swarajya essay of 2019 says Chitpavan families “came with the Maratha army and set up separate localities”, and that marriage between them and the local Brahmins was uncommon until recently. [n54; Swarajya 2019] In the district as a whole, 8,722 Chitpavans and 1,006 Deshasths were counted in 1872, the Deshasths “especially in Panvel, Bassein, Murbad, Karjat and Salsette”; the figures give no village. [n54; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 74, 77]

Nirmal’s priests

The temple at Nirmal shows the new priesthood settling. A heritage blog of 2019 says Brahmins left Nirmal during Portuguese rule, and that afterwards one family each of Konkanastha (Chitpavan), Karhade and Devrukhe Brahmins settled there, with four families of Shukla Yajurvedi Gujarati Brahmins; the Pandya family, Gujarati Brahmins, are the temple’s caretakers today. [n54; n10; Heritage Chronicles 2019] The same blog dates the Shankaracharya to “BC 497” and the samadhi to “404 BC”, which cannot be right, so its family details too should be read as the temple’s own account. [n54]

The Gujarati Brahmins and the rest

The Gujarati Brahmins of Bassein came with the traders. “About the middle of the eighteenth century (1760–1766) a considerable number of Cambay Vanis, chiefly of the Lad sub-division, and with them several Gujarat Brahmans settled in Supara, Bassein, and other coast towns to avoid the exactions of Momin Khan II.” [n35; Gaz XIII-1 p. 63] The Lad Vanis’ family priests were generally Tolakia Brahmans, and the Porvad Vanis’ priests were Gujarati Brahmans. [n54; Gaz XIII-1 p. 112] A few families of Kanara Saraswat Brahmins were settled in Bassein. The Gaud Brahmins, by contrast, were “found over the whole district except in Bassein and Dahanu”. [n54; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 77, 83]

So Bassein in 1882 had at least four sets of Brahmins, each with its own work and its own history on this coast: the Samvedis, who were husbandmen; the Palshes, the old priests and clerks; the Gujarati Brahmins who served the trading families; and the Chitpavans, Deshasths and Karhades of the Maratha administration and the temples. [n54] They did not intermarry, and in the case of the Palshes and the Chitpavans, they did not always speak well of each other.

VII. Agris, Bhandaris, Kunbis and the Bassein-only Nakri Kunbis

The Agris and the returners

The Agris were the salt-makers, toddy-drawers, drummers and rice farmers of the coast. In 1882 they formed three divisions that neither ate together nor intermarried. The Sudagris were subdivided into Mithagris (salt-makers), Jasagris (toddy-drawers) and Dholagris (drummers), with surnames such as Bhoir, Chaudhari, Gharat, Mhatre, Naik, Patil, Pawar and Vaze, and family “badges” taken from trees and fruits. The Dasagris lived along the Tansa and Vaitarna. And the Urap Agris were the returners. [n08; n54; Gaz XIII-1 p. 116] The Agris spoke a “rough” Marathi, ate nagli or coarse rice with fish, and favoured “Cheda and other local spirits or devs”. [n08]

The Urap Agris are the community in which the history of conversion on this coast can be seen running backwards. “Urap or Varap Agris, found in several villages in Salsette and Bassein, are said to be Christian Agris who reverted to Hinduism, some in 1820 and others in 1828.” The Gazetteer’s footnote names the Bassein villages: Umelman (Umela), Manikpur, Agashi, Mukam, Padrichiwadi, Jot, Virar, Kolowda, Navapur, Achole and Juchandra. [n54; Gaz XIII-1 p. 117 and n. 1] Four of those, Umela, Manikpur, Achole and Juchandra, are in the south of the taluka; Achole is told with Nallasopara in Chapter 25. The Uraps were “considered lower than Sud or Das Agris”, who “neither marry nor eat with them”; they had a separate priest and headman, and were also called Nave, “new”, Marathas. The only trace of their Christian past was their surnames: Gomes, Soz (Souza), Fernan, Frutad (Furtado) and Minez (Menezes). [n54; n08; Gaz XIII-1 p. 117; Enthoven I] Their name was explained three ways, from the Persian urf, “alias”, from “Europe”, or from the Marathi orapne, to sear with a hot iron, for the purification ordeal; the Gazetteer preferred the third. [n49; n08]

The returns were tied to the cholera of 1818–20 and 1828 and to quarrels inside the Church. Some Christians propitiated the goddess of cholera and left or were driven out of the Church; Vaupell, the British official the Gazetteer quotes, blamed the 1828 reversion on “an attempt to extend the priestly prerogative”. [n49; Gaz XIII-1 p. 208 and n. 3] The Bassein Agris of 1820 paid their Palshe priest Rs 1,200. [n54; Gaz XIII-1 p. 117] A British report blamed the loss of about 3,000 Christians in the cholera of 1820–21 on the lack of “respectable ministers”. [n34; Mendiratta p. 220] So the Urap Agri villages are a map of where the return of 1820–28 reached Agri families: and Manikpur, a Christian Vadval parish, stands on it, which means that in the 1880s the same village held Christian Vadvals and Hindu Agris whose grandparents had been Christians. [n54]

Agri salt-making in Vasai today is known only through pieces of news. Vasai has about 2,000 acres of salt pans; fraudulent sales of 208 acres of salt-pan land at Rai-Murdhe near Vasai have been attempted; and monsoon floods have stranded salt-pan workers at Vasai’s Mithagar. [n08; Outlook; Asian Age; Deccan Herald] None of these sources names the workers’ community. There is no modern source on Agri salt-workers at Naigaon or Juchandra. [n54]

The Bhandaris

The Bhandaris, the palm-tappers, numbered 13,088 in the district in 1882, “found over the whole district except Murbad and Bhiwandi”. Their name came, the Gazetteer said, from the Sanskrit mandhārak, distiller. They said they came with Bimb; others claimed the Portuguese had brought them from Goa, which the Gazetteer thought unlikely. [n08; n54; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 149–150] They had four divisions: Kirtes, coconut-palm tappers and the highest; Gavads, brab-palm tappers; Sindes; and Kirpals, “once Christians, and perhaps get their name from kriyapal”, allowed to use Hindu rites. Their common surnames were Surve, Jadhav and Kadam; they spoke Marathi and were fairer than the Kunbis. [n54; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 149–150]

The Gazetteer’s picture of them at work is one of its most vivid: a Bhandari was “often seen with a hollow gourd full of palm-juice on their head”, carrying “on their left thigh a heavy broad-bladed tapping knife”; “they sing while they tap the trees.” At work they wore a scarlet waistcoat and a skull-cap; their women decked their hair with flowers; they worshipped Cheda with goats and fowl and fasted on Ganesh Chaturthi; their headman was the mukadam. “Since (1877) the recent rise in the palm tree cess, many have become husbandmen and labourers.” [n08; Gaz XIII-1 p. 150]

The Kirpals are the Bhandaris’ own record of the conversion and its undoing. The census of 1911 said they “were forcibly converted to Christianity by the Portuguese, but soon after the conquest of Bassein by the Marathas (about 1739) they were given the choice of reverting to Hinduism, which they accepted.” [n34; Census 1911] The Gazetteer counted them among the groups that went back after 1739. [n54; Gaz XIII-1 p. 202] On the other side of that line stood the Christian Bhandaris, who with the Kolis and Kunbis made up “the bulk” of the Christians of the coast. [n54] There is no modern source on Bhandari toddy-tapping in Vasai. [n54]

The Kunbis

The Kunbis were the largest Hindu community of the district, 183,144 in 1872, but they were an inland people. Only 1,050 Talheri Kunbis lived in Bassein, against 33,000 in Shahapur. [n35; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 124–125] The Gazetteer read the Talheri Kunbis as a local element “apparently little different from the Son Koli”, with a foreign element of two periods, one before the Muslims and Portuguese and one after; “Talheri” it read as “lowlander”, opposed to the Varli “uplander” and the Malhari “highlander”. [n35] On the coast, as already noted, Kunbis made up a large part of the Christian population; Manickpur’s groups, by its Wikipedia page, include Kunbis. [n54; n40] The Gazetteer’s three-group frame for the cultivators of Thana placed the coastal cultivators of Vasai in a third group, “probably of part Gujarat descent”, the Malis, Chavkalshis, Somvanshi Kshatris and Panchkalshis, rather than with the Kunbis. [n35; Gaz XIII-1 p. 124]

The Nakri Kunbis

One small community was found in Bassein and nowhere else. “Nakri Kunbis, found only in Bassein, are husbandmen.” The Gazetteer called them a “rough wild-looking class” who spoke incorrect Marathi. They worshipped Sati on the day after the birth of a girl, or on the fourth day after the birth of a boy. Their weddings were unlike any other in the district: the rite used a water-pot and a floating coconut; the marriage god was believed to enter one of the people present; a man tied the marriage ornaments, and a woman fixed the auspicious hour and performed the ceremony; in the marriage dance the guests lifted the bride and groom on their shoulders; and the celebrations lasted three days, with liquor and processions. [n54; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 128–129 and n.] A footnote suggested that they were an early tribe, “probably the same as the Surat Naiks”. [n54]

That is the whole of the record. The name “Nakri” was not found in any source after 1882; it may be a local form of Naik, as the Gazetteer’s own footnote hints. [n54] A community with its own wedding rite, in which a woman presided, appears in one paragraph of 1882 and then disappears from writing. Whether its families still live in Vasai under another name, or have merged into the Kunbi and Agri villages round them, is unknown.

Kharpatils, Baris and the smaller castes

The Kharpatils, 8,359 in Mahim, Bassein and Dahanu, were in some places “Agri families who have been in charge of salt lands”, and elsewhere a separate caste, the Kharvis, “apparently of Gujarat origin”: coastal tillers of rice and salt land who ate fish and flesh, drank liquor, worshipped Gaondevi, Cheda, Munja and Bhavani, and employed Chitpavan, Deshastha or Palshe priests; their holidays were Shimga, Diwali and the death anniversary. [n54; Gaz XIII-1 c. pp. 122–123] Kharpatils were among the castes from which the Christians of Bassein came. [n41]

The Baris, 2,458 in Bassein, Mahim and Dahanu, were “dark and stoutly made” and “said to have come from Gujarat”; some of their women kept Gujarati speech and dress. They were “husbandmen, cartmen and labourers” who owned cows and oxen and lived in houses of mud or raw brick; they allowed widow marriage and worshipped Sati on the twelfth day after a birth. [n54; Gaz XIII-1 p. 117] They were the second carting community of Bassein, Hindu and Gujarat-linked, beside the Christian Vadvals.

The Gazetteer’s lists also record a scatter of small crafts in Bassein: twenty-two Rhaoli weavers in Salsette and Bassein; Gavandi masons at Bassein, Vada and Bhiwandi; seventeen Jingar saddlers in Bassein and Karjat; fifteen Hajams in Bassein and Mahim; and the Phudgis, found only in Mahim and Bassein, whose speech Grierson found almost identical to Vadvali and among whom Enthoven recorded a Kirpal division, descendants of Phudgis “forced by the Portuguese to embrace Christianity” who reverted after the Maratha conquest. [n54; n49; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 132–153; Enthoven III] Each of these is a line in the record and nothing more.

VIII. The East Indians: one name over many

The Christians of Vasai today are, in law and in much of their own usage, East Indians. The name was chosen in Bombay. On 26 May 1887 about thirty “leaders of the community” met to change their designation from “Bombay Portuguese” to “Bombay East-Indian”, and founded the Bombay East Indian Association and a weekly paper. The name recalled a century and a half as subjects of the East India Company and the claim to be sons of the soil, and it separated them from the Goans and the Portuguese. [n34; Mendiratta pp. 234–236] Before 1887 the Gazetteer recorded the upper-class Christians calling themselves Firgi, from Firangi, and the lower classes calling themselves by their caste names, while Hindus called them Firangis or Kiristans. [n34; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 199–200]

The Christians of Bassein were drawn into the name as “rural” or “samvedi” East Indians, Mendiratta writes, partly because Brahmin and Prabhu converts were a minority in their villages. [n34; Mendiratta p. 218] Elsie Baptista’s standard book of 1967, which this book could not read, is cited for five groups within the community: the Kulbis, the Samvedi Christians “commonly called Kuparis”, the Koli Christians, the Vadvals and the Salsette Christians. [n34] So the Kuparis, Vadvals and Christian Kolis of this chapter are three of the five groups under one umbrella, which was raised in Bombay while the Bassein villages stayed, until 1928, under the Portuguese Padroado. [n34; n09]

Inside the umbrella, the old caste lines held. In 1882 the Gazetteer said the lower classes kept their old caste distinctions in marriage, and that “until lately the feeling of the impurity of the Mhars remained so strong that in some places they were not allowed to draw water from the village well or to enter the church”. Parish priests came from all classes except Mhars, Bhandaris, converts and illegitimate children. [n49; n54; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 200–201, 206] In 2019 the Swarajya essay, a polemical piece, said the Catholic community of Vasai is still “divided along caste lines of its original faith”, with separate community organisations and little marriage across them. [n54] The state of Maharashtra lists “East Indians” at serial number 341 of its list of Other Backward Classes, by a government resolution issued in 2006; a proposal to add East Indian Catholics to the central list was, in October 2026, awaiting the Union Cabinet’s approval. [n34; FPJ, 4 Oct 2026] Chapters 35, K5 and T4 tell the East Indian story at length.

IX. The Muslims

Before the Portuguese

Muslims were on this coast long before Vasai had a fort. The Gazetteer recorded from Abulfeda that Arabs had settled at Sopara, “Sofala”, in very early times. [n08; Gaz XIII-1 p. 61 n.] Its account of the Konkani Muslims, “the largest and most prosperous class of Thana Musalmans”, traced them to Arab and Persian refugees, merchants and adventurers who settled along the coast in the eighth, ninth and tenth centuries, joined by “local Hindus who were converted to Islam either by the preaching of missionaries or by the compulsion of Ahmednagar or Moghal rulers”; their tradition said the first came from the Persian Gulf in AD 699 to escape the governor Hajjaj, in three bodies. [n54; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 216–217, 232–233]

The most precise trace of Muslim traders at Bassein itself comes from the Portuguese moment. The Gazetteer recorded that “Garcia d’Orta mentions Naitas of Bassein who had married women of the country and were very rich and enterprising traders.” [n54; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 216–217] Garcia d’Orta, the Portuguese physician who held the island of Bombay on lease from the crown, was writing of the 1530s and 1540s. The Naitas, the Navayats, were the Arab-descended trading Muslims of the coast; the Gazetteer added that “the Hindus know the word Naita well but the Musalmans seem to consider it a term of abuse and disclaim it”. [n54; Gaz XIV p. 315 n. 1] Chapter 25 follows the Naitas at Sopara. At Pali, now in Naigaon West, the CRIT survey recorded a dargah of the sixteenth century, with a step well, dome, minarets and stone screens, and a mosque of the eighteenth century combining Islamic, Portuguese and wada features, and noted that “before the Portuguese domination” the place had “a significant Muslim population due to the traders and merchants who had settled here”. [n40; CRIT Vol I items 107–109]

The Portuguese century

The Portuguese conquest was a blow to this community, and the record says so in two ways. After the capture of Bassein, “certain Musalman mosques were pulled down and in their place a cathedral of St Joseph was built.” [n10; Gaz XIV, Bassein entry] And when the Portuguese held the coast, “the Musalmans were forced to leave the coast tracts” and settled in Kalyan, Bhiwandi and Thana. [n54; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 216–217] The money followed the buildings. From 1550 the Portuguese crown paid the Bassein Franciscans 2,070 pardaus a year, “derived from a source which before the conquest was solely applicable to the lighting of the mosques in Bassein”; the sum, later shared with the Jesuits and entered under the heading Conversão da Fé, conversion of the faith, was raised to 3,000 pardaus in 1634. [n34; da Cunha p. 235] The oil that had lit the mosques of the sultanate port paid for the missions that made the Christian villages of this chapter.

1872 and today

By 1872 the Muslims of Bassein were few: 2,025, of whom 1,866 were Sunni and 159 Shia, against 8,778 in Bhiwandi and 5,656 in Salsette. [n54; Gaz XIII-1 p. 216 n. 2] Agashi alone had 155 in 1881. [n10] The Konkani Muslims of the district spoke Marathi at home and Hindustani with other Muslims, dressed much like Hindus, and were traders in rice, landholders and husbandmen; they carried 115 surnames, mostly village names or professional titles; Bohora, Meman and Khoja traders came later “from Gujarat through Bombay”. [n54; n35; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 64, 216] The 1882 Gazetteer also noted modern mosques and shrines in the Sopara country whose builders “have made free use of the older masonry”. [n54; Gaz XIV p. 315]

In 2011 Muslims were 8.45 per cent of the taluka and Urdu the first language of 2.52 per cent. [n54] Beyond that, Muslim Vasai is almost undocumented in the sources reached: the Gausia Masjid at Koliwada (prayer shed, then a building begun in 1982, with an Arabic school for about fifty students) and the Pali dargah and mosque are the only Muslim places of southern Vasai with any recorded history. [n10; n40] There is nothing in the notes on Vasai town’s mohallas, its Konkani Muslim jamaats or headmen, or its Muslim families. [n54] That is a gap in the record, not in the town.

X. The Parsis

The Parsis appear in the record of Bassein at one moment, in the 1530s, and then vanish from it for two centuries. Soon after the Portuguese took Bassein, Garcia d’Orta noticed merchants and shopkeepers “called Coaris (Gaurs) at Bassein and Usparis (Parsis) in Cambay”. The Portuguese called them Jews; but, d’Orta observed, they were uncircumcised and ate pork, came from Persia, had “a curious written character”, exposed their dead, and used a special door. [n54; Gaz XIII-1 p. 254] “Coaris” is the Persian gabr, Gaur, a name for Zoroastrians. They were, then, Parsi traders in the Sultanate port at the moment of conquest.

The Parsis were old on this coast. Pahlavi inscriptions of 999 and 1021 at the Kanheri caves were cut by Parsis who were, from their names, fresh arrivals from Persia; the friars Jordanus and Odoric found Parsis at Thana in 1322 and 1323; and they were “very numerous until about 1533 … when the Portuguese conquered Salsette”. A tradition recorded by B. B. Patel says the Parsis feasted the Portuguese officers, made them drunk and left Thana for Kalyan, where a Tower of Silence was built in 1533. [n35; Enthoven III pp. 192–195] The Gazetteer said the only record of Parsi prosperity in the Konkan between about 1530 and 1774 was the Tower of Silence at Nargol in 1767, and that the Parsis did not venture back into Thana until about 1780, under British protection. [n54; Gaz XIII-1 p. 254]

When they came back they came in new roles. In 1882 the Parsis of the district were liquor contractors, Government servants, and a few timber and firewood dealers. [n54; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 29, 65, 98] A few Parsis came to the Nirmal fair in the 1880s, and Parsi women brought thank-offerings to the feast at Bandra. [n54; Gaz XIV p. 292; XIII-1 p. 209] No Parsi agiary, institution or family in Vasai West is documented in any source reached, and searches in Marathi for Vasai’s Parsis returned only general history. [n54] The best reading of the evidence is that the Parsis of Vasai were a British-period presence of officials and contractors, not a Portuguese-era settlement, and that d’Orta’s “Coaris” of the 1530s left no documented descendants here. [n54]

XI. Jains and the Vanis of Papdi; Gujaratis and Marwaris

The Lad Vanis

The Lad Vanis are the best-documented trading community of the coast. “Of about 120 families of Lad Vanis about forty are in Thana, thirty-five in Supara, and the rest in Bassein, Agashi, Nala, Papdi, and Dahanu.” [n35; n54; Gaz XIII-1 p. 112] The Gazetteer argued that the Lads had traded along this coast “from very early times”, because the Arab geographer Masudi had said in 915 that the Lar language was spoken in the coast towns as far south as Chaul; but it judged that “most of the present families are late settlers, who about the middle of the eighteenth century fled from Cambay to escape the tyranny of Momin Khan II.” [n35; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 112–113] Enthoven derived their name from Lat-desh, the old name of south Gujarat. [n35; Enthoven III p. 428]

They spoke Gujarati among themselves and Marathi with others. They were shopkeepers, moneylenders and superior landholders, who kept bullock carriages, were vegetarians, and employed Tolakia Brahmans as family priests. They were Vallabhacharya Vaishnavs who “observe Shaiv fasts and feasts” because of their “close connection with Maratha Brahmans”; they had stopped giving daughters to the Thana Lad Vanis and took brides from Cambay, Jambusar and Bombay. [n35; n54; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 112–113] They came to Bassein by the Cambay sea route, more than a century before the railway. [n35]

The Porvad Vanis of Bassein and the Vanis of Papdi

The same page of the Gazetteer records two more Vani communities of the south, and these are new to this book. “Porvad Vanis of the Dasha sub-division are found only in Bassein, where they are said to have been settled for about 200 years.” Their girls were married between six and twelve; they were Bhagvats, “worshipping Vishnu under the name of Thakurji”, with Gujarati Brahmans as priests; and “the opening of the railway has lowered their profits, but they are still well-to-do.” [n54; Gaz XIII-1 p. 112] Two hundred years before 1882 is about 1680, under the Portuguese. If the tradition is right, the Porvads were the one Gujarati trading caste the record places in Bassein during Portuguese rule.

And: “Shrimalis and Modhs are found in Papdi near Bassein.” [n54; Gaz XIII-1 p. 112] The Shrimalis are, in Enthoven’s account, predominantly Jain, with Hindu sections called Meshri and Jain sections called Shravak; Shrimali is also the name of a Brahmin sub-caste, the Gujarati parallel. [n35; Enthoven III pp. 422–430] The Gazetteer does not use the word Lad for the Papdi families; so “the Lad Vanis of Papdi” is a modern shorthand, and the record’s own words are that Lad Vanis lived at Papdi and that Shrimali and Modh Vanis lived there too. [n54]

Papdi’s buildings still carry names that fit a merchant presence. The CRIT survey of 2008 recorded, round the Papdy pond, a Shah Wada of the 1850s, the Mohanlal Vakil bungalow and the Govardhan Vidyalaya, besides the Ram Mandir, the Thomas Baptista School and the Bondali and Taam ponds. [n40; CRIT Vol II assets 36–42] “Shah” is a common Gujarati Vani surname, but no source says who built the Shah Wada, and this chapter does not assert it. Papdi in the nineteenth century was thus three villages in one: a Christian parish, a Maratha Brahman settlement, and a home of Gujarati Vani families. No present-day Lad, Porvad, Shrimali or Modh Vani institution in Papdi was found. [n54]

The Jains

Jains were few in the district in 1872, 1,823 in all, counted as Shravaks. [n08; Gaz XIII-1 p. ~60] Sopara’s place as a medieval Jain tirtha belongs to Chapter 25, and the Agashi tirth to Chapter 31. In southern Vasai the record holds only a modern Digambar Jain temple to Mahavir Swami at Navghar, Vasai West, known from a listing, and a historian’s suggestion, reported in Live History India, that the name “Vasai” itself comes from the Jain vasati, settlement; the Gazetteer does not support that derivation, and it is a conjecture. [n54; jainmandir.org; Live History India] In 2011 Jains were 1.75 per cent of the taluka. [n54]

The Marwaris and the others

The Marwari Vanis, the Gazetteer said, “are believed to have come to Thana from Rajputana or Marwar, almost entirely since the British conquest”. Their first movement “followed the very liberal and general reduction of rent … between 1835 and 1838. The reductions left a large margin of profit to the landholder and the Marvadis came, advanced money at from 100 to 200 per cent to the husbandmen, and sold them up.” In 1846 the Collector, Mr Law, noticed that “the thrifty avaricious Marvadi had begun to settle even in the remotest villages”. [n35; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 64, 113–114] Those are a colonial officer’s words, and the judgement is his. The rent reductions of 1835–38 are the same reductions that the 1837 report on Manikpur recorded; so the Christian husbandmen of the south and the Marwari moneylenders who followed them appear in the record within a decade of each other. The Marwaris were Jains, kept weekly fasts in Bhadrapad, and, the Gazetteer complained, “carried their gains to their own country”. [n35] Lohanas and Bhatias from Cutch and north Gujarat were grain and cloth merchants in most of the leading towns. [n35]

In 2011 Gujarati was the first language of 8.60 per cent of the taluka and Marwari of 1.64 per cent. In Navghar-Manikpur, the railway-side council of Vasai East, Gujarati speakers were about 23.78 per cent in 2001, more than speakers of Hindi. [n54; n52; Wikipedia: Navghar-Manikpur] No source in the notes describes Gujarati or Marwari commerce in Vasai’s bazaars, or the dates when its trading families arrived. [n08]

XII. The newcomers

The first migrants of the record

The Gazetteer of 1882 already listed the “recent settlers” of the district: Pardeshis from Upper India as messengers and servants, Kachis as market gardeners, Sindhi sweet-makers, Gujarati Brahmans and traders, Marwari Vanis and Lingayat Vanis. [n35; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 64–65] The cast of the twentieth-century migration was on the stage 140 years before it began.

The boom

Until the late 1980s Vasai was mostly farmland. In 1981 Vasai town had 34,900 people and the whole Vasai-Virar area fewer than 250,000. In 1988 the state government removed the agricultural reservation of the land, against the advice of the metropolitan planning authority, and builders moved in at once. By 1991 the area had grown about 150 per cent, and Vasai, Virar, Sopara and Sandor about 290 per cent, growth attributed to commuters to Mumbai. [n52; n35; TIFR] Vasai town itself grew only to 49,346 by 2001; the rail-side East grew much faster, Navghar-Manikpur reaching about 116,700. [n52] Eastern Vasai is now said to hold about 12,000 industrial units, and Navghar, Gokhivare, Waliv and Sativali are large industrial areas of private estates outside any state industrial zone. [n52; n54] The 1990s townships of Vasai East, Evershine City among them, are told in Chapter 18.

Who came

The census of 2011 shows the newcomers only through language. Hindi (24.78 per cent of the taluka), Bhojpuri (2.92), Urdu (2.52), Malayalam (1.41), Bengali (1.28), Konkani (1.25) and Kannada (0.93) mark a taluka that is no longer a Marathi, Konkani and Catholic place. [n54] No ward-level count of migrants, no table of their states of origin, and no study of Vasai’s Bihari, Uttar Pradesh, Rajasthani or Gujarati neighbourhoods was found. [n54]

Some communities can be seen through their worship. The Kerala Catholics appear at parish level: the Syro-Malabar St Joseph’s centre at Naigaon, which began in a rented classroom, and St Michael’s, Manickpur, as the Marathi-language Sunday Mass for Syro-Malabar Catholics. [n54] The Bhojpuri and Bihari communities appear through Chhath: in October 2025 the municipal corporation, after demands from the MLAs Sneha Dubey-Pandit and Rajan Naik and under court directions, allowed devotees to offer arghya in natural ponds on 27 and 28 October, for the offering only, with no garlands or lamps left in the water, and planned artificial ponds. The report did not name the community or any pond. [n54; Free Press Journal, 25 Oct 2025]

The clearest single sign is a temple. In April 2026, at Sun City in Vasai West, a replica of the Badrinath temple of Uttarakhand was consecrated. The pran-pratishtha of Lord Badri Vishal was completed on Wednesday 22 April 2026, at the end of a five-day festival from 18 to 22 April, conducted by twenty-one Vedic acharyas and Brahmins from Kashi and organised with the Uttaranchal Mitra Mandal; people from Vasai, Nalasopara and Virar took part. [n54; Free Press Journal, April 2026] Three centuries after Shankarji Keshav rebuilt Nirmal for the Peshwa, a community from the Himalaya built its own shrine in the suburb.

Buddhists were 2.53 per cent of the taluka in 2011; their history in Vasai town is not recorded in the notes, and the revival round the Sopara stupa belongs to Chapter 25. [n54]

Part Two: Sacred Places and Festivals of Vasai

The south of the taluka is the densest sacred ground on this coast: the ruined churches of the fort, the nine parish churches founded outside it, the temple sanctuary of Nirmal and the hill shrine of Tungareshwar, with wayside crosses, village temples, a dargah and a mosque among them. Chapter 42 tells the Catholic history of the whole coast, Chapter 44 the heritage a visitor can see, and Chapter 17 the fort. What follows is the evidence for each sacred place of the south as the communities above built and used it, with the dates and costs the record holds, and the festivals that belong to it.

The six churches of 1881

The 1882 Gazetteer’s account of Bassein describes “six modern Catholic churches in and near Bassein”, and gives for each what a revenue officer would want to know: where it stood, when and at what cost it was built, how large it was and how many people it served. [n54; n40; Gaz XIV pp. 36–37]

  • Our Blessed Lady of Mercy, Merces: 974 people; built by private subscription; its vicar paid Rs 17 a month by the Portuguese Government, not the British.
  • Our Blessed Lady of Remedies, Dhauli (Remedi), two miles north of Bassein: 3,238 people; built in 1821 at £1,860 (Rs 18,600), of which £1,800 came from private subscription and £60 from a Government grant; 108 feet long, 33 broad and 23 high.
  • St Michael the Archangel, Manikpur, four miles north-east of Bassein: 800 people; rebuilt in 1851 at £1,500 (Rs 15,000) raised privately; 120 feet long.
  • St Thomas the Apostle, Sandore, three miles north of Bassein: 1,725 people; side walls of the sixteenth century, chapel 1838, frontispiece 1858.
  • Our Blessed Lady the Mother of God, Pali, three miles east of Bassein: 900 people; built in 1840 at £1,500 (Rs 15,000); a parish school of thirty pupils.
  • Our Blessed Lady of Grace, Papdi, about two miles north-east of Bassein: 1,294 people; built in 1865 at £1,800 (Rs 18,000) of private subscription; 102 feet long; a Government-supported parish school of fifty-six pupils.

Each vicar had a house and a Government stipend of Rs 14-8 a month, and each church had violin music masters who played at services. [n54; Gaz XIV pp. 36–37] Four of the six were rebuilt or newly built between 1821 and 1865, almost entirely with the parishioners’ own money; the Government’s share at Remedi was £60 of £1,860. These are the churches that the Vadval farmers, Koli fishermen and Christian cultivators of the Manikpur report rebuilt for themselves in the half century after the British arrived.

The Gazetteer’s district chapter gives a second list: nine priests and nine churches under the Vicar Vara (rural dean) of Bassein, at Sandora, Papdi, Pali, Manikpur, Davli, Nirmal, Koprad, Agashi and Bassein. [n54; Gaz XIII-1 p. 206 and n. 3] The Koprad church on that list is the Holy Spirit church, today’s Nandakhal parish; Nirmal’s 2,184 people are given separately. [n54; n40] In 1876 da Cunha had counted the Native Christians of Bassein’s parishes: Palli 800, Espirito Santo (Nandakhal) 2,000, Papari 1,200, Merces 700, Monte Calvario 1,500, Manikpura 900, Remedios 3,000, Agasi 900, Sam Thome (Sandor) 1,000. He gave the total as 12,400; the figures as transcribed add up to 12,000, a difference the OCR of his book may explain. [n40; da Cunha p. 161]

Other sources add more dates still: the CRIT survey gives Papdi 1574 and Remedi 1577 (founded by the Dominicans), GCatholic gives Remedi 1577, the Sandor parish page gives both 1556 and 1566, and the Palle parish gives 1585 for Pali. [n40; n54] The best reading is that “foundation” in Vasai means different events — a first chapel, the grant of parish status, a rebuilding after 1739, a rebuilding in the nineteenth century — and that no list says which event it records. [n54] Hull’s Manickpur, “built 1550–1570”, is older than the 1606 at which the parish itself says Porim ceased to be part of Sandor; the parish’s account of a chapel on land given by a Dona Ines Francisca in 1572 comes only from a search summary of a site that, when opened, held a placeholder. [n40; n54]

The dedications also moved. The Gazetteer’s district list names the Remedi church “Davli, Our Lady of Help”, while its Bassein entry names the Dhauli church “Our Blessed Lady of Remedies”; and Sandor’s own history says the first chapel at Sandor was dedicated to “Our Lady of Help”. [n54; Gaz XIII-1 p. 206 n. 3; XIV p. 37] The earlier research for this book guesses that the names Help and Remedies were moved between Sandor and Davli between the sixteenth and nineteenth centuries; it is a guess to test against parish archives, none of which was available online. [n54] Earlier chapters said Manickpur’s patron was unverified; both the Gazetteer and Hull give St Michael the Archangel, and that question is closed. [n54]

Papdy: the cathedral

Papdy’s church of Our Lady of Grace was, by its parish and the CRIT survey, built in 1574 by Jesuit missionaries, “the second parish to be founded in South Bassein outside the Fort”, drawing people from Davly (Remedi), part of Sandor and Maferegua; “the Jesuits nurtured this parish until the Maratha invasion in 1739. This church escaped destruction during the Maratha occupation.” In 1839 the parish got a vicar of its own; originally it had about 1,388 parishioners. [n40; n54; CRIT sheet 40; EI Papdy] The CRIT survey graded it II B and singled out two features. Its false-front façade, with a belfry and rose window in three tiers, is one of the oldest surviving fronts of the coast. And its open congregational ground, partly a graveyard, used at festivals and weddings, is “a peculiar feature of the majority of churches in the Vasai-Virar sub-region”. [n54; CRIT sheet 40] The church is painted for its festival in May; one directory places the feast on the last Sunday of May. [n54] Two directories disagree on its patron, one giving Our Lady of Health; the heritage record and the Gazetteer give Our Lady of Grace. [n54]

On 22 May 1998 Pope John Paul II created the Diocese of Vasai, and on 15 August 1998 Bishop Thomas Dabre was installed at Papdy, which became its cathedral. [n40; n09; GCatholic] In 2025 the cathedral’s restoration won an Award of Merit in the UNESCO Asia-Pacific Awards for Cultural Heritage Conservation. The restoration, Outlook Traveller reported in February 2026, was “led by the parish community rather than state funding or corporate support”. [n54; n40; Outlook Traveller, 25 Feb 2026] The parishioners who paid £1,800 of their own money to rebuild their church in 1865 had successors who did the same 160 years later.

Sandor and its December feast

Sandor’s story is told by its parish in detail, and the details do not agree with each other. After the conversions of 1564, a Portuguese noblewoman, Donna Irina, built a chapel of Our Lady of Help, where the Jesuits said the first Mass on 11 November 1566. It was renamed St Thomas the Apostle when his picture came from the fort; the parish gives the year as 1565, before the first Mass. Forces of the Gujarat Sultanate destroyed it in 1571, and it was rebuilt in 1573; it was damaged by Arabs from Gujarat in 1679 and by “Kakaji” in 1690. [n54; n40; Wikipedia: Sandor] Fr Pedro de Almeida SJ was “Father of the Christians” over Sandor and Papdy in 1574–76. [n40] A Sandor tradition says St Gonsalo Garcia, born in Bassein about 1556, was present as a boy at the foundation of 1566. [n40] The CRIT survey calls St Thomas “the oldest church building that presently exists outside the Vasai Fort”, dating it from 1571, and the parish says it escaped destruction by the Marathas in 1739, after which the Jesuits left and it came under the secular clergy. [n40; CRIT Vol III asset 103] In 1889 the chapel was enlarged with three altars brought from one of the ruined churches of the fort; a cross and a Lourdes grotto were added later. [n54; n40]

Sandor’s annual feast runs from 9 to 15 December, with a large evening celebration. [n54; Wikipedia: Sandor]

Pali (Palle), the Mother of God and 18 December

The Mother of God church at Palle, in Naigaon West, is called historically the Palle church. The CRIT survey says it was built in 1585, “which is recorded in the Franciscan Chronicler”; the Franciscans left it when the Marathas took the region in 1739, and it was rebuilt between 1840 and 1844 on a new site a hundred feet west of the original. [n40; CRIT Vol III asset 107] The Gazetteer’s Pali church of 1840 is the same building. [n54] Da Cunha in 1876 thought the old church “has long been in a dilapidated condition”. [n40] The parish itself gives a founding “between 1577 and 1585”, settles on 1585, and names Fr Achilles Meersman for a date of 1595. It says that Fr Paolo da Trindade’s book of 1638 called it the first church outside the fort, which other parishes dispute; that a resident priest, Fr Antonio, wrote to Goa in 1635; and that a revered statue of Our Lady “pierced by a sword” fell in a storm on 16 May 1618. [n54; Prayers and Petitions, Mother of God Church] The annual feast of Our Lady, Mother of God, is on 18 December. [n54] Mulgaon’s parish of St Mary Magdalene was carved from Palle in 1945; its church was consecrated in 1966, and its parish remembers Fr Clement de Sa, who died on 13 November 1963 while giving Communion. [n40; EI Mulgaon]

Remedi: a shrine for everyone

Our Lady of Remedies at Remedi was the largest parish of 1881 and is the shrine the diocese treats as its Marian centre. The CRIT survey says the Dominicans founded it outside the fort in 1577, the second parish separated from Sandor; da Cunha found it in the charge of the Dominicans in 1876 and “in a fair state of preservation”. [n40] The Gazetteer’s church of 1821 is the one rebuilt from subscriptions. [n54] The old door of a Vasai fort church is said to be kept at Remedi, a tradition only. [n40] Round it, the CRIT survey recorded the Holy Market precinct on the Remedy–Par Naka road, where vendors from Sandor, Kaular Budruk and neighbouring villages paid Rs 5 a day for a pitch, and two houses of the nineteenth-century landowning farmers, the Patil Wada of the 1850s and the Matkari Wada of 1868. [n40; CRIT Vol I items 21–25]

Remedi’s image drew people of every faith. In February 1986 Pope John Paul II, speaking at St Augustine High School in Vasai, said the images of Our Lady at Remedy and Pali attracted devotees, “Christian and non-Christian”, in great numbers. [n54; Vatican, 9 Feb 1986 (search summary)] The creation of the Diocese of Vasai was announced at the shrine on 28 June 1998. [n40] About 7,000 to 8,000 women belong to the diocese’s Federation of Our Lady of Remedy. [n09] A Remedy Youth Group held a Taizé prayer service at the shrine on 15 April 2025. [n54] Near the church stands the Milagres Cross, called miraculous, where devotees are often seen praying. [n09; n54] Felix Machado, Bishop of Vasai from 2009 to 2024 and an archbishop, was born at Remedi on 6 June 1948. [n40; GCatholic] No source gives the date or form of Remedi’s own feast. [n54; n09]

Merces, Giriz and the newer parishes

Merces, between Sandor and Remedi, was named after its church, Nossa Senhora das Mercês; the village had been Kaular, and the church stands in Kaular Khurd. Its date is disputed: Jesuit reports of 1669 and Fr Conti point to 1606, and the CRIT survey says the parish began in 1606 under the Augustinians until 1739, when the church was destroyed and its priest fled; Hull’s list gives an Augustinian foundation in 1584 and parish status in 1606; da Cunha credits the church to Archbishop Dom Aleixo de Menezes and the Franciscans. [n40; n54] It was rebuilt in 1856, and its first secular vicar, Fr Sebastião José Florencio de Faria, came on 17 January 1862. [n40; EI Merces] In 1881 the Portuguese Government still paid its vicar, the one church of the six that Lisbon kept. [n54] Its feast date was not found. [n54]

Giriz was built by its own villagers. Its foundation stone was laid on 14 February 1917 and the church of St Francis Xavier finished a year later “without foreign aid”; Giriz became a parish under Fr Augusto D’Souza, celebrated its golden jubilee in 1968 with a porch and the Marshal Pereira Community Hall, was extended in 1977 with German aid, and replaced its twin spires in May 1981. Its feast is on 3 December, the feast of St Francis Xavier. [n40; n09; EI Giriz; Wikipedia] Beside it the CRIT survey recorded the Girij pond, of the sixteenth century, with stepped banks. [n40]

The twentieth-century parishes of the south, Giriz (1917–18), Gass (1942), Mulgaon (1945), Bhuigaon (1946), Gokhivare (1943), Chulne (1963) and Gomes Ali (2004), are the work of villages too far from their mother churches, and are told above with their communities. [n40; n09]

The fort feast and the saint of Bassein

St Gonsalo Garcia, born in Bassein about 1556 or 1557 to a Portuguese father and an Indian mother, crucified at Nagasaki with twenty-five companions on 5 February 1597 and canonised in 1862, is the patron of the Diocese of Vasai. [n09; n42] His feast at the ruined church of the Holy Name of Jesus inside the fort, with a fair the people call Killa cha Sann, “the fort’s feast”, was held on Sunday 11 January 2026, the same day as the feast at St Bonaventure, Erangal. Families arrive the evening before and camp in enclosures among the ruins, cooking East Indian moile and stew; pilgrims once came by bullock cart and by boat. [n09; n52; FPJ, 11 Jan 2026] A pilgrim-centre listing says the fort feast falls on “the first Sunday nearest to the neap tide following Christmas”, while other sources give 5 to 7 February; the conflict is unresolved. [n54] The Gass church and its February feast belong to Chapter 25.

Crosses

Almost every Catholic gaothan of Vasai has its wayside cross, and at the cross’s annual feast the villagers gather for the rosary. [n09; VasaiCity] A 2015 blog listed some of the south’s: the Community Cross at Bangli Naka; a cross in the premises of St Peter’s, Koliwada; a cross on the road to the Vasai court; the Milagres Cross near Remedi church; the Parvati Cross toward Vasai station, “at the entrance of a Catholic village”; and the Furtado Family Cross at Kirawali-Chobare, Vasai West. [n54; n09; Vasai Catholics blog, 9 Oct 2015] It gave no dates or inscriptions. At Bhuigaon the Madikar Cross is the village’s gaondevi in all but name. [n40]

The temples

Nirmal

Nirmal, the Gazetteer said, six miles north of Bassein, “is one of the most sacred places in the district”, the burial place by tradition of one of the great Shankaracharyas. [n54; n10; Gaz XIV pp. 292–293] Its own Mahatmya told how the demon Vimal, harassing the Brahmins by the Vaitarna, was overcome by Parashurama with Shiva’s help and pardoned, and how the linga Vimaleshvar was set up where he fell, “from Vimal it came to be called Nirmal or stainless”; the Gazetteer offered a second derivation, from the Dravidian nir, water, and mal, hill, “a wooded mound rising from bare flats once under water”. [n10; n40; Gaz XIV p. 292 n. 2] A dressed stone with letters “which seem part of a Sanskrit inscription of the seventh century” stood about 500 yards north of the chief temple. [n10]

“The Portuguese had pulled down the temples and destroyed the ling. On the fall of Bassein (1739) Nirmal was purified, prints of the feet of Shri Dattatraya took the place of the ling, and a reservoir was built.” Eight temples there were “all built about 1750 by Shankarji Keshav, Sarsubhedar of Bassein”, the Maratha governor, and the stones the Portuguese had built into churches “were probably used to raise the temples of the older gods”. [n10; Gaz XIV p. 293] In 1882 the Government paid yearly grants to the Shankaracharya temple (Rs 421, managed by a committee), Mahavishnu (Rs 118), Vimaleshvar (Rs 100), Bhuteshvar (Rs 60), Ganpati, Durga and Hanuman together (Rs 35) and Siddheshvar (Rs 25), and to a Brahman alms-house (Rs 454) and a Bairagi alms-house (Rs 148), about Rs 1,330 in all; the Gazetteer read the grants as showing that “the temples were built from public not from private funds.” [n54; n10; Gaz XIV p. 293] Nirmal was, by that measure, the greatest Hindu sanctuary of the taluka after the fort’s Trivikram temple.

Its fair was the greatest gathering of the coast. On Kartik Vadya 11, in November, the anniversary of the Shankaracharya’s death, a fair of a week drew “six to seven thousand pilgrims, Hindus, Musalmans, Christians, and a few Parsis, from all parts of Thana, Gujarat, Bombay, the Deccan and south Konkan”, who bought brass and copper vessels, dried plantains, sweetmeats, cloth and cattle worth about Rs 10,000. [n54; n10; Gaz XIV p. 292] A modern blog describes a fourteen-day yatra in November and a shrine of Kartikeya that women may see only on Kartik Purnima. [n54; n10] Which Shankaracharya lies at Nirmal is disputed: the Gazetteer thought him one of the great Shankaracharya’s religious descendants; a blog says Vidyaranya, “5th Shankaracharya of Puri”, with impossible dates in the fifth century BC; others say a later Shankaracharya from Gujarat or Dwarka. [n10] A listing of 2015 places the samadhi in Wakiwadi, Vasai West, reached by bus to Nirmal Naka. [n54] On the bank of the lake the small Surleshwar temple, with its goddess Surleshwari, is known for funeral rites; its date is not recorded. [n10]

The Hindu and Christian stories of Nirmal are one story. The temples were raised about 1750 from stones taken from churches; the church was rebuilt in 1856 by parishioners who had worshipped for a century in its graveyard; and the November fair was attended by both.

The fort, Tungareshwar and Vajreshwari

Inside the fort in 1882 stood “two modern Hindu temples … one to Hanuman close to the sea gateway and the other to Trivikram”, with a modern Mahadev temple near the hospital church. Trivikram’s yearly allowance of Rs 1,061 was the largest temple grant in the taluka. [n10; Gaz XIV, Bassein] A trekking site says the Veer Hanuman by the sea gate was consecrated on 27 July 1739, and that the Nageshwar temple, destroyed under the Portuguese, was rebuilt beside the Nagesh Mahatirtha pond after the conquest; those dates are tradition. [n10] Chapter 17 tells the fort’s temples and churches together.

On Tungar hill, the Tungareshwar temple was in 1882 “a group of four square buildings … said to be of great age”, rebuilt about 1750 by the same Shankarji Keshav. Its Mahatmya, part of the Padma Purana, told how Vimal, driven by Parashurama, settled on Tungar and won immortality from Shiva. The temple held Datta’s footprints under the Nandi canopy; the Peshwa had granted it “a plot of ground about three miles square”, which the British continued. On the summit were unfinished Jain cells “supposed to have been cut about the middle of the thirteenth century (1234)”, and a stone Jain saint “lately … broken to pieces by the Roman Catholic hotel-keeper”, for the hill had been a failed British hill-station between 1865 and 1880. [n10; Gaz XIV pp. 366–368] Today a bhandara is held at Mahashivratri and on the Mondays of Shravan. [n10]

Vajreshwari lies outside Vasai, at Vadavli in Bhiwandi taluka, but it belongs to this chapter’s people. The Gazetteer said its temple was “built … by the well-known Shankarji Keshav, the Peshwa’s Sarsubhedar, to commemorate a victory over the Portuguese”, and that its Chaitra fair drew about 5,000 people of all castes. [n10; Gaz XIV pp. 373–374] The popular story makes it Chimaji Appa’s vow, fulfilled in 1739; that is tradition. [n10] Vajreshwari is a household goddess of the Vadvals. [n41] In May 2025 Vasai’s Vijayotsav, the municipal festival of the 1739 victory, billed as the “287th”, lit its torch at the Vajreshwari temple; it was cut to one day, 12 May, by rain. [n52; FPJ, 9 May 2025]

The village temples of the south

Inside the Christian villages, the CRIT survey recorded the Hindu temples of the Maratha century: at Sandor, a Dev Talav and a Hanuman temple, both of the eighteenth century; at Papdy, a Ram Mandir rebuilt early in the twentieth century on a site that had marked Maratha dominance from 1739 to 1818; at Rangaon, an eighteenth-century Hanuman temple. [n40] At Bhuigaon the Champavati temple stands on the same road as the church. [n40] On the Giriz-Holi-Rangaon road at Wakiwadi, Bhuigaon, stands a math of Swami Samarth of Akkalkot, for which a blog of 2015 gives no founding date. [n54] Marathi Wikipedia lists Datta temples at Girij and Dongri. [n54] At Gokhivare the Gazetteer saw a shrine of Mahadev beside a pond. [n10] A Walmikeshwar temple is listed at Naigaon Koliwada. [n54] No jatra of a village goddess at Kaman, Pelhar or Gokhivare was found in any source. [n54; n10]

Mosques, a dargah and a derasar

The Pali dargah of the sixteenth century, with its step well, dome, minarets and stone screens, and the Pali mosque of the eighteenth century, with its mixture of Islamic, Portuguese and wada features, are the oldest Muslim buildings of the south in the record. [n40; CRIT] The Gausia Masjid at Koliwada, begun as a prayer shed and rebuilt from 1982, runs an Arabic school. [n10] The cathedral of St Joseph in the fort stood, the Gazetteer said, on the site of mosques the Portuguese pulled down. [n10] The Digambar Jain temple of Mahavir Swami at Navghar is known from a listing alone. [n54]

The calendar of the south

Assembled from the sources, the sacred year of southern Vasai runs like this:

  • January: St Gonsalo Garcia’s feast and Killa cha Sann at the fort (11 January in 2026; other sources say early February).
  • February to March: Shimga, the Koli Holi, with areca-branch pyres; Mahashivratri at Tungareshwar.
  • April: Vajreshwari’s Chaitra fair (1882); the Badrinath pran-pratishtha at Sun City in 2026.
  • May: Papdy’s festival (last Sunday of May, by one directory); Vasai Vijayotsav (around 12–16 May).
  • June to July: the fishermen ashore; the Pachubandar seminar of St Peter’s, Koliwada (17–20 June 2025).
  • August: Narali Purnima, the new fishing season; the Mondays of Shravan at Tungareshwar.
  • October to November: Diwali and Waghbaras among the Warlis; Chhath at the ponds (27–28 October 2025); the Nirmal fair on Kartik Vadya 11.
  • December: Giriz on 3 December; Sandor from 9 to 15 December; Palle on 18 December; Christmas, with door-to-door carols and crib contests in the gaothans.

[n54; n09; n10; n52; n08]

What the record does not hold

The gaps in this chapter are as much a part of the history as its facts, and they should be named.

  • No community counts. No census since 1931 has counted castes; none has ever counted Kuparis or Vadvals. No village-by-village religion or caste table for 2011 could be opened. [n54; n41]
  • The Adivasi hamlets. No source names the padas of Kaman, Pelhar, Chinchoti or Poman, their clans, their deities, or their Forest Rights claims. [n54]
  • The Koli landings. Nothing on Killa Bandar as a Koli village, on Naigaon Koliwada’s Hindu and Christian families, on the Pachubandar jetty, or on Narali Purnima at any southern landing. [n54]
  • The parish archives. No Livro do Tombo or parish register of any southern parish was available online; the heritage records of Remedi, Merces, Sandor, Giriz and Manickpur in the CRIT series were not located; the feast dates of Merces, Manickpur and Remedi, and Papdy’s exact dedication, are unresolved. [n54]
  • The Vadval carts and the Vadvali tongue. No source on the carts, cart races or timber trade of Chulne and Gokhivare today; no speaker count after 2000; nothing on whether Hindu and Christian Vadvals speak the same variety. [n54; n41]
  • The Nakri Kunbis. One paragraph of 1882, and then nothing. [n54]
  • Muslim Vasai. No history of Vasai town’s mosques, mohallas or Muslim families beyond the Gausia Masjid and Pali. [n54]
  • The Parsis and the Vanis. No Parsi institution, and no Lad, Porvad, Shrimali or Modh Vani trust or family at Papdi, was found. [n54]
  • The newcomers. No ward-level migrant counts, no origin-state tables, no study of any migrant neighbourhood of Vasai. [n54]
  • The Christian village older than the Portuguese. The Gazetteer heard of one in 1882 and never confirmed it. Which village it was remains unknown. [n54]

Local Marathi newspapers, the 1982 Thane district gazetteer, parish chronicles and the memories of the old families of each village could fill many of these gaps. They lay outside what this book could reach.

From the carts to the stupa

The carters of 1882 drove their timber down from the forests to Gokhivare, across Manikpur and on to the marts of Bassein. North of their road, across the salt flats and the old causeway past Chulne, lay the garden island of Sopara, its creek already dry, its broad market place lined with carved houses, its Christian husbandmen and Naita traders, its Samvedi and Bhandari farmers and its Lad Vani shops; and beyond them the mound the villagers called the Burud king’s fort, which Bhagwanlal Indraji opened that same year to find the relics of a stupa. That town, and the city of more than half a million that took its name, are the subject of the next chapter.
