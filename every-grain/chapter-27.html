Period covered: from the making of the land to 28 November 1864

In this chapter. The land and its few records before 1864: the eclipse grant of 1049, the headmen of Vattaraka, the stones in the fields and the long silence before the Portuguese.

Part Nine, “Virar”, begins here. This chapter and the next replace the early sections of Chapter 27, which told Virar’s whole story in one sweep; they go back over the same ground village by village and folio by folio, and add what P1 left out.

On the night of the full moon of Phalguna in the Saka year 970, which fell on 20 February 1049, the moon went dark over the north Konkan. A lunar eclipse was a moment of great merit for a Hindu king, a time when gifts to learned Brahmins counted many times over, and the Shilahara king Mummuniraja, who ruled the coast from his capital at Puri, chose it for a grant. His clerks drew up copper plates in Sanskrit. Most of what he gave lay in a district called Varetika. But the plates also gave produce, partly in coin (drammas) and partly in grain, from fields in two other groups of villages, to Brahmins named one by one. One group lay in the Abhyantara district, round Mulund and Borivali. The other lay in “the Shurparaka sixty-six”, the old revenue district of Sopara. There the clerks wrote five village names: Brihad-Adanika and Laghu-Adanika, the greater and the lesser Adane; Khanuvada; Pedhala; and Vīrāra. They added that a place called Uppalapallikā was a hamlet of Virara. [n46 §4; n03; CII VI pp. 115–116]

The eclipse is real; the editor of the plates checked it against the astronomical tables and found that the date “corresponds regularly to the 20th February AD 1049, when there was a lunar eclipse”. [n46 §4; CII VI p. 115] The plates are real too. They were dug up in 1956 “between the Church and the District Office at Thana”, passed to the Director of Archives in Bombay, and are kept in the Prince of Wales Museum (now the Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj Vastu Sangrahalaya). V. V. Mirashi edited them, first in Marathi in 1961 and then in the Corpus Inscriptionum Indicarum. [n46 §4; CII VI p. 114] So the first time the name of Virar appears in writing, it appears on a bronze sheet, cut by a royal engraver, on the night the moon went out.

Nothing on the plates describes the place. They do not say who lived at Virara, what they grew, what gods they kept or what language they spoke at home. The plates say only that it was a village worth taxing and worth giving away, that it had a hamlet, and that it belonged to Sopara’s district. This chapter is about everything that can honestly be said around those few words: the ground Virar stands on, the long silence before and after the plates, the Portuguese register that made Virar the most valuable estate in the Bassein lands, the Maratha capture of the hill above it, and the decades of Company rule before the railway arrived. Much of it is thin. Where the record is empty, the chapter says so and looks at what the neighbours were doing.

The ground: a hill, a river and an island that was not quite one

Jivdhan

Stand on the platforms of Virar station and look east. About a mile away, across the tracks and the market, a rocky hill rises out of the plain. The 1882 Gazetteer of the Bombay Presidency calls it Jivdhan; the town calls it Jivdani, after the goddess on its top. [n46 §1; Gaz XIV p. 116] Its height is quarrelled over. Wikipedia’s temple article gives about 1,500 feet, then concedes that more reliable sources give 728 feet, a figure from an environmental summary prepared for the Maharashtra Pollution Control Board in March 2020. [n45 §3; n49 §2c] The smaller number is the better one. The hill is not alone. The Gazetteer’s description of the district, written from the high ground, places “a cluster of hills of which Baronda, Jivdhan and Nilemora are the most marked peaks” north of the flat laterite-capped Tungar, which reaches 2,800 feet, and north of the lofty peak of Kamandurg at 2,160 feet. [n48 §2; Gaz XIII-1, physical description] Its Part II volume gives “Nilemora, Baronde and Jivdhan” heights of 1,500 to 2,000 feet; that is a round figure for the whole group, not a measurement of Jivdhan. [n46 §8; Gaz XIII-2 pp. 678–680]

These hills are the westernmost knuckles of the basalt country that climbs eastward to the Sahyadri. The Gazetteer of 1882 noticed that the trap rock was “easily quarried without blasting, and has been largely used on the Bombay and Baroda railway”, and that laterite capped the highest hills, “red, of very coarse grain”, soft when cut and hardening in the air. [n48 §2; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 48–49] The planners of 2010 called the whole region “Deccan Lava plateau” and noted traces of bauxite in the Tungar range. [n48 §3; VVSR CDP p. 26] The deep history of how the lava was laid down and the sea came and went over it belongs to Chapters 01 and 02; no source in the notes gives a geology of Jivdhan itself. [n48 §2 Gaps]

What the hill holds is known from the Gazetteer. On its top in 1882 were “ruins of fortifications visible from the railway”, and within them “some very old-looking caves and cisterns said to be the work of the Pandavs”. [n46 §1; Gaz XIV p. 116] The Gazetteer lists the caves among the remains of the district “either Buddhist or Brahmanic”, as “caves or cells … at Jivdhan in Bassein”, and lists Jivdhan as one of the two inland forts of the Bassein sub-division, with Kamandurg, beside the three sea forts of Arnala, Bassein and Kharlio. [n46 §1, §6; Gaz XIV pp. 10, 98] There is a second Jivdhan in the same volume, a fort at the Naneghat pass on the Shahapur side, whose rock-cut steps were blown away “at the time when (1803–1818) the Pendharis infested the country”. It is a different hill, eighty miles inland, and nothing said of it belongs to Virar. [n46 §1, §7; n45 §1]

The garden island

West of the hill the land falls to a plain so low that the city planners of 2010 put its average height at 1.5 to 2 metres above the sea, with a coastal strip “hardly 3 to 4 ft. above sea level”. [n48 §3; VVSR CDP pp. 24–25] Across this plain, in 1882, ran a narrow belt of exceptionally rich land. The Gazetteer describes “the strip of garden land about three miles broad, that runs from the Bassein creek to the Vaitarna”. Its middle part, including “Bolinj, Koprad and Umbrala in the north, Nirmal and Vagholi in the west, Gas in the south, and Sopara and Mardesh in the centre”, was “the garden or agar of Sopara”, bounded on the north by the agar of Agashi and on the south by the agar of Bassein. [n46 §1; n48 §1; Gaz XIV p. 314] Under the Portuguese, the Gazetteer adds, this whole strip was called the island of Bassein; before them it had been “the island of Sopara”. [n46 §1; Gaz XIV p. 314]

It was called an island because a backwater cut it off from the mainland. The channel “leaves the Bassein creek a little above the railway bridge and winds north to the Vaitarna”. In 1882 it had three local names along its length: the Manikpur creek in the south, the Sopara creek in the centre, and the Bolinj creek in the north. [n46 §1; n48 §1; Gaz XIV pp. 314–315] The Bolinj creek is Virar’s water. The note-takers who worked through these sources could not find which arm of it, if any, reached Virar village itself; the “Virar creek” of common speech is best read as the northern end of the Bolinj system, but that is an inference. [n46 §1 Inferences]

The island was never quite an island. In 1728 a Portuguese officer reporting on the defences complained that the creek could be forded at low water for about 4,000 feet between Sopara and Bolinj, and warned that unless it was dug out “as deep as the height of a man” it would silt up level with the fields. A Portuguese general, Louis de Mello Pereira, had built a bridge across it at Gokirva, today’s Gokhivare. [n37 §6; n40 A11, B8; Gaz XIV p. 322] By 1882 the warning had come true. The backwater “was once a considerable arm of the sea overflowing a large breadth of land on both banks”, but with “the mud-laden Vaitarna on the north and the Bassein creek or Ulhas river on the south, silting has been rapid”. Boats of ten to twelve tons still came at high tide as far as Gas in the south and Bolinj in the north. “At Sopara the creek is dry.” [n48 §1; Gaz XIV pp. 314–315] Teak rafts had once floated down to the Sopara landing, but “for many years boats have been unable to pass beyond Bolinj.” [n40 A11; Gaz XIV p. 338] The Gazetteer named one more cause of the silting, which belongs to the next chapter: “especially since the long railway embankment has dammed the flow of water from the south.” [n48 §1; Gaz XIV p. 315]

What the plain looked like in the last decades before the railway can be pieced together from the Gazetteer’s general description of the coast. Near Bassein the villages were “thriving and populous”; on the outskirts lay “dreary salt marshes” and “wide treeless rice flats … bare and untilled during most of the year”; nearer the villages were “palm gardens, sugarcane fields, and betel leaf or plantain orchards, sheltered by high hedges”, with ponds “gay with water lilies”. [n48 §1; Gaz XIII-1, general description] Within the garden island the lanes ran “four or five feet below the level of the fields”, winding “among mango and plantain orchards, fields of rice and sugarcane”, and “till the month of March the island is full of water”, with runnels from the garden wells crossing the lanes and “round ponds and long winding lakes, whose banks are fringed by high pile-supported Persian wheels”. [n48 §1; Gaz XIV p. 315] The wells of Bassein and Mahim, the Gazetteer says, “are much the best in the district” and “are watered by Persian wheels from unbuilt wells”. [n48 §1; Gaz XIII-1 p. 281] In 1881–82 the Bassein sub-division counted 191 ponds and 2,624 wells. [n21 §2; Gaz XIII-2 pp. 678–679]

The Vaitarna and the sea

North of Virar the land ends at the Vaitarna, which the Gazetteer calls “the most extensive inlet in the north Konkan”; for its last twenty miles it is “a fine broad river”, and the island fort of Arnala “commands the southern and main entrance” to it. [n46 §1; n48 §1; Gaz XIV p. 10; Gaz XIII-1] North of its mouth, the Gazetteer wrote, the shores turn flat, “with long sandy spits running into muddy shallows”, and “broad waters open a scene of almost perfect loveliness”. [n48 §2; Gaz XIII-1] The Tansa runs nine miles through the Bassein sub-division and falls into the Vaitarna at Chimana; it is tidal for many miles and navigable for eight, and Usgaon, Khanivade, Bhatane and Chimana were loading stations for vessels of fifty khandis, about 124 tons. [n48 §1, §3; Gaz XIII-1, Rivers] Those are the villages of Virar’s eastern hinterland, and in the nineteenth century they were river ports.

The sea at the river’s mouth was not stable. The Gazetteer records that “at the mouth of the Vaitarna … since 1724, four villages have been submerged”. [n48 §1–2; Gaz XIII-1 p. 281] It does not name them. Elsewhere the coast was built outward, not eaten away: the Gazetteer describes “the steady deposit of silt, from the mud-charged waters of the gulf, that has made these reclamations possible”, and says that salt land embanked from the sea needed “twenty or twenty-five years” before it sweetened into rice land. [n48 §1–2; Gaz XIII-1 p. 281] From Tungar, it added, a person could see “how large an area is still flooded at spring tides, and how completely the whole coast belt of rice-land is intersected by salt water channels”. [n48 §1; Gaz XIII-1] Virar sat on the inland edge of this living, shifting shore, between the rock of Jivdhan and the mud of the creeks.

Three worlds within an hour’s walk

It helps, before the history begins, to see the three kinds of country that met at Virar, because they explain who came to it and why. To the east were the hills and forests: the Gazetteer says that west of the Tungar range the forests were “extensive”, chiefly on the slopes of Tungar and Kamandurg and in Pelhar, Kaman, Chichoti, Poman and Paya villages. [n48 §1; Gaz XIII-1, Forests] Those forests were the home of the Varli (Warli), Katkari and Thakur peoples. To the west and south-west was the garden island with its wells and orchards. To the north and north-west were the salt flats, the creeks and the sea, the world of the Koli fishermen, the Agri salt-makers and the boat-builders of Agashi. The Gazetteer of 1882 caught all three meeting in one sentence about Virar’s Saturday market, “thronged by Kolis and Varlis, selling fish, garden stuff, and cloth”. [n46 §8; Gaz XIV p. 382] That sentence is the end of this chapter’s story, not its beginning; but the meeting it describes was made possible by the land.

Before the record

What has not been found

No prehistoric site has been found at Virar. The researchers who built the notes for this book looked for one and found nothing: no excavated Stone Age, Mesolithic, Neolithic or Chalcolithic site, and no published archaeological survey of Jivdhan hill by the Archaeological Survey of India, Deccan College or the University of Mumbai. [n46 §3; n01; n24] One search turned up a blog that spoke of “19 rock-cut monuments” at Jivdani, but it appeared to confuse other sites and was not used. [n46 §3 Gaps] The silence is not proof of absence. The notes’ own judgement is that it “may reflect unpublished or uncatalogued survey work rather than absence”. [n01] But it means that the human story of Virar before the first inscriptions has to be told from the neighbours.

The neighbours’ Stone Age

The nearest Stone Age evidence lies fifteen to thirty-five kilometres south, on the Salsette coast that is now north Mumbai. In the 1930s a naval officer, Commander K. R. U. Todd, found what he called Lower and Upper Palaeolithic tools at Khandivli (Kandivali), in a stratified sequence of clays and gravels, and in 1935 excavated at Marve, near Malad, where he found microliths, potsherds and hearths. [n01; Ruikar, Antiquity project gallery] Later archaeologists disputed parts of his work: H. D. Sankalia in 1959–60 found no handaxes at Khandivli and questioned the stratigraphy. [n01; n24] Statira Guzder and others reported Middle and Late Stone Age tools in the nala sections at Borivali and Kandivali and on the surface of the headlands at Erangal, Manori and Gorai. [n24] In 2018 the Salsette Archaeological Exploration Project of the University of Mumbai reported Middle Stone Age tools at Manori hill. [n46 §3; n01] None of these sites has an absolute date. [n24]

The Manori and Gorai headlands face the mouth of the Vasai creek. When the sea stood far lower in the last Ice Age, about 101.5 metres below today’s level some 14,500 years ago according to a core from the Bombay shelf, the creek did not exist and the hills of Salsette and the hills behind Virar were parts of one coastal plain. [n24; Nigam et al. 1992] The sea reached roughly its present level about 6,000 years ago. [n24; Kale & Rajaguru 1986] So the people who chipped stone at Kandivali could have walked to Jivdhan. Whether they did, nobody knows. The notes are right to call the Salsette sites “fair stand-ins for Vasai’s own Stone Age” and equally right to insist that a book “should say so explicitly”. [n01]

Who might have been here

The same caution applies to the peoples who later counted as the oldest inhabitants of the Virar side. The genetic and linguistic evidence discussed in Chapters 03 and T2 suggests that the Warli descend largely from the region’s ancient indigenous population, which later adopted an Indo-Aryan language. That is consistent with calling them a “first people”, but it does not prove that they were at Jivdhan, or that they made the microliths of Salsette. [n01] The notes’ own layered model, offered as a model and not as a finding, runs from ancient hunter-gatherers, to farming and rice spreading in, to coastal fisher-traders specialising on the creeks as Sopara’s port grew, and only later to the Brahmin and Kshatriya-claiming garden castes and Jain merchants. [n01] Every one of those layers would one day meet at Virar’s Saturday market.

In Sopara’s shadow

The port three and a half miles away

For most of the two thousand years before the railway, the place of consequence on this coast was not Virar but Sopara, which the 1882 Gazetteer placed “about three and a half miles south-west of the Virar station”. [n46 §1; Gaz XIV p. 314] The Gazetteer explained why people chose Sopara: “Its height above the surrounding salt marsh, its rich soil and fresh water, and its nearness to the holy Vaitarna, Jivdhan, Tungar and Nirmal must early have made Sopara a chosen settlement.” [n37 §6; Gaz XIV p. 316] Jivdhan, Virar’s hill, is in that sentence as one of the holy places that made Sopara’s site attractive. The Gazetteer did not say why Jivdhan was holy; it took it for granted.

Chapter 04 tells Sopara’s ancient story. For Virar the essentials are these. Sopara’s oldest securely dated layer is Mauryan, of the third century BCE, with Northern Black Polished Ware that ties it to the Gangetic world. [n01] At Madres (Mardes), in the centre of the agar, stood a stupa, and the Gazetteer records part of an Ashokan edict among the finds; the 2008 heritage survey speaks of the ninth edict. [n40 A8; CRIT Vol I; Gaz XIV p. 386] Under the Shilaharas Sopara was a district headquarters and a toll port. [n03] The Arab geographers of the tenth to twelfth centuries knew it. Al-Masudi, who visited the coast in 915–916, named “Subara” with Thana and Saymur (Chaul) as towns where the Lariya language was spoken; al-Biruni placed it correctly about 1030; and al-Idrisi in 1153 called “Soubara” “one of the emporiums of India”, a mile and a half from the sea, very well peopled, with pearl fisheries, and said that on a nearby island called Bara, which the Gazetteer takes to be the Sopara-Bassein island itself, grew coconut palms and costus. [n03; Gaz XIV pp. 321, 355–356]

Virar was the inland fringe of that island and of Sopara’s district. Whatever moved through the port in those centuries (rice, salt, coconuts and betel, laced shoes and leather sent to Arabia, pearls from the creeks, in the Gazetteer’s synthesis of the Arab sources) passed within a few miles of the village. [n03; Gaz XIII-2 pp. 429–430] The notes contain no evidence that Virar itself took part, and the chapter does not claim it.

Six centuries without a word

Between the Mauryan layers at Sopara and the plates of 1049 there is nothing at all about Virar. What was happening around it is known from inscriptions elsewhere on the coast. In the sixth century the Konkan Mauryas ruled from a capital called Puri. The Chalukya king Pulakeshin II took it by sea about 610–640. The Rashtrakutas became overlords next, and under them the Shilaharas of the north Konkan rose: Pulashakti and Kapardin II appear in the Kanheri cave inscriptions of 853 and 877 as subordinates of the Rashtrakuta king Amoghavarsha. [n03] The Shilaharas’ coast held, by their own reckoning, about 1,400 villages, and they ruled it for about 450 years, from roughly 810 to 1260, from their capital at Puri, with Sanjan, Thana, Sopara, Chaul and Lonad as their places of note. They were worshippers of Shiva. [n03; Gaz XIII-2 pp. 421–424]

The Kanheri caves, on the hills of Salsette across the creek, were a working Buddhist monastery through much of this period. The small plain caves and cisterns on Jivdhan are of the kind that monks and hermits cut all along this coast, and the Gazetteer listed them among the remains “either Buddhist or Brahmanic”. But it did not call them Buddhist, and no archaeologist has studied them. A modern heritage blog says the Jivdani cave “was originally part of a set of Buddhist caves”; that is an opinion, not a finding. [n46 §3; n10; Inditales] The notes’ instruction is plain: “Do not date the Jivdhan caves to the Buddhist period without an archaeological source.” [n46 §3] They are old, they were cut by people, and they were holy by the nineteenth century. That is all.

The eclipse grant of 1049

What Mummuniraja gave

The plates of 1049 belong to the middle of the Shilahara centuries. Their genealogy runs from the legendary Jimutavahana, the Vidyadhara who offered himself to Garuda on a rock (shila) to save a serpent and who gave the dynasty its name, through Kapardin I to Chhittaraja and his brothers Nagarjuna and Mummuni. [n46 §4; n03; CII VI p. 115] Chhittaraja had issued the Bhandup plate of 1026, whose donee was a Brahmin of the Chhandoga branch of the Sama Veda, from Nahur in Salsette; Mummuniraja, his brother, now ruled. [n03]

The main gift of 1049 went to six brothers of the Jamadagnya-Vatsa gotra, students of the Rig Veda: Narayana Pandita, Ramba Pandita, Lakshmidhara Pandita, Karnata Keshavabhatta, Gopati Pandita and Dhareshvarabhatta, sons of Tikkapaiya. [n46 §4; CII VI p. 115] Another summary of the grant counts fourteen Brahmin donees in all, some of them from Karahata, the Karhad of the Deccan plateau. [n03] The produce of the fields in the Sopara district went to particular Brahmins individually, partly in drammas and partly in grain. [n46 §4; CII VI p. 116] The researchers could not find the lines of the plate that say which donee received the fields at Virara, nor a translation of the relevant lines, because the copy they used is an OCR transcription and the printed volume of 1977 was not consulted. [n46 §4 Gaps]

What can be read is the geography. The editor identified the villages carefully. “Virara still retains its ancient name and is a station on the Western Railway.” Pedhala is Pelhar, “about 8 miles N.N.E. of Bassein”. Brihad-Adanika and Laghu-Adanika, following the epigraphist Dikshit, are the two parts of the modern village of Adane, “situated 8½ miles to the east of Virar”, divided by a small stream; Khandivali lies to the west of Adane and was the western boundary of Laghu-Adanika; Khanuvada lies to the north. “Uppalapallika, a hamlet of Virara, seems to have merged itself in Virara.” [n46 §4; CII VI p. 118] One detail does not fit: on the same page the editor identifies a Khanuvada in the Borivali group as “Khanivade, east of the same place”, so it is not certain which Khanuvada the Sopara-district list means, and whether it is the Khanivade on the Tansa north-east of Virar is left open. [n46 §4]

What the plate tells about Virara

Read cautiously, the plate says more than its few words suggest. The grant names fields by their levies, among them a Khairondha field and a Khamuvada field, and some paid in drammas, the Shilahara silver coin of Sasanian type. [n46 §4 Inferences; n03] So Virara in 1049 was a settled village with cultivated land that yielded a measurable surplus in grain and money. It had at least one hamlet. It was surveyed closely enough for a royal office to assign its produce to named men. [n46 §4 Inferences] And it lay inside a district still called after the port of Sopara, a sign that Sopara, though its trade was already shifting toward Thana, was still the administrative centre of the island. [n03; Gaz XIV p. 321]

The plate also shows the shape of the Virar side. Virara, Pelhar and Adane are the west, north-east and east of the modern eastern Vasai taluka. [n46 §4 Inferences] Between them lies almost the whole of the belt that the Portuguese would call the pragana of Solgão and that in the twenty-first century is Virar East. The villages of 1049 were not a scatter on an empty plain; they were a network, spaced along the foot of the hills, with the hill of Jivdhan in the middle of it.

Brahmins from the plateau

Who were the men who ate Virar’s rice? The plate’s donees were learned Brahmins, Vedic scholars, some from the plateau. [n03; n46 §4] The grant belongs to a pattern seen across the Shilahara coast: kings settling Brahmins from inland on coastal land. [n03] The Shilahara administration itself spoke many tongues. Its ministers’ names are largely Kannada and Telugu (ending in -aiya, like Tikkapaiya, the donees’ father), Kayasthas appear among its officers, its grants were written in Sanskrit, and the Gazetteer judged that the vernacular of the coast was “a corrupt Prakrit, the mother of the modern Marathi”. [n03; Gaz XIII-2 p. 428] So the language of a Virar cultivator in 1049 was an early form of Marathi; the language of his overlord’s charter was Sanskrit; and the names of the court around the king pointed south to Karnataka and Andhra. [n03]

The plate does not name the cultivators, and that silence is itself a fact of the period. Mummuniraja gave away their surplus without telling us who they were. Two later sources hint at the people who worked such land. The Samavedi donee of 1026 shows that Brahmins of the Sama Veda were already receiving grants on the coast, and the Samvedi Brahmins of later centuries would be the headmen of villages next to Virar. [n03] And a century later, a stone from Agashi would name the headmen of a neighbouring village by their own names.

Vattaraka and its headmen, 1150

A hundred and one years after the eclipse grant, in the Saka year 1072 (AD 1150), the cyclic year Pramoda, in the reign of the Shilahara king Haripaldev, a prince who held “the village of Vattaraka in Shurparaka” gave land to a scholar, the Upadhyaya Brahmadevabhatta, and had the gift cut on a stone. The stone, three feet three inches by one foot five, was found at Agashi and by 1882 stood in the Collector’s garden at Thana. Its witnesses include “Risi Mhatara, headman of Vattaraka village, Naguji Mhatara, Ananta Nayaka, and Changdev Mhatara”. [n46 §4; n40 A13; Gaz XIV p. 385 and n. 1] The prince’s own name is garbled in the OCR of the Gazetteer and cannot be given. [n46 §4]

The Gazetteer identified Vattaraka as “the modern Vatargaon, two miles north-west of Sopara”, and added that the French scholar Anquetil du Perron had noticed it in 1760 as “Outar”. [n46 §4; Gaz XIV p. 385 n. 1] Vatar lies on the coast road between Virar and Agashi and is counted today as one of Virar’s villages. So by the middle of the twelfth century two places on the Virar side, Virara and Vattaraka, had entered the written record as villages of the Sopara district. [n46 §4]

The stone of 1150 does something the plates of 1049 do not: it names local people. Risi Mhatara was the headman of Vattaraka; two other Mhataras and a Nayaka stood with him. They were witnesses to a royal act, men of standing in their village. Their name, Mhatara, matches the Mhatre surname that still occurs among Samvedi and Vadval families of the coast, and Nayaka matches Naik, but no source tests either link, and the notes mark the match as an inference only. [n40 A13; n46 §4] Surnames on this coast cross community lines, too: Mhatre and Naik are also Agri surnames in the Gazetteer’s lists, and Vartak, Mhatre, Naik and Patil occur among Samvedis, Pachkalshis, Agris and Kolis alike. [n49 §1a-bis, §1e] Risi Mhatara cannot be placed in any one of the later communities. He can only be placed in his village, in 1150, as the first person on the Virar side whose name survives.

Haripaldev, in whose reign the stone was cut, left another stone of 1149 built into a house plinth at Sopara, and is called on a stone from Agashi “chief of the Mahamandaleshvaras”. [n03; Gaz XIII-2 p. 426 n. 3] The king who had sent the scholar Tejakantha from Sopara to a literary congress in Kashmir, Aparaditya I, had reigned a few years before; his successor Mallikarjuna would leave a stone at Bassein dated 1160 recording a field given for the restoration of a temple, before he was defeated and killed by the general of the Gujarat king Kumarapala. [n03; Gaz XIII-2 pp. 426, 436–437] A merchant-friendly dynasty, which in 1094 had exempted the ships of two merchant-ministers from tolls at Sopara and other ports, was beginning to lose its grip on the coast. [n03; Gaz XIII-2 pp. 425–426]

Stones in the fields

The grant-makers of these centuries left more than copper plates and named stones. In 1882 the Gazetteer’s compilers found, “about 100 yards east of the station, on the dam of a rice field”, a stone two feet ten inches by one foot four, “with a roughly-cut cow and calf, a symbol of eternity or perpetuity, which, in some land-grant stones, takes the place of the ass-curse”. [n46 §3; Gaz XIV p. 382] The ass-curse was the threat carved at the foot of many Konkan grant stones against anyone who broke the gift; the cow and calf, the Gazetteer explains, could stand in its place. On the west bank of the Virar lake they recorded another carved stone, “about three feet long and nine inches broad”, with “a group of female figures” below, a pilaster above and a niche at the top. [n46 §3; Gaz XIV p. 382] And at Dahisar, “about six miles east of Virar”, they found a worn inscribed stone with a broken ass-curse whose date they could not read. [n46 §3; Gaz XIV p. 388]

None of these stones is dated. Taken together with the plates of 1049 and the Vattaraka stone of 1150, they are evidence that land was being granted, and the grants advertised in stone, across the Virar belt in the Shilahara and Yadava centuries. [n46 §3 Inferences] Whether the cow-and-calf stone, the carved figures by the lake or the Dahisar stone survive in 2026 is not known; no later source in the notes describes them. [n46 §3; P1]

A little further west the Gazetteer found the ruins of a larger place. “Bolinj was formerly a large town and probably had rich temples, but no remains were seen.” A Devanagari inscribed stone, two feet two inches by one foot by eight inches, was being used as a doorstep in the house of a Christian whom the compilers wrote down as “Juju Manu Lop, that is Joseph Manuel Lopez”, and carved stones were said to lie in the Devhara pond. [n46 §8; n40 A11; Gaz XIV pp. 341–342] Old raised stone foot-pavements were traced in the lanes near Bolinj. [n12; Gaz XIV] At Vagholi, a mile west of Sopara, an inscribed stone of fourteen illegible lines was taken from a well to the Collector’s garden. [n40 A7; Gaz XIV pp. 342, 419] A Yadava stone brought from Nila (Nala), about a mile north of Sopara, names the king Ramchandradev, whose reign the Gazetteer reads as 1280–1298, and names Shurparak twice. [n40 A15; Gaz XIV p. 387] The garden island of the Middle Ages was dotted with temples and grant stones, and the later builders, Portuguese and Indian alike, reused them as doorsteps and dumped them in ponds.

What is in a name

Ekvira, the Pandavas and the hero

Ask in Virar today where the town’s name comes from and the usual answer is the goddess Ekvira (Ekaveera, “Eka-vira”). Wikipedia’s Virar page says the name “is believed by some” to derive from her, and reports a tank called “Viraar Tirtha”, with a carved stone on its west bank, and a Puranic pilgrimage, the “Shurparaka Yatra”, said to end there. [n45 §2] The fullest version of the legend is attached to the goddess of the hill. During their exile, the story runs, the Pandava brothers of the Mahabharata came to Shurparaka, halted on the Vaitarna, and worshipped the goddess Ekaveera on the banks of the Viraar Tirtha; they cut caves in the hill and installed her there as “Bhagavati Jeevadhani”. [n45 §2] The Palghar district’s tourism page repeats the derivation. [n13]

The legend fits some evidence. The Gazetteer of 1882 records a carved stone on the west bank of the Virar lake, which may be the stone the modern Viraar Tirtha story points to, and caves on the hill “said to be the work of the Pandavs”. [n46 §3; Gaz XIV pp. 116, 382] Ekvira is a real goddess of the coast: Enthoven, writing in 1922, lists her among the tutelary goddesses of the Vadval division of the Pachkalshis, with Mahalakshmi, Vajreshwari and Jogeshwari. [n49 §1d; Enthoven III p. 165]

But the legend has two weaknesses. The first is that the Gazetteer, which offered an etymology for almost every village it described, never links Virar to Ekvira; it attributes the caves to the Pandavas and gives the name no meaning. [n45 §2] The second is the plate of 1049. Nine centuries ago the name was already plain Virara, with no “Eka” in front of it. [n46 §4 Inferences; n45 §2] The plate gives the name but no cult, so it cannot disprove the story; but any account that derives “Virar” from “Ekvira” has to explain why the supposed short form was already established in 1049. [n46 §4 Inferences] A derivation from the Sanskrit vira, “hero”, is possible and would sit as easily with Ekvira as without her, but no source makes it. [n45 §2] A third derivation, from a philosopher “Jeevan Virar”, appears only on low-quality websites and is not credible. [n45 §2] Wikipedia’s article on the goddess carries a “needs more citations” tag, and a search for a scholarly etymology of Virar found none. [n45 §2]

“Vihar” in the chronicle

Wikipedia adds one more early form. It says that Virar “is mentioned as Vihar” in the Mahikavatichi Bakhar, the Marathi chronicle of the north Konkan, citing a 2012 article by Dipesh Karmarkar, “Understanding Place Names in ‘Mahikavati’s Bakhar’”. [n46 §5; Wikipedia: Virar] The chronicle’s oldest chapters were written by Keshavacharya about 1448 for the revenue officer of Malad, and it was finished in the seventeenth century; it names 396 places, 336 of them in the north Konkan. [n03] The researchers searched the OCR of the Marathi text (Rajwade’s edition) for Vihar, Virar, Chandansar, Agashi, Nala and Kopri. They found Agashi, a “Nale” and a “Kopri” in a revenue list of villages round Thana and Salsette, which are probably not Vasai’s Nala and Kopri, and a “Virali” near Malad; they found no “Vihar” and no “Virar”. [n46 §5] The OCR is poor, and a miss is not proof that the word is absent; Karmarkar’s article itself could not be read. [n46 §5 Gaps] Until it is, “Vihar” is a reported identification, not an established one. [n45 §2]

The honest position

The honest position, then, is this. The name Virar is at least as old as 20 February 1049. Its meaning is unknown. The Ekvira and Pandava derivations are devotional traditions, recorded in modern sources and not in the nineteenth-century record. Uppalapallika, the hamlet named on the plate, has vanished into the village; the editor of the plates thought it had “merged itself in Virara”. [n46 §4] And one more name joined Virar’s in the record nearly seven centuries later, when a Portuguese clerk wrote “Virar Naringuy”. Of the two, Naringi survives as a living place-name, as the next sections show.

The long silence, 1150–1534

Yadavas and Delhi

After the Vattaraka stone, the record for Virar itself goes dark for nearly four hundred years. The neighbours are not silent, and their story frames whatever Virar was.

About 1260 the Shilaharas fell, and the north Konkan passed to the Yadavas of Devagiri, who governed it through viceroys “whose head-quarters were at Kalyan and Bassein”. Grants of 1273 and 1291 near Thana record gifts by two viceroys of the Yadava king Ramachandradeva, and stones of 1280 and 1288 by his officers were found near Bhiwandi and Bassein. [n03; Gaz XIII-2 p. 437] The stone from Nila naming Ramchandradev is one of them. [n40 A15] About 1290 Marco Polo described a “kingdom of Tana”, rich, its king in league with corsairs, exporting brown incense, cotton cloth and leather. [n03; Gaz XIV p. 356] By the fourteenth century, the Gazetteer says, Thana had replaced Sopara as “the chief centre of trade”, though Sopara “was still a place of consequence”. [n03; Gaz XIV p. 321]

Then came Delhi. Forces of the Delhi sultans reached the coast between about 1312 and 1318; Sanjan was sacked, and after Devagiri fell Mubarak Shah Khalji’s outposts reached the sea and occupied Thana and Salsette in 1318. [n03; Gaz XIII-2 pp. 438–439] In 1321–24 the Franciscan friar Jordanus travelled from Thana to Broach “by Supera”. At Sopara he buried four Franciscans executed at Thana, found Nestorian Christians and a church of St Thomas, and in a stay of fifteen days baptised and gave communion to about ninety Christians. [n03; Gaz XIV pp. 321–322] He and his companion Odoric reported that the “Saracens” had lately seized the whole country and “destroyed an infinite number of idol temples and likewise many churches”. [n03; Gaz XIII-2 pp. 439–440] That is a friar’s account, partisan and general, but it is the nearest description of the coast in Virar’s lifetime. The temples whose stones the Gazetteer would later find in Bolinj’s doorsteps and ponds may have fallen in this century, or in the Portuguese one, or both; the Gazetteer itself blamed both, saying that Muslims and then the Portuguese “destroyed temples and stone-faced reservoirs by the score”. [n03; Gaz XIII-2 pp. 428–429]

Gujarat and the Bimb kings

Real control from Delhi lapsed after about 1350, and Hindu chiefs went on granting land. [n03] From about 1390 the Gujarat sultanate held the coast through garrisons and tributary Hindu rais. In 1429 the Bahmani minister Malik-ut-Tujjar seized Mahim and Salsette, and the Gujarat sultan Ahmad Shah sent seventeen ships and an army under his son Zafar Khan, who retook Thana and drove the Deccanis out. [n03; Gaz XIII-2 pp. 441–442] Mahmud Begada (1459–1511) took the hill forts of north Thana about 1465, crushed a rebel’s raids on Mahim with the Bahmanis’ help in 1493, divided his realm about 1495 into five parts “one headed by Thana”, and about 1503–1507 “effected his designs against Bassein and Bombay”. [n03; Gaz XIII-2 pp. 442–443; Gaz XIV p. 28] Bassein timber went to Egypt to build the Mamluk fleet that fought the Portuguese in 1505–07. [n03; Gaz XIII-2 p. 449]

In this world the Mahikavatichi Bakhar sets its stories of the Bimb kings: Pratap Bimb arriving about 1138 and founding a capital at Mahim near Kelve, the Yadava prince Bimbdev of about 1294, and the settlement of the coast by the communities who claimed to have come with him. [n03] The kings and their dates are legendary or uncorroborated. But the place-name was real. An inscription of 1368 found at Deonar in Salsette names “Konkan-Bimbasthana”, and a stone found near Virar names “Bimbstan” too. [n03] The Gazetteer’s view was that copies of local grants referring to Bimb belong to about 1280, when the Yadavas were overlords, and that “the question is doubtful”. [n03; Gaz XIII-2 p. 437 n.] The notes’ instruction is to treat the Bimb story as a founding myth with a real kernel, and on no account to say that “Bimba founded Virar”. [n03; n46 §5 Inferences]

The Koprad stone, 1464

The stone near Virar is the only dated object from the Virar belt between 1150 and the Portuguese. It stood in 1882 in Koprad, a hamlet half a mile south-west of Bolinj, “near the house of the headman Bhiku Patil, a Samvedi Brahman”. It measured three feet five inches by one foot two by seven inches. It was dated in two eras at once: Saka 1386 and Hijri 868, that is AD 1464. Its script was Devanagari. It carried Muslim names and the names of “Mahim, Thana, Koprad and Bimbasthan”. [n46 §4; n03; n49 §1a; Gaz XIV pp. 210, 341–342]

The stone is small, but it holds a whole political world. A Hindu-script inscription, dated by both the Indian and the Islamic calendars, naming Muslims, under a sultanate whose officers sat at Mahim and Thana, and naming the old Bimb country: this is the bilingual, layered order of the Gujarat century on the Virar side. [n03 Inferences] And in 1882 it stood beside the house of a Samvedi Brahmin headman. Four hundred years after it was cut, the village of Koprad was still run by a family of the community that the 1026 plate had first shown receiving grants on this coast. Whether Bhiku Patil’s ancestors were headmen in 1464 the stone does not say.

Agashi and Arnala

West of Virar the Gujarat century left more. Agashi was, in the Gazetteer’s words, “probably an old timber trade and ship-building centre”, and about 1530 it built ships “as good as Portuguese ships, able to make the voyage to Europe” (the printed Gazetteer says “1580”, probably a slip for 1530). [n40 A2; n46 §5; Gaz XIV pp. 1–2] Its customs yielded £234 in 1536 and £32 in 1543. [n46 §5; Gaz XIV pp. 1–2] At the mouth of the Vaitarna, on the island the Portuguese would call Ilha das Vacas, the Cows’ Island, “about 1530, the Portuguese found Arnala a strong fort in the hands of the Gujarat Musalmans with domes and Saracenic arches”. [n46 §5; n45 §1; Gaz XIV pp. 10–11] A popular date of 1516 and a builder, Mahmud Begada, appear on Wikipedia and a Marathi fort site without a primary source; the Gazetteer supports only a Gujarat fort that stood before 1530. [n45 §4]

Virar between them is silent. Nothing in the notes names a ruler, a temple, a market or a family at Virar between 1150 and the Portuguese. The likeliest picture, drawn from the neighbours, is a rice-growing village at the foot of the hills on the road between Jivdhan and the creek, inside the old Sopara country, paying its dues first to Shilahara and Yadava officers and then to the sultanate’s men at Bassein, Mahim and Thana. That is inference, and it is marked as such. [n46 §5; P1]

The Portuguese arrive

Fire on the coast

The Portuguese came to the coast as raiders before they came as rulers. In 1526 they had a factory at Bassein. In 1528–29 Heitor da Silveira, after destroying a Gujarat fleet near Bandra, entered the Bassein river by night, stormed its cannon-defended works, beat 3,500 men and “plundered and burnt Bassein”. [n03; Gaz XIII-2 pp. 450–451] In 1530–31 they destroyed Agashi, burning the town and three hundred boats, and on the way back from a failed attack on Diu, Antonio de Saldanha burnt Bulsar, Tarapur, Kelve-Mahim and Agashi again. Faria y Souza records a second destruction of Agashi in March 1531. [n03; n40 A2; Gaz XIV pp. 1–2; Gaz XIII-2 p. 451] Facing the Mughal emperor Humayun in the north, the Gujarat sultan Bahadur Shah ceded Bassein and its dependencies to the Portuguese by a treaty signed on 23 December 1534 aboard the galleon São Mateus; da Cunha gives the treaty as 1533, and the date of the first possession is variously given. [n03; n40 §2; Tombo p. 327 n. 9] The treaty kept one clause for Islam: the 5,000 larins of revenue assigned to the mosques “was to continue to be so applied”. [n37 §2; Danvers vol. 1 p. 416]

With Bassein came its hinterland, and Virar with it. The Portuguese divided the land north of Bassein, “limited by the Ulhas and Vaitarna rivers”, into the town’s own district (the caçabé), the coastal districts of Agashi and Sopara, and four inland praganas behind the town: Anjor, Camão (Kaman), Herá and Solgão. The historians André Teixeira and Silvana Pires describe the inland praganas as “a flat and rather flooded territory in the rainy months, suitable for large rice production”, with the Ghats as a natural barrier behind. [n46 §1–2; Tombo p. 327] Virar fell in the pragana of Solgão.

Converts

The decades after 1534 changed the religion of half the garden island, and the reasons matter for every later chapter of Virar’s history. Chapter 34 tells the conversion of the Samvedi Brahmins who became the Kuparis; here the question is what reached the Virar side, and why.

The first missionaries were Franciscans. António do Porto, between 1534 and 1552, “destroyed 200 temples, made over 10,000 converts, built twelve churches, and, by founding orphanages and monasteries, secured a supply of native priests”; the temples he destroyed were at Agashi, Bassein and Thana. [n37 §2; Gaz XIII-2 p. 461] In 1535 he founded at Agashi an orphanage for forty boys, Nossa Senhora da Luz, Our Lady of Light; da Cunha says that “Agasi was the first place of this district visited by Franciscan missionaries”. [n40 A2; n37 §2; da Cunha p. 159] In April 1540 a Gujarat force under the general the Portuguese called “Bramaluc” raided the district and was repelled at Bassein; by the Portuguese accounts it burned the Agashi orphans, and da Cunha tells it as five who refused conversion to Islam and were burnt, “a batch of Christian martyrs … consisting entirely of native converts”. [n40 A2; n37 §2; da Cunha pp. 159–160] The Jesuits came after 1548, with a seminary at Bassein, and between 1570 and 1590 “took pains to make Brahman and other high-caste converts, knowing that if the Brahmans became Christians, many of the lower classes would follow”; baptisms at Bassein rose “from 1600 in 1573 to 9400 in 1588”. [n37 §2; Gaz XIII-2 p. 461; Gaz XIV p. 41]

The parish churches outside the fort followed, though their dates conflict between sources: Nirmal 1557 or later, Agashi 1568, and the church of the Holy Spirit at Nandakhal, between Agashi and Nirmal, about 1573. [n40 §2, A3] Nandakhal’s church was the nearest parish to Virar’s west, and in later centuries it would serve Virar’s Catholics. [n46 §6]

Why did people convert? The sources disagree, and both sides must be heard. The Portuguese stacked incentives and pressure together. Converts, “especially high caste converts, were treated with honour and distinction, and, for the first fifteen years after conversion, the poorer class of Christians were freed from the payment of tithes and first fruits”. [n37 §2; Gaz XIII-2 p. 462] “Men of rank were admitted into the best Portuguese society”, married Europeans and received land on small quit-rents, while “those of low birth were left in a state little removed from servitude”. [n37 §2; Gaz XIII-1 p. 202] Royal orders of 1546 and 1555, enforced from 1581, banned idols, Hindu feasts, Brahmin preaching and cremations; “half of the property of a man found with idols went to the church”. [n37 §2; Gaz XIII-2 pp. 462–463] Orphan laws handed Hindu children without parents to the missions; from 1560 the Inquisition worked through a commissary at Bassein. [n37 §2, §4; Gaz XIV p. 41] Money flowed to the missions: from 1550 the Crown gave the Bassein Franciscans 2,070 pardaus a year from a revenue that before the conquest had lit the mosques of Bassein. [n37 §2; da Cunha p. 235]

Against this, the Gazetteer’s compilers concluded that “force may have been used in some cases, but the Portuguese accounts seem to show that persuasion and interest were the chief means of conversion”, and pointed out that Bandra stayed Hindu till about 1580. [n37 §3; Gaz XIII-1 p. 201 n. 2; Gaz XIII-2 p. 462] The Goan historian A. K. Priolkar, writing in 1961, argued that conviction played “a very insignificant role”; the Jesuit historian H. Heras argued the opposite. [n37 §3] A Jesuit’s own letter from Goa in 1547 admitted that many converts came “purely for temporal advantage”, slaves for their freedom, others for protection or “for the sake of a turban, a shirt”. [n37 §2; Priolkar p. 55] On the Hindu side the families who later returned told a darker story: the Uraps said “their forefathers were forced to become Christians by having pieces of flesh thrown into their wells”. That is a family tradition, recorded in 1882, three hundred years after the events. [n37 §3; n49 §1g; Gaz XIII-1 p. 201 n. 2] The notes’ fair summary is that payment, tithe relief, land, status, protection and the orphan laws pushed the poor and the vulnerable; high-caste converts were honoured and drawn by ceremony; forced baptism is documented most clearly for orphans; and no source documents a mass forced baptism in Bassein by name. [n37 §3 Inferences]

Conversion also emptied land. The Jesuit Goez, about 1603 (the Gazetteer also gives 1630), wrote that “the persecution of the Portuguese had made many Hindus, Mussulmans and Parsees abandon their homes and live in the dominion of Shah Jehan”, so that “between Bassein and Damaun there are few natives and the greater part of the village lands being uncultivated”. The Gazetteer added that, if the account was correct, “the districts soon recovered their prosperity”. [n37 §2; Gaz XIII-2 pp. 463–464]

Who in Virar converted? The notes cannot say for the village itself. The Gazetteer’s list of “the chief castes of which the present Christian population is composed” (Brahmans, Prabhus, Pachkalshis, Chaukalshis, Sonars, Khatris, Bhandaris, Kharpatils or Agris, Kunbis, Kumbhars, Nhavis, Dhobis, Kolis, Bhois, Mhars and Chambhars, with “the bulk of them” Bhandaris, Kolis and Kunbis) is a list for the whole Bassein district. [n49 §1g; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 200–201] What the 1882 footnote on the Urap Agris shows, as the chapter will come to, is that by the early nineteenth century Virar held Christian Agris. Converts did not leave their castes behind: the Gazetteer says the lower classes kept to their old caste distinctions in marriage, and that “until lately the feeling of the impurity of the Mhars remained so strong that in some places they were not allowed to draw water from the village well or to enter the church”. [n49 §1g; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 200–201]

The making of the Kuparis on the Virar side

For the villages west of Virar, the garden villages from Bolinj and Umrale to Vatar, Wagholi, Mardes, Rajodi, Nandakhal and Agashi, the conversions of these decades created a people who are still there: the Kuparis, the Samvedi Christians. Their story deserves to be told in its own right here, because more of Virar’s modern Catholic population descends from them than from any other group, and because the evidence for how they came to be is better than for almost anyone else on the coast.

The best period evidence for who they were comes from the Gazetteer of 1882, more than three centuries later. At Sopara “the husbandmen … are Christians, many of them converted Samvedi Brahmans”; at Agashi, Vatar and Koprad about seventy-five families of “Native Christians originally Samvedis and Pachkalshis” lived beside about eighty-five Hindu Samvedi families, and the Christians were the “gardeners who grow the fruit”. [n34 §1; Gaz XIV p. 315; Gaz XIII-1 p. 395] So the ancestors of the Kuparis of the Virar villages were Samvedi Brahmins, a landed farming community and not a priestly one, and Pachkalshis, the garden caste whose Hindu branch the Vadvals represent; the Gazetteer did not separate the two sharply. [n34 §1 Inferences] The Gazetteer’s district-wide verdict was that the Christians left by the Portuguese were “chiefly converted Brahmans, Prabhus, Pachkalshis and Kolis”, though “the bulk of them are Bhandaris, Kolis and Kunbis”. [n34 §1; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 63, 200] Against this stands the Goan scholar da Cunha, who wrote in 1876 that among Bassein’s Catholics “there is no descendant of any high-class native”; he was a hostile and caste-conscious witness writing in the middle of a quarrel between Goan and local Catholics, and his claim is an opinion, not a count. [n34 §1; da Cunha pp. 250–251] Modern historians stress that most converts on this coast were cultivators, fishers and toddy-tappers. The fair reading is that “converted Samvedi Brahmins” has genuine nineteenth-century support for the northern villages, but describes part of the community, not all of it. [n34 §1]

How were they converted? The Jesuits’ own letters, written from Bassein at the time, are frank. In 1558 Fr Gonçalo Rodrigues wrote that “those who come to conversion are commonly the poorest and most needy, and are converted more drawn and moved by hope of some interest and remedy of life than by preaching, which they do not understand”, and described the method: “we set them the snares of human things and, once caught in them, we teach them the divine … we invite the rustics, farmers and those who live in woods and hedges”. At a new Christian village near Thana converts were given houses, cheap land, clothes, rice until their first harvest, seed and oxen. [n34 §3; Documenta Indica IV pp. 98–102] In 1562 Fr Francisco Cabral wrote that the Portuguese of Bassein, who “all have villages”, had at first opposed conversion, fearing it would empty their estates; after preaching, “the inhabitants who before would riot if a vassal of their villages was made [Christian] now come to beg us to go and convert their villages”. Converts were baptised in procession with the captain and the whole city, with garlands, psalms and flutes, and then feasted. [n34 §3; Documenta Indica V pp. 628–631] The historian Sidh Mendiratta concludes that between about 1570 and 1620 the Jesuits and Franciscans converted “a majority of the population” of Salsette and of the Bassein and Agashi subdivisions, that “whole villages were converted, normally following the conversion of the respective ‘matará’ or headman”, and that converted farmers and fishermen received particular benefits. [n34 §3; Mendiratta 2017 p. 211] He also notes that the harshest Goan laws “were never enforced in the Northern Province’s subdivisions closest to the mainland”, a caveat that matters for the northern villages. [n34 §3; Mendiratta p. 211]

Three ways of conversion thus ran side by side: livelihood grants to the poorest, pageant baptisms for leading men who expected to be honoured, and landlords bringing in their tenants. A landed Samvedi community would fit the second and third best. [n34 §3 Inferences] The census report of 1931, writing of the Bassein coast, explained what happened to names: “in many villages every tenant in the village was baptized and given the surname of the owner of the village, e.g., Demonte, Pereira, Ferreira”. [n34 §5; Census 1931 Vol VIII Pt I pp. 363–364] No document names a Samvedi convert, dates the conversion of any single Kupari village, or says whether the Samvedis were baptised in one wave or over generations. [n34 §3 Gaps]

The word “Kupari” itself appears in no text the researchers could find between 1876 and 1931. [n34 §2] The usual derivation is from the Portuguese compadre, “godfather” or “co-father”, feminine comadre; it is unproven, but it fits the fact that the Christians’ kinship vocabulary was Portuguese, with padrinho and madrinha for godparents in 1882 and padron and madron in Grierson’s word list of 1905. [n34 §2; Gaz XIII-1 p. 210] A second, unsourced folk etymology, “those who drank from the well (kup)”, ties the name to the old story of conversion by polluted wells; it is folklore, and many in the community find it offensive. [n34 §2] In 1882 the Christians’ upper classes called themselves “Firgi”, from Firangi; the lower classes used their caste names; and Hindus called them Firangis or, more often, Kiristans. [n34 §2; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 199–200]

The Kupari villages’ parishes date from these decades. Agashi, where the Franciscans began in 1535, had its church by 1568; Nandakhal’s Holy Spirit church, built about 1573 “primarily for the convenience of those parishioners of Holy Cross who lived closer to Nandakhal”, served the villages nearest Virar; Nirmal’s Holy Cross served the south. [n40 A2, A3, A1] The community’s own list of thirteen Kupari places is Nirmal, Agashi, Nandakhal, Gass, Bhuigaon, Umrale, Nanbhat, Shirlaiwadi, Bolinj, Mardes, Wagholi, Gomes Ali and Rajodi; a parish table adds Vatar. [n40 §1] Navapur and Narangi, on Virar’s creek side, are not on it: in the record they are Agri and Koli villages, and Navapur was a village of the Urap Agris. [n40 §1, C2; n49 §3c]

The register of 1727–1730

A last inventory

Between 1727 and 1730, a decade before they lost the province, the Crown’s officers at Bassein drew up a register of everything the king owned and leased out in the jurisdiction. Its title page reads “Foros das Aldeas e mais propriedades que tem Sua Magestade … nesta jurisdição de Baçaim”, “rents of the villages and other properties that His Majesty holds … in this jurisdiction of Bassein”. It lists 362 titles: 36 in the caçabé of Bassein, 28 in Agaçaim (Agashi), 4 in Sopará, 12 at Thana, 8 at Mahim, 3 in Bombay, 8 in Karanja, 101 on the island of Salsette, 21 in the pragana of Solgão, 21 in Herá, 29 in Camão, 25 in Anjor, 36 in Panchana, 20 in Cairana and 10 in Sabaio. [n46 §2; Tombo pp. 338–339] For each title it gives the folio, the lease-holder, the holding, the tenure, the annual rent in pardaus, larins and reis, and marginal notes. [n46; Tombo] André Teixeira and Silvana Pires published a transcription in 2007, open to all. [n46; Tombo]

The register is a snapshot taken just in time. From the early 1730s, the same authors write, the Marathas began occupying the more inland praganas, and the Estado could neither pay nor send an expedition. [n46 §2; Tombo p. 336] So the Tombo de Baçaim is the last Portuguese picture of the inland Virar belt before 1739. [n46 §2]

Aldeia Virar Naringuy

At folio 121 the register records the aldeia “Virar Naringuy”. Its lease-holder is “Pedro de Meneses (D.)”, Dom Pedro de Meneses, a nobleman. Its rent is 2,000 pardaus a year, reckoned at four and a half larins to the pardau and paid in quarters of 666 pardaus 3 larins. The table does not state the tenure, and no marginal note could be attributed to this row. [n46 §2; Tombo, PDF p. 33]

Two thousand pardaus was a great deal. It is the highest rent among the 21 titles of the Solgão pragana. When the researchers parsed every row of the published table and sorted the whole register by rent, it came out as the highest rent in the entire jurisdiction. The next were the caçabé of Bombay at 1,432.5 pardaus (fol. 65), the pacarias of Bandrastar and Cascarida at 1,400 (fol. 27v), Chandansar at 1,128 (fol. 123v) and Mar at 936 (fol. 82). The parse may have missed rows whose cells were split in the PDF, so “the highest in the register” carries that small caution. [n46 §2]

Dom Pedro held more than Virar. In the same pragana he leased “Valla” or “Villa” (fol. 117, 200 pardaus), “Moura” (fol. 117v, 150) and “Val Grande e Val Pequeno” (fol. 122v, 150). His holdings in Solgão added up to about 2,500 pardaus a year. [n46 §2] He was one of the great lessees of the province, and Virar was the jewel of his estate.

Why Virar was worth so much the register does not say. The researchers’ inference is that a rent so far above its neighbours suggests a large area of rice and garden land, comparable with the biggest aldeias on Bassein island. [n46 §2 Inferences] The Gazetteer’s description of Virar a century and a half later, “a rich village well-wooded and with many patches of sugarcane”, is consistent with that. [n46 §8; Gaz XIV p. 382] Good rice land under the hill, sugarcane, palm groves, the road from the hill country to the coast: these are reasonable guesses, and they are guesses. [P1]

The second word survives. “Naringuy” is probably the place still called Naringi or Narangi, a part of Virar with a Naringi Talav and a Naringi Zilla Parishad school listed in the 2008 heritage survey of the sub-region. [n46 §2 Inferences; n40 B11; CRIT Vol I] Whether Naringuy in 1727 was a hamlet, a tank or a family name the register does not say. [n46 §2] In the twenty-first century Narangi is described as a village of the Mangela Koli fishing community, and its revenue limits include an island in the Vaitarna creek. [n49 §3c; n48 §5] The register thus links the two halves of Virar’s later geography: the inland village and the creek-side fishing hamlet, leased together as one estate.

The Solgão run

The 21 titles of the pragana Solgão run in folio order from 116v to 126v. Read in order, with their holders and rents in pardaus a year, they are the clearest picture that exists of the country east and north of Virar under the Portuguese. [n46 §2; Tombo PDF pp. 32–33]

Anchola (fol. 116v), 620, held by the procurator of the Colégio de São Paulo Novo at Goa, a Jesuit house. Valla or Villa (117), 200, and Moura (117v), 150, both Dom Pedro’s. Inty Gocurvem (118), 375 pardaus 2 larins 30 reis, held in enfiteuse (a hereditary emphyteutic lease) by Fernão de Silveira e Meneses. Satuly e Morumba (118v), 90, held “for lives” by a woman named Maria. Balião (119), 400, D. Antónia Pereira da Silveira, for lives. Ambalvary (119v), 170, Fernão de Silveira e Meneses again in enfiteuse. Danoa (120), 250, Jorge Pereira Coutinho, for lives. A piece of “terra salgada Valvão”, salt land (120v), leased to the Jesuit college of Bassein for 5 pardaus. Virar Naringuy (121), 2,000. Matunga (121v), 100, João Baptista Lopes de Lavre, for life. Tulinge (122), 160.5, António de Melo in enfiteuse. Val Grande e Val Pequeno (122v), 150, Dom Pedro. Dainca Talauly (123), 280, held by the Jesuit Procurador Geral of the Goa province. Chandanaçar or Sandanaçar (123v), 1,128, held by the same Jesuit procurator as “administrator of the Royal Hospital of Goa”. Vancem (124), 132.5, Isabel de Oliveira in enfiteuse. Peral (124v), 200, Maria do Rosário, for lives. Sirgão or Sirigão (125), 858 pardaus 0 larins 20 reis, D. Tomás Manuel de Távora, for lives. Two small terras, Batoly (125v) and Antulicar (126), “in the aldeia Gocorvem”, leased to the Jesuits at 15 pardaus each. And Cassumba (126v), 850, D. Maria Soares de Brito, for lives. [n46 §2; Tombo PDF pp. 32–33]

The researchers’ candidate matches for these names, by sound and context only, are: Chandanaçar for Chandansar, Sirgão for Shirgaon, Anchola for Achole, Tulinge for Tulinj, Inty Gocurvem and Gocorvem for Gokhivare, and Peral, possibly, for Pelhar. [n46 §2 Inferences] None is asserted by the register. If they are right, the Solgão run is the country of modern Virar East and the Nallasopara–Vasai east: Chandansar and Shirgaon under the hills, Tulinj and Achole to the south, Gokhivare towards Vasai.

The marginal notes add texture. Chandansar’s 1,128 pardaus was paid in uneven quarters, 514 in February and 514 in July and 100 in October. Shirgaon’s rent was paid in two quarters, February and June, 429 pardaus 10 reis each; its holder was then in a second “life” of the lease, during that of D. Ângela da Silva, by a carta de aforamento and the confirmation of a purchase. Vancem was re-vested on 14 October 1728 in Francisco Moreira, nominated by his grandmother Isabel de Oliveira. Peral’s holder was “foreira na segunda vida”, a tenant in the second life. [n46 §2; Tombo PDF p. 34] (The mapping of these notes to rows is the researchers’, made by order and by the quarter-rent amounts, which tie out exactly for Chandansar and Shirgaon. [n46 §2])

These details show how the inland estates worked. Leases ran “for lives”, usually three, passing from holder to nominated heir, grandmother to grandson, and could be bought and sold within those lives. [n46 §2; n37 §2] The tenants who actually ploughed the fields do not appear at all. The Gazetteer’s summary of Portuguese tenure is blunt: the grants were nominally for three lives, and “no right in the land was conceded to the husbandmen or tenants”; the English traveller Fryer in 1675 described the gentry living “like petty monarchs”, holding the people “in a perfect state of villainage”. [n37 §2; Gaz XIII-2 p. 460; Gaz XIII-1 p. 202] The 2,000 pardaus of Virar Naringuy was squeezed from the rice of people whose names no Portuguese clerk thought worth writing down.

The Jesuits loom large in the run. They held Anchola through their Goa college, Dainca Talauly and Chandansar through their provincial procurator, the salt land at Valvão through the Bassein college, and two small terras at Gokhivare. Teixeira and Pires note the Jesuit hold on “many and the best-populated” aldeias of the province. [n46 §2, §6; Tombo p. 337] The most striking entry is Chandansar, the second-richest estate in Solgão after Virar itself, whose rent went to the Royal Hospital of Goa. [n46 §2] A village under Jivdhan was paying for the care of the sick in the Portuguese capital six hundred kilometres to the south.

Pragana Herá

East of Solgão lay the pragana of Herá, another 21 titles at folios 127 to 137. Its rents were smaller. They include Calambona, 30 (fol. 127); Mandovi de Herá, 340 (127v); Vergar, Binar e Gotegar, 30 (128); Caner, that is Kaner, 235, held for lives by Francisco de Mendonça Bacelar (128v); Caranjavana, 70 (129); Hera or Era, 25 (129v); Batana Belvar, 150 (130); Usagão, Usgaon, 200, held by Ana de Sacadura Escovar (130v), who also held Chandip or Sandipo, 140 (131); Casipo Cempor, 150 (131v); Arnem Candoly, 150 (132); Merem, 50 (132v); Majoly, 160 (133); Nausem, 120, held by the capitão-mor do campo, the captain-major of the field forces, by right of his office (133v); Saibana, 130 (134); Parol Dapoly, 220 (134v); Vanoty, 18 pardaus 3 larins (135); Sirauly Pornuquem, 60 (135v); Sircanacer, 103 (136); Sircar, 100, held in enfiteuse by André Toscano (136v); and Tilery, 250 (137). [n46 §2; Tombo PDF p. 33]

Kaner, Usgaon and Parol are the villages of Virar’s far east, along the Tansa; Chandip, Usgaon and Parol are still named among the river villages that flood each monsoon. [n46 §2; n48 §2] Usgaon was the Tansa river-port of the nineteenth century, and in the twentieth its dam would supply Virar’s water. [n48 §3] The Toscano of folio 136v, a licenciado (a man with a university degree), had taken over several hortas (gardens) by purchase, recorded in a margin note of 7 May 1727, as well as the aldeia of Sircar. [n39 §1] His surname matches the Vasai Christian surname Tuscano, but nothing shows that any Vasai family descends from him. [n39 §1] The pragana of Camão began at folio 137v with Pomão, 210, Paloly, 150, and Camão itself, 377 pardaus 3 larins 45 reis, and later lists a “Compry” held by Francisco Ferrão at 220 (fol. 142), which may be a Kopri, though it is inland and not the coastal Koprad. [n46 §2]

The Agashi pacarias and the coast

The coast north and west of Virar was listed not as aldeias but as pacarias in the caçabé of Agashi, 28 titles at folios 38v to 54v. [n46 §2; Tombo PDF p. 25] Among them were Vagoly, 146 (fol. 41v, Manuel de Abreu de Lima), and Vastal Vagoly, 60 (fol. 45, Fernão da Silveira e Meneses), two halves of Wagholi; Vatar Rajoly, 700, held for lives by Manuel Varjão da Fonseca (fol. 45v), the highest rent in the Agashi list; Vastar Vendegana, 45 (fol. 47); Mardes Sirgoly, 180, Lucas de Almeida de Andrade (fol. 47v); Nala Magir, 157, Miguel Francisco Mira de Vasconcelos (fol. 43); Sancardão Poir, 256; Cholna Roba, 130, held by the Jesuits (fol. 49); and Bolinja e terra Belvar, 450, leased for lives to D. Maria Henriques Coelho da Silva (fol. 54v). [n46 §2; n40 §2; Tombo]

The pacaria of Agashi itself, 450 pardaus, was held by a woman, D. Domingas Pereira de Azevedo (fol. 42). She also held “the rent of the coloria of Agaçaim, Ilha das Vacas, Temby” at 300 (fol. 41), where the coloria appears to have been a levy on the Koli fishing and boat people of Agashi and Arnala island, and a rent on the wild palms of Agashi at 300 (fol. 44v). [n46 §2; n40 A2] (That reading of coloria is an inference from the word and its place; the register does not define it.) Sopara, once the capital of the Konkan, appears as a single title: “caçabé de Sapora e seu mandovi”, 400 pardaus, held by Nicolau de Melo Pereira (fol. 55). [n46 §2]

These pacarias are, almost one for one, the Kupari villages. Vatar, Bolinj, Wagholi, Mardes, Nala and Agashi are the places where, a century and a half later, the Gazetteer found Christian plantain-growers who were “originally Samvedis and Pachkalshis”. [n40 §1, §2] The register therefore names the Portuguese lords under whom the Kuparis’ great-grandparents tilled: Varjão da Fonseca at Vatar, Coelho da Silva at Bolinj, Abreu de Lima and Silveira e Meneses at Wagholi, Almeida de Andrade at Mardes, Pereira de Azevedo at Agashi. If the census report of 1931 is right that tenants took their landlords’ surnames, some Kupari families’ Portuguese names may go back to holders like these; but no source ties any surname to any Kupari village, and the notes treat the match as unproven. [n34 §5; n39 §1] One native name does surface in the register’s Bassein section: a horta in the pacaria of Gris held by “Jasaman Naique”, a rare trace of a local landholder. [n40 B9]

So, on the eve of the Maratha war, the Virar side was carved into a dozen or more leaseholds. Vatar, where Risi Mhatara had been headman in 1150, paid 700 pardaus to a Fonseca; Bolinj, the old town with its buried temple stones, paid 450 to a Coelho da Silva; the Kolis’ boats at Agashi and Arnala paid a levy to a Pereira de Azevedo; and Virar with Naringi paid 2,000 to a Meneses. The names of the lease-holders (Pereira, Melo, Meneses, Coutinho, Almeida, Brito) are the same stock as the Vasai Christian surnames of later centuries, but whether any local family took its name from a landholder or a godfather is not documented anywhere the researchers could reach. [n39 §1]

What the register does not tell

The register gives no populations, no house counts and no names of cultivators. [n46 §2 Gaps] It does not say whether Virar had a church, or whether its tenants were Christian or Hindu. The table does not spell out “Pelhar”, “Adane” or “Koprad” in any form the researchers could match with confidence; and a “Sanvar Palle Talauly” held by the Jesuits at folio 105, which an earlier note linked to Pelhar, lies in the Salsette block of the register, not in Solgão, and should not be used for Pelhar. [n46 §2 Inferences, Gaps] The original register at the Torre do Tombo in Lisbon or the Historical Archives of Goa was not consulted. [n46 §2 Gaps] What it does tell, beyond doubt, is that in the last years of Portuguese rule the estate called Virar Naringuy was the most valuable on the books.

The church, the well and the tower

The Portuguese left their mark on Virar’s ground, and a century and a half after they left, the Gazetteer’s compilers could still see it. “About fifty yards south of the station, are the remains of a Portuguese church and a Portuguese well, and on a knoll about a mile to the south, stands a notable Portuguese tower or fortified mansion. … On another rocky hill, a little further east, are the ruins of a Portuguese fort.” [n46 §6; Gaz XIV pp. 382–383] Two miles east of Jivdhan, south of the village of Shrigaon (Shirgaon), stood “an old Portuguese fort”. [n46 §3; Gaz XIV p. 383]

Of the church nothing more is known. No source reached by the researchers names its saint, its builder, its date or its parish, and no Portuguese document describes it beyond the register’s rent line for the aldeia. [n46 §6] It stood not in a fort-town but in the fields, fifty yards from where the railway would later run. The notes infer that Virar’s Christians were served by a village chapel that the Maratha conquest destroyed or left to decay, and warn that any claim that it was a parish church is unsupported. [n46 §6 Inferences] Whether “Naringuy” in the register refers to a chapel or a parish is unknown. [n46 §6 Gaps]

The “tower or fortified mansion” a mile south is just as obscure. Fortified manor houses of lease-holders stood elsewhere on the Bassein coast; a tower for Dom Pedro de Meneses or his agent would fit the register, but nothing says so. [P1] The fort on the hill east of Virar and the old fort at Shirgaon may have been watch-posts on the inland edge of the province. A modern Marathi trekking page places a small post, Veer Shirgaon Kot, about seven kilometres from Virar station between Jivdhan and Mandvi, with a ruined two-storey building about thirty feet square, and says the Portuguese built such posts in the sixteenth century; the page is uncited. [n46 §3; durgbharari.in]

The fortifications on Jivdhan itself are a puzzle of their own. The Gazetteer saw ruins on the hilltop and listed Jivdhan as an inland fort, but did not say whether the fort was Portuguese, Maratha or older. [n46 §6] A modern fort website says a fort named Jivdhan was “found” there in the late seventeenth century and was later transformed into the temple; that source is of low reliability. [n45 §3] What is certain is that by 1738 there was a fort on Jivdhan worth attacking, and men inside it willing to defend it.

The Portuguese worried about their northern frontier long before the Marathas came. The 1728 report that complained of the fordable creek between Sopara and Bolinj also described Sopara as “a fortified post with four wooden stockades”, General Louis de Mello Pereira’s watch-towers of coconut-palm stems, and his bridge at Gokirva, unguarded. [n37 §6; n40 A11, B8; n12; Gaz XIV p. 322] The island of Bassein was being defended with palm trunks and stockades against an enemy who was already in the hills.

The fall of Jivdhan, 21 March 1738

Arnala first

The Maratha conquest of the Province of the North, told in full in Chapters 08 and 09, began at Virar’s doorstep. “In 1737 they seized the small fort of Arnala to the north of Bassein,” the Gazetteer says, “and soon after, by occupying the islands of Versova and Dharavi and the creeks between Bassein and the mainland they completely isolated the city.” [n45 §1; Gaz XIV p. 34] Wikipedia, citing Naravane, Scholberg and Kantak, tells how a first assault on Arnala failed against the Portuguese navy and a second, on 28 March 1737, surprised the garrison, who abandoned the island; the general Shankarji Pant had urged the attack. [n45 §4] Bajirao I ordered a new fort, and a Marathi inscription over its northern gateway, as the Gazetteer read it, records that it “was rebuilt in the reign of Bajirav I in AD 1737 (Shak 1659) by an architect named Baji Tuleji”. [n46 §7; n45 §4; Gaz XIV pp. 10–11] A Marathi fort site gives the completion as 23 May 1737; that date rests on one modern source. [n45 §4]

With Arnala in Maratha hands, the Vaitarna’s mouth was closed to the Portuguese and the north of the island lay open. The inland praganas had been slipping away since the early 1730s. [n46 §2; Tombo p. 336] Jivdhan, a mile from Virar, was the last Portuguese strongpoint between the hills and the garden island.

The letter from Papdi

The capture of Jivdhan is known from a single letter in the Peshwa’s records, published in 1931 in a volume of selections called The Bassein Campaign, 1737–1739. Letter number 118, dated 21 March 1738 (11 Zilhej), was written from Papdi, in the Vasai prant, by Chimnaji Bhivrao and Shankaraji Keshav (Phadke) to their commander, Chimaji Appa, the Peshwa’s brother. Its Marathi text says that Barvaji Takpir had been sent against “the fort of Jivdhan”, and that he “took Jivdhan; twenty-five men were killed; ten to twenty were wounded; forty to fifty fled; the fort was taken this morning”. [n46 §7; The Bassein Campaign No. 16, letter 118] The editor’s English head-note reads: “The capture of the fort of Jivdhan is reported to Chimaji Appa by Chimnaji Bhivrao.” [n46 §7]

Three cautions go with the letter. The Marathi OCR is partly corrupt, and the casualty line in particular is uncertain. [n46 §7] The letter does not say which Jivdhan it means; the identification with Virar’s hill rests on the Gazetteer’s listing of Jivdhan as one of only two inland forts of the Bassein sub-division, which makes it probable but not certain. [n46 §7] And the letter says nothing about who the defenders were, Portuguese soldiers or local men in Portuguese pay, or what became of the forty or fifty who fled.

Read for what it does say, it is the most dramatic document in Virar’s early history. On the morning of 21 March 1738 Maratha troops stormed the fort on the hill above the village. Some two dozen defenders died. The rest broke and ran. The news was carried to Papdi and written down the same day. Fourteen months later, on 16 May 1739, after a siege that began on 17 February, Bassein itself surrendered. [n46 §7; n40 §2; da Cunha p. 148] The man who co-signed the Jivdhan letter, Shankaraji Keshav Phadke, would become the Maratha governor of the conquered province and rebuild the Hindu temples of the Virar side.

Maratha Virar, 1739–1818

Bajipur and its rulers

After 1739 Bassein was renamed Bajipur, and its province was ruled under the Peshwa’s system of sarsubhedars, who were nominally in charge but “lived in Poona” and deputed mamlatdars to the districts. [n46 §7; n37 §5; Gaz XIV p. 34; Gaz XIII-2, Land History] The Gazetteer’s Land History chapter divides the eighty-seven years of Maratha management (it counts from 1730 to 1817) into three periods: thirty years of little change; thirty years of fresh surveys and cesses, with revenue farming becoming general; and “twenty-seven years when revenue farming was universal and exactions unlimited”. When Thana passed to the Peshwa it was “in a wretched state. The people were few and poor, and large areas of land had passed out of tillage.” [n46 §7; Gaz XIII-2, Land History] That description is for the whole district, not Virar. The notes found no Peshwa-era village record (a kaul, an inam grant or a rent roll) naming Virar, and the British survey of 1818 for the Bassein villages was not accessible. [n46 §7 Gaps] Who collected Dom Pedro’s 2,000 pardaus after 1739, and how much, is not known.

The new rulers set out to rebuild a Hindu countryside. “To restore a Hindu population grants of rent-free land were offered, and a tax was levied for the support of Brahmans who were brought to purify the Christians and make them fit to take their place in their old castes.” [n37 §5; Gaz XIV p. 34] Local story, as the Gazetteer recorded it, said that after 1737–43 “numbers of the Christians were … purified by Brahmans and admitted into Hinduism”, among them probably the Bhandari palm-tappers known as Kirpals. [n49 §1g; n37 §5; Gaz XIII-1 p. 202] The Gazetteer offered a possible meaning for Kirpal, from kriyapal, “allowed to make use of Hindu rites”. [n49 §1f-bis; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 149–150] Enthoven later described the Kirpal Phudgis, cowherds of the Mahim and Bassein talukas, as descendants of Phudgis “forced by the Portuguese to embrace Christianity, and who on the conquest of Mahim and Bassein by the Marathas reverted to Hinduism”. [n49 §1f; Enthoven III] These were the first large reversions on the coast. A local story credited Shivaji, decades earlier, with having “taken much care that the people should be purified by Brahmans and brought back to Hinduism”, about 1665–70. [n37 §5; Gaz XIII-1 p. 202]

Temples rise

The Hindu landscape around Virar was rebuilt in these decades, and Shankarji Keshav Phadke rebuilt much of it. At Nirmal, where the Portuguese “had pulled down the temples and destroyed the ling”, the site was purified after 1739, footprints of Dattatreya took the place of the linga, a reservoir was built, and about 1750 Shankarji Keshav, “Sarsubhedar of Bassein”, raised eight temples with government grants. [n37 §5; n40 A1; Gaz XIV pp. 292–293] At Agashi he built a large temple of Bhavanishankar, with a holy bathing tank “supposed to cure skin diseases”, and a temple of Hanuman. [n49 §2a; n40 A2; Gaz XIV p. 2] The Gazetteer dates the Bhavanishankar temple to AD 1691 (Shak 1613), which cannot be right for a commander of the 1730s to 1750s; the 2008 heritage survey labels both Agashi temples “18th century, patron Peshwa”, and the notes suggest the Gazetteer’s Shak 1613 may be a slip for Shak 1673, AD 1751, though this has not been checked against the printed page. [n49 §2a; n40 A2] The Gazetteer’s general rule fits: “On the decline of Portuguese power (1740) Brahmanism revived, and except those that are more modern, most of the present Hindu temples date from the eighteenth century.” [n37 §5; Gaz XIII-1 p. 66] Inside Arnala’s walls stood temples of Trimbakeshvar and Bhavani, and a Ram Mandir is dated 1806 by the heritage survey. [n40 C1; n45 §1; CRIT Vol III]

Nothing in the notes records a temple built at Virar itself in the Maratha period. Whether the cave cult on Jivdhan, which the Gazetteer would find flourishing in 1882, was revived or patronised after the fort fell, as Nirmal was, is not documented. [n46 §7; P1] The fort on the hill passes out of the record after 1738; the ruins that the Gazetteer’s compilers saw from the railway are consistent with a small Maratha garrison post on an older, rock-cut base, but that too is an inference. [n46 §7 Inferences]

Christians under the Marathas

The Christians who stayed were treated better than their priests had feared. The capitulation of 1739 gave free passage to noble families, Christians who wished to leave, all religious orders and priests; it promised that Christians would “enjoy liberty of worship”, and preserved the privileges of five churches, three in Bassein city, one in the Bassein district and one in Salsette. [n37 §5; n49 §1g; Gaz XIII-1 p. 203] The Portuguese nobles and regular clergy left for Goa; “the ordinary people remained, a native priest taking charge of their souls with the approbation of the Mahrattas”, under a Vicar General at Kurla who ran “Canarin” (native) priests. [n37 §5; Hull vol. 1 p. 76] Dom Pedro de Meneses, or his heir, presumably went with the nobles; the notes do not say.

The Gazetteer’s verdict was that the Marathas “allowed them the freest exercise of their religion, their processions and festivals were respected”, and that the native Christians “showed more constancy and the Marathas more toleration than could have been expected”. [n37 §5; Gaz XIII-1 p. 203] “When their possession of the Portuguese territory was secure, the Marathas did not interfere with the practice of the Christian religion.” [n46 §7; Gaz XIV p. 2] On 16 December 1760, the church feast day, Anquetil du Perron rode into Agashi “par les plus beaux vergers du monde”, through the finest orchards in the world, and found the lanes “full of people going to church as freely as in a Christian country”; the priest was entertaining the native priests who had helped at the service. [n40 A2; Gaz XIV p. 2; da Cunha p. 164] On the same journey he noticed Vatar, which he wrote as “Outar”. [n46 §4; Gaz XIV p. 385 n. 1] The church at Agashi had been burnt in 1739 and was rebuilt, by one account, in 1760. [n49 §2b; CRIT Vol III] The Holy Spirit church at Nandakhal, by the parish’s own tradition, escaped destruction altogether, and a later legend says the Maratha soldiers who stormed it retreated before a swarm of bees from the woods. [n40 A3; CRIT Vol III; EI Nandkhal]

The Kupari villages after the conquest

For the Samvedi Christians of the villages west of Virar, 1739 was the moment their church lost its state. “All the Portuguese ‘reinóis’ and ‘descendentes’ fled or were expelled,” Mendiratta writes, “leaving only the Indian converted population behind.” [n34 §6; Mendiratta pp. 216–218] The Marathas accepted only non-white priests, so Goan priests, secular clergy and Oratorians, took over, and reorganisation began from Kurla in 1740. [n34 §6] The parish of the Kupari belt that the treaty kept was probably the one church allowed in the “Cassabé” subdivision; Nandakhal’s survived, by its own tradition, without even being damaged. [n34 §6; n40 A3] Agashi’s church was burnt and rebuilt; Nirmal’s was destroyed, and from 1739 until 1856 its people used their cemetery as their church, by the words of a plaque on the bell tower. [n40 A1, A2]

The pressure to return was real. Da Cunha says that the Peshwa Madhavrao (who ruled 1761–72) “instituted a tax in support of Brahmans to purify the native Christians, whom they regarded as polluted Hindus, before receiving them into their former castes”, and that land grants and the tax together “brought down a crowd of Hindus from Maharashtra and Gujarat, especially Prabhus”. [n34 §6; da Cunha p. 149] The census of 1911 says the Kirpal Bhandaris were given the choice of returning soon after 1739 “which they accepted”. [n34 §6] Mendiratta thinks the Marathas “probably managed to bring some Catholics back into the Hindu fold through purifying rites, and economic pressure”. [n34 §6] But the Kuparis did not go back. At Agashi in 1760 Anquetil du Perron saw their lanes full of churchgoers; at Nirmal a Hindu revival, eight temples built about 1750, rose beside a Christian parish that worshipped among its graves. [n34 §6; n40 A1] The notes call this coexistence “documented, not just remembered”. [n34 §6 Inferences] No Maratha-period document concerning any named Samvedi Christian village has been found. [n34 §6 Gaps]

Winners and losers

The conquest redistributed status as well as land. The Pachkalshis, the garden caste whose Vadval branch managed the coconut and palm groves of Bassein, Mahim and Dahanu, were rewarded for helping the Marathas: a footnote in the Gazetteer, from W. B. Mulock, says their help at the siege of Bassein (dated 1743 in that note) led the Peshwa to make one family chief Patils of Salsette and others proprietors of Angaon and Anjar in Bhiwandi. [n49 §1b; Gaz XIII-1 p. 130 n. 1] Enthoven adds that the Naik family of Anjore received the title of Sarpatil, and that many Vadval families held patelships for the same reason. [n49 §1d; Enthoven III pp. 160–161] The Palshe Brahmins, priests to the Prabhus and Pachkalshis and keepers of the right to fix wedding times “for all classes in Salsette, Bassein, Mahim and Dahanu”, fared worse: the Gazetteer says they “suffered much since the Maratha conquest of western Thana (1740)” at the hands of Deccan and Chitpavan Brahmins, who mocked their name as pal-ashin, “flesh eater”. [n49 §1c; Gaz XIII-1 p. 81] Brahmins from the Deccan came with the new regime; the Chitpavans who were to be so prominent in Thana district in 1872 owe their presence partly to it, though no source counts them at Virar. [n49 §1c Gaps]

Traders came too. At Sopara, the Gazetteer says, Gujarat Vanis of the Lad division arrived from Cambay about 1760, and Muslim Naitas were the other traders of the place. [n40 A15; n48 §1; Gaz XIV pp. 315–316]

The coast in the Maratha years

The sea did not stop working while the armies passed. Agashi remained a port; in 1837 the Gazetteer could still call it “the chief town on Bassein island with a large Christian and Hindu population, and a brisk trade to Gujarat and Bombay in grain, garden produce, and salt. Very good fishing boats and coasting craft were built.” [n48 §1; n40 A2; Gaz XIV p. 2] Arnala became a Maratha naval station; a single Marathi site says that records of 1772 mention a large Maratha fleet based there. [n45 §4] In 1781, during the first war between the Marathas and the British, Arnala’s commandant refused to surrender until the British prepared to bombard the fort from Agashi; accounts differ on whether the British then captured it or merely threatened, and the Treaty of Salbai returned the coast to the Marathas. [n46 §7; n45 §4; Gaz XIV pp. 10–11] At the river’s mouth four villages had gone under the sea since 1724. [n48 §2; Gaz XIII-1 p. 281]

In 1817–18 the Peshwa’s power collapsed, and Arnala and the Bassein country passed to the East India Company. The Gazetteer says Arnala came under the British in 1817; a Marathi site says 1818, after resistance. [n46 §7; n45 §4]

Under the Company, 1818–1864

Cholera and the returners

The British inherited a district battered by disease. Cholera in 1818 and 1819 lowered the population so much that the district did not recover for years. [n21; Gaz XIII-2, Health] It came back in 1820–21 and in 1828. And in those years something happened at Virar that the Gazetteer recorded in a single footnote, one of the few events of the period in which the village is named.

The Agris of the Thana coast, salt-makers, toddy-drawers, drummers and rice farmers, were divided in 1882 into three groups that neither married nor ate together: the Sudagris, the Dasagris and the Urap Agris. The Uraps (also Varap Agris, or “Nave”, new, Marathas) of Salsette and Bassein were “said to be Christian Agris who reverted to Hinduism some in 1820 and others in 1828”. [n49 §1e; n46 §6; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 116–117] The footnote lists the villages where they lived. In Salsette: Balkham, Ralodi, Turbhe, Kavesar, Vadavali, Uthalsar, Mulund, Kopri, Pavai, Kalva, Sanghar, Ovale, Gavhan and Bhandup. In Bassein (in the OCR’s spellings): Umelman, Manikpur, Agashi, Mukam, Padrichiwadi, Jot, Virar, Kolowda, Navapur, Achole and Juchandra. [n49 §1e; n46 §6; Gaz XIII-1 p. 117 n. 1]

So in 1820 or 1828 there were Christian Agri families at Virar, and some or all of them returned to Hinduism. The Gazetteer gives the mechanics. The Bassein Agris who reverted in 1820 paid Rs 1,200 for their readmission (the OCR is uncertain and may read 1,290). The priest who took them back was Ramchandra Baba Joshi, a Palshe Brahmin, who was put out of his caste for a time for doing it; his successor, Bhai Makund Joshi, was also excommunicated. On 12 November 1828 a second group was admitted by Vithal Hari Naik Vaidya, “a Palshe Brahman of Bassein”. [n49 §1e, §1c; n37 §5; Gaz XIII-1 p. 117] The name Urap was explained three ways: from the Persian urf, “alias”; from “Europe”; or, the Gazetteer’s preference, from the Marathi orapne, “to sear with a hot iron”, supposedly for a purifying ordeal, which the Gazetteer compared with the branding of Christian reverts at Tanjore in 1701. [n49 §1e; n37 §5; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 117, 203 n. 4] The only trace of their Christian past by 1882 was their surnames: “Gomes, Soz, Fernan, Frutad, and Minez”, Gomes, Sousa, Fernandes, Furtado and Menezes worn down in Marathi mouths. [n46 §6; n49 §1e; Gaz XIII-1 p. 117] The Sud and Das Agris “neither marry nor eat with them”; they had separate priests and their own headman. [n49 §1e]

Why did they return? Two explanations survive, and they do not agree. The Gazetteer’s account of the Christians says that in the cholera years 1818–20 and 1828 “some of the Christian Kolis, finding that they suffered as much as their Hindu neighbours, took to propitiating the goddess of cholera, and either left or were driven from the Church. Some of these people with the help of a Palshi Brahman became Hindus, and are known as Uraps or Varaps”; others who were expelled “still attend their parish churches at festivals”. [n49 §1e, §1g; n37 §5; Gaz XIII-1 p. 208 and n. 3] A writer of 1838, Vaupell, blamed instead “an attempt to extend the priestly prerogative”, that is, the clergy overreaching. [n49 §1e; n37 §5] Da Cunha, quoted in the same footnote, notes that the Maratha rulers had levied a tax to support Brahmins who purified Christians before readmission. [n49 §1g; Gaz XIII-1 p. 208 n. 3] And the Uraps’ own family story, already quoted, was that their forefathers had been made Christian by force, with flesh thrown into their wells. [n37 §3; Gaz XIII-1 p. 201 n. 2] Epidemic fear, friction with the church, a Hindu priesthood willing (at a price, and at a cost to itself) to take people back, and a memory of having been converted unwillingly: the evidence supports all of these, and no source weighs them for Virar. [n49 §1g Inferences]

There were others like them. The Thalkars (Thankars), found in small numbers in Bassein, Thana and Bhiwandi, were “descendants of Christian Kolis, who in the great cholera year (1820–21) sought the protection of Devi, Khandoba and Vithoba and left the Catholic Church”, and had taken to wearing the top-knot. [n49 §1g; Gaz XIII-1, Christians chapter] There were Urap Kolis and even Urap Katkaris. [n49 §1e] The notes sum up the result: by 1882 the Hindu fishing and farming population of north Vasai held three layers, never-converted Hindus (Samvedi Brahmins, Pachkalshis, Sud and Das Agris), Christians who had become Hindus again (Urap Agris, Urap Kolis, Thalkars, Kirpal Bhandaris and Phudgis), and Christians (Kuparis, Christian Vadvals, Christian Kolis). The reconversions were “a response to epidemic and church-caste friction, not a single event”. [n49 §1g Inferences]

The British reckoned the Bassein subdivision early in the nineteenth century at eight working churches and about 5,000 Catholics. [n34 §6; Mendiratta p. 219] In the cholera of 1820–21, by a British report, “about 3000 Christians rejoined the Hindu fold”, which the report blamed on the lack of “respectable ministers”; a British letter of 1819 complained that the Goa-linked clergy “form a sort of separate caste in the middle of the community”. [n34 §6; Mendiratta p. 220] The Gazetteer adds that the Christians “did not receive any special encouragement from the British Government”, and that early British accounts noticed them “in terms of contempt”. [n34 §6; Gaz XIII-1 p. 203]

Why the Kupari villages a few miles west of Virar did not return, when the Urap Agris of Virar and Navapur did, is one of the open questions of this book. The Kuparis had their parishes and their Goan priests, villages where whole communities were Christian together, land of their own and standing as the most skilled gardeners of the district; the Uraps were a smaller and poorer group, whose Christian neighbours in their own caste were few, and who were cut off from both their old caste and the Christian landholding families. That contrast is an inference from the general history in Chapter 35 and from the Gazetteer’s descriptions, not a finding about Virar. What is documented is that by 1882 the two groups lived a few miles apart, the one Christian and the other Hindu, both with Portuguese surnames.

Revenue, rain and want

The Company’s first decades on the coast were hard. In 1824–25 rain failed in Dahanu, Bassein and Salsette; crops were very scanty and grain sold at famine rates, though no one died of starvation, and the government spent £1,550 clearing reservoirs. In 1837 the crops failed in Sanjan and Bassein for want of rain, and remissions of about Rs 45,000 were granted. In 1838–39 they failed across the whole district. In 1848 most of the salt rice-land crop failed; in 1850 the coast suffered drought; in 1854 “in Bassein the salt rice crops were partially injured by worms”. [n48 §1; n21; Gaz XIII-1 p. 303] These are district and sub-division notices; none names Virar. But Virar’s tenants grew rice on the very lands, sweet and salt, that these notices describe.

The British also brought new enterprises to the island. In 1829 a Mr Lingard got a forty-year rent-free lease of about 83 acres on the Bassein fort esplanade and an advance of £2,300 to make sugar; he died in 1832. McGregor Brownrigg and Company took a perpetual lease in 1841; in 1852 the old church of Nossa Senhora da Vida inside the fort became a sugar factory; and by 1881 the Bassein Sugar Company “has long ceased to exist”. [n21 §2; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 394–395; Gaz XIV p. 36] The cane they hoped to crush came from the garden belt, Virar’s sugarcane among it. In 1826 the traveller Clunes found Sopara a kasba of 400 houses with a sugar factory. [n37 §6; Gaz XIV pp. 315, 323] In 1827 Bassein opened the first government vernacular school in Thana district. [n21] A survey of 1851 counted 22 salt-water and 18 fresh-water fisheries in Bassein. [n48 §1, §5; Gaz XIII-1]

Caste and claims

The Company’s courts and censuses encouraged communities to state their rank, and the garden castes of the Virar side did so. The Vadvals, who in 1882 were said to have separated from the other Pachkalshis “some time in the fourteenth century”, pressed their claim to be Panchkalshis or Somvanshi Kshatriyas with letters from the Shankaracharya in 1836 and 1852 and a resolution of the Palshikar Brahmins of Bassein taluka in 1855. [n49 §1d; Enthoven III p. 161; Enthoven I] Their own tradition was that they had come with Raja Bimb from Paithan at the end of the thirteenth century, or by way of Anhilvada Patan in Gujarat, and that under the Portuguese “much persecution” had forced many of them into Catholicism. [n49 §1d; Enthoven III pp. 160–161] The Gazetteer’s compilers, characteristically, thought the Pachkalshis were of one stock with the Pathare Prabhus and had come from Gujarat. [n49 §1b; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 129–130] These are traditions and colonial theories, and they are given as such; what is documented is that, before the railway came, the garden castes of Bassein were working to fix their place in a new order.

The last years before the line

Two events of these final years show the coast as it was when the surveyors arrived. The first is the railway itself. The Bombay, Baroda and Central India Railway was begun in May 1858; its chief difficulty, the Gazetteer says, “was the number of creeks and streams”. [n46 §9; Gaz XIII-1 p. 329] Through 1858–64 its gangs worked north from Bombay, bridging the Bassein channel twenty-nine and thirty miles from Bombay and the Vaitarna channel at forty-four and forty-five, and laying the long embankment across the garden island. [n46 §9; Gaz XIII-1 p. 329]

The second is a revolt. On 5 December 1860, when the British tried to collect a new income tax at Bassein, about 4,000 people gathered before the Mamlatdar’s office and many threw down their notices and forms. The next day the special officer, a Mr Hunter, addressed a crowd of 1,200 to 1,500, “half of whom were Kolies”. When he seized the arm of their spokesman, Govardhan Gangadas (also written Sheth Gordhandas), the crowd rose. Hunter fled by the back door to the creek to catch a country boat, but “the kolies and other agriculturists followed him up to the creek, jumped into water, and after stopping the country craft rushed into it and brought it back to the shore”. They made him sit and “simply urged him to drop the collection of the tax”. Gordhandas had told him that “one-fourth of the salary of one single English officer is enough to cover the expenditure of the whole establishment of his office”. Two or three men were later convicted. [n43 §1; SM-I pp. 22–25] This was Bassein town, not Virar, and it was a tax grievance, not nationalist politics. [n43 §1 Inferences] But the Kolis and cultivators who waded into the creek were the same people who, a few years later, would crowd the market by Virar’s new station.

In 1864 Bassein got a municipality. [n21] And on 28 November 1864 the line opened. [n46 §9; Gaz XIII-1 p. 329]

The Virar side on the eve of the railway

No one described Virar in 1864. The first description of the village was printed in 1882, eighteen years after the line opened, and the next chapter begins with it. But the Gazetteer’s account of the district’s peoples drew heavily on the census of 1872 and on officers’ reports of the preceding decades, and much of what it says about the peoples, languages, work and food of the Bassein coast describes ways of life that were already old when the railway arrived. Used with that caution, it gives a picture of the people who lived around Jivdhan in the last years before the trains.

Peoples

The Samvedi Brahmins. The census of 1872 counted 2,563 Samvedi Brahmins in Thana district, “found only in Bassein and Mahim”. [n49 §1a; Gaz XIII-1 p. 74] The Gazetteer called them “strong, tall and fair, with regular features”, “husbandmen and gardeners by craft”, living in one-storeyed houses with plank walls and tiled roofs; the men wore a short waistcloth, a shoulder cloth and “red broadcloth caps like Telegu Brahmans”. [n49 §1a; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 82–83] They were “on the whole prosperous”. They had no headman and settled disputes by majority of the caste. [n49 §1a] They were the headmen of villages next to Virar: Bhiku Patil at Koprad, and Bab Naik at Gas. [n49 §1a; n40 A6] Their own tradition traces them to temple musicians and dancers from Orissa who served the Nirmal Shankaracharya samadhi, the Sureshwar temple and the Bhavanishankar temple at Agashi; that tradition comes from a community blog and a poorly sourced Wikipedia article, and the “1500 BC” dates attached to it are unreliable. [n49 §1a-bis] The same tradition says paddy was added to their sugarcane and betel cultivation late in the seventeenth century and flower gardens in the eighteenth, after Bombay rose. [n49 §1a-bis] What is documented is that they were split by the Portuguese conversions. The Christians of Agashi, Vatar and Koprad were “originally Samvedis and Pachkalshis”, and “the husbandmen of Sopara are Christians, many of them converted Samvedi Brahmans”. [n49 §1a; n37 §3; Gaz XIII-1 p. 395; Gaz XIV p. 315]

The Kuparis are those Samvedi Christians. The Gazetteer never uses the word Kupari; the community’s own list of thirteen villages includes Nirmal, Agashi, Nandakhal, Bolinj, Umrale, Wagholi, Mardes and Rajodi, nine of which the Gazetteer independently names as plantain-drying villages. [n40 §1] In 1876 da Cunha counted 2,000 native Christians in the parish of Espirito Santo (Nandakhal) and 900 at Agashi. [n40 §2; da Cunha p. 161] Their way of life on the eve of the railway can be reconstructed from the Gazetteer’s long account of the Christians of Thana, written in 1882 from reports of the preceding decades, which includes some points specific to Bassein; it describes “Thana Christians” generally, not Kuparis by name, and is used here with that caution. [n34 §5]

What stands out is how much the Christian and Hindu Samvedis still shared. Both guarded a newborn against the spirit Sathi or Sati on the fifth or sixth night after birth. [n34 §5; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 82, 210] Both wore the same ornaments, mugdya and gathe among them. [n34 §1] The Hindu Samvedi men wore “red broadcloth caps like Telegu Brahmans”, and red broadcloth caps were worn by Christian men too, so the red cap later remembered as a Kupari mark was a regional cap, not a badge imposed on converts. [n34 §5 Inferences; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 82, 204–205] Christian houses were “substantial tile-roofed houses with walls of wooden planks, mud, or brick and stone”, matching the Hindu Samvedis’ plank-walled, tiled houses closely. [n34 §1] But the church had reshaped the rest of life. The child was baptised between the eighth and fifteenth day, carried by a padrinho and a madrinha; “the mother never goes to the christening”, and on the fortieth day she was purified in church. [n34 §5; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 210–211] “In Bassein the proposal comes from the boy’s side”; couples were instructed in doctrine for two or three months before marriage; a groom of the poorer classes might wear “the full European dress of bygone days, a scarlet or black military coat with cocked hat, epaulets, knee-breeches”, hired for the day, and walk under a silk umbrella; the bride wore the Hindu robe over a white overall and received a gold necklace she wore while her husband lived; the feast had two pork dishes with vinegar and spices, and the poor ate off leaf plates. [n34 §5; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 211–214] The dead were buried within a day, with the church bell tolled and candles round the coffin, and a widow put away her ornaments for good. [n34 §5; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 214–215] The Hindu Samvedis, by contrast, burned their dead, married their daughters before ten and forbade widow marriage. [n34 §5; n49 §1a]

The Christians ate rice, nagli, vari, pulses, vegetables, fish, mutton, beef, pork and fowl, drank palm spirit (phul) and double-distilled bevda, and ate together as a family; “pigs are found in most Christian villages”. [n34 §5; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 55, 204] They took the first fruits of the rice harvest to church to be blessed. Passion plays had been brought to Bassein by the Jesuits in 1552, and each church paid a music master who played the violin. In outlying villages of Salsette and Bassein some still made secret offerings to Ganpati and Gauri and vows to Shitladevi, which the priests disapproved of. [n34 §5; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 207–210] And they spoke Marathi: the Gazetteer heard little difference from the speech of Kunbis and Kolis, while Grierson, in 1905, recorded the Hindu Samvedis’ speech as “a mechanical mixture of Konkan Marathi and Gujarati” and the Christians’ as ordinary Marathi with Portuguese words such as pay and may, father and mother, and padron and madron, godparents. The modern Kupari dialect, Kadodi, is best seen as the Samvedi base with Portuguese church and kinship words, though no linguist has written its history. [n34 §4; LSI VII] Part Seven tells their story in full.

The Pachkalshis and Vadvals. The Gazetteer counted 15,367 Pachkalshis district-wide, “in strength along the coast”, a single community with many names (Sutars, Malis, Vadvals, Chaukalshis, Somvanshi Kshatris, Pathares) whose members, except the Chaukalshis, ate together and intermarried. Their surnames were Raut, Vartak and Chodhri (Chaudhari); they were husbandmen, gardeners, carpenters, a few writers and labourers; and a proverb said “Pachkalshi ani kon mhanel alshi”, “who can call a Pachkalshi idle?” [n49 §1b; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 129–130] At Agashi, Vatar and Koprad fifteen Hindu Pachkalshi families dried plantains beside the Samvedis and Christians. [n40 §1; Gaz XIII-1 p. 395] Virar has a ward called Vartakwada, which may have been a Pachkalshi ward, though no source confirms it. [n40 B11; CRIT Vol I] The Christian Vadvals were cartmen of the southern Bassein villages, “the best axemen in the district”. [n49 §1d; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 97–98]

The Palshe Brahmins, priests to the Pachkalshis and the Prabhus, fixed the auspicious times for weddings and thread ceremonies for every class in Bassein; it was Palshes who readmitted the Urap Agris in 1820 and 1828. [n49 §1c; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 81, 117] Their tradition, like the Pachkalshis’, was that they came with Bimb from Paithan in 1297. [n49 §1c]

The Agris of the Bassein sub-division numbered about 8,461 in 1881, if the OCR’s leading digit is right. [n21 §1; Gaz XIII-2 p. 680] Their staple food, the Gazetteer said, was nagli (finger millet) and fish; their chief god was Cheda, with local spirits. [n49 §1e; Gaz XIII-1 p. 116] Khanivade, north-east of Virar on the Tansa, was one of four Bassein villages of the Dasagris. [n49 §3c; Gaz XIII-1 p. 116 n.] Virar itself held Urap Agris, as the last section showed.

The Kolis. About 8,000 Son Kolis lived “chiefly along the coast south of the Vaitarna”, a people the Gazetteer thought had “mixed with foreign settlers from beyond the sea”; they were fishers, sailors, husbandmen and labourers. [n49 §1f; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 148–149] The professional fishermen of Bassein were “the Uraps and other Christian Kolis”, and “the mass of the sea-fishing population are Son Kolis”; their boats lay idle in June and July, when some took to tillage. [n49 §1f; Gaz XIII-1 p. 55] The Vaitis, 4,596 people “found in North Bassein and South Mahim”, were cultivators, fishers and sailors of the same tribe. [n49 §1f; Gaz XIII-1 p. 181] The Mangela fishers, 2,375 of them in the Bassein sub-division in 1881, would give their name to Narangi’s people in the twenty-first century. [n21; n49 §3c]

The Bhandaris, palm-juice drawers, tapped the coconut and brab palms with a knife and a gourd, singing as they climbed; they were divided into Kirtes, Gavads, Sindes and Kirpals, the last “once Christians”. [n49 §1f-bis; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 149–150] Their tradition said the Portuguese brought them from Goa, or that they came with Bimb; the Gazetteer thought the first unlikely. [n49 §1f-bis]

The forest peoples. The Varlis (Warlis) of Bassein numbered 6,099 in 1872, most of them of the Nihiri division, people who loved their forests so much “that … they cannot be induced to go so far from their woods”. [P1; n08; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 182–183] Enthoven placed the Nihir Varlis in “South Mahim, Bassein, Jawhar and Vada”. [n49 §1f] Katkaris and Thakurs lived in the same hills. [n49 §1f-bis] The place-names ending in -pada on the slopes east of the railway (Phulpada, Manvelpada) fit the hamlet geography of these peoples, but no record links a named pada to a particular tribe, and the notes forbid stating it as fact. [n49 §3 Inferences]

Others. Kunbis formed “the bulk of the agricultural class” across the district, farming embanked rice fields. [n49 §1f-bis; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 301–302] Baris, said to have come from Gujarat, some of whose women kept Gujarati dress, were husbandmen and cartmen in Bassein, Mahim and Dahanu. [n49 §1f] Phudgis, cowherds, lived only in the Mahim and Bassein talukas. [n49 §1f] Mhars served as village servants; the story of Virar’s goddess, recorded in 1882, turns on one of them. [n46 §8] Muslims were few on the Virar side: 155 at Agashi in 1881, the Naitas of Sopara, and two saints, Shahali and Hajiali, buried at Arnala with an endowment from the government. [n49 §1h; n46 §8; Gaz XIV p. 11] A Jain temple of Parasnath was built at Agashi about 1820 by Motilal, a Vani of Bombay; a later legend says its image rose from a pond at Chakreshwar in Sopara. [n49 §2a; Gaz XIV p. 2]

Languages

The Virar side spoke Marathi, but many Marathis. The Gazetteer found the Samvedis speaking “incorrect Marathi”, saying ade for kothe (“where”) and had for ka (“why”). [n49 §1a; Gaz XIII-1 p. 82 n.] The Pachkalshis spoke “incorrect Marathi using l for j and w for z”. [n49 §1b; Gaz XIII-1 p. 130] The Son Kolis spoke Marathi “with many strange words and so curious an accent”, pronouncing d as r and l as n. [n49 §1f; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 148–149] The native Christians spoke at home a Marathi “very little different from the Marathi spoken by the Kunbis and Kolis”; a few knew ungrammatical Portuguese, and Latin was the language of the church. [n49 §1g; Gaz XIII-1 p. 204] At Agashi the parish school still taught Portuguese reading. [n40 A2; Gaz XIV p. 2] The Warlis had their own speech, and the Baris’ women their Gujarati dress. [n49 §1f]

Layered under these were the languages of earlier rulers: the Sanskrit of the Shilahara plates, the “corrupt Prakrit” that the Gazetteer called “the mother of the modern Marathi”, the Devanagari and Hijri dates of the Koprad stone, the Persian of the sultanate’s officials, the Portuguese of the register and the lease-holders’ names, and, after 1739, the Marathi of the Peshwa’s letters. [n03; n46 §4, §7] Words travelled with them: the Christian growers called their oil-cake manure phod while others called it khap. [n48 §1; Gaz XIII-1 p. 291] The researchers’ notes on the modern Samvedi, Kupari and Vadval dialects (Chapters 36 and T2) take the story further; in 1864 they were already distinct enough for an English official to notice.

Work

The work of the Virar side followed its three kinds of land. On the garden island, the “most hardworking and skilful husbandmen are the Christians of Bassein, originally Brahmans, Bhandaris, Chavkalshis, Pachkalshis, Kharpatils, and Kolis, who grow sugarcane and plantains, and have turned the light sandy country about Bassein into an evergreen garden”. [n49 §1f-bis; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 301–302] Rice came first each year; sugarcane was planted in May after ploughing from November, and about 750,000 canes were sent yearly “chiefly to Bombay, Surat, and Broach” at Rs 14 the hundred. [n48 §1; Gaz XIII-1 p. 291] Nine kinds of plantain were grown in Bassein (basrai, mutheli, tambdi, rajeli, lokhandi, sonkeli, bankeli, karanjeli and narsingi) in light, sandy soil manured with fish, which was cheaper than other manures and gave a better return, though it bred worms. [n48 §1; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 291–292] Betel vines were grown with the plantains, coconuts were watered every week or so, and sonchafa trees were planted among the palms because “their strong perfume drives off the bees”. [n48 §1; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 293–297] Persian wheels lifted the water from the shallow wells. [n48 §1]

On the rice flats the Kunbis, Agris and Kolis worked embanked fields of sweet and salt land, the salt land needing a generation of embanking to sweeten. [n48 §1–2; n49 §1f-bis] On the creeks and at the coast the Kolis fished and the Agris made salt, from January to May, in pans embanked from the tidal flats, with a reservoir called the khajina, “treasury”, brine pits and evaporating pans. [n48 §1, §5; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 365–366] At Agashi carpenters built fishing boats and coasting craft. [n48 §1; Gaz XIV p. 2] In the forests to the east the Varlis and Katkaris cut wood and grass. [n49 §1f-bis] And the Bhandaris climbed the palms. [n49 §1f-bis]

Money moved through small local credit centres. The old system of hundis, bills of exchange, survived at Bassein and in the villages of Nala, Agashi, Sopara and Navghar, where bills could be cashed up to Rs 10,000. [n48 §1; n40 A15; Gaz XIII-1] Agashi’s sea trade carried grain, garden produce and salt to Gujarat and Bombay, and from the landings at Gas, Bolinj and Vagholi small boats took molasses, rice and betel leaf to Surat, Broach, Jambusar, Dholera, Bhavnagar and Gogha. [n48 §1; Gaz XIV pp. 2, 315–316] That was the trade the railway would take over.

Food and festivals

The Bassein garden belt was famous for one food above all: dried plantains. They were made only from the rajeli plantain, “only in the villages of Agashi, Vagholi, Vatar, Bolinj, Koprad, Nala, Umrale, Rajodi, and Murdes, all in Bassein”, every one of them on the Virar side of the island. The ripe bunch was kept in a basket of rice straw for six or seven days to heat, peeled, spread “on a booth close to the sea shore”, dried in the sun all day, heaped at night under dry plantain leaves and a mat, smeared each time with clarified butter, and the process repeated for seven days. [n48 §1; n21 §2; Gaz XIII-1 p. 292] The season ran from October to January. [n21 §2; Gaz XIII-1 p. 395] The flower spike, kelphul, was eaten as a vegetable; the leaves served as plates; and dried, powdered bankeli, the wild plantain, was given to the sick and to women after childbirth. [n48 §1; Gaz XIII-1 p. 292] Molasses and cane juice came from the sugarcane fields. The Agris ate nagli and fish; the Christians drank a palm spirit called ful, of three to five per cent alcohol; the whole Christian family ate together, unlike their Hindu neighbours. [n49 §1e, §1g; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 116, 204]

The calendar of feasts can be glimpsed in the record. At Nirmal, a few miles south-west, a fair on the eleventh day of the dark half of Kartik (November) drew six to seven thousand pilgrims, “Hindus, Musalmans, Christians, and a few Parsis”, and sold brass and copper vessels, dried plantains, sweets, cloth and cattle. [n40 A1; Gaz XIV p. 292] At Agashi the church feast fell in mid-December. [n40 A2] Samvedi weddings were lit with rice-flour lamps in a bamboo basket, the boy rode a horse, and a cloth was held between the couple. [n49 §1a; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 82–83] At a Son Koli wedding, the bride’s bangles for the right hand were “consecrated and thrown into the sea”. [n49 §1f; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 148–149] Among the Vadvals a sword or dagger was held aloft through the canopy during the antarpat rite, which Enthoven read as “a survival … of the old Kshatriya custom”. [n49 §1d; Enthoven III pp. 162–165] And on Jivdhan, through “the fair season”, women climbed to the caves to pray for children. [n46 §8; Gaz XIV p. 116] When that cult began is unknown. In 1882 it was already a custom.

What the record does not hold

The chapter has been built from a few dozen documents, and it is worth setting down plainly what they do not say.

There is no prehistoric site at Virar or on Jivdhan in any published source, and no archaeological survey of the hill. [n46 §3; n24] The age and purpose of the Jivdhan caves are unknown; “said to be the work of the Pandavs” is a tradition, and “Buddhist” is an opinion. [n46 §3] Between the Mauryan layers at Sopara and the plates of 1049 there is nothing about Virar at all. The plates name the village but not its people, its gods or its crops. Which donee received Virara’s produce is in lines that have not been read. [n46 §4 Gaps] Uppalapallika cannot be located. The meaning of the name Virar is unknown; the Ekvira derivation is tradition, and “Vihar” in the chronicle is unconfirmed. [n45 §2; n46 §5]

Between 1150 and 1534 there is no record of Virar itself; the Koprad stone of 1464 is the nearest. [n46 §5] Under the Portuguese the register gives Virar’s rent but no population, no house count, no cultivator’s name, no tenure and no church. The church fifty yards south of the later station has no recorded dedication, builder or date; the “tower or fortified mansion” has no owner; the forts on Jivdhan and at Shirgaon have no builders. [n46 §6] The Portuguese originals, Bocarro’s Livro das Plantas, the Franciscan and Jesuit chronicles and Teixeira’s dissertation on Bassein’s territory were not consulted. [n46 §6 Gaps]

The capture of Jivdhan rests on a single letter with a partly corrupt text that does not say which Jivdhan it means. [n46 §7] For the eighty years of Maratha rule there is no village record naming Virar. [n46 §7 Gaps] For the Company years there is one footnote, naming Virar among the villages of the Urap Agris, and it gives no number of families, no names and no account of what happened in Virar itself in 1820 or 1828. [n49 §1e Gaps] There is no population figure for Virar before 1981. [n46 §10] A web page that credits Chimaji Appa with founding or renaming Virar has no primary support and is contradicted by the plate of 1049. [n46 §7]

That is a long list. It is also the reason this chapter is careful: every sentence above the list rests on something written, cut or dug up, and the reader can follow each one back to its source.

The surveyors’ line

In the years before 1864, the gangs of the Bombay, Baroda and Central India Railway came up the coast from Bombay, bridging creeks and raising the embankment. Somewhere near the village of Shirgaon, two miles east of Jivdhan, they came to a small rocky hill that people called Pandav Dungri, “so called from a set of small caves”. The caves “were cut away at the time of making the railway”. [n46 §3; Gaz XIV p. 383] Nobody described them before they went. Nobody recorded an inscription. They are known only from that one sentence, written eighteen years later.

The railway that destroyed them gave Virar a station at the thirty-eighth mile from Bombay, and in less than three years made it the end of the line for the first train that carried people from the country into the city to work. The next chapter follows the village that the railway made into a town.
