Part Four — Sultans and the Portuguese

The sultans of Delhi and Gujarat reach the sea, a king called Bimba is remembered in a chronicle, and the Portuguese arrive. These three chapters take the coast from 1300 to 1739: the port of Basai and its fortress, the treaty of 1534 that handed it to Portugal, the rise of Baçaim as a walled city and a mission field, and its life and fall as the Court of the North. This is the best-documented stretch of the whole book, and the longest.

Period covered: 1300 – 1534

In this chapter. The armies of Delhi reach the sea, a king called Bimba is remembered, Gujarat takes the coast, and the Portuguese begin to raid it. This chapter ends in December 1534, when a sultan hands a port to a foreign king.

In the spring of 1321 a Dominican friar walked north out of Thana carrying the dead. Jordanus Catalani had come up the coast with four companions: three Franciscans, Thomas of Tolentino, James of Padua and Peter of Siena, and a Georgian lay brother and interpreter, Demetrius of Tiflis. At Thana the four were arrested and beheaded for blasphemy against the Prophet, on dates the sources give variously as the 7th, 8th or 11th of April. Jordanus, who had gone ahead to Sopara before he heard of the arrests, brought their bodies back with him and buried them in a church at Supera dedicated to St Thomas the Apostle. He stayed fifteen days. In that time, by the Gazetteer’s summary of his account, he instructed many people, baptised them and gave Holy Communion to about ninety Christians, whom he found to be Nestorians, members of the old eastern church that had reached this coast centuries before the Latins. [n03; TG-XIV pp. 321–322] [n15; Thana Gaz. XIV pp. 321–322] [n25; Wikipedia: Thomas of Tolentino]

It is a small scene and a strange one: a Catalan friar digging graves in a Konkan town for Italians and a Georgian, among Christians who looked to Persia rather than Rome, in a country whose new masters were Muslim soldiers from Delhi. But it is the right door into the two centuries that follow. Between 1300 and 1534 this coast was claimed in turn by Delhi, by Gujarat and by the Bahmani sultans of the Deccan; it was held on the ground by Hindu chiefs who paid them tribute or ignored them; it was remembered in a Marathi chronicle as the kingdom of a prince called Bimba; and at the end of the period it was handed to the Portuguese by a sultan who had more frightening enemies to deal with. Through all of it Sopara slowly faded, and a harbour six miles to the south, which the Muslims called Basai, slowly rose.

Jordanus himself thought Supera worth a mission. In a letter of 1321 he wrote that “there are three places where the friars might reap a great harvest, and where they could live in common. One of these is Supera, where two friars might be stationed.” He recommended it as a good starting point for missionaries bound for Ethiopia. [n15; Yule, Cathay, via Hobson-Jobson; Thana Gaz. XIV p. 322]

The coast in 1300

To understand what the friars walked into, it helps to look at the coast as it stood at the turn of the fourteenth century.

Sopara was old, and it knew it. For more than a thousand years it had been the port of the northern Konkan, and under the Shilahara kings, who ruled the “Konkan Fourteen Hundred” villages from about 800 to about 1260, it was still a district headquarters and a customs port. A Shilahara grant of 1094 had freed the ships of two merchant-ministers from tolls at “Shurparaka” and its sister ports, and in the 1130s or 1140s a scholar of Sopara named Tejakantha had been sent by his king to a literary congress in faraway Kashmir. [n03; TG-II pp. 425–426] But the creek that fed Sopara was silting. The Arab geographer al-Idrisi, writing in 1153, already placed “Soubara” a mile and a half from the sea, and the 1882 Gazetteer judged that “before the beginning of the fourteenth century Thana had become the chief centre of trade. But Sopara was still a place of consequence.” [n03; TG-XIV p. 321] [n15; Hobson-Jobson]

What was traded here is known in outline from the Arab writers the Gazetteer gathered. Rice went out, and salt was carried inland to the Deccan capital at Devagiri. Coconuts and betel grew along the shore, and on an island Idrisi called Bara, which the Gazetteer reads as the island of Sopara itself, there were coconut palms and costus, an aromatic root. Pearls were fished in the creeks. Laced shoes made at Sopara and Sanjan, and good leather, went to Arabia. The partner ports ran in a great arc from Quilon and Calicut to Cambay and Somnath, across to Siraf, Hormuz and Aden, down the African coast to Mogadishu and Mombasa, and east to Java and China. [n03; TG-II pp. 429–430; TG-XIV pp. 321, 355–356]

The political map was simpler. After the last Shilahara, Someshvara, whose final grant is dated 1260, the northern Konkan was ruled by viceroys of the Yadava kings of Devagiri, and the Gazetteer says their headquarters were at Kalyan and at Bassein. Stone inscriptions of 1280 and 1288, set up by officers of the Yadava king Ramachandradeva, were found near Bhiwandi and near Bassein. The second volume of the same Gazetteer is more cautious, calling the Bassein reference “doubtful”. [n03; TG-II p. 437; TG-XIV p. 28] Either way, the harbour that would become Vasai appears in these centuries mostly as a place where stones were found, not as a town anyone described. No inscription securely naming “Vasai” or “Basai” itself has been found from before the fifteenth century. [n03; TG-XIV p. 28 n. 4]

A traveller had just passed. Marco Polo, writing of about 1290, described a “kingdom of Tana”, large and rich, its people idolaters with a language of their own, its king tributary to no one and in league with corsairs who brought him captured horses. Brown incense, cotton cloth and leather went out; gold, silver and copper came in. [n03; TG-XIV p. 356; TG-II p. 438] It is the last picture of this coast under a Hindu king answerable to no one, and it was about to be overturned.

Two older documents name the traders behind that picture. The Shilahara grant of 1094 gives the names of the two merchant-ministers whose ships were freed from tolls “at the ports of Sristhanaka [Thana], Nagapura, Surpparaka, and Chemuli”: the brothers Bhabhana and Dhanama, ministers of King Anantadeva. They are the only people known by name to have owned ships at Sopara before the Portuguese, and they were royal officials as well as shipowners. And a Jain novel of 778, the Kuvalayamala, as a modern study reports it, sends its merchant hero Lobhadeva from Takshashila in the far north-west to Sopara to sell horses, where a shreshthin, a guild head or banker, helps him sell them and grow rich; “Sopparaya traders had their own clubs and customs”. The novel is literature, not record, but it shows what a reader of the eighth century expected Sopara to be: a horse market with a merchant guild. [n36 §3; Indraji 1882 p. 278; Shah 2020, citing Upadhye 1970]

Horses are the thread that runs through this whole age. The Gazetteer’s summary of the Arab writers for the Shilahara centuries says that as many as ten thousand horses a year were said to be imported on this coast, and that “no ships came to Thana without horses”; Marco Polo’s corsairs, around 1290, gave the king of Thana all the horses they captured. Ibn Battuta, in 1344, noted that from Thana “ships of large burden went to Aden”. Horses are the one import that appears in every century of the record, from the novel of 778 to the Portuguese treaties. Whoever held these harbours held a gate to the horse trade, and that, as the end of this chapter will show, was written into the treaty that gave Bassein away. [n36 §3; Gaz. XIII-II pp. 430–431; Gaz. XIV pp. 354–357] [n36 §3 Inferences]

In 1300 the chief ports of this stretch of coast, by the Gazetteer’s reckoning for the next two centuries, were Thana, Chaul, Mahim and Sopara, “a place of consequence”. One more stone shows the old name still in official use at the very end of Hindu rule: an inscribed slab “brought from Nila about a mile north of Sopara”, naming the Yadava king Ramachandradeva (whose reign the Gazetteer puts at 1280 to 1298), uses the name Shurparak twice. Nila is the village the Gazetteer elsewhere calls Nala, a plantain village and trade centre in 1882; whether it gave the first half of the later name Nallasopara the sources do not say outright, but this stone is that village’s oldest trace. [n36 §3; Gaz. XIII-II p. 444] [n40 A15; Gaz. XIV p. 387]

The armies of Delhi reach the sea (1300 – 1318)

The blow came from the north. Ala-ud-din Khalji, Sultan of Delhi, had made Alp Khan his governor of Gujarat, and Alp Khan held that post from 1300 to 1318. At some point, probably between 1312 and 1318, he came south to Sanjan, the old Parsi port forty-eight miles north of Sopara, and defeated its chief and “his warlike subjects the Parsis”. [n03; TG-II p. 438 & n. 4]

Here the evidence splits, and the split should be kept. Parsi tradition, preserved in the Kisseh-i-Sanjan, remembers the sack of Sanjan but blames a general of Mahmud Begada of Gujarat, about a hundred and fifty years later. The Gazetteer preferred the earlier date and Alp Khan, partly because the geographer Abu-l-Fida, writing in the early fourteenth century, already called Sanjan the last town of Gujarat. Both readings agree on the outcome: the Parsis who survived scattered to Nargol and into the hills. [n03; TG-II p. 438 & n. 4; TG-XIV Sanjan entry]

In 1318 Devagiri itself fell, and the Yadava kingdom that had held the Konkan through its viceroys was gone. Mubarak Shah Khalji, who ruled at Delhi from 1317 to 1320, pushed his outposts to the sea and occupied Thana and Salsette. When Muhammad bin Tughluq came to the throne in 1325, coins were struck in his name at Daman. [n03; TG-II pp. 438–439; TG-XIV p. 356]

What this conquest meant on the ground comes through, unusually, in the words of the Christian friars who arrived just after it. Jordanus and Odoric of Pordenone, travelling between 1321 and 1324, reported that the “Saracens” had lately seized the whole country and “destroyed an infinite number of idol temples and likewise many churches”. There was a military governor, a malik, and a qazi to judge. The people they met were Hindus, Parsis who “worshipped fire”, Muslims and scattered Nestorian Christians. [n03; TG-II pp. 439–440] The Gazetteer, summing up the longer story, wrote that Muslims (it says “in the early thirteenth century”, probably a slip for the fourteenth) and later the Portuguese “destroyed temples and stone-faced reservoirs by the score”. [n03; TG-II pp. 428–429]

For Sopara itself, the claim of Muslim destruction rests on a single hedged sentence. “Of stone temples,” the Gazetteer’s Sopara entry says, “many are said to have been destroyed by the Musalmans in the beginning of the fourteenth century.” The words “are said to” matter, and so does the footnote: the only source given is page 23 of Jordanus’s Mirabilia, which, on the reading made for this book, does not obviously support it. The same entry goes on to say that “enough were spared, or repaired, to excite the admiration” of the first Portuguese, in 1530 to 1540, and that the real stripping of Sopara came in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, when the lake banks were stripped and the temples pulled down and their stones carried off to build Portuguese churches, forts and “the great walls … of Bassein”. The fair summary is this: the Delhi conquest of the 1310s and 1320s was violent and the friars saw temples and churches destroyed somewhere on this coast; that Sopara’s own temples fell then is a tradition; and Sopara’s temples were still standing, many of them, two centuries later. [n37 §1; Gaz. XIV p. 323] [n36 §6; Gaz. XIV p. 323]

Four friars and a church of St Thomas (1321 – 1324)

The Gazetteer’s notes on Jordanus’s own account let the scene be told more fully, and it deserves to be, because it is the first event on this coast that anyone wrote down from the inside. The party reached Thana and was received there by Nestorian Christians, “of whom there were fifteen families”. Jordanus, by this account, “came to Thana with the four friars, went off at once to preach at Sopara”, and so was not with the others when they were arrested. Under questioning before the Muslim governor, Thomas is reported to have called the Prophet “the son of perdition”. The friar James was thrown into a fire and, the account says, came out unharmed; the qazi still urged the governor to put them to death. Three were beheaded. Peter, who had stayed behind, was taken and cut asunder the next day. [n38 §1; Gaz. XIV pp. 356–357 and notes]

Then Jordanus, “on hearing of the massacre, came and taking the bodies to Sopara, buried them there”. The burial is the hinge of the story. Within a year another friar, Odoric of Pordenone, “came to Thana within a year of the martyrdom, visited the tomb, and carried off the martyrs’ bones”. The Gazetteer takes this from Jordanus’s Mirabilia and from Henry Yule’s Cathay and the Way Thither. [n38 §1; Gaz. XIV p. 357, citing Mirabilia V and Yule’s Cathay I]

The date is not settled. Wikipedia’s account of the cult of Thomas of Tolentino gives 8 April 1321; the Gazetteer and the Acta Sanctorum prefer 1322; the feast is kept on 9 April. The later journey of the relics, as Italian cult sources tell it, is extraordinary: Thomas’s skull went with Odoric east to Quanzhou and to Khanbaliq, and came back to the Franciscans of Tolentino in 1330, where it was later set in a silver bust in the cathedral. Those details rest on a secondary source and should be read as the tradition of Tolentino. But the core is secure: four Latin friars died at Thana, and their bodies lay for a time in Sopara’s soil before travelling to the far ends of Asia and Europe. The tomb at Sopara linked this creek-island to Tolentino, to Quanzhou and to the story of the Franciscan order. [n38 §1; Wikipedia: Thomas of Tolentino; Gaz. XIV pp. 356–357]

What Jordanus found among the living is as interesting as what he buried. “There seems to have been a relic of the old Kalyan bishopric at Sopara,” the Gazetteer wrote, “as Jordanus found many Nestorian Christians and a church dedicated to St Thomas the Apostle” there. In his fifteen days he instructed and baptised “many people” and gave communion to about ninety Christians. Between Thana and Sopara, the Gazetteer’s population volume adds, he made thirty-five converts, and he found the “pagans”, by whom he meant Parsis and Hindus, “willing to listen”. In his day, it notes, the Parsis’ worship of fire and their exposure of the dead were still common on this coast. [n38 §1; Gaz. XIV p. 322] [n37 §1; Gaz. XIII-I p. 200; Gaz. XIII-I p. 66]

The Sopara church’s roots are older than Jordanus and harder to see. The sixth-century traveller Kosmas, writing in about 535, had recorded a bishop at Kalyan appointed from Persia, and Christians were reported at Chaul in 942. The Gazetteer suggested, as a hedged inference, that the Christians of Kalyan and Sopara had been strengthened in the fifth century by Nestorian refugees from persecution in the Byzantine world. So the ninety communicants of 1321 were the tail of a church perhaps eight centuries old, linked by its bishops to Persia, not Rome. The Latin friar who gave them communion belonged to a different church; the sources do not say how either side understood the encounter. [n37 §1; Gaz. XIII-I p. 200; Gaz. XIII-II p. 432 n. 4] [n38 §1 Inferences]

Jordanus’s letter of 1321, asking for two friars to be stationed at Supera, is often read as the Catholic Church’s first formal request for missionaries on this coast. It would be two centuries before the request was answered, and the answer, when it came, would come with guns. [n38 §1 Inferences]

The killing at Thana that sent Jordanus north with his companions’ bodies was not, it turned out, a matter of local indifference. According to the friars’ account the Emperor recalled the governor responsible and had him executed for it. [n03; TG-II pp. 439–440] Odoric, passing through in 1323 or 1326 (the sources disagree), is said by one source to have carried the martyrs’ relics east as far as Quanzhou in China. [n25; Wikipedia: Thomas of Tolentino]

For Sopara the episode is a window. There was still a church here, dedicated to St Thomas, with a congregation that a visitor could count. The Gazetteer believed that Christian settlements at Kalyan and Sopara might have been strengthened as far back as the fifth century by Nestorian refugees from Persia and Mesopotamia, and Kosmas Indikopleustes in the sixth century had described a Christian bishopric at Kalyan. [n03; TG-II p. 420 n. 4] Jordanus’s ninety communicants are the last clear sight of that old eastern Christianity at Sopara. When Christianity next appears in the record of this coast, two centuries later, it will arrive from the opposite direction, on Portuguese ships, and it will bear little relation to the church Jordanus found.

Jordanus went on to be appointed bishop of Columbum, Quilon in Kerala, in 1328 or 1329, and wrote a book of wonders, the Mirabilia descripta, which the historian Beazley judged better on India than Marco Polo. [n25; Wikipedia: Jordan Catala] The cult of Thomas of Tolentino was approved by Popes Pius VII in 1809 and Leo XIII in 1894. [n25; Wikipedia: Thomas of Tolentino]

A sultanate in name (1325 – 1390)

After the friars leave, the record for Sopara and for the harbour to its south goes quiet for most of a century.

That silence is itself evidence of a kind. When the geographer Abulfeda, writing around 1330, came to the entry for the town, he gave it a Latin-rendered epitaph: “Sufalah Indica. Biruni nominatur Sufarah… De eo nihil commemorandum inveni” — “Sufala of India, called Sufara by Biruni… I have found nothing worth recording about it.” [n15; Hobson-Jobson] The famous port had become a name in older books.

The neighbouring towns kept their fame. Abu-l-Fida called Thana the best city of al-Lar, famous for tanasi cloth and for tabashir, the “bamboo manna” collected in the hills. Rashid-ud-din in 1310 wrote “Konkan-Tana”, Ibn Battuta in 1342 “Kukin-Taina”, and the Catalan Atlas of 1375, drawn far away in Europe, marked “Cucintana”. [n03; TG-XIV p. 356] Ibn Battuta’s own route, in 1343, bypassed the Thana ports, and no mention of Sopara or Bassein has been found in his travels. [n03; TG-II p. 440] [n15]

What we can see is that Delhi’s grip loosened fast. Under Muhammad bin Tughluq, who ruled from 1325 to 1351, Delhi took little interest in the Konkan. In 1341 the chief of Jawhar, in the hills inland, was recognised as lord of twenty-two forts. In 1350, when the summons went out to the nobles of the province to gather at Daulatabad, no Konkan nobles came. And as late as 1404 a Hindu chief of Bhiwandi was still issuing land grants in his own name. The Gazetteer’s verdict is blunt: “Musalman supremacy can have been little more than a name.” [n03; TG-II pp. 440–441]

The trade of the coast was shifting too, and for reasons decided far inland. In 1347 the new Bahmani sultans of the Deccan made their capital at Kulbarga, Gulbarga, and the Deccan’s sea trade turned south to their ports at Chaul and Dabhol. When the Ahmadnagar sultanate rose in 1496, it raised Chaul again. Horses from Hormuz and Aden kept the old link to eastern Arabia alive, but it ran through the southern harbours. The North Konkan ports, the Gazetteer judged, were reduced by the fifteenth-century conquests of the Ahmadabad kings. Through all this Sopara stayed, in the Gazetteer’s words, “a place of consequence”, a lesser harbour on a silting creek, still trading but no longer leading. [n36 §3; Gaz. XIII-II p. 444]

A small sign of how the old town was being forgotten abroad sits beside a sign of how it was remembered at home. Abulfeda found nothing to say about it around 1330. But in the far north, in Kashmir, a Sanskrit poem of the 1140s, Mankha’s Shrikanthacharita, had recorded Sopara’s delegate Tejakantha at a literary assembly under King Aparaditya; and in the 1300s the Jain pilgrim’s guide of Jinaprabhasuri still counted Soparaka among the holy places of the Jains. Sopara’s reputation now lived in books and in pilgrim memory more than on the charts of the sea. [n36 §3; Indraji 1882 p. 278 n. 8] [n22; Wikipedia: Sopara]

Somewhere in this loose, half-governed world, a local polity with a name of its own was taking shape. In 1368 a king called Hambirarao set up an inscription at Deonar, in Salsette, that mentions “Konkan-Bimbasthana”, the Konkan seat or land of Bimba. It was found in 2016 by the Salsette Explorations Project, and as of 2023 five such inscriptions were known. [n03; Wikipedia: Mahikavatichi Bakhar; Dalal et al. 2023; New Indian Express 2016] To understand why that name matters to so many families in Vasai today, we need to turn from stones to a chronicle.

Raja Bimba: the king the chronicle remembers

In about 1448 a man named Keshavacharya sat down to write for Nayako-rao, the Desala or revenue officer of Malad. His purpose was practical: to set down the history that justified the hereditary revenue rights of his patron’s family. What he wrote became the core, chapters two and three, of a Marathi chronicle known as the Mahikavatichi Bakhar, also called the Shri Bimbakhyana. Over the next two centuries others expanded it, and it was finished in the seventeenth century. It names 396 places, 336 of them in the northern Konkan. It has been edited by Rane in 1877, by the historian Rajwade in 1924, by Dhere in 1973 and by Dahisarkar in 2024. No English translation exists. [n03; Wikipedia: Mahikavatichi Bakhar; Rajwade edition, archive.org]

The story it tells is a dynastic saga. A prince named Pratap Bimb, brother of Govardhan Bimb of Champaner in Gujarat, arrives in about 1138. He holds the coast from Chikhli to Tarapur and makes his capital at Mahikavati, which is Mahim near Kelve in today’s Palghar district. His general, Balkrishnarao Somvanshi, takes Thane from a Shilahara king the chronicle calls “Yashwantrao”. Later Pratap Bimb founds a second Mahikavati at the Mahim that is now part of Mumbai, in Salsette. After him come Nagar-shah of Gandevi; a Yadava prince called Bimbdev, son of “Ramdevrao”; an affair called the Bhagadchuri under a Delhi officer called “Nika Malik”; the Tughluq conquest; local chiefs of the Nayate and Bhongale families; the Gujarat sultans, whom the chronicle dates from 1429 to 1534; and finally the Portuguese and “the Treaty of Bassein”. [n03; Wikipedia: Mahikavatichi Bakhar] (Two Mahims run through this story, and readers should keep them apart: Kelve-Mahim on the Palghar coast north of the Vaitarna, and Mahim on Bombay island.)

How much of this is history? The honest answer is: the frame, some of the geography, and probably a kernel, but not the kings and not their dates. Modern scholars note that “archaeological, epigraphic, and numismatic evidence does not corroborate many smaller early dynasties” in the chronicle. Rajwade judged its geography reliable. A royal seal attributed to Keshavdev has been noted by Dipesh Karmarkar. [n03; Wikipedia: Mahikavatichi Bakhar; Karmarkar 2012] The Indian Antiquary concluded that there is no authentic record of Bimb’s identity and rejected the popular claims that he was a Solanki or a Yadava, though it allowed that he apparently moved his capital from Thana. [n18; Indian Antiquary vol. 55] The 1882 Gazetteer recorded that people generally believed Bimb was a prince of Paithan on the Godavari, but suspected a confusion between Paithan and Patan (Anhilvada), the Solanki capital of Gujarat. It added that copies of local grants referring to Bimb belong to about 1280, when the Yadavas of Devagiri were overlords, and that “Bimbsthan”, apparently an old name of Bhiwandi, also pointed to a Deccan Bimb. “The question is doubtful,” it concluded. [n03; TG-II p. 437 n.]

And yet the stones are there. Besides the Deonar inscription of 1368, a stone found at Koprad, a hamlet of Bolinj about ten miles north of Bassein and close to Sopara, is dated in two calendars, Shaka 1386 and Hijri 868, which is 1464. It is written in Devanagari, carries Muslim names, and names Mahim, Thana, Koprad and “Bimbstan”. [n03; TG-XIV Koprad and Sopara entries; TG-II p. 441 n. 4] So in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries there really was a “Bimbasthan”, a place or polity with Bimba’s name on it, known to people writing inscriptions within a short walk of Sopara. The person behind the name remains out of reach. The safest reading, and the one this book follows, is that “Raja Bimba” is a founding figure written down between the fifteenth and seventeenth centuries, wrapped around a real Bimbasthan of the thirteenth and fourteenth. [n03; Inferences]

Three Bimbas

Newer reading, done for this book in the colonial ethnographies, a Bombay society paper of 1838 and Edwardes’s Rise of Bombay, shows that the story of Bimba was never one story. It comes in three main versions, and each was told by someone with a reason to tell it that way. [n41 §1; n35 §2]

The Bimba of Devagiri and Paithan. The first version, the one most families on this coast tell today, makes Bimba a prince of the Yadava royal house of Devagiri who came down to the coast from Paithan on the Godavari at the end of the thirteenth century. The communities who gave Enthoven their account for his Tribes and Castes of Bombay (1920–22), through informants he names and through a community association called the S. K. Hitechhu Mandali, put it this way: they “claim to have come to these parts of Bombay, Salsette, Kelva-Mahim, the Ashtagar sea-coast villages of Alibag taluka and the adjoining districts, with Raja Bimba or Bhima, about the end of the thirteenth century, from Paithan on the Godavari south of Devgiri. Hence their Kshatriya origin.” The last three words are the point. A Yadava prince for a founder made his followers Kshatriyas, members of the warrior order. [n41 §1; Enthoven III pp. 160–161]

The Devagiri version has documents behind it, of a kind. The verse chronicle Bimbakhyan, as Edwardes summarised it in 1902, says that Bimbadev came to the Konkan in Shaka 1216, which is 1294 CE, and halted on Mahim island. With him, it says, came nine families of Yajurvedi Brahmins of the Madhyandin branch and sixty-six other families: twenty-seven Somavanshi (lunar-line) clans, twelve Suryavanshi (solar-line), nine Sheshvanshi, five Panchal, seven Kunbi or Agri, and families of Lads, Modhs and others. A Persian paper kept by the Sirdesai family of Malad, dated 901 of the Hijra, 1495 CE, says that “Bimbshah” fled after Alauddin Khalji defeated his father Ramadev of Devagiri, travelling with the royal guru Purushottam Pant, and took Parnera, Bardi, Sanjan, Daman, Sigaon and Mahim, dividing the land into twelve parts (fifteen in another place). And a Marathi prose chronicle by Janardan Ganesh of Mahim, written shortly after the Maratha capture of Bassein in 1739, begins with Ramadev’s defeat in about 1294 and makes Bimb Raja his second son, who left in 1295 “with twelve other Solar princes, 64 Lunar Kshatriya families” and took fifteen districts and 444 villages from Chikhne and Tarapur to Salsette. Edwardes, who reported much of this, warned that the dates are inaccurate and the statements “so very contradictory” that none can be accepted without independent evidence; but he still concluded that the settlers came “from Paithan with King Bhimdev, the son of Ramdev, Raja of Devgiri”. [n41 §1; Edwardes 1902 pp. 22–25, 30–31; Trans. Bombay Geog. Soc. I pp. 128–137]

The Bimba of Gujarat. The second version moves the prince north. Enthoven’s own informants offered it as an alternative: that “king Bimba, though descended from the royal family of Devgiri, had a principality at Anhilvada Patan in Gujarat proper, whence he migrated on the overthrow of the Hindu principalities by the Muhammadans … during the regime of the Khiljis”, and made Mahim, Mahikavati, his capital. The historian José Gerson da Cunha went further: Bhima was “evidently … the Solanki ruler of Anahilavada in Gujarat, and not the Yadav Bhima of Devagiri”. [n41 §1; Enthoven III pp. 161–162; da Cunha, Origin of Bombay pp. 42–43]

The 1882 Gazetteer built the strongest case for Gujarat, and it is worth setting out because its reasons are evidence, not tradition. It doubted the Paithan story for four reasons. There is no record that Paithan was sacked by Muslims before 1318, so a flight from Paithan in 1294 or 1297 lacks a cause. The fifty years between 1297 and the fall of Bimba’s chiefship (which it dated to Muhammad bin Tughluq in 1347) seemed too short for all that Salsette developed under them. The Prabhu and Palshe records agree that their first settlements were on the coast, at Kelve-Mahim, Bassein and Salsette, not inland. And their speech and customs looked northern: the Prabhus spoke an “incorrect Marathi” with Gujarati words for household things (dadar for a ladder, takabari for a wall bookcase, ovara for a room, ota for a veranda), though the Gazetteer admitted that two centuries of Prabhu service in Gujarat might explain some of these; the Palshes followed the White Yajurveda, the Gujarat form; and, as in Gujarat, they forbade marriage between people whose mothers’ fathers came of the same stock. A grant to a Palshe priest, whose privileges were “still enjoyed” in 1882, said that the priest came from Pattan and that Bimb was of the Anhilvada family. [n35 §1; n41 §1; Gaz. XIII-I pp. 61–62 and nn.]

The Gazetteer found two Gujarat Bimbas in the chronicles. The Arab historian Ibn al-Athir names a Bimb, nephew of the king of Anhilvada, who came to relieve Somnath when Mahmud of Ghazni attacked it in 1025, and records that many of Somnath’s people escaped by sea. And another Prabhu account, in the Gujarat chronicle Ras Mala, makes Bimb “Bhimdev II of Anhilvada or Patan, who fled … on the approach of Kutub-ud-din in 1194”. Its conclusion was that “the Thana coast [was] a favourite resort for refugees and settlers from Kathiawar”, and that “one large settlement seems to have supplied the foreign element” in the Palshe Brahmins, the Patane Prabhus, the Pachkalshis, Chavkalshis, Somvanshi Kshatris, Sutars and Malis, and, by their own account, some of the Agris and Bhandaris. “The correctness of this story is doubtful,” it wrote of Paithan. [n41 §1; Gaz. XIII-I pp. 61–62]

Language, the most independent witness, leans the same way. In 1905 George Grierson’s Linguistic Survey of India recorded the Vadval dialect of Bassein, Mahim and Dahanu as Marathi with “some forms and words” that “are Gujarati and not Marathi”, and with sound changes like those of the Bhil dialects of the Gujarat border: ch turning to s or h, s and sh to h, so that gold is hona and a field hat. Grierson noted that the Vadvals “are supposed to have immigrated from Gujarat”. That fits a Gujarat background far better than a Godavari one. [n41 §1, §8; LSI VII pp. 145–146]

The Bimba who was no prince. The third version comes from the other side of a caste quarrel. In 1838 a paper in the Transactions of the Bombay Geographical Society compared five manuscript accounts of Bimb. One, from Bassein, made him “a Shudra chief”, whose followers divided into Palshe priests, Prabhu ministers and “humbler followers … settled in the gardens as cultivators and carpenters, the forefathers of the present Panchkulseas”. The writer added that Brahmins “treat the pretensions to caste of all these three classes with contempt, maintaining that they are all sprung from one Shoodra origin”. Another paper in the same bundle called the Pathare Prabhus “Purbheej”, illegitimate. [n41 §1, §4; Trans. Bombay Geog. Soc. I pp. 128–137]

Seen side by side, the three Bimbas are partly arguments about rank. A Yadava prince of Devagiri, or a Solanki king of Gujarat, makes his followers Kshatriyas; a Shudra adventurer makes them nothing of the kind. The 1838 paper shows that the argument was already alive in Bombay in the 1830s, and it would still be alive in Enthoven’s day. The dates cannot be reconciled: 1025 (Ibn al-Athir’s Bimb at Somnath), about 1138 (Pratap Bimb in the Mahikavatichi Bakhar), 1194 (the Ras Mala’s Bhimdev II), 1294 to 1297 (the Bimbakhyan and the community tradition), and 1495, the date of the Persian paper that tells the story. The Gujarat and Paithan versions may both hold something true, since the Bimbakhyan itself has Bimb passing through Anhilvada on his way. What the evidence supports is a migration, or several, from the north to this coast in the centuries before the Portuguese, remembered through a founder whose identity each community shaped to its own needs. [n41 §1 Inferences; n35 §2 Inferences]

The families who came with Bimba

The Bimba story matters in Vasai because so many communities say they arrived with him.

The Palshe Brahmins, who call themselves Vajasaneyi Brahmans, were the old priestly class of this coast. The Gazetteer recorded that their name probably comes from Palsavli, a village in Kalyan which, according to the Bimbakhyan, Bimb gave to his family priest. They were “generally believed to have come in 1297 from Mungi Paithan… with Bimb”, though the Gazetteer’s own guess was that they more likely came from Gujarat alongside the Prabhus and Pachkalshis. In its day they held the privilege of fixing the auspicious times for marriages and thread ceremonies for all classes in Salsette, Bassein, Mahim and Dahanu, and worked as priests, physicians and astrologers. [n08; Gazetteer 1882 (Population) p. 81]

The Pachkalshis, also known in the nineteenth century as Sutars, Malis, Vadvals, Chaukalshis, Somvanshi Kshatris and Pathares, said they came “with Bhimdev from Paithan on the Godavari at the close of the 13th century”. Their name, in their own explanation, comes from the five earthen pots, the kalash, used in their ceremonies; a modern telling describes a headman’s throne topped by five kalash, and explains that the Vasai and Palghar branch were “Chaukalshi”, using one pot fewer. Here too the Gazetteer wondered whether they had really come from Gujarat. [n08; Gazetteer 1882 pp. 129–130; Jio Institute exhibit, Babhai Gaothan] [n03; Vartak (Wikipedia); wikiwand: Somvanshi Kshatriya Pathare] The Gazetteer summed up the whole cluster: the upper coastal castes, Palshe, Pathare Prabhu, Pachkalshi, Chaukalshi, Somvanshi Kshatri, Sutar, Mali, and in part the Agris and Bhandaris, said by their own accounts that they came from one settlement “under Bimb, a prince of the Devgiri family who established a chiefship at Mahim”. [n08; Gazetteer 1882 pp. 61–62]

These are community traditions, and they should be told as such. They are also interesting evidence in themselves. The dates they give, 1297 and “the close of the 13th century”, fall exactly in the Yadava twilight, the decades just before Devagiri fell. Whether or not a Bimba led anyone anywhere, the families of this coast remembered their own arrival as belonging to that last Hindu kingdom of the Deccan, before the sultans.

The Palshes: three origins for one priesthood

Enthoven’s survey, read for this book, gives the Palshes’ case in detail. He treats the Palshikars as Yajurvedi Brahmins of the Madhyandin branch, counted under the Deshastha group, and records that their name comes from Palsavali in Kalyan, “presented by Bimba to his family priests” according to the Bimbakhyan. They “are generally believed to have come in 1297 A.D. from Mungi Paithan on the Godavari with Bimba. But it seems,” he added, “that they formed part of the large settlement which from Gujarat supplied the foreign element to the Thana district.” They worked as “priests, physicians and astrologers”. The census figures show how large a priesthood this was: 2,311 Palshes in the Thana district in 1872, by one count in the Gazetteer, and 9,311 by another; Enthoven’s 1901 return of 478 was a narrow count. [n35 §1; Enthoven I pp. 248–249; Gaz. XIII-I p. 63]

So the Palshes, like Bimba, have three origins on offer: Paithan, Anhilvada Pattan, and the general Gujarat settlement. The reason the argument mattered comes later in this book. After the Maratha conquest of 1739, Chitpavan and Deshastha Brahmins from the Deccan settled as priests and, in the Gazetteer’s words, “to a great extent ousted the Palshis and other older classes of priests”; Chitpavans refused to dine with them, and the Palshes defended their rank with Peshwa-era grants. A Paithan origin, near the old Deccan heartland, was a stronger claim to Brahmin rank than a coastal one. The story of 1297 was told, in part, by a priesthood defending itself in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. [n35 §1 and Inferences; Gaz. XIII-I p. 63]

What the Palshes did on this coast is better attested than where they came from. They were the priests of the Pachkalshis, of the Pathare Prabhus, and of the Mangela fishers, whose weddings they held at sunset; and they held the right of fixing the auspicious times of marriages and thread ceremonies “for all classes”. A shared priesthood is a sign of a shared society, and it may be the strongest evidence that the coastal castes of the Bimba story did, in some sense, arrive or grow up together. [n35 §10; Enthoven III pp. 160, 250; Gaz. XIII-I pp. 81, 147]

The Pathare Prabhus, whom the Palshes served as priests, have a parallel argument about their own origins. Their tradition, Enthoven wrote, and “to some extent the evidence of their home speech”, suggest descent from Rajputs of Anhilvada Pattan; the scholar P. B. Joshi derived “Pathare” from the Pratihara dynasty; and a story in the Sahyadrikhanda explains the name “Pattan Prabhu” through a curse at Paithan. Enthoven’s own judgement stitched them together: Pratihara kings, then Paithan on the Godavari in the eleventh and twelfth centuries, and then the north Konkan “at the close of the 13th century … along with their gurus … the Shukla Yajurvediya, otherwise called Palshikar, Brahmans”. Their home Marathi, he noted, differed by “the Gujarati element” and carried “a large number of Hindustani, Portuguese and English words in every day use”. [n35 §2; Enthoven III pp. 249–250]

The cultivating castes of the inland Thana district had their own layers, which the Gazetteer read in their surnames. The Talheri Kunbis, it thought, were made of “a local [element] apparently little different from the Son Koli” and a foreign one that came in two periods, “before and after the times of the Musalmans and Portuguese”. The earlier foreign layer showed in Rajput-like surnames, More, Salunkhe, Jadhav, Yadav, Povar, Chohan and Shelar; the later one in the Maratha names Bhosle, Kadam, Shirke and Samble, which came with the conquerors of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. In Bassein the Talheri Kunbis were few, a thousand or so in 1872 against thirty-three thousand in Shahapur; the island’s own cultivators belonged to what the Gazetteer called a third group, “probably of part Gujarat descent”, the Malis, Chavkalshis and Somvanshi Kshatris, “roughly brought under the general term Pachkalshis”. The Gujarat thread again. [n35 §4; Gaz. XIII-I pp. 124–125]

Five pots, four pots, and a garden

The names of the gardening castes are themselves small histories. “Panchkalshi”, Enthoven’s informants explained, comes from the five kalashas, “little cupolas” on the bridegroom’s chair of state, or five earthen water-pots set at either side of the entrance at a wedding; the Gazetteer gave the simpler version, five earthen pots used in their marriage ceremonies. The Chaukalshis of Vasai and Palghar used four. A Marathi editor of 1935, Gajanan Govind Naik, argued that “Panchkalshi” began not as a caste name at all but as a badge of Kshatriya rank, five pots on a throne or at a wedding, which those who lost the right to one pot exchanged for “Chaukalshi”. [n41 §2; Enthoven III p. 160; Gaz. XIII-I p. 129; Naik 1935 p. 15]

“Vadval” is a working name. In Enthoven’s words it “literally means the managers of wadis or orchards, viz., of cocoa-nut and palm groves”, the title of the subdivision found in the Bassein, Mahim and Dahanu talukas, the country the Portuguese would call the Feringana. The 1909 Gazetteer of Bombay City and Island described the Vadvals of its own day as people “whose chief occupation was the tending of orchards and gardens, the weaving and preparation of palm-leaves for thatching, and the manufacture of coir-rope and brooms”. The 1838 paper put the same idea in a soldier’s terms: the Panchkalshis “received the name of Wadavul, or gardener, merely from having given up war, and taken to the cultivation of the cocoanut gardens”. Whether or not they had ever been warriors, by the time the record catches them the Vadvals were the people of the coconut wadi. [n41 §2; Enthoven III p. 160; Bombay City Gaz. I p. 238; Trans. Bombay Geog. Soc. I p. 136]

The split of the fourteenth century

Within a few generations of their supposed arrival, the community divided. “The Vadvals are said to have separated some time in the fourteenth century,” Enthoven recorded, and the reason given was striking: “The split between Vadvals and the rest of the caste is reputed to be due to the admission of a Koli into the caste. Both the Vadvals and the Sashtikars allege that the Koli was admitted to the sub-division to which they do not belong. The fact remains that for some such reason Vadvals became a separate endogamous sub-division several centuries ago.” [n41 §4; Enthoven III pp. 161–162] [n35 §2]

The same memory appears in an older form. The verse Bimbakhyan, as the 1838 paper summarised it, explained that the Panchkalshis were “Lunar Kshatriyas who were degraded and forced to become carpenters for having admitted to their caste and eaten with a Koli who had assisted in seizing their enemy in the water”. That is a scene with a story behind it: a Koli, a man of the creeks, who helped catch an enemy in the water, and was taken into the caste in gratitude, at the cost of its rank. Nobody can date it, and each side blamed the other. But the story keeps returning to the same fact, that the newcomers of the Bimba tradition mixed with the Kolis who were already here. [n41 §1, §4; Trans. Bombay Geog. Soc. I pp. 128–137]

The language evidence agrees. The 1838 writer found that the five “oldest races” of the island, Kolis, Bhandaris, Palshes, Pathare Prabhus and the “Panchkulseas, Wadavuls or Carpenters, which caste also take care of all the cocoanut gardens on the island”, shared a “Colee bhasha”, a Koli speech, strongest among the Kolis themselves. Seventy years later the Bombay Gazetteer said the home dialect of a Panchkalshi farming woman was “hardly distinguishable from the dialect of a Koli fish-wife”. A people who came from the north, if they did, took up the speech of the shore. [n41 §1, §8; Trans. Bombay Geog. Soc. I pp. 128–131; Bombay City Gaz. I p. 239]

The Vadvals’ right to the name Panchkalshi was still being argued five centuries later, with letters from the Shankaracharya in 1836 and 1852 and a resolution of the Palshikar Brahmins of Bassein taluka in 1855, documents that Enthoven saw and that satisfied him the Vadvals were “entitled to style themselves Panchkalshis”. The quarrel that the fourteenth century started belongs, in its later rounds, to later chapters. [n41 §4; Enthoven III p. 162; Enthoven I p. xix]

Other arrivals, other memories

Bimba’s is not the only arrival story of these centuries. Several communities of the Thana coast remember journeys that fall in the sultanate age, and they are worth gathering, with the same caution. [n35]

The Thakurs, a hill people of the Thana district, told Enthoven that “the Rajput element in the tribe is said to be due to certain fugitives from Gujarat having taken refuge among them during the reign of Mahmud Begada (1459–1511)”; Dr John Wilson, in his earlier work on the tribes, had heard that they descended from “the Thakurs of Gujarat, who fled to the hills in the time of Mahmud Begada’s persecutions”. A copper plate found with a Thakur at Nashik shows the word in use since about 650 CE. The Son Kolis of the coast held, by a “probable inference from tradition”, that most of them descended from hill people who had come down to the coast some four hundred years before 1920, which would put the move in the early sixteenth century; Enthoven’s contributor doubted it, since he found it hard to imagine hillmen turned suddenly into fishers. [n35 §3, §5; Enthoven II p. 259; Enthoven III pp. 376–377]

The Gujarat thread runs through the fishing castes too. The Gazetteer listed Machhis, Mitne Machhis, Mangelas, Kharvis and Kharpatils among arrivals from Gujarat who “dress in Gujarat fashion and speak Gujarati at home”; Mangela speech was full of Gujarati words. Some Bhandaris and Agris kept “the remembrance of a hurried flight from the south and some traces of Lingayat customs”. And the goldsmiths who called themselves Daivadnya Brahmins said they had settled in Thana “on the Portuguese conquest of Goa in 1510”, which, if true, would make them refugees of the first Portuguese conquest on this coast, a generation before Bassein fell. None of these accounts is dated by a document. Together they describe a coast that people kept arriving on, from Gujarat in the north, the Deccan in the east, and Goa and the south. [n35 §3, §4; Gaz. XIII-I pp. 63–64]

The Parsis, by contrast, were leaving. Their tradition, as Enthoven’s Parsi entry records it, says they were “very numerous” in Thana until about 1533, when the Portuguese conquered Salsette, and that they then left for Kalyan, where a tower of silence was built in 1533; one telling has them feast the Portuguese officers and slip away while their hosts were drunk. Pahlavi inscriptions at Kanheri from 999 and 1021, left by Parsis whose names suggest fresh arrivals from Persia, show how long they had been in the neighbourhood. [n35 §7; Enthoven III pp. 192–195]

The Samvedi Brahmins of north Vasai have their own story, and it sits uneasily with Bimba. The oldest documentary trace of Samaveda Brahmins on this coast is a Shilahara copper plate from Bhandup, dated 1026, which grants land to a Brahmin of the Chhandoga branch of the Sama Veda living at Nahur in Salsette. That proves Samavedi Brahmins received grants here three centuries before our chapter opens; it does not prove that the modern community descends from them. [n03; TG-II p. 425 n.] In 1882 the headmen of Koprad and of Gass, beside Sopara, were Samvedi Brahmins, and the Koprad stone of 1464 lay near the headman’s house. [n03; TG-XIV Sopara and Koprad entries] [n11; Gaz. 1882 pp. 341–342] The community’s own modern account says they came to Shurparaka from Utkala or Kalinga, in Odisha, as expert singers and temple musicians; that their Vedic practice lapsed under Buddhism and was restored by Adi Shankaracharya at Nirmal; and that under “Raja Pratap Bimba” their work shifted to growing sugarcane and nagvel, the betel vine. The page that carries this account rests on a single reference, is tagged for original research, and gives internally impossible dates. [n08; Wikipedia: Samvedi; nirajpatilsblog 2008] Notably, the two oldest written layers, the Gazetteer and the Bimbakhyan as the Gazetteer reports it, tie the Palshes, not the Samvedis, to Bimb. The Samvedi–Bimba link appears only in modern community tellings. [n08; Inferences] No scholarly source has been found that dates the Samvedis’ arrival. [n03; Gaps]

One more name belongs here, and it belongs to a living place. At Agashi, a single local source holds that the Bhavanishankar temple, built by the Maratha governor Shankarji Keshav in the 1700s, stands on the site of a Bimb-era fort named in the Mahikavati Bakhar, and that large stones and old images were found when its lake was dug. [n11; Durg Bharari] It is a tradition, not a finding. But it shows how close to the surface this story still runs.

Gujarat takes the coast (1390 – 1432)

From about 1390 a new power began to matter here: the Sultanate of Gujarat. Under Muzaffar Shah I (1390–1412) and Ahmad Shah I (1411–1442), Gujarat held the northern Konkan not by ruling every village but by placing garrisons at key points and keeping the Hindu rais friendly or tributary. By 1429 there was a Gujarat garrison under one Qutb Khan at Mahim, and another over Thana. The Gazetteer describes the coast from Navsari to Bombay as under Hindu chiefs, but so far under Gujarat that an army could march through it unopposed. [n03; TG-II p. 441]

That year the arrangement was tested. The Bahmani sultans of the Deccan, rivals of Gujarat, sent their minister Malik-ut-Tujjar raiding into the Konkan under Ahmad Shah Bahmani (1422–1435). When Qutb Khan died, the Deccanis seized Mahim and Salsette. Ahmad Shah of Gujarat answered by land and sea. Seventeen ships came down from Diu, Gogha and Cambay, and a land army under his son Zafar Khan and Malik Iftikhar Khan marched south. According to the Persian historian Firishta, they took Thana by land and sea, stormed the Deccani stockade at Mahim, routed the enemy at Bombay, and beat back a counter-attack on Thana. [n03; TG-II pp. 441–442; Wikipedia: Mumbai under Islamic rule]

This is the moment a hill fort just north of our area first enters the record. Tandulwadi, on the southern point of the Mahim ridge across the Vaitarna, above today’s Saphale station and overlooking the Vaitarna and Surya rivers, is first recorded around 1429 under “Jafar Khan” of the Gujarat Sultanate, very probably the same Zafar Khan. Little of it survives; the climb from Lalthane village takes about two hours. It lies in Palghar taluka, not Vasai, but it watched the same river mouth that Arnala would later guard. A second account, on a Wikipedia mirror, gives a different date and a different name: that in 1454 the Sultan of Ahmedabad took Mahikavati and made his sardar Mallik Allauddin chief of Tandulwadi. The two dates conflict, and neither rests on a primary source reached for this book. In the Gazetteer’s list of forts in 1882, Tandulvadi was one of six inland forts of the Mahim sub-division. [n39 §3; Wikiwand: Tandulwadi fort] [n13; Wikipedia: Tandulwadi Fort] [n13; Wikipedia: Tandulwadi Fort; Treks and Trails] [n12; Gaz. 1882]

The Bahmanis came again, in 1436, 1453 and 1469 (this last under the great minister Mahmud Gawan), but their expeditions mostly struck the central and southern Konkan. Their only permanent posts on the coast were Chaul and Dabhol, far to the south. [n03; TG-II p. 442] The northern coast stayed Gujarat’s. In about 1432, Ahmad Shah sealed his hold in the oldest way: he married his son to the daughter of the chief of Mahim. [n03; TG-II pp. 441–442]

The stones of this period are bilingual in a way that tells its own story. A Devanagari land-grant stone from Sanjan is dated 1432. [n03; TG-II p. 441 n. 4] The Koprad stone of 1464, as we have seen, carries both a Shaka and a Hijri date and both Hindu and Muslim names. Land was still granted in the old script and the old way; but the calendar of the sultans had come to sit beside it.

The chronicler and the stone (1448 – 1464)

It is worth pausing on the two documents of the mid-fifteenth century together, because they are the only two voices of the period that come from inside the community rather than from Delhi, Ahmedabad or Europe.

Keshavacharya, writing in about 1448, was a man of the Gujarat century. He was writing for a revenue officer, about revenue rights, at a time when those rights were held under the sultans. When the finished chronicle came to speak of the Gujarat ruler Bahadur Shah, it remembered him as “Khan-saheb”, a ruler who settled disputes among the Desalas by looking into their genealogies. It records persecution by the Portuguese, but no comparable persecution by the Muslims. [n03; Wikipedia: Mahikavatichi Bakhar] That is worth setting beside the friars’ report of temples destroyed in the 1320s: the Delhi conquest was violent, but the long Gujarat century that followed was remembered locally, by at least one Hindu revenue family, as a time when the old arrangements went on.

The Koprad stone, sixteen years later, is the physical counterpart. It lay in 1882 near the house of Bhiku Patil, the Samvedi Brahman headman of the hamlet. [n11; Gaz. 1882 p. 342] Its mixture of calendars and names is the period in miniature.

The Gazetteer placed Koprad exactly: “half a mile south-west of Bolinj”, a hamlet of the northern garden land of Sopara’s island. In 1882 it was a village where Hindu Samvedis, Hindu Pachkalshis and Christians lived side by side, drying plantains for the Bombay market: about eighty-five Samvedi families, fifteen Pachkalshi families and seventy-five Christian families, “originally Samvedis and Pachkalshis”, across Koprad, Agashi and Vatar. The stone of 1464 had stood there through all the changes between: Gujarat, Portugal, the Marathas and Britain. It is the oldest dated object in the village, and the first time Koprad’s own name appears in writing. [n40 A12; Gaz. XIV p. 342; Gaz. XIII-I p. 395]

Villages that step into the record

Koprad is one of several places on the island whose first written trace falls in or near this period, and it is worth gathering them, because the villages that later chapters will follow, Kupari villages, Vadval villages, Hindu and Muslim villages, mostly enter history this way: one stone, one line, one name.

Vatar, two miles north-west of Sopara, had already appeared in 1150, in a Shilahara grant found at Agashi, which gave the village of “Vattaraka” in Shurparaka and named its headmen as witnesses, among them Risi Mhatara and Changdev Mhatara. Nila, a mile north of Sopara, appears on the Yadava stone of Ramachandradeva in the 1280s or 1290s. Koprad appears in 1464. Virar appears, as “Vihar”, in the Mahikavatichi Bakhar, whose text grew between the fifteenth and seventeenth centuries. Agashi appears as a ship-building port in Portuguese accounts of the 1530s. Bassein itself enters as a “design” of Mahmud Begada between 1503 and 1507 and as Barbosa’s “Baxay” in 1514. The surname Mhatara on the Vatar grant is worth noticing: in 1882 the Gazetteer met a Degu Pobria Mhatara at Gass, with an inscribed stone in front of his house. [n40 A2, A13, A15, C; Gaz. XIV pp. 1–2, 28, 341, 385 and n. 1, 387] [n42 §2]

Gass, on the island’s southern edge, has its own medieval traces, though they are not securely dated to these two centuries. At a field called Sonarbhat and on the shore of the Gass lake at a spot called Mala, the 1882 surveyors found a mound with a broken cross-legged female figure, perhaps Ambika, and the foundations of a temple “said to have been dedicated to Parasnath”; a damaged image, perhaps the Sun holding a lotus in each hand, “probably of the eleventh or twelfth century”; a richly carved stone lotus-pendant; and the foundations of “probably a Brahmanic temple of the eleventh or twelfth century”. The 2020 study by Monish Shah and his colleagues adds two dated objects from the age of the sultans: an image of Surya from Gass inscribed with the year 1412, now held by the Sopara Acchalgachha Jain Sangh, and seven Jain bronzes dated between 1458 and 1555, held by the Jain community of Chalpeth in Agashi. They fit the 2019 finding, by Siddharth Kale, Shah and two Jain monks, of thirteenth- to sixteenth-century inscriptions recording a Jain clan of the Modha caste at Sopara. In the middle of the Gujarat century, the Jains of the island were dedicating images. [n42 §2; Gaz. XIV p. 341; Shah 2020] [n03; Dalal 2019]

One claim must be set aside. The Gazetteer’s population volume mentions “one recent report” of “a Christian village in Bassein that claims to be older than the Portuguese”, adding: “No confirmation of this statement has been received.” It has not been confirmed since. Jordanus’s Christians of 1321 are the last clear sight of an older Christianity here; no source links them to any village of 1534. [n40 §2; Gaz. XIII-I pp. 199–201 n. 7]

Other traces of this plural world are harder to date but should be named. Research in 2019 by Siddharth Kale, Monish Shah and two Jain monks found inscriptions of the thirteenth to the sixteenth century showing a Jain clan of the Modha caste at Sopara. [n03; Dalal, Live History India 2019] The fourteenth-century Jain writer Jinaprabhasuri, in his Vividhatirthakalpa, listed Soparaka among eighty-four Jain holy places, with an image of the first tirthankara, Adinath. [n25] [n22; Wikipedia: Sopara] At Sopara’s Chakreshvar lake, the Gazetteer found a six-inch image of Parshvanath, the twenty-third tirthankara, in the hollow of a great banyan, “probably of the tenth century or earlier”. [n03; TG-XIV pp. 336–337] In the Gass field called Sonarbhat, “the gold field”, stood the tomb of a Muslim saint, Shaikh Akbar-moi-ud-din, edged with very large old bricks; and in 1882 the Muslims of Sopara, called Naitas, were described as “descendants of Arab and Persian refugees and traders”. Neither the tomb nor the Naita settlement is dated in the notes behind this book, but both belong to the long centuries when the Arab and Persian sea-trade touched this shore. [n11; Gaz. 1882 pp. 315, 341] [n25]

Mahmud Begada’s coast (1459 – 1511)

The strongest of the Gujarat sultans, Mahmud Begada, ruled from 1459 to 1511, and under him the hold on the northern Konkan tightened.

In about 1465 he took the hill fort of Bavur, which the Gazetteer tentatively identifies with Bagvada, and Parnera in north Thana. Then trouble came from the south. Between about 1480 and 1493 a rebel from Dabhol named Bahadur Khan Gilani defied both Gujarat and the Bahmanis. In 1490 he sent an Abyssinian commander, Yaqut, with twenty ships to waste Mahim. A Gujarat fleet sent in response was lost in a storm. Begada persuaded the Bahmani sultan to crush Gilani himself, which was done in 1493, and Mahim and the ships were restored. [n03; TG-II pp. 442–443]

In about 1495 Begada reorganised his kingdom into five parts, and one of them was headed by Thana. In 1503, the Persian history Mirat-i-Ahmadi says, he “effected his designs against Bassein and Bombay” and garrisoned Nagothna. The Gazetteer’s second volume gives the Bassein reference as 1507, and calls it its first “certain notice” of the place. [n03; TG-II pp. 442–443; TG-XIV p. 28] It is a sobering fact for anyone who lives in Vasai today: the town’s first certain appearance in written history is as a “design” of a Gujarat sultan, around 1503 to 1507. The Gazetteer explained why it came so late. “As Bassein lies only six miles south of Sopara it can hardly have been a place of consequence so long as Sopara (A.D. 1100) remained a centre of trade.” [n15; Thana Gaz. XIV p. 28] [n32; BG 28]

Sopara was not yet forgotten. A notice of 1500 in the Mirat-i-Ahmadi lists a “Sorab”, probably Sopara, among the Konkan’s places of trade with Gujarat. [n03; TG-XIV p. 322] [n15]

Bhagwanlal Indraji, who knew Sopara’s ground better than any scholar of his century, summed up the hand-over between the two towns in two sentences in 1882: “In the fifteenth century … Sopara seems to have passed to the Musalmán rulers of Ahmadabad. Early in the sixteenth century the building of the Musalmán fort of Bassein, and later … the establishment at Bassein of the Court of the General of the North took from Sopára its former importance.” The fort he meant was Gujarat’s, the stockades and citadel of the 1520s and 1530s. The court was Portugal’s, and belongs to the next chapter. Between them, in about a century, the ancient port became a market village behind its younger neighbour. [n36 §3; Indraji 1882 p. 279]

The name of the new town also settles in this period. “The Musalmans called it Basai,” the Gazetteer notes, “and the Portuguese Baçaim.” [n03; TG-XIV p. 28 n. 2] The word “Vasai” is generally derived from Sanskrit vas, a dwelling or residence; one source glosses it instead as vaas or baas, a settlement, “often of Jain monks”, which is speculative. [n22; Wikipedia: Vasai; Jatland Wiki]

Who actually ran the coast for the sultan is a revealing question. Many of Gujarat’s officials here were Hindus. The governor of Chaul in 1503 and the governor of Surat in 1514 were Hindus, and the “lord of Thana” who would become tributary to the Portuguese in 1529 was apparently a Hindu chief. [n03; TG-II pp. 449–450 nn.] The sultanate on this coast was a Muslim crown over a largely Hindu administration and a largely Hindu countryside of Kolis, Agris, Bhandaris, Samvedis and the gardening castes, with Muslim traders and garrisons in the ports.

How Islam came to the creeks

Two centuries of sultans raise an obvious question: did they make this coast Muslim, and if they did not, why not?

The 1882 Gazetteer’s answer is one of its most quoted sentences. “Though most of Thana was for over 400 years (1300–1720) nominally under Musalman rulers, their power was never thoroughly established, and, unlike Gujarat and the Deccan, Thana seems never to have been the scene of any forcible conversion of Hindus to Islam.” The Muslims of the district, it said, came instead from “traders, refugees and adventurers from Africa, Arabia and Persia”, arriving from as early as 640. [n37 §1; Gaz. XIII-I p. 216]

The largest group, the Konkani Muslims, “owe their origin to the Arab and Persian refugees, merchants, and adventurers, who settled along the coast in the eighth, ninth, and tenth centuries”. Among them, in the Gazetteer’s day, were the Naitas of Sopara, “descendants of Arab and Persian refugees and traders”. The sixteenth-century Portuguese physician Garcia d’Orta knew the “Naitas of Bassein” as men who had married local women and were rich traders when the Portuguese arrived. That is the mechanism the sources describe: merchants who came by sea, settled, married into the coast, and raised families who spoke its language. [n37 §1; Gaz. XIII-I pp. 216–217; Gaz. XIV p. 315 n.] [n35 §6]

The community’s own memory goes back further. Enthoven recorded that the Konkani Muslims of Bombay were known among Muslims as “Kufis”, a name that, he wrote, showed their origin in the Euphrates valley, and that their tradition made “the bulk of them” refugees from the Euphrates valley about the year 700, fleeing “the fierce governor Hajjaj bin Yusuf”. Later waves came with the Rajput-era settlers of Anhilvada and, “during the fourteenth, fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, when much of the Konkan was in the possession of the Bahmani and Bijapur kings”, from “the demand for Arab and Persian soldiers”. “As late as the Marathas the services of Arab seamen were still in demand.” So the sultanate centuries did add to the coast’s Muslims, but through soldiers and seamen hired from abroad, not through converting the villages. [n35 §6; Enthoven III, “Musalmans”]

The Shilahara kings before the sultans had already set the pattern. Their court, the Gazetteer says, was liberal to Arab merchants, “allowing them to have mosques and a headman to settle disputes”. By the tenth century, the Arab geographer al-Istakhri wrote of the coast from Cambay to Chaul: “there are Musulmans in its cities … There are many mosques in these places.” The Muslims of Sopara and Bassein were part of that older world of merchant colonies, under Hindu kings and then under Muslim ones. [n37 §1; Gaz. XIII-II p. 432] [n36 §3; E&D I p. 34]

The fairest account must keep the other side too. The same Gazetteer page that denies forcible conversion also says that the Konkani Muslims include “most of the local Hindus who were converted to Islam either by the preaching of missionaries or by the compulsion of Ahmednagar or Moghal rulers”, and it names Bawa Malang of Malangad as a missionary of “considerable success”. The two statements stand on the same pages without being reconciled. Read together, they suggest a coast where conversion came mostly through traders and saints; compulsion is claimed for Ahmadnagar and the Mughals, not for the Gujarat sultans who held this stretch of shore. [n37 §1 and Inferences; Gaz. XIII-I pp. 66, 217]

The evidence of the ground fits the gentler reading. There is the Koprad stone of 1464, with its Hindu and Muslim names and its two calendars. There is the Bakhar of the Malad revenue family, which remembers Bahadur Shah as a judge of genealogies and saves its complaint of persecution for the Portuguese. At Agashi there was a “great mosque” large enough that the Portuguese made it their commandant’s residence in 1554. At Arnala the Portuguese found “domes and Saracenic arches”. In Gass, a mosque near the silted landing is still called the Bandar Masjid, the harbour mosque; in the field called Sonarbhat stood the tomb of Shaikh Akbar-moi-ud-din; and at Pali, in south Bassein, a modern heritage survey speaks of “a significant Muslim population due to the traders and merchants who had settled here” before the Portuguese. A dargah of Rehmatullah at Sopara is dated 1555, just after the cession. None of these, apart from the Koprad stone, is dated within the sultanate; together they show where the Muslims of this coast lived: by the harbours. [n40 A2, A15, B; Gaz. XIV pp. 1–2, 10; CRIT Vol I] [n36 §1; Shah 2020] [n42 §2; Gaz. XIV p. 341]

Their language followed the same course. Grierson found that the Konkani Muslims’ speech was Marathi containing “a considerable amount of Hindostani words”; Enthoven described the home speech of the leading Konkani Muslims as “Konkani Marathi with a slightly peculiar pronunciation and a mixture of Urdu, Persian and Arab words”. They followed the Shafi’i school of law, unlike the Hanafi majority of the Deccan. Persian inscriptions of the Gujarat sultanate at Arnala or Vasai, if any survive, were not reached for this book. [n35 §6; Enthoven III] [n22 §1, Gaps]

In Gujarat itself, the sultanate centuries were the age in which several Muslim trading communities took shape, and though their arrival on this island came much later, their origins belong here. The Bohoras, Enthoven wrote, were converts made from the eleventh century by Ismaili missionaries, who had been treated kindly by the Hindu kings of Anhilvada; their name probably comes from the Gujarati word for trading. The Khojas, by tradition, were converted from the Lohana trading caste by Pir Sadruddin around 1430, and still bore the Lohana title Thakkar. The Memons trace their conversion, also from Lohanas, to 1421 in Sind. In each case the converts were merchants and the agents were preachers; and the Gazetteer records the Bohoras and Memons as recent arrivals who came to the Thana coast “from Gujarat through Bombay”, long after the sultans’ day; the Khojas’ arrival in Vasai is undocumented and appears to be later still. [n35 §6; Enthoven I pp. 197–198; Enthoven II pp. 217–220; Enthoven III pp. 51–53; Gaz. XIII-I pp. 64, 216]

The treaty that ended the sultanate here is the last witness. When Bahadur Shah gave Bassein to the Portuguese, one of its clauses provided that the 5,000 larins formerly paid from Bassein’s revenues to the mosques should continue. The sultan had endowed the mosques of his port; he did not, so far as any source shows, compel his Hindu subjects to fill them. [n37 §1; n36 §4; Danvers I p. 416]

Newcomers in the Indian Ocean

It is in Begada’s reign that the Portuguese first appear in the story of this coast, and they appear first not as conquerors but as the reason for an alliance of others.

The notes on which this book rests do not chart the first Portuguese voyages to India; they pick up the newcomers already established in the western Indian Ocean and already at war with its older powers. Between about 1505 and 1507, timber from Bassein was sent to the Red Sea, to Mecca and Egypt, to build the fleet of the Mamluk sultans that was meant to drive the Portuguese from the sea. [n03; TG-II p. 449] That single fact puts this harbour inside one of the great naval contests of its age: the forests behind Basai were felled to build ships for Egypt against Portugal.

The Portuguese answered in kind. In 1507, the Gazetteer says, they apparently stirred up the Hindu chiefs of the coast, and Begada had to “settle disturbances at Bassein”. [n03; TG-II p. 449] In 1509 Francisco de Almeida, in the account of the Portuguese historian Faria y Souza, captured a Gujarati ship in Bombay harbour. [n04; Military history of Bassein, Wikipedia] In 1510 a “Gujarat and Bassein minister” wrote to the Portuguese commander Albuquerque, which shows that by then Bassein had an official important enough to correspond with him. [n03; TG-II pp. 449–450 nn.]

Barbosa’s Baxay (1514)

The first description of the town by a European comes in 1514, from Duarte Barbosa. He called it “Baxay”: “a town of Moors and Gentiles, a good seaport belonging to the king of Gujarat.” Much merchandise was exchanged there, and there was “a great movement of shipping from all parts”, with many boats coming from Malabar laden with areca nuts, coconuts and spices. [n03; TG-XIV p. 28] [n15; Stanley’s Barbosa p. 68, via Thana Gaz. XIV p. 28]

“Moors and Gentiles” was the Portuguese shorthand for Muslims and Hindus. Barbosa’s sentence is the founding text of Vasai as a port. It shows a town, not a village; a seaport, not a creek landing; Gujarat’s, not anyone else’s; and already busy enough to draw the Malabar coasting trade. Eighty years after the Koprad stone and two hundred years after Abulfeda found nothing to say about Sopara, the centre of gravity of this coast had moved six miles south.

The Gazetteer’s history volume, citing Barbosa in another place, calls the Bassein of 1514 simply “a great place of trade”. What made it so? Barbosa’s own list is short, areca, coconuts and spices coming in from Malabar, but the shape of the trade can be filled in from what the Portuguese found when they took over twenty years later. Their first half-year’s revenue from the Bassein district, in 1535, came to 158,475 fedeas, raised from taxes and duties on coconut oil, opium, cotton, toddy, vegetables, fish, sugarcane and betel, and on the trades of butchers, dyers, Koli fishermen and shepherds. That is the Gujarat-era tax base, inherited whole. Agashi had a custom-house. Sopara, still a place worth guarding, had four wooden stockades. [n36 §4; da Cunha pp. 158–159; Gaz. XIII-II p. 465 n. 4]

Barbosa saw the other face of the coast too. At Thana, he complained, pirate “watch-boats” preyed on shipping, much as Marco Polo’s corsairs had two centuries earlier. And at Chaul, further south, he described how the great seasonal trade worked: a fair of four months, December to March, when “great caravans of oxen” came down from the Deccan and stopped a league from the city. The Gazetteer thought that Barbosa’s account of Chaul “is probably true of the trade of the Thana ports from the earliest times”. That is an extrapolation, but it gives a plausible picture of Bassein’s year: a closed sea in the rains, then, after the monsoon, the ships from Malabar and Gujarat, the caravans from inland, and a dry-season market at the water’s edge. [n36 §3; Gaz. XIII-II pp. 438 n. 2, 446]

Who were the traders? The Gazetteer’s general picture of the Thana ports before the Portuguese names “local Hindu, Musalman, and Parsi traders, and Hindus and Musalmans from Gujarat and from the Malabar coast”, with Persians, Arabs and Jews among the foreigners. It judged, from the tenth-century geographer al-Masudi’s remark that a language called Lariya was spoken on this coast, that “the trade tongue of the Thana ports was Gujarati, and the leading traders were probably Gujarat Vanis”. Some of those Vanis may already have been the Lad Vanis, named after Lat-desh, the old name of south Gujarat, whose families the Gazetteer would find at Sopara, Nala, Agashi and Bassein in 1882; but most of those, it said, came later, in the 1760s. Barbosa’s “Moors and Gentiles” were, in all likelihood, Konkani Muslim and Gujarati Hindu merchants, with Malabar boatmen at the quay. No individual trader of Gujarat-era Bassein is named in any source reached for this book. [n36 §3; Gaz. XIII-II pp. 431–432] [n35 §8; Gaz. XIII-I pp. 112–113]

Arnala’s island fort (1516)

At the mouth of the Vaitarna, the broadest inlet of the northern Konkan, lies a low island the Portuguese would call Ilha das Vacas, the Island of Cows. On it the Gujarat sultans built a fort.

The date usually given is 1516, and the builder named is Mahmud Begada. [n13; Wikipedia: Arnala fort] [n11; Wikipedia: Arnala Fort] The two cannot both be right, since Begada died in 1511; either the fort was begun under him and finished later, or the date or the attribution is wrong. [n10] What is not in doubt is what the Portuguese found there around 1530: “a strong fort in the hands of the Gujarat Musalmans with domes and Saracenic arches”. [n15; Thana Gaz. XIV pp. 10–11] They destroyed those domes and arches and handed the island to a Portuguese gentleman of Bassein; the fort there is described as about seven hundred feet square. The fort that stands on the island today is a later, Maratha rebuilding, and its story belongs to a later chapter. [n11; Gaz. 1882 pp. 10–11]

Inside the walls today is a tomb of two Muslim saints, Shahali and Hajiali, with a stone-lined eight-cornered pond and many wells. In 1882 the tomb had a yearly endowment of Rs 80. Its date and the saints’ origins are not recorded in any source reached for this book. [n10; Gazetteer 1882, Arnala Fort] [n25]

Raids and fire (1526 – 1531)

What followed Barbosa’s visit was a decade and a half of raids, in which the Portuguese tried to force Gujarat’s ports to submit and Gujarat tried to stop them.

A Portuguese factory, a trading post, was set up at Bassein in 1526 according to the Gazetteer; one source cited on Wikipedia says 1518. [n03; TG-II pp. 450–451] [n04; Military history of Bassein]

Then, in 1528 or 1529, came the first great blow. Heitor da Silveira destroyed the Gujarat fleet of Alishah near Bandra and Mahim, taking seventy-three prizes. Then he entered the Bassein river by night, a passage the Gazetteer calls difficult, stormed its cannon-defended works, defeated Alishah’s 3,500 men, and plundered and burnt Bassein. The “lord of Thana” made himself tributary to the Portuguese. [n03; TG-II pp. 450–451; TG-XIV p. 28 n. 7] Diogo do Couto gives the scene in detail. [n90 §2] Before the city, scouts found “a timber stockade of two faces, filled with earth, with three great bastions and fronts, in which there were sixty pieces of artillery”; Alishah had fled there after his fleet’s defeat and gathered “three thousand foot and five hundred horse”. At dawn two hundred Canarese landed first, under a captain of the sailors called Malu, to take the first sally, and Silveira followed with the banner of Christ and stormed the stockade, shouting “Portugal! Portugal!”. Alishah had set an ambush outside the city and charged the Portuguese who were inside, but Silveira formed a square of musketeers and “the horses, frightened by the noise, turned back, breaking their own foot”. The Portuguese “entered and sacked the city, robbing much goods, gold and silver, because it was rich and prosperous”, and set fire to it, “in which all burned”. “The neighbouring tanadars were so frightened that the one of Thana offered Heitor da Silveira four thousand pardaos of páreas every year, which he accepted.” A first Portuguese plan to buy food at Baçaim is older still: in 1508 Viceroy Dom Francisco de Almeida meant to put in there, “knowing it was a land well supplied”, but, with the coast in alarm, found the brick fort of Mahim empty, with only some rice hidden in pits. [n90 §1]

Agashi, at the northern end of the Bassein island, was in these years a place of real consequence. In about 1530 it “had a rich timber trade, and built ships as good as Portuguese ships, able to make the voyage to Europe”. (The Gazetteer’s printed text reads “1580” at this point, almost certainly a slip for 1530.) [n11; Gaz. 1882 pp. 1–2] [n15; Thana Gaz. XIV p. 1] Its shipwrights were exactly what the Portuguese could not afford to leave in Gujarat’s service. In 1530 and 1531 Daman and Agashi were destroyed, and three hundred ships were burnt at Agashi. Faria y Souza notes a second destruction of Agashi in March 1531. [n03; TG-II p. 451] [n11; Gaz. 1882 pp. 1–2] One reading of the Gazetteer places the burning of the town and its three hundred boats in 1536 instead; the notes keep both dates. [n15; Thana Gaz. XIV p. 1]

What did those three hundred boats look like? No hull survives, but a Portuguese chronicler of the period, Gaspar Correa, described the craft of the Konkan coast, and the Gazetteer preserved his description. Their planks were either sewn together with coir thread, in the keeled boats, or nailed, in the flat-bottomed ones. Pitched cloth and mats of cane kept the sea out. The cargo was stowed in chambers roofed with woven palm-leaf. Each had one large mast and a long-sprit lateen sail, and they were sailed close to the wind. That is the fleet that burned at Agashi: sewn and nailed boats of local teak and coconut fibre, built by the shipwrights whose skill the Portuguese would soon put to their own use. Within a generation, the Gazetteer says, “great ships built at Agashi and Bassein made many voyages to Europe”. [n36 §4; Gaz. XIII-II pp. 468–469]

The Bassein harbour itself was guarded in Gujarat’s last years by “works” with cannon that Silveira had to storm at night, and Agashi had its “great mosque”, which the Portuguese turned into their commandant’s house in 1554. A town with a great mosque, a timber trade, shipyards and three hundred boats was no fishing village. Agashi in 1530 was, by every sign the sources give, the most important shipbuilding harbour on the island. [n40 A2; Gaz. XIV pp. 1–2]

The raids blur into one another in the sources, and the various Silveiras are easily confused. Wikipedia’s summaries, flagged there as resting on unreliable sourcing, say that António da Silveira burnt Bassein in 1530 and António de Saldanha burnt it again in 1531, and that in 1533 Diogo da Silveira burnt the coast from Bandra to Surat and returned with “4000 slaves and spoils”. [n04; Vasai Fort, Wikipedia] [n25] The Gazetteer’s account of the 1531 campaign is firmer. That year the governor Nuno da Cunha mustered a great armada of four hundred sail in Bombay harbour to attack Diu, Gujarat’s chief island port, and failed. On the way back Saldanha burnt Bulsar, Tarapur, Kelve-Mahim and Agashi. [n03; TG-II p. 451] One later account says the burning of Baçaim in 1531 was meant to punish Bahadur Shah for refusing to cede Diu. [n18; Wikipedia: Fort Vasai]

For the families of Agashi and the Bassein creek, the years from 1529 to 1533 must have meant boats lost, houses burned and trade broken. The record gives no names of the people who suffered them.

What Correia saw: 1529, 1530 and the muster at Bombay (1531)

Gaspar Correia, who sailed in the fleet of 1531 “in a catur of my own”, adds three pieces to this stretch of the story. [n103] In February 1529 the Nizam Shah of Ahmadnagar sent word to Governor Lopo Vaz de Sampaio that, if he went to take Diu, he would give provisions and the crews’ pay, “provided that he gave him Baçaim when he took it, because it stood within his lands”. The Governor was angry and did not agree. [n103 §1] In 1530 the captain António da Silveira de Meneses burned the open city of Rander and the cotias loaded for Diu, routed a mass of Gujarat horse with arquebus volleys (“eleven horses fallen in the field” against three Portuguese dead), stormed the stone fortress of Damão “with four towers and a copper-plated door” and, on the way back to Chaul, “destroyed the island of Bombay and many villages along the sea, in such a way that all the places on the sea-fringe were depopulated, and inland ten leagues there was no people”. [n103 §2]

In January 1531 Nuno da Cunha arrived at Chaul and ordered the whole Armada to gather at “the island of Bombay”, where he waited eight days and held a muster: 3,560 men-at-arms, 1,450 Portuguese seamen, 2,000 Malabar and Canarese soldiers from Goa, 8,000 slaves who could fight, over 3,000 arquebusiers, 4,000 native rowers and more than 800 merchants of the junks, “with married and single women and people who went with their merchandise and provisions to sell, and small household, all counted… more than thirty thousand souls”. The Governor was so startled “for fear there would not be so much food” that he thought of sending the families back to Chaul, until his council assured him that each ship carried “food that each took for more than five months”. Over several days he inspected the ships, the galleons, the galleys and galliots, where, among the captains, Correia names Jorge Cabral and Luís Falcão; the fleet dressed in sail-devices and mottoes, “all shining with arms, a beautiful thing to see”. He left at the end of January, the big ships well out to sea and the fustas raiding the shore, “which was little, because all the sea-fringe was withdrawn inland more than ten leagues”, and on Sunday 13 February 1531 the vicar general said Mass beyond the river at Damão in a wooden house the Moors had left. [n103 §3] The fleet then went on to the failed attack on Diu. The strip of coast that was to become the Portuguese north, from Bombay to Damão, had already been emptied of its people by the raids.

Malik Tokan’s citadel (1532 – 1533)

The Sultan of Gujarat by now was Bahadur Shah, who ruled from 1526 to 1537. In 1532, or early 1533 according to Portuguese sources, he decided “to put a stop to these raids”. He ordered Malik Tokan, Governor of Diu and son of Malik Ayaz, to fortify Bassein. [n03; TG-XIV pp. 28–29; TG-II p. 451]

Malik Tokan built a citadel with ramparts and a ditch filled with salt water, and garrisoned it, by the Gazetteer’s second volume, with fifteen thousand men; the first volume says twelve thousand, and a Wikipedia summary fourteen thousand. [n03; TG-XIV pp. 28–29; TG-II p. 451] [n04; Vasai Fort, Wikipedia] It was the first real fortress at Vasai.

Nuno da Cunha came against it on 20 January 1533 with a hundred and fifty sail and about four thousand men; the Gazetteer’s other volume says three thousand Portuguese and two hundred Kanarese. Malik Tokan offered terms and was refused. Diogo da Silveira and Manuel de Macedo led the assault, scaled the walls, and the defenders fled. The Portuguese took four hundred guns, razed the citadel to the ground, and sailed away. [n03; TG-XIV pp. 28–29; TG-II p. 451; Wikipedia: Vasai]

Gaspar Correia, who lived in India and wrote his Lendas in the 1560s, gives the earliest and fullest account, and with it the date: 20 January 1533, St Sebastian’s day. [n102] His story begins two years earlier. In 1531 the captain-major Diogo da Silveira raided Thana, whose tanadar refused tribute because “Melique Tocão of Diu had sent a captain of his, with two thousand men of garrison, to be in Baçaim, where it was making itself very strong”. Silveira went up the creek at half tide with ten fustas, took the stockade with fire-lances, and loaded his boats with the white cloth that Thana made until the ebb left them stuck in the mud with archers on both banks. He then burnt a ship of rice for Diu, the thatched “granaries” at Bandra, and cotias carrying rice, oil and “timber that they carried to Diu from Baçaim”. [n102 §1] When he reported to the Governor, he said that merchants “of credit, who traded in the lands of Cambay” had told his spies that “in Baçaim there was a great escala of ships, which from there passed to Mecca loaded with coarse and fine timber, with which the galleys of the Rumes and the whole of the Strait were supplied”; that its timber-dealers “had put it under the mud, where it cured and was made much better and would last many years”; and that the Ottomans had in mind, “if they came to this coast of India, to put themselves and make themselves strong in this Baçaim, because they had better supply for their Armada here than in any other”. Malik Tokan had sent “a nephew of his called like him, with much people”, to build “a very strong fortress, with towers, turrets and stockades on land and palisades in the river, so that he was making it as strong as another Diu”. The council hesitated, since a fortress there would be so near Chaul that “if Baçaim were made, Chaul would be unmade”, and decided “that Baçaim only should be destroyed, and that the Governor should go in person”. [n102 §1]

The Governor left Goa at the end of December 1532 and gathered, with the ships of Manuel de Albuquerque and Diogo da Silveira, “more than a hundred sail, in which there would be about two thousand Portuguese, very bright people, and eight hundred Canarese archers and dart-men, men of Goa”. [n102 §2] Correia’s fortress was “of very thick dressed stone with wide walls”, square, with square corner towers, thirty heavy iron breech-loading guns, and a half-buried plastered magazine holding twenty pipes of powder, standing “at the bend that the river made”. A gunshot downstream stood a round stone casemate with six heavy guns at the water-line and a trebuchet between it and the fortress; stakes were set in the mud. Upstream lay a flooded field, and beyond it a long earth-and-timber rampart with ditches full of water and ox-carts carrying iron swivel guns that fired to the rear; the city itself was “inland almost half a league”. Malik Tokan had come from Diu with “twelve thousand men of garrison, among whom there was a great number of archers”. [n102 §3] He tried to detain the Governor by a message through “a Moorish merchant known at Hormuz”; Nuno da Cunha demanded that the fortress and the casemate be pulled down, hostages be given that no fortress be built there again, and a hundred thousand gold pardaos paid for the Armada’s cost; the Melique answered that “God did not consent to do harm to one who did none”. [n102 §3]

The assault came on the day itself. The night before, “each one settling his soul, making his testament, and mending his arms”, the whole force landed on the beach, “the place where now our fortress stands”. On the feast, the Governor had the vicar general and the Franciscan commissary lead the prayer of St Sebastian and a general confession, and advanced in three battalions: Silveira’s six hundred and three hundred Canarese in the van, Albuquerque’s eight hundred Portuguese and four hundred Canarese, and the Governor’s thousand Portuguese with as many Canarese. Silveira crossed the ditch at a shallow place and climbed the rampart on his men’s lances; “the first was a young man called António Ramos, who took the pennant on top”. The Gujarat horse and foot broke, Malik Tokan “stayed behind and returned to the river’s edge” and crossed it, and the Portuguese gathered at a mosque on a rise, with a well and a tank of dressed stone, where the Governor ordered fifty barrels of wine, biscuit, roast hens and the fat oxen they had taken, and “there was great abundance for everyone, with much pleasure”. Toward evening some men crept off to plunder; the Gujarat men by the river thought they were attacked and crossed; the garrison in the fortress fled “leaving the door open”; the Portuguese saw it, ran in “shouting and throwing down the banners”, and found the fortress taken “without deaths or blood of the Portuguese”. Three named men had died in the day. The Governor made knights, among them António de Lemos, Martim Afonso de Melo Jusarte and João Tição, and some mocked them as “cruzado knights”, a cruzado being what each gave the trumpeters. [n102 §4]

For the next twenty days the soldiers burnt the gardens (Malik Tokan’s, with tanks, fountains and Portuguese fruit trees, among them) and, by the Governor’s proclamation, pulled cured timber out of the mud on rafts of thirty or forty poles, “which in Goa were worth much money”. At Goa the King’s officers took the timber to pay for it, “which was worth more than ten thousand cruzados, which has never been paid to this day, which is in the era of 563 that I write this; but God will have it paid in the other world”. Then the Governor “had mines made under the fortress and the bastion and the gun-ports”, and when they were fired at dawn “it seemed that the world was sinking… not a stone of the foundations was left”, and Correia himself “saw a stone as big as a pipe, full of mud” that had fallen farther than a falcon’s shot. Old gunners told the Governor that the ground was weak: “if it had been hard the fire would all have pushed upward.” [n102 §5]

Fernão Lopes de Castanheda gives a third account, which agrees on the day. [n104] He has “the entry of the year 1533”, a fleet of “near eighty sail, with seven galleons”, two thousand Portuguese and two thousand Canarese archers, and Malik Tokan with “seventeen thousand fighting men, on foot and on horse, all chosen”. His fortress is an earth rampart “a fathom and a half in height and very wide, which began at the bulwark and extended half a league beyond the fortress toward the settlement”, with bastions in which “three hundred pieces of artillery” were mounted, and a ditch a fathom deep filled from the estuary, so that the rampart could be entered only at its end; at the mouth of the estuary stood a bulwark, and “a bombard-shot toward the bar a mosque on a mound of earth”. Malik Tokan tried to delay the Governor by messages through a Hormuz merchant, and by a meeting on a carpet on the grass with Martim Afonso de Melo, at which he offered to pull the fortress down if the Governor repaid his expenses and gave fifty of his horses. In the council “Diogo da Silveira and Manuel de Albuquerque swore very differently”, and when the fleet “murmured” at the delay the Governor ordered the landing for “the day of St Sebastian”. Silveira’s squadron attacked the end of the rampart, “the Moors recovered heart to defend themselves for a quarter of an hour, fighting very bravely, and then were routed”. “This victory was won in three hours,” Castanheda writes, “and was one of the principal that had been won in India to that point.” About five hundred Gujaratis died, including “an Abyssinian horseman, held as a brave knight, who killed João Jusarte Tição”, and up to six Portuguese. The garrison fled when it thought the door was being taken, and the Governor knighted several men at its gate. Afterwards “the land was cut and destroyed, so that the Portuguese begged one another to leave some trees for shade; and at the request of a Gujarati Gentile, an old man of honourable presence, the Governor ordered them to cut no more trees.” The fortress, the bulwark and the rampart were pulled down “to the foundations” over eight days. [n104 §1–§5] The three chronicles disagree on the numbers and on the shape of the fortress, and the book keeps their ranges: between ten and seventeen thousand defenders, and an earthwork and stone fort.

Diogo do Couto’s chronicle, written later, gives the same story in a different shape, and corrects one name. [n100] He puts the fleet at “more than a hundred and fifty sail”, with three thousand Portuguese and fifteen hundred lascarins in two junks, and says that at Chaul, “where Manuel de Macedo was Captain”, the Governor learnt that Malik Tokan (“Melique Tocão”) had been sent to Baçaim with ten or twelve thousand men, “very fortified and proud from the earlier success at Diu”. Nuno da Cunha chose Baçaim because it was “one of the principal cities of the Kingdom of Cambay, and from which it was supplied with provisions”. Diogo da Silveira surveyed the river under fire and reported what he saw: “over the channel of the bar, on the north side, a very large bastion with a very wide moat all round, which filled with sea water”; from it “a wall ran inland along the beach, which was the face of the city that looked to the sertão”, with “many towers and sentry-boxes, all garrisoned with much good artillery”; and, between the wall and the city, “a good fortress, on a large and beautiful rise, with its bastions”. He found a landing place further up the river, away from the beach forts. The Governor divided the men into three squadrons, under Silveira, Dom Fernando Deça and himself, and next day, on the rising tide, over a hundred and twenty oared vessels “finely dressed with flags, sounding many drums and fifes, trumpets and shawms” went up the river past the forts through “clouds of shot and smoke”, and Silveira landed more than fifteen hundred men. Malik Tokan was in the field with ten thousand. “The first arquebus volley brought down more than four hundred”; then “Melique Tocão, seeing himself beaten, did not wish to withdraw into the fortress but went past it into the sertão”, and the garrison “threw themselves out by the other side”. In Couto’s telling the fort was found empty and handed over to plunder. Manuel de Macedo, in Couto, is not at the assault but at Chaul, where he received the Governor; the Gazetteer’s “Manuel de Macedo” in the assault is not supported. [n100 §1–§3]

The Portuguese took “four hundred pieces” of artillery and plundered the fortress; then they “made some mines, which were filled with barrels of powder, and burst the whole fortress to its foundations”, doing the same to the beach forts after throwing “all the enemy dead (which were more than five hundred and fifty) into the moat”, so that “upon them fell the whole mass of the buildings”. The Governor cut the fields and palm groves and destroyed the settlements along the river on both banks, and the fleet attacked Thana, Karanja, Bombay and other places of the King of Cambay. [n100 §4] A few months later, with Manuel de Albuquerque cruising the coast from Baçaim to Tarapur, “the Tanadars of Thana, Bandra, Mahim and Bombay” and of Salsette offered tribute on condition that their villages not be burnt, and “promised 400 pardaos each year each of these Tanadars, and this year they paid at once, all in silver”. Couto had seen the entry in the accounts of the Armada’s Factor. [n100 §5]

So the “fort at Vasai before the Portuguese” was, as far as the evidence goes, the Gujarat stockades of 1529 together with Malik Tokan’s citadel of 1532 or 1533, which stood for a matter of months. [n03; Inferences] The walls that stand at Vasai today were begun by the Portuguese three years later. Two centuries on, in 1634, a Portuguese writer would describe a small round bastion near the pillory yard as having been the Moors’ former residence: a last trace, perhaps, of Gujarat’s Basai inside Portugal’s Baçaim. [n32; BG 39; DC 217]

The Portuguese epic poet Luís de Camões, in the tenth canto of The Lusiads, sang the victory. In the Gazetteer’s English: “Bassein, though so strong in verity, / Shall yield thy guns to him through smoke and flame.” [n15; Thana Gaz. XIV p. 29 n.]

A port given away (1534)

Bahadur Shah had lost his fort, but his real danger was elsewhere. He was fighting the Rajputs; he was fighting the Portuguese; and in Delhi the Mughal throne had passed in December 1530 from Babur to his son Humayun, who was now coming for Gujarat. [n04; Military history of Bassein] Worse, Nuno da Cunha was negotiating with Humayun. The Gazetteer puts the sultan’s choice plainly: “To win them from their alliance with Humayun, Bahadur made a treaty with the Portuguese, ceding Bassein and its dependencies.” [n03; TG-II p. 451]

The terms that the notes record are these. Bahadur gave up Bassein with its dependencies and revenues. Gujarat’s ships trading from Cambay to the Red Sea had to touch at Bassein, pay dues there, and carry a Portuguese pass. Gujarat was to build no warships, and to make no alliance with the Turks. [n03; TG-II p. 451] Other summaries add that captives were to be returned and the horse trade regulated. [n04; Military history of Bassein] The cession list, as later accounts give it, took in not only the harbour and its villages but Salsette, Bombay, Parel, Wadala, Sion, Worli, Mazagaon, Thana, Bandra and Mahim, and in some lists Uran. [n03; Wikipedia: Fort Vasai] [n04; Vasai Fort, Wikipedia]

The treaty in its own words

Later research for this book reached the treaty’s terms as F. C. Danvers set them out in The Portuguese in India, and they are fuller and more revealing than the summaries. Sultan Bahadur gave Bassein “with all its revenues” to Portugal, “provided that all the vessels from the kingdom of Guzerat, bound for the Straits, should first call at Bassein for passes, and on the return voyage call there again in order to pay the customary dues, under penalty and risk of seizure. No ships of war were to be built at any of the ports in the kingdom of Guzerat, but any number of trading ships might be constructed.” [n36 §4; Danvers I p. 416]

Then came the horses: “All horses from the Straits of Mecca, and from other parts of Arabia, during the first three years after the completion of the fortress of Bassein, were to proceed thither, in order that Sultan Bedur and his vassals might purchase them there on payment of the customary dues to the King of Portugal, and they were not to be taken to the Deccan, Canara, or Malabar.” [n36 §4; Danvers I p. 416]

And then the mosques: the 5,000 larins formerly paid from the revenues of Bassein to the mosques were to continue. [n37 §1; Danvers I p. 416]

Read slowly, the treaty explains why Bassein, of all the harbours of Gujarat, was the one the Portuguese wanted. The “Straits” are the Red Sea straits, as the treaty’s own phrase “the Straits of Mecca” shows. Every Gujarati ship bound for Mecca and Egypt would now have to stop at Bassein twice, once outward to buy a pass, the Portuguese cartaz, and once homeward to pay duty. Bassein became the toll-gate of Gujarat’s Red Sea trade, the chokepoint the Portuguese had been trying to create by burning harbours for five years. The horse clause did the same for the oldest trade of this coast: for three years every Arabian horse was to come to Bassein, to be bought there by Bahadur and his vassals under Portuguese duties, and kept out of the hands of the Deccan sultans and the south. A modern summary of the cartaz system adds that other voyages needed a pass but not the duties, and coastal trade needed no pass at all; that summary could not be checked against its sources. [n36 §4 and Inferences; GKToday; Gateway House]

Danvers also recorded what happened next. At the end of March 1536 Nuno da Cunha “went to Bassein”, “found all the materials ready”, and, “having fixed upon a site”, began the fortress, with Garcia de Sá as its captain. The walls that still stand at Vasai grew from that site. [n37 §6; Danvers I; Gaz. XIV pp. 28–29]

On the date, one more voice should be added to the three already given. José Gerson da Cunha, writing in 1876, gives the signing as 23 December 1533 and the taking of possession of the Bassein district as 23 December 1534, with the city itself founded in 1535. That may be the simplest way to reconcile the Gazetteer’s 1533 with the standard 1534: a treaty in one year, a handover a year later. It is offered here as a possibility, not a settled fact. [n40 §2; da Cunha pp. 137, 157]

When was it signed? Here the sources part company, and the reader should see all three dates.

  • The 1882 Gazetteer dates the treaty to 1533. [n03; TG-II p. 451] [n32; BG 28–29]
  • The modern standard date is 23 December 1534, when the treaty is said to have been signed at sea aboard the galleon São Mateus by Nuno da Cunha and Sultan Bahadur. Wikipedia’s infobox also lists an Ottoman envoy as mediator; that detail is unverified. [n03; Wikipedia: Treaty of Bassein (1534)] [n04]
  • Some accounts describe a separate treaty of peace and commerce of 25 October 1535 that formally surrendered Bassein and the seven islands; 1535 is also the year in which Bahadur, beaten by Humayun, allowed the Portuguese a fort at Diu. [n03; TG-II p. 451; Wikipedia: Mumbai under Islamic rule] [n04]

The Portuguese text of the treaty, which might settle the question, was not reached for this book. [n03; Gaps] This book follows the usual practice and calls it the Treaty of Bassein of December 1534, while keeping the Gazetteer’s 1533 and the 1535 instrument in view.

On the eve of the handover, the Portuguese found the land guarded by stockades and fortified posts. Land revenue was taken in kind, with cash cesses on coconut oil, opium, cotton, palm spirit, vegetables, fish, sugarcane and betel, and taxes on butchers, dyers, fishermen and shepherds. [n03; TG-II p. 451] That list is the best picture we have of what the people of the Bassein island did in 1534: they tapped palms, grew sugarcane and betel and vegetables, pressed coconut oil, fished, dyed cloth and kept flocks. Four years later, in 1538, the Portuguese viceroy João de Castro found Bassein “large, the resort of many people and nations”. [n03; TG-II p. 451]

The island in 1534

Before the Portuguese walls go up, it is worth standing on the island once more and taking stock of what the sultanate centuries had made of it, thread by thread.

Land and water. The island of Sopara was still an island, cut off from the mainland by the backwater the 1882 Gazetteer called the Manikpur, Sopara and Bolinj creeks. But the creek was silting, and the trade that once came to Sopara’s quays now went to Bassein’s open river mouth in the south and to Agashi’s bay in the north, where, except for one channel, a reef closed the mouth and only boats and small vessels could come in. Arnala’s island guarded the Vaitarna mouth; Tandulwadi watched it from the hill across the river. [n36 §6; Gaz. XIV pp. 1, 314–315] [n39 §3]

Sea and trade. The coast lived by coasting trade: areca, coconuts and spices from Malabar; timber from the forests behind Bassein, felled even for the Mamluk fleet in Egypt; ships built at Agashi; horses from Arabia passing through on their way to the armies of the Deccan and Gujarat; and a Red Sea traffic in Gujarati ships that the Portuguese were now about to tax at Bassein. The traders, so far as anyone can tell, were Gujarati Hindus and Konkani Muslims, with Malabar boatmen and the ever-present horse dealers of the Gulf. [n36 §3, §4; n03; TG-II p. 449]

Settlements. Bassein had become a town; Sopara was a lesser town, guarded by stockades; Agashi was a shipbuilding port; Koprad, Vatar, Nila and the garden hamlets had their names on stones. In Mahim to the north and Salsette to the south, the families of the Bimba tradition held lands and offices. The Vadvals of the coconut wadis were a separate subdivision by now, by their own account since the fourteenth century. [n40; n41 §4]

Peoples. Kolis on the creeks; Samvedi Brahmins in the gardens round Sopara, Koprad and Gass, already headmen in later record; Palshe priests; Pachkalshi, Chaukalshi and Vadval gardeners and carpenters; Bhandaris and Agris; Konkani Muslims and Naitas in the ports; a Jain Modha clan at Sopara dedicating images; Gujarati Vanis in the trade; Parsis in Thana, about to leave. A few Abyssinians appear in the record as commanders, like the Yaqut who led Gilani’s fleet in 1490. [n35; n41; n03; TG-II pp. 442–443]

Religion. There was no single faith. The old Hindu holy places, Nirmal, Tungar, Jivdhan, Sopara’s Chakreshvar lake, were still honoured, though no temple standing today can be dated to before 1534. The Jains of Sopara and Agashi kept their images. The Muslims of the ports had their mosques, endowed by the sultan’s revenue. The Nestorian church Jordanus found had vanished from the record. And none of the conversions that would transform the island had yet begun. What change there had been in the sultanate centuries came, as far as the sources show, through trade, marriage, soldiers hired from abroad and the preaching of saints, not through compulsion. [n03 §4 Gaps; n37 §1]

Languages. The people of the island spoke forms of Marathi; the 1838 writer would call the shared speech of the old coastal communities a “Colee bhasha”, and their later dialects, Vadval, Samvedi and Mangela, kept Gujarati words and Gujarat-border sounds that Grierson recorded in 1905. Gujarati was, the Gazetteer thought, the trade tongue of the ports. Stones were cut in Devanagari, with Shaka dates and now Hijri ones beside them. A revenue officer of Malad commissioned a chronicle in Marathi in about 1448, while the Sirdesai family of Malad kept a Persian paper of 1495, a sign that Persian served in the business of the sultans’ revenue. Portuguese, the language that would mark the Kuparis’ speech for centuries, had not yet been heard on the island except from the decks of raiding ships. [n41 §1, §8; n22 §1; n36 §3; n03]

Work, food and festivals. The tax list of 1534 is the best guide to daily work: palms tapped for toddy, coconuts pressed for oil, sugarcane and betel grown in the gardens, vegetables, fish caught by Kolis, cloth dyed, sheep and goats herded, cotton and opium traded. The Samvedi community’s modern tradition, that under Bimba they turned to sugarcane and nagvel, the betel vine, fits that list, though the tradition itself is late. Rice was grown and, in earlier centuries, salt was carried inland. Of festivals in this period the sources say almost nothing; the November fair at Nirmal and the coastal Coconut Day are first recorded much later. [n03; TG-II p. 451] [n41 §7; n08]

The Kuparis’ ancestors. One more thread runs through this chapter unnamed, and it is the one Part Seven of this book follows. The Kuparis, the Samvedi Christians of the northern garden villages, did not yet exist in 1534. But their ancestors were already on the island. When the Gazetteer came to describe Sopara in 1882, it wrote that “the husbandmen of Sopara are Christians, many of them converted Sámvedi Bráhmans”; at Agashi, Vatar and Koprad, the Christian plantain-driers were “originally Samvedis and Pachkalshis”. A sociologist’s account of the East Indian community places the first converts among Samvedi Brahmins settled between Agashi and Nalasopara, on the Vaitarna side of the island. So the gardeners of Gass, Koprad, Nirmal, Bolinj and Umrale in the sultanate centuries, Samvedi Brahmins and Pachkalshi families tending sugarcane, betel and coconut on the raised agar, under Gujarat’s distant crown, were the people from whom the Kuparis would come. Their names in this period are lost. The headman of Koprad who lived beside the stone of 1464 in 1882, Bhiku Patil, and the Samvedi Bab Naik of Gass, beside his inscribed basalt block, are their cousins four centuries on. [n40 §1, A12; Gaz. XIV pp. 314–315, 341–342; Gaz. XIII-I p. 395] [n35 §1; Sahapedia]

That was the island the treaty gave away.

What the treaty meant to the Hindu revenue families is caught, again, by the Bakhar. It remembers Bahadur, “Khan-saheb”, as the ruler who settled their disputes by genealogy, and it remembers what came after him as persecution. [n03; Wikipedia: Mahikavatichi Bakhar] For the Kolis and gardeners and toddy tappers of the creeks, the change of masters may at first have seemed like one more tribute-taker in a long line: Yadava viceroy, Delhi malik, Gujarat garrison, and now a Portuguese captain.

It would prove to be something else entirely. Within three years the Portuguese would lay the first stone of a walled city on the Bassein shore; within fifteen, Francis Xavier would walk its streets; within a lifetime, temples across the island would be pulled down and tens of thousands of its people baptised. The sultan had signed away a customs port. The friars who came after the captains had come for souls.
