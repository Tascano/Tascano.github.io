Part Six — The Republic

India is independent and the coast is a quiet place of gardens and salt pans, until 1988, when a green belt opens to the city. These three chapters follow the republic’s years from the quiet decades to the megacity’s edge, and end in November 2026.

Period covered: 1947 – 1989

In this chapter. The republic begins quietly: gardens, salt pans, a Socialist decade and a state reorganisation. This chapter follows the years from 1947 to 1989.

At four in the afternoon, for most of the twentieth century, a particular kind of passenger boarded the Bombay train at the stations of the Bassein coast. He wore half-pants. Across his shoulders lay a kawad, a pole with a loaded basket hanging from each end: bananas, betel leaf, the vegetables of the season. He rode south to Victoria Terminus and walked into the markets of the Fort and Dadar, and when he set his baskets down the call went up, baaju-baaju, Vasaiwale aale, “make way, make way, the Vasai people are here.” His customers called him Vasaiwale mama; the women who came with him were Vasaiwale maushi. Nobody wrote down his name. Nobody, it seems, thought he was history. [n17, n08; India Food Network 2015]

That figure, the farmer-vendor with his shoulder pole on the afternoon train, is the best image we have of Vasai in the first forty years of the Indian republic. It is a quiet image, and it belongs to a quiet period. For most of the years between 1947 and the late 1980s the record for this coast thins almost to nothing. The research behind this book found no Vasai-specific event for the partition months of 1947, nothing local for the long campaign that created the state of Maharashtra, no opening date for the national highway that now cuts the taluka in two, no founding years for the municipal councils of Vasai, Virar or Nallasopara, and no census figures for the individual towns before 1981. [n28; Chronicle 1947–1989, Gaps] What survives is a short list of election results, a handful of railway milestones, a few church dates, two archaeological finds and some family memories of farming that were written down only when the farming had nearly gone.

Later research lengthened the list, though not in the places one might expect. The government’s freedom-movement volumes, read page by page, still give nothing for 1947 or for Samyukta Maharashtra. The census of 1951 survives; those of 1961, 1971 and 1981 do not, in any form the research could reach. [n43; n21] What did come to light was the record the villages kept themselves: the parish histories of Gass, Bhuigaon, Gokhivare, Chulne, Mulgaon, Umrale, Nanbhat and Gomes Ali, with their petitions, foundation stones and first Masses; the heritage survey’s register of schools, libraries and markets; the births of bishops in villages that the Gazetteer of 1882 had described as plantain gardens. [n40; n42; n38] It is a history written in church dates and schoolrooms rather than in elections and riots, and for the Kupari and Vadval villages it is the fullest record of these decades that exists. The sea has its own few dates too: an ice factory in 1950–51, a change of nets in the mid-1960s, a record catch in 1979. [n36]

This chapter tells what that short list can tell, and says plainly where it cannot. It is the story of the last generation in which Vasai was what it had been, in one form or another, since the Portuguese: a belt of garden villages, salt pans and fishing hamlets feeding a great city to the south. It ends in 1988 and 1989, when two decisions in Bombay and one killing on a railway platform opened a very different age.

The 1940s: independence arrives on the railway line

India became independent on 15 August 1947. In Bassein taluka of Thana district, as far as the sources reachable for this book can show, the day left no specific trace. No Vasai-specific event of the partition period was found: no record of riots, of departures, or of arrivals. [n28; Chronicle 1947] The researchers flagged the gap honestly, and it should be kept honestly. Absence of evidence on the open web is not evidence that nothing happened. Partition-era Muslim migration, for example, is listed among the things nobody found, not among the things that did not occur. [n05; Gaps]

What we do know is the shape of the place in those years, and it comes largely from what happened just before and just after. Five years earlier, on 14 August 1942, a crowd of eight to ten thousand people from the villages of Palghar taluka had marched on the tehsil office at Palghar, thirty kilometres up the line. Police fired; five men died, among them Govind Ganesh Thakur, shot holding the tricolour. A memorial, Hutatma Chowk, was built there in 1951, and the town still stops at 12:39 every 14 August, the minute of the first shot. [n16; FPJ, Palghar martyrs] Further north, in Talasari and Dahanu, the Warli Adivasis had risen between 1945 and 1947 under Godavari and Shamrao Parulekar and the Kisan Sabha, declaring an end to debt slavery and suffering police firings at Talwada and Namivali. [n16; Parulekar, Revolt of the Warlis] Neither of these struggles is documented inside Bassein taluka itself. They are the neighbours’ stories. But they tell us what the district looked like on the eve of freedom: Congress nationalism along the railway towns, Adivasi agrarian revolt in the forested north and east, and Vasai in between, a Christian and Hindu farming coast whose own part has not yet been found in print. [n16; Inferences]

A later, deliberate search of the government’s own freedom-movement volumes, which had brought to light Gandhi’s visit to Bassein in 1921 and the death of the fifteen-year-old Bal Samant at Chowk Bazar in September 1942, ended with the same result for the day itself: on 15 August 1947 in Vasai, “no source found at all”, in Marathi or in English. [n43; §6] The last police reports of the Raj that mention Bassein are of Sane Guruji’s meetings in June 1945 and January 1946, when he spoke of the innocent killed in 1942 and urged the young to join the Rashtra Seva Dal, and of a New English School at Bassein whose students were told to unite under the national flag. [n43; SM-IV pp. 130–131, 143] Those students were the young men of 1947. What they did on the day, the record does not say.

The Church of the villages entered independence with a grievance of long standing. In the 1920s the East Indian Association had complained of “the neglect of education in the districts of Salsette and Bassein” and of “the paucity of vocations to the priesthood among the community”. [n34; Mendiratta 2017, p. 236] The parish schools of the 1930s, at Nandakhal from 1935 and Agashi’s English school of 1938, were the first answer; the high schools and colleges of the 1960s to 1980s would be the second. [n40] The figures that would show how far that answer went, literacy by community or by village, were never published for the Kuparis or the Vadvals; the census stopped counting castes after 1931. [n34; n41; Gaps]

The closest Vasai-specific thread of the freedom years runs through a Koli from Uttan. Augustine Koli, born in 1921, left a railway job in 1940 and worked underground in Dahisar, Borivali and on Panju island in Naigaon, in the mouth of Vasai creek. After independence he went home and served as sarpanch of Uttan from 1954 for seventeen years. He died in January 2022, aged 101. [n16, n26; FPJ, Augustine Koli] His life spans almost exactly the period of this chapter and the next, and it is a reminder that the people who lived through the quiet years were not quiet people. Their records are simply in places this book could not reach: the Thane District Gazetteer revised in 1982, the state’s freedom-fighter pension rolls, Marathi local histories and the back numbers of Christian periodicals such as Suvarta. [n16; Gaps]

The railway that had carried the nationalists and would carry the kawadis kept its old colonial name for four more years. On 5 November 1951 the Bombay, Baroda and Central India Railway, the BB&CI that had laid the line through Bassein in 1864, was renamed the Western Railway. [n28; Wikipedia: Western line] The suburban electric trains had run as far as Virar since 1936. [n28; Wikipedia: Western line] So at independence Virar was already the practical northern limit of Bombay commuting, and would remain so for more than sixty years. [n28; Inferences]

The new parishes of the 1940s

If the political record of the 1940s on this coast is thin, the parish record is not, and it tells of villages that were reorganising their own lives while the empire around them ended. The Catholics of Bassein had belonged since 1928, when the old “double jurisdiction” of Padroado and Propaganda ended in India, to the Archdiocese of Bombay, after four centuries under Goa and forty-two years under the Diocese of Daman. [n38; Archdiocese of Bombay; Diocese of Vasai snippet] Under Bombay, in the 1940s, the old mother parishes of the Portuguese began to divide.

Gass came first. The village petitioned for a parish of its own in 1941; it was formed in 1942, carved out of Holy Cross at Nirmal, with a Fr Thomas as its first parish priest, according to the community’s village pages. [n40; n42; EI Ghass] Another account credits the building of the 1942 church to Msgr Louis Caetan D’Souza. [n38; Indian Catholic Matters; Catholics India] The two are not necessarily in conflict, a monsignor building and a younger priest serving, but no source joins them. The church took the name of St Gonsalo Garcia, the son of Bassein martyred at Nagasaki in 1597 and canonised in 1862. [n38] Why the village chose him, and why it wanted its own church in that year of all years, no source says. [n42; Gaps]

The next year it was the turn of the Vadval south. At Gokhivare, east of the railway, the villagers had made a vow to St Roque when cholera struck them in 1914; two hundred of them had built a chapel forty feet by eighteen, to which Archbishop Lima added a sanctuary, and it had been blessed on 30 June 1930 by the Dean of South Bassein. In 1943 it became an independent parish, with a Jesuit, Fr Nubiola, as its first priest. [n40; EI Gokhivare] On 5 January 1943, at Nandakhal, Rev. Frank Lobo was ordained, the parish’s first priest of its own blood, “after 370 years”, as the parish history puts it. [n40; Wikipedia: Holy Spirit Church Nandakhal] In 1945 Mulgaon was carved from Pali, with Fr Ayres Fernandes as its first parish priest. [n40; EI Mulgaon]

Bhuigaon’s story is the best told of all. The village lay two kilometres south-west of Nirmal, and “out of the thousand Catholics of the village only a handful attended services at Nirmal”. A priest, Fr (later Mgr) Raymond Mendez, began catechism classes there; a simple church was built in 1946 on land donated by the poor villagers themselves, with money from the Archbishop of Bombay and from the parishes of Bandra; and on 2 June 1946 Bhuigaon became an independent parish, the second after Gass to leave the mother church, with Fr Acuico Dourado as its first priest. A plain single-storeyed building, begun in 1948, took seven years to finish. [n40; EI Bhuigaon] The money from Bandra is a small, telling detail: the old Salsette parishes of the city were helping the farming villages of the north.

In March 1949 the Jesuits came back to the parish of St Michael at Manickpur, which they had founded in 1606 and lost in 1739, after two centuries in the hands of the secular clergy. [n40; EI Manikpur] And in 1949, under Mgr Mendes, Mass began to be said at Umrale and Nanbhat, two hamlet-groups of the Nandakhal parish that would become parishes themselves in the 1970s. [n40; Wikipedia: Holy Spirit Church Nandakhal] A Kupari born at Gass or Bhuigaon at independence would grow up in a parish that was younger than his parents.

The 1950s: the Socialist decade, and a stone from Bhuigaon

The count of 1951

The decade began with the first census of the republic, and for Bassein it is the last census whose taluka figures survive in the notes until the town counts of 1981. Bassein taluka held 133,523 people, 69,259 men and 64,264 women, on 203 square miles. Of them 92,393 lived on 189 rural square miles and 41,130 on fourteen urban ones. [n21; Census 1951 Vol IV Part II-A] The whole Thana district held 1,518,050, a little over a third of them urban. [n21; Census 1951] Set beside the 79,978 of 1911, the taluka had grown by about two-thirds in forty years, though the boundaries had changed, from about 225 square miles in 1911 to 203 in 1951, and the two counts are not strictly comparable. [n21; Inferences] The urban figure is the one that matters for what came later. One person in three on the island already lived in a town, mostly in Bassein itself, a market town that had held about ten thousand people in 1881 and 12,689 in 1931. [n21; Gaz XIV p. 28; Census 1931]

The censuses of 1961, 1971 and 1981 were taken too, but their district handbooks for Thana could not be found for this book, through any search of the archives; nor could the Maharashtra State Gazetteer for Thane district of 1982, which would have drawn on them. [n21; Gaps] So for the thirty years between 1951 and 1981 the population of Bassein taluka is unknown here, and every statement about its growth or decline in those decades rests on later regional figures that may use other boundaries.

The first general elections of the republic, in 1952, returned a member for the Bassein constituency to the Bombay legislature. The constituency tables used for this book, which had to be partly reconstructed from Wikipedia’s election-results sections, give the winner as Sadanand G. Warty of the Socialist Party; in 1957 the same man won again, now under the Praja Socialist Party label. [n26; Wikipedia: Bassein Assembly constituency] Nothing else about Warty, not his village, his profession or his margins, was found. The runners-up and the vote counts for 1952 and 1957 were not read. [n26; Gaps] We know only that the first two elected voices of Bassein in independent India were socialists, and that this would not last.

The same decade produced one of the most remarkable finds in the long history of the coast, and it came from a village field rather than an excavation. On 1 January 1956, at Bhuigaon, near old Sopara, a broken basalt slab was found carrying a little over half of one of the Emperor Ashoka’s Major Rock Edicts. The find was reported by N. A. Gore, Librarian of the Bombay Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society; one later account says it was found by a little boy from Bhuigaon. The stone went to the Prince of Wales Museum in Bombay, where the epigraphist D. C. Sircar copied it in January 1957. [n02; Sircar, Epigraphia Indica 32; Shah 2020] Sources disagree over which edict it is. Sircar’s primary publication reads it as Rock Edict IX; Wikipedia, citing the Archaeological Survey’s Indian Archaeology 1955–56, calls it a fragment of Edict XI. [n02, n15, n18] The primary publication is the safer guide, but the disagreement belongs on the record. Either way, a Bhuigaon child in the first decade of the republic had turned up words that an emperor had ordered cut into stone at Shurparaka some twenty-two centuries earlier.

The churches of the green belt kept growing in these years, and their dates are among the few firm markers we have. The church of St Gonsalo Garcia at Gass, carved out of the old Nirmal parish and built in 1942, was renovated or expanded in 1957. [n07, n09; CatholicsIndia; Prayers & Petitions] It was dedicated to the Franciscan martyr of Japan whom Vasai claims as its own son, the first Indian-born saint, and it would become the region’s great shrine to him. [n07, n25; Wikipedia: Gonsalo Garcia] Across the water, at Erangal on the Madh shore, the church of St Bonaventure, built by the Portuguese in 1575 and destroyed in 1739, was repaired in the 1950s; families from Vasai still came to its January feast by cart and by boat. [n09, n07; FPJ, 11 Jan 2026] On Jivdhan hill above Virar, one Marathi press account puts the installation (prana-pratishtha) of the goddess Jivdani’s image by devotees in 1956, though other accounts give the temple a seventeenth-century or a roughly 150-year age; the dates conflict and cannot be reconciled from these sources. [n20; Pudhari, Lokmat, eSakal snippets]

Of the great political argument of the decade, there is nothing local at all. Between 1956 and 1960 Marathi-speaking India campaigned, often violently, for a state of its own, the movement called Samyukta Maharashtra, and for Bombay to be its capital. Three separate research passes, in English and Marathi, looked for Vasai’s part in it and found none. [n16, n20, n28; Gaps] A fourth pass went to the government’s own source volumes, the series that had turned up Gandhi’s visit to Bassein in 1921 and the Chowk Bazar firing of 1942, and read the two parts of its volume on the Goa freedom struggle “vis-à-vis Maharashtra”, 1946 to 1961, from end to end. Bassein, Vasai and Virar do not appear in them. [n43; SM-VIII Parts I and II] An “Agashi” does: a dispatch from Belgaum reports the arrest of Goan satyagrahis “at Kete, Panjim, Agashi, Bicholim, Sawardam and Durbhat”. That Agashi is Agassaim in Goa, not the Agashi of the plantain gardens, and the coincidence of names is the closest the coast comes to the Goa satyagraha of 1955 in the record. [n43; SM-VIII Part I] Web searches for Vasai volunteers in the 1955 march to Goa, or for Samyukta Maharashtra martyrs or agitations in the taluka, returned nothing. [n43; Gaps] The researchers add, without a source, that Bassein went to Maharashtra in 1960 with no boundary dispute they could find. [n43; note] It would be easy to supply the missing scene from general knowledge of the movement in Bombay. This book will not. What can be said is that Vasai sat on the railway line between the city where that argument was loudest and the Gujarati-speaking north, in a taluka whose own speech was a mix of Marathi, Konkani, Gujarati and Portuguese, and that its people would wake up in 1960 inside the state the movement won. [n13, n22; Wikipedia: Kadodi language; Grierson 1905]

Nets, ice and the boats of the 1950s

The sea gave the coast the one modern institution of the decade whose date survives. Thirty kilometres up the line, at Satpati in Palghar taluka, the fishermen’s co-operative society built the first co-operative ice factory, of seven tons, in 1950–51. [n36; ICSF via search summary] Ice changed what a fishing village could do with its catch: it could hold fish for the train to the city instead of drying it on the beach. In Vasai taluka itself, eleven primary fishermen’s co-operatives would eventually be counted, though the source gives no founding dates. [n36; ICSF] The method of fishing was still the old one. Arnala was, and remained, one of the major landing centres for the dol, the bag net staked in the tidal stream, in which Bombay duck, bombil, was the main catch; pomfret too was caught in dol nets until the mid-1960s, when nylon gill nets, first used at Satpati, took over that fishery. [n36; ICSF; PARI, via search summaries] These come from search summaries of modern reports, not from pages read, and they describe the Satpati–Dahanu coast as much as Vasai’s; but they are the only account of the fishing of these decades the notes contain. [n36; Gaps]

The fishing peoples themselves were the Kolis, Christian and Hindu, of Arnala, Pachubandar, Naigaon and the Vasai Koliwada, and the Mangelas and Vaitis of the 1882 Gazetteer. No boat count, no catch figure and no fishermen’s census for Vasai before the 1990s has been found. [n17; n36; Gaps] A modern study cited for 1997 counted 5,007 active fishermen in Vasai taluka, which gives at least the order of magnitude at the end of this chapter’s span. [n36; search summary]

The parishes and the schools in the 1950s

The parish record goes on. At Chulne, one of the five Vadval carting villages of the Gazetteer, whose people were “practically all Catholics”, Sunday Mass began to be said on 9 September 1951 by Fr Philip Tavares; the village had until then belonged to the old parish of St Michael. [n40; EI Chulne] At Nandakhal the sisters of the Queen of the Apostles arrived in 1952. [n40; Wikipedia: Holy Spirit Church Nandakhal] The parish had opened Marathi schools from 1935 and St Joseph’s School in 1939, after an English school founded in 1923 had closed for lack of pupils: “we get bread and butter from farming”, the parish history quotes the villagers, “and not by studying”. [n40; Wikipedia: Holy Spirit Church Nandakhal] That sentence, recorded of the 1920s, is the attitude the next forty years would reverse. At Agashi, the Holy Martyrs English primary school had opened in 1938. [n40; EI Agashi]

Markets moved too. Nirmal Bazaar, on the Nirmal–Kalamb road in Vagholi gram panchayat, dates from 1957 by the heritage survey’s register. [n40; CRIT Vol I item 69] And in the field of writing, the old administrative script of Marathi, Modi, in which the Peshwas’ records of Bassein had been kept, passed out of official use about 1952 by one account, giving way finally to Devanagari. [n22; Wikipedia: Modi script] The documents of Maratha Vasai became, in that decade, a script few could read.

The 1960s: Maharashtra, Bhausaheb Vartak, and the first road bridge

On 1 May 1960 the bilingual Bombay State was split in two. Maharashtra was formed with twenty-six districts; Thana, with Bassein taluka inside it, was one of them. The boundary was not drawn without cost to the old district: forty-seven villages and three towns of Umbargaon taluka, at Thana’s northern end, went to the Surat district of the new state of Gujarat. [n28; Wikipedia: Thane district] Bassein lay well south of that line and stayed in Maharashtra.

Two years later the taluka elected the man who would dominate its politics for a decade. Hari Govindrao Vartak, known to everyone as Bhausaheb, won Bassein for the Congress in 1962, and the constituency tables give him the seat again in 1967 and 1972. [n26; Wikipedia: Bassein Assembly constituency] His birth date is given as 9 February 1914 in one source and 14 February 1914 in another; he died on 7 October 1998. [n26, n20; Wikipedia: Hari Govindrao Vartak] He served as a cabinet minister under Chief Minister Vasantrao Naik, holding public health and the portfolio of salt lands and fisheries, and later revenue; he received the Padma Shri in 1991. [n26, n16; Wikipedia: Hari Govindrao Vartak] It is fitting that a man from a coast of salt pans and fishing boats should have held the salt-land and fisheries brief. He appears in the Congress party’s 1963 Year of Freedom, which suggests a record in the freedom movement, but no details were found, and the Marathi newspaper pieces that might supply them could not be read. Until they are, he should not be called a freedom fighter in so many words. [n16, n20; Wikipedia; Gaps]

The research for this book went back to the government’s freedom-movement volumes with his name in mind: the police abstracts of 1930, the Congress digests of 1942 to 1946, the ICHR martyrs’ dictionary. None mentions him. [n43; Gaps] The name index of the volume covering April to September 1930 includes a “Vartak, Govind Dharmaji”, but whether he was a Vasai Vartak, or any relation, nobody has checked. [n43; SM-XI index]

His community is a question too. Wikipedia’s article on the Pachkalshis lists “Hari Govindrao (Bhausaheb) Vartak, Padma Shri recipient from Vasai” among the community’s notable people, and Vartak is one of the three Pachkalshi surnames the Gazetteer of 1882 names, beside Raut and Chodhri. [n41; Wikipedia: Pachkalshi; Gaz XIII-I p. 130] But his own biography does not state his community, and the Vartak surname is shared with a Chitpavan Brahman clan from Kelshi, so the surname alone proves nothing. [n41; Inferences] If the list is right, the man who dominated Bassein’s politics in the 1960s came from the Hindu branch of the same Vadval-Pachkalshi stock whose Christian branch drove the timber carts of Chulne and Sandor in 1882. This book records the claim and leaves it unconfirmed.

The constituency results for these elections give only the winners. The runners-up and the margins for 1952 to 1972 were not read, and no source says which Lok Sabha seats covered Vasai before the Palghar seat was created in 2009; the researchers name the old Thane and Dahanu seats as the likely ones, without confirmation. [n26; Gaps]

Bhausaheb Vartak’s name survives most visibly in the institutions linked to his family: Vartak College in Vasai and the Vidyavardhini education society. [n16; Wikipedia: Hitendra Thakur] The college matters to this story out of all proportion to its size, because two of the most famous young men of the next generation would pass through it. Govinda, the film star, took his commerce degree there. [n27; Wikipedia: Govinda] So did a boy from Virar born in October 1961, Hitendra Thakur. [n26; Wikipedia: Hitendra Thakur] The Vartaks built the ladder; others would climb it.

The 1960s left a few more marks. In 1967 the scholar Elsie Wilhelmina Baptista published The East Indians: Catholic Community of Bombay, Salsette and Bassein, still the standard academic account of the Christian community to which most Vasai Catholics belong. [n28, n07; Catholic Connect] The book is cited for the five-group division of the community still in use: the Kulbis, the Samvedi Christians “commonly called Kuparis”, the Koli Christians, the Vadvals and the Salsette Christians. [n34; Catholic Connect] That phrase deserves a pause. A search of every primary text available for this book, from the Gazetteer of 1882 through Enthoven, Grierson and the censuses of 1901, 1911 and 1931, found the word “Kupari” nowhere; the earliest uses the researchers could read were a community blog of 2007 and Wikipedia. [n34; Headline finding] If Baptista used it in 1967, as the secondary citation says, her book may be the first time the name of the Samvedi Christians of north Vasai was set in print. The book itself is not online and could not be checked, so that remains a possibility, not a finding. [n34; Gaps]

The decade’s church dates fill in the map of the Kupari and Vadval villages. On 8 March 1962 Cardinal Gracias, the Archbishop of Bombay, blessed the church of St Gonsalo Garcia at Gass, twenty years after the parish was formed and five after the building was renovated. [n40; n42; EI Ghass] At Chulne the foundation stone of a church was laid on 22 April 1961; the parish of Our Lady of Fatima was canonically established on 1 June 1963; and the church was blessed on 17 May 1964. [n40; EI Chulne] At Mulgaon, Fr Clement de Sa died on 13 November 1963 while giving communion, and the church was consecrated in 1966. [n40; EI Mulgaon] At Gokhivare the Jesuits handed the parish to others from 1968 to 1971, then took it back. [n40; EI Gokhivare] And at Agashi, the St James Marathi School opened in 1968. [n40; EI Agashi]

One small detail from Gass belongs here. On 4 November 1960 a boy named Simon Almeida was born in the village. He went to the parish’s St Gonsalo Garcia Primary School in the 1960s and then to Holy Cross High School at Nirmal, the mother parish’s school; in 1978 he entered the Prakash Bhavan minor seminary at Pune; he would be ordained priest in 1990 and, in November 2025, appointed Bishop of Poona. [n42; Wikipedia: Simon Almeida; Diocese of Poona] His schooling is the clearest record we have of how a Gass child of these decades was educated: in the village’s own parish school, then at Nirmal, then away. At Giriz the church of St Francis Xavier celebrated its golden jubilee in 1968 with a new porch and the Marshal Pereira Hall; at Nandakhal, at about the same time, a statue of Christ the King went up beside the lake. [n09; Wikipedia: St Francis Xavier’s Church, Giriz; Holy Spirit Church Nandakhal]

And in 1968, at the taluka’s southern gate, the first road bridge was built across Vasai creek on the Mumbai–Ahmedabad highway. [n12; Mumbai Live; Swarajya; FPJ] Until then the creek had been crossed by the railway bridges of the BB&CI, and before them by boat. A weak, single source dates an earlier road crossing from Ghodbunder to Naigaon to 1928, but it conflicts with the better reports and should not be relied on. [n28, n12; InfraLens] The 1968 bridge is the one that matters. It tied the green belt to the city by road for the first time in a way that trucks could use, and it would carry the traffic of the boom that came twenty years later until, worn out, it had to be supplemented in 2023. When the national highway itself was built through the taluka, no source found by this book says. [n28; Gaps]

Under these few dated facts lay the slow life of the gardens. A community history of the Kupari, the Samvedi-speaking Christians of north Vasai, says that until the late 1960s and early 1970s the community depended mainly on farming. [n07, n11; Rajan D’Mello blog] That is the hinge of the decade in human terms: the last years in which a Kupari son could expect to follow his father into the wadi.

The languages of the coast in the 1960s

The village speech of the island had been described once, in 1905, and not again by any linguist in the period of this chapter. Grierson had found three small “broken dialects” in Thana, Vadval, Phudgi and Samvedi, which “still have preserved many of the characteristic features of Gujarati Bhili”: the Samvedis’ “mechanical mixture of Konkan Marathi and Gujarati”, the Vadvals’ habit of turning ch into s or h and s into h, so that sona, gold, became hona, and Marathi chand, the moon, became sand. [n41; n22; Grierson 1905, pp. 144–147] The census of 1931 had still listed “Vadval” as a name of a Marathi dialect. [n41; Census 1931 Vol VIII Pt I] Through the decades of this chapter those dialects went on being spoken in the villages, by Hindus and Christians alike, while the schools, the Church’s Marathi publications and, from 1960, the new state taught standard Marathi above them. [n22; Inferences] The Christian villages had their own printed tradition in the language. The Kristapurana of the Jesuit Thomas Stephens, the Marathi life of Christ of 1616, had been printed in Devanagari in the East Indian dialect at Vasai in 1922 and reprinted in 1927, and it was still read. [n38; Wikipedia: Krista Purana; Indian Christian Writings blog] What happened to the dialects between 1931 and the community dictionaries and machine-translation projects of the 2020s is not recorded in any source found.

The Vadval and Kupari villages at work

The village economy of the 1960s is known only from later memories, and they agree. The Kupari community histories say that farming dominated until the late 1960s and early 1970s, and that families then moved into engineering, medicine, banking and later IT, and abroad, to the Gulf, Europe, the United States and Australia. [n34; Vasai Kupari blog 2007; Post in Mumbai 2015] Those blogs are uncited and given to boasts; one claims “100% literacy”. [n34] They do not count anything. But the Gazetteer had recorded the beginning of the same movement in 1882, when “a considerable number who know English” among the Salsette Christians were clerks in Bombay and “the morning trains from Andheri and Bandra are crowded”. [n34; Gaz XIII-I pp. 205–206] By the 1960s the morning trains from Bassein Road and Virar carried the same kind of passenger, alongside the kawadis with their baskets.

Among the Vadvals, the community’s own accounts and the modern encyclopaedias give an occupational shift of the same kind: from wadis, coconut groves and carpentry to building contracting, architecture and engineering in the city. [n41; Wikipedia: Pachkalshi] Christian Vadval women, a sociological summary says, still wore red saris and their men red caps, and a village council fined those who shirked their duties at deaths and marriages. [n41; Sahapedia, relaying Rodricks] The summary is undated and secondary; it describes a custom of the recent past rather than a dated scene. It was in these villages, Sandor, Chulne, Manickpur, Gokhivare, that the new parishes of the 1940s to 1960s grew, and Sandor, the mother church of the south, was by the community’s own description “predominantly Vadval”. [n40; Wikipedia: Sandor]

The 1970s: Tarabai Vartak, and a coast that stood still

The 1970s began with a woman’s first. In 1972 Tarabai Vartak became president of the Thane Zilla Parishad, the first woman to head a Zilla Parishad anywhere in India. [n26, n16; Wikipedia: Tarabai Vartak] Born at Bordi on the Dahanu coast in 1926, she had married Narsinh Vartak in 1944 and came into the same family as Bhausaheb. [n26; Wikipedia] Her birth date is given as 19 August 1926, and she was the daughter of Atmarampant Save; Save and Vartak are both surnames that the community lists name among the Somvanshi Kshatriya families of the north coast, though her own biography does not state her community, and this book does not assert it. [n41; Wikipedia: Tarabai Vartak; Wikipedia: Pachkalshi] She would later serve as Minister of State for Social Welfare (from 1980) and found schools and colleges across Vasai and Virar, and her role among the founders of the Vidyavardhini institutions is one of the few things the sources say about the building of local education in these decades. [n16, n27; Wikipedia: Tarabai Vartak] No freedom-struggle role is recorded for her. Her monument is the classrooms.

Other classrooms were opening. St Augustine’s High School in Vasai was started by the Christian Brothers in 1970 or 1972; the sources disagree. [n28; Wikipedia: St Augustine’s High School (Vasai)] In 1970 the new parish of Umrale was carved out of Nandakhal, and on 1 June 1975 Nanbhat followed. [n09; Wikipedia: Holy Spirit Church Nandakhal] At Nandakhal itself the old church was opened up in 1973 when its dividing wall was removed; at Giriz a 1977 extension and parochial house were built with German aid. [n09; Wikipedia] These are small items, but they show a Christian countryside that was still growing in numbers and confidence even as its farming economy began to fray.

The Vidyavardhini education society, whose colleges carry the Vartak name, is dated by its own college site to 1970. [n41; avcjr.ac.in] The decade’s parish register fills in around it. Umrale’s new parish took St Joseph as its patron; Nanbhat’s, independent on 1 June 1975 “due to the efforts of Monsignor Philip Tavares”, took St Paul. [n40; Wikipedia: Holy Spirit Church Nandakhal; Bharatpedia “Kupari”] Both had been Mass centres of Nandakhal since 1949, and both were Kupari hamlet-groups of the old Sopara agar; Umrale was one of the nine plantain-drying villages of 1882. [n40; Gaz XIII-I p. 292] At Sandor a conference of the St Vincent de Paul Society was formed in 1971. [n40; Wikipedia: Sandor] At Umela, a Somvanshi and Agri village of the south, a chapel of the Immaculate Conception was built in 1974 under St Michael’s, Manickpur. [n40; EI Manikpur] Remedy’s church was extended in 1974–75, Agashi’s St James in 1975, and Agashi opened St James High School in 1977. [n40; CRIT Vol II asset 22; CRIT Vol III asset 44; EI Agashi] On 31 October 1971 Thomas Dabre, a Bhuigaon boy born on 23 October 1945, was ordained a priest. [n40; GCatholic; Catholic-Hierarchy] He would become the first Bishop of Vasai.

At the end of the decade the Church reached into the last unparished corner of the old Nirmal territory. On 2 September 1979 Fr Edwin Colaco, then vicar of Nirmal and later Bishop of Amravati, said the first Mass for the cluster of five villages of Chowkala, Gomes Ali, Vanda, Rambai and Darseng, in the primary local board school at Chowkala. [n40; EI Nirmal] It would take another twenty-five years for them to become a parish. A Mass in a village schoolroom, in a Kupari hamlet named after a Portuguese surname, is as good an image as any of how the Church of these villages grew in the republic’s quiet decades: from below, by distance and by numbers, one schoolroom and one chapel at a time.

The old villages in the republic

The heritage survey commissioned by the metropolitan authority in 2008 walked every village of the Vasai–Virar sub-region and listed its temples, ponds, schools and houses with their dates. Read for the years of this chapter, it gives a picture of village institutions that were already old when the republic began and that carried on through it. [n40; CRIT 2008]

At Agashi the Sarvajanik Vachnalaya, the public library, founded in 1914 by Seth Shah Prabhudas Bhagvandas, had moved into its present building in 1939; the Zilla Parishad school dated from 1886; Phadkewada, the house of the Maratha Subhedar’s family, still stood on the Chalpeth road among the Maratha-era houses, and would be lost to high-rise building only in the 2000s. [n40; CRIT Vol I items 43–46; Heritage Chronicles 2019] The Bhavani Shankar temple’s fair continued at the tank. [n40; CRIT Vol III] Agashi was the one village in the whole region with more than ten thousand people in 1981. [n17; TIFR]

At Nirmal the Shankaracharya’s samadhi and its seven companion temples on the wooded hill kept their November jatra, the fair that in 1882 had drawn six or seven thousand pilgrims of every faith for a week of trading in brass pots and dried plantains. [n40; Gaz XIV p. 292; n20] Below the hill, Holy Cross kept its September feast. [n38; UCA News directory] The two religious centres of the belt sat, as they had since 1750, within sight of each other across the Vimal and Malai ponds. [n40; CRIT Vol I items 82b–c]

At Arnala, the fishing village at the Vaitarna mouth, the Kolis had a church of their own only since 1931: Fr Ismail Da Costa, an assistant at Agashi, had built a hut chapel in 1919 and a tin shed dedicated to St Peter in 1921 “with the Kolis’ help”, and Archbishop Joaquim Lima had blessed the church on 27 December 1931, with Rs 3,000 contributed by a Hindu community association. “Kolis consider Fr Da Costa as the true Apostle of Arnala.” [n40; EI Arnala] The village’s salt-makers and fishermen had been at the centre of the salt satyagraha of 1930 by the secondary account of the district gazetteer, and a Gandhi Smarak stands there. [n40; CRIT Vol I p. 9 and item 52; n43] Christian Kolis of Bassein, Agashi and the Salsette shore were counted at about nine thousand at the end of this chapter’s span. [n07]

At Remedy, the Matkari Wada of 1868 was the home of the advocate Raja Ram Keshab Matkari, who lived from 1920 to 1963, one of the few named professional men of the village in these decades; the Patil Wada of the 1850s nearby is the survey’s type-house of the landowning farming families. [n40; CRIT Vol I items 21–25] At Papdi the Ram Mandir, rebuilt early in the twentieth century on the site of the Maratha temple, stood beside the Talav, the Govardhan Vidyalaya and the Thomas Baptista School, and the Shah Wada of the 1850s recalled the Gujarati traders of the betel-nut market. [n40; CRIT Vol II assets 36–42] At Rangaon a Zilla Parishad school had opened in 1917, and at Juchandra in 1872. [n40; CRIT items 93–96, 111–114]

At Sopara the old town had its Burhanpur Chowk market, named after a nineteenth-century Mr Burhanpur Harris, its Chakreshwar Talav, its dargahs, and the stupa mound in the fields of Mardes, where local Catholics gathered round the cross at the Madres Talav for prayers and processions at Christmas. [n40; CRIT Vol I items 8–11, 70–71] The Ashokan stone of 1956 from Bhuigaon and the Satavahana coins of 1972 came out of exactly this landscape.

None of these entries records a dramatic event between 1947 and 1989. That is their point. In the villages of the green belt the institutions of the nineteenth century, the library, the board school, the parish, the temple fair, the weekly market, went on into the republic almost unchanged, until the decision of 1988 and the building that followed it began to remove them.

Gass, 1942–1989

Of Gass in these decades the record is a handful of dates, and it is best to set them down plainly. The parish was formed in 1942 out of Nirmal, after a petition of 1941. [n42; EI Ghass] The church was renovated or expanded in 1957, “to maintain its stability and serve the growing parish better” in one directory’s words, and blessed by Cardinal Gracias on 8 March 1962. [n42; Prayers & Petitions; EI Ghass] It held, then as now, a week-long feast in February around the saint’s day, which the Indian bishops had fixed on 7 February so that it could be kept apart from the Nagasaki martyrs. [n42; n38; Archdiocese of Bombay] The parish ran a primary school named after the saint, where Simon Almeida studied in the 1960s; the older children went on to Holy Cross High School at Nirmal. [n42; Wikipedia: Simon Almeida] Fr Peter Almeida, his elder brother, became a priest of the diocese. [n42]

What the village was like is a matter of inference from what came before and after. In 1882 it was a garden village of sugarcane and plantains on raised land, with a landing that boats of eight to ten tons could still reach at high tide, a long winding lake fringed with Persian wheels, and basalt blocks from Brahma hill built into the plinths of its houses. [n42; Gaz XIV pp. 315, 341] In 2009 Wikipedia described it as “a natural village with a big lake”, known for salt farming and green-vegetable farming, with about five thousand Catholics. [n42; Wikipedia: Gass, Maharashtra] In 2025 a newspaper called it “a predominantly Christian village often described as ‘Mini Goa’ for the ancestral mansions that dot the surrounding farmland”. [n42; Hindustan Times via Magzter] Between those points lay the decades of this chapter, in which the landing silted finally out of use (no source gives the year), the farming continued, and the village, like the other Kupari villages, began sending its sons into the professions, the Church and abroad. [n42; Inferences] The two inscribed stones that the Gazetteer had seen at the houses of Degu Pobria Mhatara and Bab Naik are not mentioned in any source after 1882; whether they survived the republic’s quiet years in a Gass courtyard, or were lost, nobody has recorded. [n42; Gaps]

The village’s claim to be the site of old Sopara, recorded in 1882, would find modern support of a kind in the archaeologists’ finds of later decades in the Taki Pada part of Gass: Jain temple lintels, a Vishnu with the ten incarnations, hero stones, a Surya image inscribed in 1412 now held by the Sopara Jain community, and in 2018 a stone anchor near Gass–Taki Pada, where a “Bandar Masjid”, a port mosque, still stands. [n42; Shah 2020] When those objects were found is not stated; the anchor at least is outside this chapter. [n42; Gaps]

Migration: to Bombay and beyond

The movement of the Kupari and Vadval young out of the villages is the great unrecorded story of this chapter, and the sources can only outline it. The pull of the city was old. The railway had carried Salsette’s English-speaking Christians to clerks’ desks in Bombay by 1882, and the electric trains had run to Virar since 1936. [n34; n28] After independence the pull widened: the professions that the community histories list, engineering, medicine, banking, and the countries they name, the Gulf, Europe, the United States and Australia. [n34; Vasai Kupari blog; Post in Mumbai] The Church carried some abroad too: the bishops of these villages served in Rome, Amravati and Poona, and Nandakhal’s sixty-eight priests did not all serve at home. [n38; n40]

The census figures, such as they are, fit an exodus. The region held 194,262 people in 1971 and fewer than 250,000 in 1981, and many of its places lost people in the decade before 1981, while Bombay itself grew fast. [n06; n17; TIFR] An island that had grown by two-thirds between 1911 and 1951 almost stopped growing in the 1970s. The likeliest explanation is that its young people were leaving faster than its families were growing, but no source measures the migration directly, by community or by village, and the figures themselves disagree. [n06; n21; Inferences]

What the migrants left behind is described only in retrospect, and with affection. The red cap and red lugda of Kupari dress were, by a 2026 account, “largely absent from daily life but remain part of community identity”, lost to “urbanization, education, migration, media”. [n34; All About Vasai Virar, 29 Jun 2026] The same forces were at work in the 1970s and 1980s; they had simply not yet finished. And the migration ran in both directions. The commuters who would fill the new towns after 1988 were Bombay’s overflow moving north, and some of the earliest of them were the region’s own sons and daughters, who had gone to work in the city and could now afford to live at home and take the train. [n06; Inferences]

The bombil year

At sea, the end of the decade brought a record. In 1979 the catch of bombil, Bombay duck, reached 40,065 tons in the Satpati–Dahanu zone, a figure never reached again; by 2018 the catch for the whole state would be 16,576 tons. [n36; ICSF, via search summary] The Satpati boats that fished for bombil numbered thirty to thirty-five in the 1980s, and would fall to twelve by 2019. [n36; PARI, via search summary] Those are Palghar figures, not Vasai’s, and they come from search summaries; but they mark 1979 as the high point of the old fishery on this coast, just as the late 1970s were the last years of the old gardens.

On the eastern side of the taluka, on the slopes of Tungar hill, an ashram was founded in 1977 around the samadhi of a holy man, the Balyogi Sadanand Maharaj. [n10; HT via PressReader; DNA; CAT] It was built in the forest that would, a quarter of a century later, be notified as a wildlife sanctuary, and the clash between the two would end up in the Supreme Court. In 1977 nobody could have foreseen that.

At old Sopara, archaeology quietly continued. In 1972 Anwar Munshi found Satavahana lead coins there, the small change of the port that had traded with Rome. [n02, n18; Shah 2020] The ancient city was still giving up its past through the fields of the present.

What happened to the population in this decade is the strangest fact of the chapter. In 1971 the Vasai–Virar region held 194,262 people. [n06; Business Standard 2014] When the census-takers came back in 1981 they found fewer than 250,000; Vasai town had 34,900 people and Virar 23,303, and Agashi was the only village with more than 10,000. Many of the region’s places had actually lost people in the decade before 1981. [n05, n17; TIFR, The Vasai-Virar Region] The figures do not agree perfectly: a Business Standard profile gives growth of 32 per cent for 1971–81, while the TIFR account speaks of places shrinking, and the series for the corporation area gives 219,868 for 1981. [n06; Wikipedia: VVCMC; Business Standard] But the broad picture is clear. While Bombay itself boomed through the 1970s, the green belt sixty kilometres north stood almost still, and in parts emptied. Its young people, one infers, were going to the city or abroad; the Kupari history speaks of families becoming professionals or emigrating, to the Gulf, Europe, North America and Australia, though it gives no figures. [n07, n11; D’Mello blog]

One more seed was planted in the 1970s, on the low land east of the railway. A Tata Institute of Social Sciences project on Nallasopara, read only through a search summary, says that industrial zoning in the 1970s led entrepreneurs to build chawls for workers there. [n31; TISS “Make Break”] It is a thin source for a large claim, but it fits what came after. The rows of single-room chawls that would one day define Nallasopara East had their first, unrecorded beginnings in the quiet decade.

In politics the decade ended with a change of guard. In 1978, in the election that followed the Emergency, the Vasai seat went to Pandharinath Choudhari of the Janata Party. [n26, n28; Wikipedia: Vasai Assembly constituency] The constituency now carried the name Vasai rather than Bassein; the old colonial spelling had been used for the seat from 1952 to 1972. [n28; Wikipedia] No biography of Choudhari was found.

The 1980s: the last harvests

The wadis and the kawadis

To understand what was lost at the end of the 1980s we have to look at what the wadis still were at the start of it. The best account comes from a 2019 report on Vasai’s ponds and from a 2023 report on its flower growers, both looking back. Paddy was widely grown. Betel leaf was exported to Pakistan. The tiny velchi bananas, the “cardamom” plantain of Vasai, sold at a premium. [n17; Vikalp Sangam 2019] The villages of the sub-region supplied vegetables to Mumbai’s suburbs until the early 1980s. [n17; Vikalp Sangam] Garlic chives, cilantro, kantol and banana flower came in the monsoon; field beans, ghevda and toor in winter; light-green okra, brinjal, colocasia leaves for alu wadi, bottle gourd. Vasai’s sweet yellow-skinned bananas were the ones chosen for naivedya, the offering to the gods, and for shikaran, banana in cardamom milk; dried banana, sukeli, was the signature snack of the coast. [n17; India Food Network 2015]

The water for all this came from the ground, through a system that is one of Vasai’s least celebrated inventions. Each wadi was watered from a bavkhal (also spelled bawkhal or bhowkal), a pond dug down into the shallow aquifer, owned by a family group of anything from five to sixty people, and never used for drinking. [n17; Vikalp Sangam] At Bhuigaon about forty such wells, maintained mostly by women, still irrigated bananas, vegetables and flowers within living memory. [n08, n11; Mumbai Paused 2011] Marcus Dabre, editor of the Vasai Times, explains that the ponds held the rain and fed the fields through underground connections. [n17; 101Reporters 2023] The wadi, in other words, was a water machine, and it worked only so long as the land around the ponds stayed open.

The produce went south by the same railway that had carried the plantains and betel of the 1882 Gazetteer. The kawadis rode the 4 pm train to VT and sold at Bora Bazaar and near the Dadar Kabutarkhana. [n17, n05, n08; India Food Network 2015] This was the economy of the coast in miniature: no middlemen, no cold chain, a family’s labour carried on a family member’s shoulders into the city’s markets in time for the evening trade.

The decline came on several fronts at once, and the sources name each one. Betel leaf, exported to Pakistan, declined as relations between the two countries soured. [n11, n08; 101Reporters 2023] The velchi banana was hit by blight and replaced by the tissue-culture Grand Naine. [n11; 101Reporters] The kawadis faded as families moved into office jobs, as contractors tied farmers to supermarket chains, and as produce from Nashik and Pune took over the Mumbai markets. [n17; India Food Network] By 2015 a food writer could title her article “Where have the Vasaiwale kawadis disappeared?” [n17; India Food Network]

Not every grower gave up. In the 1980s many of the region’s flower farmers grew mogra, the jasmine that fetched premium prices at Dadar’s flower market. [n13; The Better India; 101Reporters] And in the late 1980s a farmer at Arnala named Moreshwar Bhoir brought in a new plant: the sonchafa, the golden champak, in a variety called “Velankar chafa” from Uday Velankar’s nursery at Kudal in the south Konkan. [n17, n11; 101Reporters 2023] His grandson Kiran Patil would one day chair the 1,100-member Arnala Farmers’ Cooperative. Whether sonchafa spread because it needed less water as the ponds began to fail is a plausible guess, but no source states it. [n17; Inferences] What is clear is that the flower belt from Arnala to Vasai, eighteen kilometres of small plots tended by some five hundred families, is the part of the wadi economy that survived, and it began its modern life at the very end of this chapter.

The politics of the decade

The 1980s returned the Vartak name to the assembly. In 1980 Tarabai Vartak won Vasai for the Congress (I), serving until 1985. [n26; Wikipedia: Vasai Assembly constituency] In 1985 the seat went to Dominic Gonsalves of the Janata Party. [n26, n28; Wikipedia] Four members from three parties in twelve years, from 1978 to 1990, is the signature of a seat in flux: still agrarian, Christian and Hindu, not yet captured by any single machine. [n28; Inferences] It was also, though nobody could have known it, the last such stretch.

The names on the list invite a reading that the sources do not allow. Choudhari is one of the three Pachkalshi surnames of the 1882 Gazetteer, beside Raut and Vartak, and the 1730s Chaudharis of Umele appear in the Marathi bakhar edition of 1935 as Somvanshi Kshatriya helpers of the Marathas. [n41; Gaz XIII-I p. 130; Naik 1935] Vartak is another of the three. Gonsalves is a Portuguese name of the kind that the 1931 census explained by the baptism of whole villages under the names of their Portuguese landlords. [n34; Census 1931 pp. 363–364] A writer looking for a pattern could build one: two Vadval-surnamed Hindus and a Christian, the island’s three great farming strands taking turns in the assembly. But no biography of Pandharinath Choudhari or Dominic Gonsalves was found, Tarabai Vartak’s and Bhausaheb Vartak’s own biographies do not state their community, and surnames on this coast cross caste and religion. [n26; n41; Gaps] The pattern is a question to be asked of people who remember the elections, not a finding.

The Vadval south in the republic

The southern parishes round the old fort, the Vadval Christian country of the Gazetteer’s timber carts, changed in these decades in the same ways as the Kupari north, and the sources for them are the same parish pages and heritage registers. Sandor, the “Mother Church of all the churches of South Bassein”, whose people were by the community’s own description “predominantly of the Vadval sub-ethnic group” speaking the Vadvali dialect, had already given birth to Giriz in 1917–18; it would give birth to Chulne in 1963. [n40; Wikipedia: Sandor; EI Sandor] Its census-town population of 8,336 in 2001, with literacy of 78 per cent, is the first count of a Vadval village in the record, and it comes just after this chapter. [n40; Wikipedia: Sandor] In its lands the heritage survey found an eighteenth-century Dev Talav and Hanuman Mandir from the Maratha years, and a nineteenth-century school building turned gram panchayat office: Hindu shrines and Marathi institutions inside a Christian village. [n40; CRIT Vol I items 99–103]

Chulne, the carters’ village of 1882, became a parish in 1963 and blessed its church in 1964; in 1970 a boy was born there, Thomas D’Souza, who would be Bishop of Vasai in 2024. [n40; EI Chulne; GCatholic] Gokhivare, the cholera-vow parish of 1943, was a Jesuit charge except for three years. Manickpur’s St Michael’s returned to the Jesuits in 1949 and gave its name to a municipal council, Navghar–Manikpur, which would be one of the four merged into the city corporation in 2009. [n40; EI Manikpur; VVCMC] Giriz, between the two belts, celebrated its golden jubilee in 1968 and built with German aid in 1977; it gave the Church Malcolm Sequeira, born in 1961, later Bishop of Amravati. [n40; n38; Wikipedia: Giriz] Remedy, the largest congregation of 1882, gave it Felix Machado, born in 1948, who would serve in the Vatican’s office for interreligious dialogue and become Bishop of Vasai. [n38; n40; GCatholic; Wikipedia: Felix Machado]

What the Vadval farmers grew in these decades is not separately recorded; the betel, bananas, vegetables and flowers of the wadi memories belong to the whole belt. The carts of 1882, which had carried timber from the Vada forests in gangs of five to thirty, are not heard of again in any source after the Gazetteer; the trucks on the 1968 bridge presumably finished what the railway had begun. [n41; n12; Inferences]

In 1982 a young couple from Mumbai began work in the village of Dahisar in rural Vasai. Vivek Pandit, born at Dahanu in 1957, and his wife Vidyullata founded the Shramajeevi Sanghatana that year, an organisation to free bonded labourers in the Adivasi villages of the district. [n26, n16, n08; Wikipedia: Vivek Raghunath Pandit] Sixteen years later the Indian Express would credit it with disproving the state’s claim that there had been no bonded labour in the area since 1992. [n16; India Environment Portal] Pandit would become, in time, the one man to beat the Thakurs at the ballot box in Vasai. In 1982 he was an outsider among the brick kilns and the forest hamlets.

The next year a Franciscan priest took over the editorship of Suvarta, a Marathi Christian periodical. Fr Francis D’Britto, born in 1942 or 1943, would edit it from 1983 to 2007. [n27, n13; Wikipedia: Francis D’Britto] He would become the conscience of the green belt.

Faith continued to mark the calendar. A new sacristy and residence at Nandakhal, built from 1978, was blessed by Cardinal Simon Pimenta on 27 November 1983. [n09; Wikipedia: Holy Spirit Church Nandakhal] At Giriz twin spires replaced the old ones in May 1981. [n09; Wikipedia] At Koliwada in Vasai the foundation stone of the present two-storey Gausia Masjid was laid in 1982, where a prayer shed had stood; it runs an Arabic school today with free lodging for about fifty students. [n10; Palghar district: Religious Places] On 9 February 1986 Pope John Paul II gave a speech that the Vatican files under the name of St Augustine’s High School, in which he spoke of how the Marian images at the Remedy and Pali churches drew devotees, Christian and non-Christian, “in great numbers”. [n18; Vatican: John Paul II speech, 9 Feb 1986] In March 1987 the Bombay Catholic weekly The Examiner described the ruined churches of Vasai Fort: the belfries with their spiral stairs climbing halfway and stopping, the bells long gone, the graves of fidalgos with their coats of arms, and the “huge hulk” of the Church of the Holy Name, where a temporary roof was put up each year for the Mass on St Gonsalo Garcia’s feast. [n09; The Examiner 1987 via Goanet] It is one of the few descriptions of the fort we have from inside this chapter’s span, and it shows the fort as the nineteenth century had left it: a ruin that the community still used.

Killa cha Sann: the feast in the ruins

The Mass under a temporary roof that The Examiner described in 1987 was the oldest continuous public event of the Christian villages, and it is worth describing as the sources allow. Every year, on the first Sunday nearest the neap tide after Christmas, the Catholics of the villages gathered at the ruined Jesuit church of the Holy Name inside the fort for the feast of St Gonsalo Garcia, a feast they call Killa cha Sann, “the fort’s festival”. [n38; FPJ, 11 Jan 2026; DNA India 2014] Families came the evening before, cooked East Indian dishes in the ruins and, in older times, arrived by cart or by boat. [n38; FPJ 2026] The youth of the Koliwada decorated the old church for the prayer meetings. [n38; DNA India 2014] The tide reckoning is a fishing people’s and a boatman’s calendar, and it tells its own story: the feast was fixed by men who came to it by water.

The church in which they met was the one in which Gonsalo Garcia had been taught by the Jesuits as a boy, if the hagiographies are right, in the 1560s. [n38; Indian Catholic Matters] His cult had lapsed “from about 1739”, when the Portuguese and their clergy left, and had been revived; by the time of the Catholic Encyclopedia, early in the twentieth century, the annual feast drew pilgrims from Bassein, Salsette and Bombay. [n38; Catholic Encyclopedia, Gonsalo Garcia] The Examiner’s writer of 1987 saw the fort as a place of ghosts, “haunted by the ghosts of dead fidalgos”, and repeated a claim that the Inquisition “was never set up in Bassein”. [n09; The Examiner 1987] That claim conflicts with da Cunha, who in 1876 described a commissary of the Inquisition at Bassein, a prison near the Captain’s palace and an inscribed slab near the travellers’ bungalow. [n38; da Cunha 1876, pp. 235–236] The primary source is the safer guide. What the 1987 account does show is a fort still empty of everything but ruins, fields, the annual feast and the visitors who came to look; no reconstruction, no lights, no festival of the state.

Across the creek mouth, at Erangal on the Madh shore, the church of St Bonaventure kept its January feast on the same principle, and Vasai families came to it too, by cart and boat. [n09; n38; FPJ 2026] The two feasts, at the two ends of the old Portuguese north, were the pilgrimage of the coast’s Catholics through all the years of this chapter.

The Hindu villages and their gods

The Hindu villages of the coast left even fewer dated traces in these decades than the Christian ones, because their temples kept no parish registers. What the notes record is continuity. The Nirmal jatra went on each November. [n20; n10] The goddess on Jivdhan hill above Virar, whose story of the Mhar herdsman and the leaping cow the Gazetteer had recorded in 1882, received a new image installed by devotees in 1956, by one Marathi press account; other accounts give the temple far older dates, and the conflict cannot be resolved. [n20; Pudhari; Lokmat] The Vadvals’ household goddesses, by the community’s own account, were Ekvira, Vajreshwari, Shitala Devi and Mahalakshmi, with Khandoba as the principal family god, and the poorer Vadvals, a Gazetteer of 1909 had said, kept a tulsi plant before the house and the new moon of Chaitra as a holiday. [n41; Marathi Wikipedia; Bombay City Gazetteer I, p. 240] The Hindu Samvedis kept their own Brahman priesthood and their gardens round Agashi, Vatar, Koprad and Nirmal, beside the Christian families who had once shared their caste. [n34; n40]

The Somvanshi Kshatriya identity that the Vadvals had pressed before the Ethnographic Survey in the 1910s was by the end of the century carried by associations. A Somvanshi Kshatriya Samaj Sangh would hold, in time, a forty-second annual sports festival, with branches at Bordi–Gholvad, Dehari and Jhai–Borigaon; the community websites offer directories, matrimony and blood-donor services. [n41; vadvalsamaj.com] The first of those festivals would, by the arithmetic of its numbering, fall in the 1980s, but the source gives no year, and this book does not supply one.

9 February 1986: a pope at Vasai

The papal visit deserves more than a sentence, because of what the Pope said and where he chose to say it. John Paul II’s visit to Bombay in February 1986 made Vasai, by the Church’s own account, its “first stop”. [n38; Vatican address] His address, delivered on 9 February at St Augustine’s High School, the Christian Brothers’ school founded in the early 1970s, named the place as the beginning of something: Vasai, he said, was “where the Christian faith was first proclaimed and Christian communities were first established”, and from here “the Franciscans and the Jesuits, the Dominicans and the Augustinians, went forth” to the villages and towns. [n38; Vatican: Address at Vasai, 9 Feb 1986] And he named its saint: “Saint Gonsalo Garcia, the young native Franciscan of Vasai, who was martyred together with his confreres in Japan.” [n38; Vatican address] For a saint whose cult had lapsed after 1739 and been revived in the nineteenth century, whose fort church had a roof only one day a year, and whose village parish at Gass had been blessed only twenty-four years before, it was the highest recognition the Church could give a local devotion short of a new feast. [n38; Inferences]

Two months later another visitor came. On 6 April 1986 Mother Teresa visited the parish of Nandakhal. [n40; Wikipedia: Holy Spirit Church Nandakhal] Of what she did or said there the parish history records nothing more.

The decade’s other church dates complete the pattern of growth. At Bhuigaon, the church begun in 1948 was finally completed in 1984 by Fr Nazareth Garbu. [n40; EI Bhuigaon] In the same year, 1984, St Gonsalo Garcia College was established at Vasai, affiliated to the University of Mumbai, the first college to carry the saint’s name. [n38; Wikipedia: St Gonsalo Garcia College] In 1988 Nandakhal opened a new Mass centre at Bolinj, the old northern landing of the Sopara creek, where in 1882 a Christian named Joseph Manuel Lopez had used an inscribed stone as his doorstep; it would become a parish in 2001. [n40; Wikipedia: Holy Spirit Church Nandakhal; Gaz XIV p. 342] And Thomas Dabre, the Bhuigaon priest ordained in 1971, became an auxiliary bishop of Bombay with charge of Vasai, in 1988 by one source and 1990 by others. [n26; n38; n40; Diocese of Vasai; GCatholic] The Vasai Catholics were on their way to a diocese of their own, which they would receive in 1998.

The Kupari villages at the end of the decade

By the late 1980s the Kupari parishes of the old Sopara agar looked like this: the mother church of Holy Cross at Nirmal; its daughters at Gass (1942) and Bhuigaon (1946); a Mass centre for the five villages of Chowkala and Gomes Ali (1979); the Holy Spirit at Nandakhal, with its own daughters at Umrale (1970) and Nanbhat (1975) and a new centre at Bolinj (1988); and St James at Agashi, with its schools. [n40; n09] Every one of these villages had been in the Gazetteer’s agar of 1882 or its plantain list. The parish map had become a map of the old garden.

What the villages did for a living had moved away from the garden. The community histories date the turn from farming to the professions to the late 1960s and early 1970s, and the region’s census figures for 1971 and 1981 show the island standing still while Bombay grew, its young people leaving. [n34; n06; n17] The Church gave the villages one of their exits. The Gazetteer had noted in 1882 that wealthy families of headmen and garden-owners brought up “one or more of their sons for the Church”, and in the 1920s the East Indian Association had complained of a “paucity of vocations”. [n34; Gaz XIII-I p. 205; Mendiratta 2017, p. 236] By 2016 Nandakhal parish alone would claim to have sent sixty-eight priests “and many nuns”. [n40; Wikipedia: Holy Spirit Church Nandakhal] Most of them were ordained in the decades of this chapter and the next, and four bishops of the twentieth and twenty-first centuries came from this small corner of the coast: Thomas Dabre of Bhuigaon (born 1945), Felix Machado of Remedy (born 1948), Simon Almeida of Gass (born 1960) and Malcolm Sequeira of Giriz (born 1961), with Thomas D’Souza of Chulne (born 1970) to follow. [n38; n40; n42; GCatholic; Fides; Wikipedia]

The Kupari estimates of their own numbers in the 2000s, forty to sixty thousand, are unsourced and come from community blogs; no census ever counted them. [n34; Inferences] What can be said is that at the end of the 1980s they were a farming people turning into a professional one, rooted in a parish map drawn in the 1940s to 1970s, speaking a dialect that the Gazetteer had glimpsed and no linguist had since studied, and living in exactly the villages that the decision of 1988 had opened to the builders.

Salt and the Kala-Krida

Two smaller threads end the decade. The salt pans of the north bank of the creek, 1,439 acres in 1880–81, are given in modern sources as about 2,000 acres at Vasai; no figure for the intervening century, and no account of the Agri salt-workers of these decades, was found. [n17; n21; Gaps] They were the land that the next generation’s plans would covet most, a story for the chapters that follow. [n17]

And at the end of the decade, by inference from its later numbering, the Vasai Taluka Kala-Krida Mahotsav, the arts and sports festival held each December, began. Its 37th edition was being prepared in October 2026, the 2020 edition was cancelled, and Mukesh Save, a former mayor of Virar who died in 2025, was described as its pillar “for the last 37 years”. That puts the first edition at about 1988 to 1990; no source states the year or names a founder. [n20; eSakal, 18 Oct 2026 and 30 Nov 2025; n13] It would grow to eighty-five competitions and sixty thousand contestants. [n13; FPJ] If the inference is right, the green belt’s great communal festival was born in the same years as the decision that would fill the green belt with strangers.

A community count from the end of the decade gives the Christian Kolis of the North Konkan coast, in Bassein, Agashi, Uttan, Gorai and Madh, at about 9,000 in 1989, mostly fishermen. [n07; Wikipedia: Koli Christians, via note 07]

The Gazetteer revised, 1982

In 1982, exactly a century after Campbell’s three volumes, the Maharashtra government published a revised Thane District Gazetteer. It is the one document of this chapter’s span that might have filled most of its gaps: its chapter on the modern period, by the secondary summaries that are all the researchers could see, covers the salt satyagraha at Arnala, Virar and Vasai in April 1930, a meeting at Virar on 15 April chaired by D. Gokhale at which seventy-five broke the salt laws, the boycott of officials at Arnala, and a Vasai cultivators’ resolution to pay only the first instalment of land revenue. [n43; Thane Gazetteer 1982, ch. 2, secondary summary] Its own chapters on the decades after independence presumably described the taluka as it stood in 1982. But the government site that hosts it refused every connection the research made, and it is not on the public archives. [n43; n21; Gaps] The reprint preface of the 1882 volumes remarks that the old Gazetteer held much valuable information “that the revised edition did not reproduce”. [n17; NVLI] For this coast the reverse is now true: the 1982 edition holds the information this chapter lacks, and it could not be read.

The land and water in the quiet years

The physical coast changed slowly in these decades, and mostly unrecorded. The Sopara creek, dry at Sopara by 1882 and navigable only to Gass and Bolinj, finished silting; no source gives the year the last boat came up it. [n42; Gaps] The mangroves of Vasai creek, put by one weakly sourced later account at about three thousand hectares along forty-seven kilometres, still lined both banks; the clearances that would halve them came after 1988. [n17; blog repost, 2009] On the eastern hills the forest of Tungar, where a hill station had failed in 1874, remained forest, with the ashram of 1977 on its slopes; it would be notified as a wildlife sanctuary only in 2003. [n10; n17] And on the low ground between the villages, the salt pans, the paddy and the bavkhals held the monsoon water every year, as they had in the Gazetteer’s day. The floods that later decades would blame on the filling of that ground were not yet a feature of the coast. [n17; Inferences]

The railway reaches capacity

The railway that had made the wadi economy possible was now also making the commute possible. The suburban lines south of Mumbai Central had been quadrupled in 1972. [n28; Wikipedia: Western line] In 1983 the original steel railway bridge across Vasai creek, the BB&CI structure rebuilt in the 1920s, was replaced by the first concrete bridge, to its east. [n28; Wikipedia: Vasai Creek Railway Bridge] And on 28 October 1986, twelve-coach trains began running between Dadar and Virar. [n28; Wikipedia: Western line] A twelve-car rake carries half as many people again as a nine-car one. The railway was getting ready for a flood of passengers before the flood arrived.

1988: the green belt is opened

The flood was released by two decisions taken in 1988. The first was a plan. On 31 August 1988 the state sanctioned a Regional Plan for the Vasai–Virar sub-region, with a Green Zone among its zones; it would be modified in 1990 and 1992. [n17; VVCMC draft DCR 2013] The second was the act that the plan allowed. In 1988, by every account in the notes, the state government de-reserved the region’s agricultural land, opening it to housing. It did so, the TIFR account says, “contrary to the recommendation of the MMRDA”, the metropolitan planning authority, and private builders began work at once. [n17, n05; TIFR] Harit Vasai’s own chronology dates the conversion of the Vasai–Virar green belt to residential use to August 1988, and adds the crucial detail: it was done without the infrastructure that housing needs. [n29; Sabrang India, “A people’s priest”, 2000]

The research for this book looked for a separate “1988 regional plan” document with details beyond these lines and did not find one. [n28; Gaps] We have the date of sanction, the existence of a Green Zone in it, and the effect of the de-reservation; we do not have the text. An earlier proposal also hangs in the background: the Free Press Journal’s obituary of D’Britto says that residents in the 1980s had campaigned against CIDCO, the state’s new-towns corporation, being made the planning agency for a projected population of 2.5 million, but it gives no date for that plan. [n29; FPJ, 25 Jul 2024] Somewhere in the 1980s the state had begun to imagine a city of millions on the green belt. In 1988 it turned the imagination into law.

What followed came quickly, and belongs mostly to the next chapter, but its first steps were already set. The metropolitan authority, MMRDA, which had advised against the de-reservation, remained the planning authority for the sub-region until CIDCO replaced it, by a notification of 14 May 1990 according to the corporation’s later regulations. [n17; TIFR; n29; VVCMC DCR] In May 1990 the Regional Plan was amended to convert about 8,500 hectares from the Green Zone to an urbanisable zone, for a city of twelve lakh people by 2011. [n17; Revised Land Use Plan] The 1988 decision is the hinge on which the whole later history of the coast turns, and Harit Vasai’s own members point to it as the moment “the plans were first drawn up”. [n17; Karmayog]

It is worth setting the decision against the land as the Gazetteer had measured it a century earlier: 2,624 wells, 191 ponds, a garden strip three miles broad watered by Persian wheels, rice on almost thirty thousand acres, salt on 1,439. [n21; Gaz XIII-II pp. 678–680; Gaz XIII-I p. 364] The 2007 and 2013 surveys of the bavkhals would find many of those ponds still in use in the Kupari villages, 39 at Bhuigaon, 55 at Nandakhal, 54 at Wagholi, more than the official records knew. [n17; Vikalp Sangam] In 1988 they were all still there. The de-reservation did not fill them; it made them land that could be filled.

The consequences belong to the next chapter. But the numbers that frame them were already in motion: the region that had held under a quarter of a million people in 1981 would hold 365,480 in 1991, an increase of about 150 per cent overall and 290 per cent in Vasai, Virar, Sopara and Sandor, driven by people who worked in Mumbai and came here to sleep. [n17, n06; TIFR; Wikipedia: VVCMC] Those are the 1991 census figures. The 1980s did the moving; the census only counted it.

Taking stock: the coast in 1988

Before the rally and the platform, it is worth standing still for a moment and looking at the coast through the threads this book follows in every age, as they stood in the last year of the old order.

Land and sea. The island of the Gazetteer was still an island in shape, a garden strip between the Vaitarna and the Bassein creek, but its inner creek had silted into fields, and its old landings at Gass, Bolinj and Vagholi were no longer landings. The creek mouth was crossed by a railway bridge, renewed in concrete in 1983, and a road bridge of 1968. Mangroves lined the creek; salt pans, paddy and ponds held the low ground. [n42; n12; n28; n17]

The sea and trade. The sea trade of the Bassein ports, which had shipped rice, molasses and plantains to Surat and the Gulf of Cambay in boats built on the island as late as the 1880s, had ended at some unrecorded date; no twentieth-century port statistics were found. [n36; Gaps] What remained of the sea was fishing: dol nets at Arnala, gill nets for pomfret, the bombil boom of 1979, a coast of Koli villages with co-operatives and ice. The island’s trade with the city went by rail, on the shoulders of the kawadis, and its betel leaf had gone, until relations soured, to Pakistan. [n36; n17; n11]

Settlements. Vasai town had 34,900 people in 1981 and Virar 23,303, and Agashi was the only village over ten thousand; the whole region held under a quarter of a million. East of the railway at Nallasopara the first workers’ chawls had been built after the industrial zoning of the 1970s. Every other settlement on the island was a village of a kind the Gazetteer would have recognised, with its gaothan, its pond, its church or temple and its wadis. [n17; n31]

Peoples. The communities of 1882 were all still there: Kupari and Hindu Samvedi, Christian and Hindu Vadval, Koli, Agri, Bhandari, Konkani Muslim, Gujarati Vani, Maratha Brahman, the Adivasi villages of the east where the Shramajeevi Sanghatana had been at work since 1982. The newcomers who would outnumber them all were, in 1988, only beginning to arrive. [n34; n41; n35; n08]

Religion. The Christian villages had built a dozen new parishes in fifty years and had been named by a pope as the place where the faith on this coast began; Gass’s church carried the name of the saint he praised. The Hindu villages kept their fairs at Nirmal and Jivdani, the Muslims their dargahs at Sopara and Pali and the new Gausia Masjid at the Koliwada. The coast’s old habit of shared holy places, Hindus, Muslims and Christians at the Nirmal fair of 1882, Hindus and others at the Marian shrines of Remedy and Pali in the Pope’s speech of 1986, was intact. [n40; n38; n10]

Languages. Standard Marathi in the schools and the Church, Gujarati among the Vanis, and beneath both the village dialects that Grierson had found in 1905: Samvedi, which the Christians of the north spoke as Kadodi; Vadvali, which a later estimate would put at about 2,500 first-language speakers around the year 2000 and call endangered; the East Indian Marathi of the southern parishes; Koli, Agri and the Adivasi speech of the east. No census counted them. [n22; n41; Wikipedia: Phudagi]

Work, food and festivals. Rice, bananas, betel and flowers in the wadis; mogra for Dadar; the first sonchafa at Arnala; salt on the north bank; fish at Arnala and Pachubandar; and in the towns, the commuter. On the tables of the Christian villages, the community lists name dishes, vade (fried bread), dhapti (steamed flour and vegetables), hingoli, fogey, dodal, fuddi, sanna, that no older source records and none contradicts; the community’s songs, collected at Nandakhal, are of brides, fodder and a child’s first shave. [n07; n34] In the calendar: Christmas and the village feasts, Aagera, the blessing of the first sheaves of the harvest at church that the Gazetteer had seen as the blessing of first fruits in 1882, the Palm Sunday procession the East Indians call Ramacha Pass, Killa cha Sann at the fort in January, the Nirmal jatra in November, and, perhaps from this very year, the Kala-Krida Mahotsav in December. [n13; n34; n38; n20]

The Kuparis. A farming people of about a dozen villages becoming a professional and emigrant one, organised in parishes that their parents had founded, sending priests and nuns in numbers out of all proportion to their size, and with no census figure of their own; a people whose name had perhaps first reached print in 1967. Their gardens and ponds were intact, and their villages lay exactly in the zone the state had just opened. [n34; n40; n17]

1989: the rally and the platform

In 1988 a young man from Virar, a Vartak College alumnus, became president of the Vasai Taluka Youth Congress. His name was Hitendra Thakur, and he was twenty-seven. [n26, n29; Wikipedia: Hitendra Thakur]

On 1 April 1989 Fr Francis D’Britto, the editor of Suvarta, convened a meeting of public figures to oppose what the state had done to the green belt. Out of it came, that year, the Harit Vasai Saurakshan Samiti, the Committee for the Protection of Green Vasai. [n29; Sabrang India 2000] An academic case study also dates the committee to 1989 and notes that sources differ on the details of its origin. [n29; IDRC case study] On 1 October 1989 it held a rally of about 35,000 people; among the speakers were the playwright Vijay Tendulkar and Kisan Mahta. [n29; Sabrang India] The Church supported it; its convenor was a priest; its crowd was drawn from the Christian and Hindu villages of the gardens that were about to be built over. [n06; Sabrang India] It was the largest public gathering this coast had seen in the records of the republic, and it was about land and water.

Eight days later, on 9 October 1989, a builder named Suresh Dube, thirty-one years old, was shot dead at point-blank range on the platform of Nallasopara station, in daylight. [n29, n26; FPJ, 24 May 2023; Lokmat Times] Prosecutors would allege that the killers were linked to the gang of Jayendra “Bhai” Thakur, Hitendra Thakur’s elder brother. The case would be tried under the TADA anti-terror law; seventeen accused would be arrested in 1992; a designated court would first acquit them all; the Supreme Court would hear an appeal brought by Dube’s brother and the state. [n29; Supreme Court, S.N. Dube v. N.B. Bhoir, via search summary] And on 24 May 2023, thirty-four years after the shooting, a special TADA court in Pune would acquit Bhai Thakur and others for lack of evidence. [n29, n26; FPJ; Lokmat Times; ThePrint] Every statement about who was responsible for Suresh Dube’s death therefore remains, as a matter of law, an allegation that a court did not uphold. That is how this book will treat it.

So the decade, and the quiet years, ended with two images a week apart: thirty-five thousand people gathered to save the gardens, and a dead man on a railway platform at the place where, in 1882, the Gazetteer had complained there was no station at all. [n31; Gazetteer 1882, p. 316] Between them they set the terms of the next twenty years: the green belt against the builders, the priest against the machine, the old villages against the new city. The quiet was over.

In March 1990 Hitendra Thakur would stand for Vasai. He would beat Dominic John Gonsalves, the Janata Dal candidate and the sitting member of 1985, by 46,468 votes. [n29; Wikipedia: Vasai Assembly constituency] The Socialist decade, the Vartak decades and the unsettled years of three parties were over; the priest of Nandakhal and the young man from Virar would meet across the green belt for the next twenty years.
