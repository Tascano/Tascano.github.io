Period covered: 1818 – 1869

In this chapter. In 1818 the Company takes the coast. This chapter follows the first half-century of British rule: the empty fort, the sugar years, the survey and the railway.

In 1825 an English bishop walked into the fort of Bassein and found no one there. Reginald Heber, Anglican Bishop of Calcutta, described the walled city as “perfectly uninhabited, a melancholy display of ruined houses and churches covered with a rank growth of trees and brushwood”. The ruins, he thought, were “of mean architecture, but striking from their lofty proportions and from the singularity of Christian and European ruins in India”. He counted seven churches still standing inside the walls. One of them, he noted, had been built by a man who made his fortune in the slipper trade. [n15; Thana Gazetteer XIV, pp. 31 n., 35; n32; da Cunha 1876, pp. 211–212]

Less than a century earlier this had been the Court of the North, the second city of Portuguese India, with its cathedral, its Jesuit college and its State House where the fidalgos stood each morning before their gouty governor. Now the gates were locked and the only inhabitants were a handful of soldiers. Yet a mile or two outside the walls, the country was not dead at all. Sugarcane grew under coconut and brab palms. Sunken lanes ran four or five feet below the fields, between high hedgerows. Round ponds and winding lakes were “fringed by high pile-supported Persian wheels”, and plantains and betel leaf went out from Agashi, Gass and Bolinj in small boats. [n11; Gazetteer XIV, p. 315]

That contrast, an empty fort set in busy gardens, is the picture of Bassein in the first half-century of British rule. The British had the old capital and did not want it. What they wanted, and taxed, and surveyed, and finally put on a railway, was the garden strip that people called the island of Bassein.

1810s: The Company takes the coast

The British had already been here. Colonel Goddard had taken Bassein by siege on 11 December 1780 and handed it back under the Treaty of Salbai. In December 1802 the fleeing Peshwa, Baji Rao II, had taken refuge at Bassein and signed away his independence there on 31 December, in the subsidiary Treaty of Bassein. A stockade of palm trunks was built at the Sopara bridge to guard the way onto Bassein island while he stayed, and a British letter of 26 March 1803 is headed “Camp Soopara”. The Peshwa left on 27 April 1803 and marched under escort through Kalyan to Poona. [n28; Thana Gazetteer XIV, Bassein; Gazetteer II, History; n15; Hobson-Jobson, “Supara”]

The final transfer came fifteen years later. Under the Treaty of Poona of June 1817, in the Gazetteer’s words, “Bassein with the rest of the north Konkan passed to the British”. The day is uncertain: most histories give 13 June, while the OCR text of the Gazetteer reads 18 June. Within a year the Peshwa’s state was gone altogether, and in 1818 the Company annexed his territories. [n16; Thana Gazetteer, History; n28; Gazetteer XIV; n04; Wikipedia: Treaty of Bassein (1802)]

The new rulers looked at their prize with a soldier’s eye and were not impressed. A British report of 1818 found the fort of Bassein of no military value: “scarcely a house habitable”, with only a small detachment kept there for a time. The same report ranked Arnala, the island fort guarding the mouth of the Vaitarna, as “next to Bassein in size and strength” among the forts of the north Konkan coast. Neither would ever be besieged again. [n28; Gazetteer XIV; n11; Gazetteer XIV, pp. 10–11]

The district these forts now belonged to was the Company’s North Konkan, with its headquarters at Thane. It was renamed Thane District in 1830 according to one account and in 1833 according to another. The exact date and manner in which Bassein became a sub-division, or taluka, of this district are not stated in any of the texts read for this book. By 1862 it appears in the records simply as “Bassein”, and the Gazetteer of 1882 calls it the Bassein sub-division. [n16; Wikipedia: Thane district; n28, gaps]

The first years of British rule were not quiet everywhere. For about twelve years, the Gazetteer says, the hill country above and below the Sahyadris was “infested with gangs of Bhil and Koli robbers”, and Koli gang robbers troubled the district until about 1830. No source names an incident inside Bassein taluka itself. [n16; Thana Gazetteer, History and Land Administration]

Then disease came. In 1819 and again in 1820 cholera broke out severely across the district. The Gazetteer remembers 1820–21 as “the cholera year”, when frightened people sought the protection of the goddess Devi and of Khandoba. How many died in Bassein is not recorded anywhere in the notes. All that survives is the general fear, and the turn to the old protectors. [n28; Gazetteer I, Population and religion]

The same year, 1820, brought one of the strangest religious stories of the period, and it is told in the Gazetteer’s account of the Agris. Under the Portuguese some Agris of Bassein and Salsette had become Christians. In 1820 a Palshe Brahman named Ramchandra Baba Joshi re-admitted a group of these Christian Agris to Hinduism, for a fee of Rs 1,200, and was temporarily put out of his caste for doing it. The people he received back were later called Urap or Varap Agris. The Gazetteer suggests the name may come from orapne, to sear with a hot iron, after the ordeal of purification. They were also called Nave, or new, Marathas, and some kept their Christian surnames for generations: Gomas, Soz, Fernan, Frutad, Minez. [n08; Gazetteer I, p. 117]

In the same years a Bombay merchant was building a temple on the other side of the island. About 1820, the Gazetteer says, “Motilal a Vani of Bombay” built a Jain temple to Parasnath at Agashi. It is probably the ancestor of the Jain tirth that stands at Chalpeth in Agashi today, although the image worshipped there now is described as Munisuvrat Swami, and the two may or may not be the same shrine. [n10; Gazetteer XIV, p. 2]

And the Christians who had not gone back were building too. In 1821 the parishioners of Dhauli, about two miles north of Bassein, put up a new church to Our Blessed Lady of Remedies. It measured 108 by 33 by 23 feet and cost Rs 18,600, of which Rs 18,000 was raised by subscription and Rs 600 came from the government. By 1881 it would have the largest congregation of the six Bassein parishes. [n11; Gazetteer XIV, p. 37]

So within three years of the British arrival, the island already showed the pattern of the century to come. Hindu, Christian, Jain and Muslim neighbours were rebuilding their places of worship, sometimes moving between faiths, while the government, from far away, counted rain and revenue.

1820s: The empty fort and the first school

In 1824 a visitor found the fort “uninhabited, locked”, with a small garrison under a conductor of ordnance. A year later Heber came and saw the brushwood over the churches. [n28; Gazetteer XIV]

The monsoon of 1824 failed. Crops were scanty in Dahanu, Bassein and Salsette and grain rose to famine prices, but the Gazetteer records no deaths from starvation. The government spent about Rs 15,500 clearing out reservoirs, a kind of relief work that kept the tanks in order for the next rains. [n28; Gazetteer I, Famines]

In 1826 Clunes’s gazetteer, one of the earliest British descriptions of the region, listed Sopara as a kasba, a market town, and a post-runner’s station. The old capital of the Konkan was now a stop on a mail route. [n31; Gazetteer XIV, quoting Clunes]

Then, in 1827, came an event that a book about Bassein should set down plainly. That year the first Government vernacular school in the whole of Thana district was opened at Bassein. The second followed at Kalyan three years later. No source in the notes names its teacher, its pupils or its building, and no source says what it taught. But it was the first, and it was here. [n28; Gazetteer II, Education]

The next year, on 12 November 1828, another Palshe Brahman of Bassein, Vithal Hari Naik Vaidya, received a second group of Christian Agris back into Hinduism. The Palshes, as the Gazetteer explains, were the old priestly families of this coast, who called themselves Vajasaneyi Brahmans. They had the privilege of fixing the auspicious time for marriages and thread ceremonies for all classes in Salsette, Bassein, Mahim and Dahanu, and they worked as priests, physicians and astrologers. They had, the Gazetteer adds, “suffered much since the Maratha conquest” at the hands of their rivals, the Deccan and Chitpavan Brahmans. Their surname list reads like a roll-call of later Bassein families: Kavle, Joshi, Phatak, Pandit, Chhatre, Mogre, Kirtane, Purandare, Paranjpe and others. [n08; Gazetteer I, pp. 81, 117]

In 1829 the government made its first attempt to bring industry into the dead fort. A Mr Lingard was given a rent-free lease of about 83 acres on the esplanade of Bassein fort for 40 years, together with an advance of Rs 23,000, to grow Mauritius sugarcane and build a sugar mill. In the same year the land inside the walls was leased to a Mr Cardoza for 30 years at Rs 400 a year. The ruined capital of the Portuguese north had become, in effect, a farm. [n28; Gazetteer I, Sugar]

1830s: Sugar, rates and hungry years

Lingard built his mill. The Gazetteer of the places of interest dates the attempt to 1830; the district volume says the lease was in 1829 and that his death, in 1832, stopped the whole scheme. From 1833 to 1836 the government tried again, leasing the estate to a Hindu, Narayan Krishna, with two years rent-free. That venture also failed. [n28; Gazetteer I; Gazetteer XIV; n32; Gazetteer XIV, pp. 35–36]

The government also began to build for its officials. A travellers’ bungalow went up at Bassein in 1834, at a cost of £167. Two years later, in 1836, two Parsi merchants, Dadabhai and Mancherji Pestanji Wadia, built a rest-house in the town. They had a precedent: another Parsi rest-house had been built by Lavji Sorabji Lakdavala as early as 1780. [n32; Gazetteer XIV, pp. 35–37; n11]

The real story of the 1830s, though, was not the fort. It was the gardens, and the tax on them. The British had inherited a high assessment on garden land, and by the mid-1830s, according to the Gazetteer, gardens had actually fallen out of cultivation “through over-assessment and lack of market”. In 1836–37 the Revenue Commissioner, Mr Williamson, revised the rates, cutting them by “about a hundred per cent” in the Gazetteer’s odd phrase. It probably means roughly by half, but the wording cannot be checked against another source. [n28; Gazetteer II, Land Administration, p. 610]

In the same year a special system for taxing toddy was set up in Bassein. Toddy was the palm sap that the Bhandaris drew from coconut and brab palms, and the Bhandaris are one of the communities whose life the Gazetteer of 1882 describes most vividly. Their name, it says, comes from the Sanskrit mandhārak, a distiller. They were “often seen with a hollow gourd full of palm-juice on their head”, and they “always carry on their left thigh a heavy broad-bladed tapping knife”. At work they wore a scarlet waistcoat and skull-cap. “They sing while they tap the trees.” Their women decked their hair with flowers. They worshipped Cheda with offerings of goats and fowls, and fasted on Ganesh Chaturthi. Their headman was the mukadam. They told the British that they had come to this coast with King Bimb, and some said the Portuguese had brought them from Goa, which the Gazetteer thought unlikely. Their highest division were the Kirtes, who tapped coconut palms; below them came the Gavads, who tapped brab palms; and lowest were the Kirpals, who had “once” been Christians. Across the district there were 13,088 of them in 1872. [n08; Gazetteer I, pp. 149–150]

When the government kept the special Bassein toddy tax in 1844, it gave a revealing reason: it did so “owing to the peculiarities of the country and the temper of its people”. What exactly those peculiarities and that temper were, the Gazetteer does not say. [n28; Gazetteer II, Excise]

The weather was cruel in these years. In 1837 the late rains failed in Sanjan and Bassein and the crops failed with them; remissions of about Rs 45,000 were granted. In 1838–39 the rains failed across the whole district, remissions reached about Rs 2,87,840, and rice had to be brought from Malabar to relieve Salsette. [n28; Gazetteer I, Famines]

In those hungry years two travellers left very different pictures. John Vaupell, writing in the late 1830s and 1840s for the Bombay Geographical Society, found Bassein “the chief market town of a petty division”, its shops mostly held by Gujarati Vanis and a few Muslims. Sopara, he wrote, was “a large place in the Agasee mahal”, with “a considerable Mussulman population, as well as Christian and Hindoo… there is a good deal of trade; and grain, salt, and garden produce are exported to Guzerat and Bombay”. [n28; Gazetteer XIV; n15; Hobson-Jobson, quoting Trans. Bo. Geog. Soc. vii. 140]

In 1837 Agashi, too, was described as “the chief town on Bassein island”, with a large Christian and Hindu population and a brisk trade in grain, garden produce and salt to Gujarat and Bombay. “Very good fishing boats and coasting craft were built” there. Agashi had been a shipbuilding town since before the Portuguese, and in 1837 it still was. [n11; Gazetteer XIV, p. 2]

But in 1838 Mrs Postans saw only the fort. To her, Bassein was “long forsaken”, with no inhabitants “except a few fishermen and hunters”. Both descriptions were true. The town had moved out of the walls, and the walls had been left to the brushwood. [n28; n15; Gazetteer XIV, p. 35]

In 1837 the district saw a riot between Muslims and Hindus at Bhiwandi, east across the creeks. The Gazetteer lists it as one of only three exceptions to an otherwise quiet early British period in Thana. It records no such trouble in Bassein. [n16; Thana Gazetteer, History]

Meanwhile the sugar estate passed to a firm. From 1837 McGregor, Brownrigg & Co had a trial of it, and in 1841 they received a perpetual lease of about 115 acres, counted from 1839. In 1838 the parishioners of Sandor, three miles north of Bassein, built a chapel onto their old church of St Thomas the Apostle, whose side walls went back to the sixteenth century. [n28; Gazetteer I; n11; Gazetteer XIV, p. 37]

1840s: Taxes lifted, a cane that would not grow

The revenue of the sub-division in 1841–42 was Rs 86,650. It would rise to Rs 1,06,440 by 1860–61. [n28; Gazetteer II]

In 1840 the Christians of Pali, about three miles east of Bassein, built a church to Our Blessed Lady the Mother of God at a cost of Rs 15,000. The same year, according to da Cunha’s history of 1876, the Court of Directors in London sanctioned an embankment across the Kalyan creek to Bassein island, an early sign that the island would not stay an island for ever. [n11; Gazetteer XIV, p. 37; n32; da Cunha 1876, p. 157]

In 1843 McGregor Brownrigg reported what Lingard had not lived to see: Mauritius cane did not thrive at Bassein. They switched to other crops, and in 1848 the estate was sold to a Mr Joseph. [n28; Gazetteer I, Sugar]

The fisher folk of the coast gained something in this decade. Until Act XIX of 1844, the Kolis, Mangelas and Vaitis had each paid a fishing poll-tax, the dāy-dene, of Rs 1-4. The Act abolished it. [n08; Gazetteer I, fisheries footnote]

The fishing communities appear in the Gazetteer of 1882 in more detail than almost anyone else, and the picture belongs here, at the moment their tax was lifted. The mass of the sea-fishing population of the district were the Son Kolis, about 8,000 of them, chiefly along the coast south of the Vaitarna. They were short and sturdy, with powerful shoulders, and the men wore a top-knot, a moustache and a red broadcloth cap. Their Marathi had “so curious an accent” that outsiders found it hard to follow. The Gazetteer calls them hardworking, hospitable and honest, though “much given to drink”. Their women wore a tightly wound robe that ended above the knee, and glass bangles on the left hand only. “At their weddings the bangles intended for the right hand are consecrated and thrown into the sea, the ocean being invoked to take care of the husband.” Their family gods were Khanderao, Bhavani, Bhairav, Babdev, Vir, Kalkai, Cheda and Marubai, kept in the houses of the older men, where the rest of the family came daily to pray. Their tribal head was the Vagh Patil of Alibag, “whence the Thana Son Kolis say they originally came”. “Most of them have a good market for their fish”, the Gazetteer says, “and on the whole are well-to-do.” [n08; Gazetteer I, pp. 148–149]

In June and July, when the boats could not go out, they made new ropes, nets and sails. Alongside them were Christian fishermen: “the Uraps and other Christian Kolis in Bassein… are professional fishermen”. [n08; Gazetteer I, fisheries]

North of them lived the Vaitis, 4,596 strong in north Bassein and south Mahim. They were close kin of the Kolis, though ranked lower, and told of a founder called Valhya Koli, which echoes the legend of the sage Valmiki. Their headman lived at Chaul, they had no surnames, and they worshipped chiefly Ram and the sea, keeping only a coconut at home as an object of worship. Further north again were the Mangelas, 10,718 of them in Mahim, Dahanu and Salsette, whose women sold the fish and whose Marathi was full of Gujarati words. A modern list places Mangela families at Pachu Bandar, Vasai and Arnala. The Machhis of Bassein spoke Gujarati at home and Marathi outside, fished, hired out boats and worshipped Maruti. And the Bhois kept three great holidays: Gauri, Shimga, and Narli Purnima, “Cocoanut-day”, when they went down to the shore and worshipped the sea. [n08; Gazetteer I, pp. 146–149, 182; Wikipedia: Mangela Kolis]

On land the weather went on failing. In 1848 the rains broke for long dry spells in June, July and September, and most of the crop on the salt-rice lands, the low fields near the creeks, was lost. [n28; Gazetteer I]

Those salt-rice lands belonged above all to the Agris, and their life too is set down in the Gazetteer. The Agris were salt makers, toddy drawers, drummers and rice farmers. Their main division, the Sudagris, included Mithagris, who made salt, Jasagris, who drew toddy, and Dholagris, who drummed. Their surnames included Bhoir, Chaudhari, Chavan, Gharat, Jadhav, Mhatre, Naik, Pawar, Shelar and Vaze, and their family “badges” were trees and fruits: pineapple, hirda, mango, fig. A second division, the Dasagris, lived along the Tansa and Vaitarna and in Bassein villages such as Kofar, Chandve, Navsai and Khanivde, with badges that included a river, a crab and a peacock. The Agris’ staple was nagli or coarse rice with fish. They favoured Cheda and other local spirits, and the Gazetteer, in the manner of its time, calls them “excessively fond of liquor”. By 1881 the Gazetteer could simply call the Agris of Bassein “salt-workers”. [n08; Gazetteer I, p. 116; n28; Gazetteer I, Salt]

A census was taken in 1846, but in the volumes read for this book it survives only as a line in a table of contents. No figure for Bassein taluka before 1872 has been found. [n28, gaps]

1850s: A sugar factory in a church

The 1850s opened with more drought. In 1850 the rainfall was far below average and the coastal tract suffered. [n28; Gazetteer I]

In 1851 the parish of Manikpur, four miles north-east of Bassein, rebuilt its church of St Michael the Archangel for Rs 15,000. Its Portuguese name had been Porim. Within a few years the name Manikpur would be attached to something else entirely: the railway station. [n11; Gazetteer XIV, p. 37; Wikipedia: Sandor]

The most startling event of the decade came in 1852. That year Mrs Xavier, a daughter of the Cardoza who had leased the inside of the fort, was allowed to turn the ruined church of Nossa Senhora da Vida into a sugar factory. Careri in 1695 had called it one of the oldest churches in Bassein, with three altars. She sublet the factory to Littlewood. When the workmen dug the foundations for the machinery they found, in a grave, the bones of a man and of a horse, with a rusty rapier: a general, da Cunha thought, buried with his charger. The space between the old Misericórdia and Nossa Senhora da Vida was dug out into a tank for the sugar machinery, and a walled place with steps was built near the sea to store sea water. The factory worked until 1874. [n28; Gazetteer I, Sugar; n32; Gazetteer XIV, pp. 36, 40; da Cunha 1876, pp. 226–227]

In 1856 a road was cut through the old town to the landing place, and the people of Nirmal rebuilt their church of Santa Cruz, the Holy Cross, at a cost of about Rs 24,000. It was a big building, 96 by 28 by 26 feet, and its parish had 2,184 Christians by 1881. The parish tradition dates the first church at Nirmal to 1557. Across the lake from it stood the eight Hindu temples that the Maratha governor Shankarji Keshav had built about 1750, after the Portuguese had destroyed the earlier shrine. The two faiths of Nirmal stood a few hundred yards apart. [n32; Gazetteer XIV, p. 36; n11; Gazetteer XIV, pp. 292–293; Rajan D’Mello blog]

The Christians of Nirmal, Gass, Bhuigaon and the other western villages were in large part the people later called Kuparis, or Samvedi Christians. The name is said to come from the Portuguese compadre, godfather. The Gazetteer does not use that word, but its description of Sopara in 1882 names the same people: the husbandmen there were “Christians, many of them converted Samvedi Brahmans”. Their speech was Samvedi, also called Kadodi, the same dialect their Hindu neighbours spoke. A community blog describes the old Kupari dress, the men in dhoti, black jacket and red cap with an earring in the left ear, and the women in red checkered saris, along with candle processions in Lent and a house-foundation rite adapted from the Hindu bhoomi pujan. It is a weak source and is offered here only as the community’s own memory. [n05; Wikipedia: Kupari; n11; Gazetteer XIV, p. 315; Rajan D’Mello blog]

Their Hindu cousins, the Samvedi Brahmans, were counted in 1872 at 2,563, and they were found, the Gazetteer says, “only in Bassein and Mahim”. It describes them as “strong, tall, and fair, with regular features… clean, neat, sober, thrifty and orderly. Husbandmen and gardeners by craft, they live in one-storied houses with walls of wood planking and tiled roofs. Their daily food is rice pulse and vegetables.” The men wore short waistcloths and red broadcloth caps, and on high days white turbans and silk-bordered waistcloths. The women wore gold ornaments with names the Gazetteer carefully records: mudaga khudi for the head, lavanga bali and mugdya for the ears, putlyachi mal and vajratik at the neck, tode, vale and phule on the feet. A boy’s thread ceremony ended with a mock pilgrimage to Benares: after he had walked about fifty paces, his maternal uncle asked him not to go and promised him his daughter in marriage, and the boy came back and begged alms from the guests. Girls married before ten, and a bride-price of Rs 200 to 1,000 was paid to the girl’s father. The Samvedis had no headman and settled disputes by majority vote. “They send their boys to school and are on the whole prosperous.” [n08; Gazetteer I, pp. 82–83]

Their deity was Nirmaleshwar, and their villages lay around the Nirmal lake and the old Sopara: Agashi, Vatar, Nandakhal, Bolinj, Kofrad, Umrale. The community’s own tradition traces them to Odisha, as singers and musicians who came to Shurparaka and were later restored to Vedic practice by a Shankaracharya at Nirmal. That story comes from modern community tellings with contradictory dates, and should be read as their legend. [n05; n08; Wikipedia: Samvedi]

The other great gardening people were the Pachkalshis, also called Vadvals, Sutars, Malis, Chaukalshis, Somvanshi Kshatris and Pathares. In 1872 they numbered 15,367, strongest along the coast. Their name, the Gazetteer says, comes from the five earthen pots, kalash, used in their ceremonies. They said they had come with Bhimdev from Paithan at the end of the thirteenth century; the Gazetteer thought they more likely came from Gujarat with the Pathare Prabhus. Their help at the siege of Bassein in 1739 had won one family the chief patilship of Salsette. Their surnames were Raut, Vartak and Chaudhari. They spoke Marathi with l for ḷ and n for ṇ. “Wives of gardeners sell vegetables, butter and milk.” In their houses images of Cheda and other local gods sat beside Ganpati, Shiv and Krishna. They had a proverb about themselves: “Pachkalshi ani kon mhanel alshi?”, “Who can call a Pachkalshi idle?” [n08; Gazetteer I, pp. 129–130]

One more find of these years would matter later. About 1850, a three-headed Brahma, carved in the eleventh or twelfth century, was found buried in a field near Sopara and set up at the Chakreshvar lake, where by 1882 people worshipped it as Dattatraya. [n10; Gazetteer XIV, pp. 337–338]

Then came 1857. Across northern India the great rising shook the Company’s rule to its foundations. In Thana district, the Gazetteer says flatly, there was nothing: “Except that Vengaon near Karjat was the birth place of the infamous Nana Saheb, Thana had no share in the 1857 mutinies.” It allows only “the alarm and disquiet” of that year. No source records any event in Bassein, and none should be invented. The next year the Crown took over from the Company, and the fort passed with everything else to the Raj. [n16; Thana Gazetteer, History; n04; Wikipedia: Vasai Fort]

In 1858 the Sandor parishioners added a new frontispiece to their church. In May of the same year, far to the south and north, construction began on the Bombay, Baroda and Central India Railway, the BB&CI. And in 1858–59 government surveyors began to measure every field in the Bassein sub-division. In 1859 the sugar estate passed again, from Joseph to Dosabhai Jahangir and J. H. Littlewood. [n11; Gazetteer XIV, p. 37; n28; Gazetteer I, Communications; Gazetteer II, p. 610]

1860s: The survey, the rails and the town council

The 1860s changed the island more than any decade since 1739.

In 1860, on 21 March, Macfarlane and Littlewood were granted a 30-year lease of land in Bassein fort at Rs 271 a year for the sugar company. In the same year the whole interior of the fort was leased for thirty years to Major Littlewood. That lease would outlive him; his widow held it later, and her son cultivated the fields inside the walls. In January 1882 the Collector, W. B. Mulock, would complain that this made “the proper preservation of the ruins almost impossible”. In 1861 the sugar business was sold to Lawrence & Co, and in 1868 it was bought by Littlewood, Worthing and Navroji Manekji. [n28; Gazetteer I, Sugar; n32; Gazetteer XIV, pp. 37–38]

It was a Littlewood, J. H. Littlewood, “the resident lessee”, who would later help the historian José Gerson da Cunha find the oldest inscription in the fort, on a ruined bastion near the citadel: “The first Captain who built this fortress was Garcia de Sá, by command of the Governor Nuno da Cunha, in the year 1536.” The sugar men who had dug into the past for their machinery became, in the end, its guides. [n32; da Cunha 1876, p. 217]

At the end of 1860 the island did something it had not done in forty years of Company rule: it rose against a tax.

The income tax was new in 1860, and the government sent a special officer, Mr Hunter, the Deputy Collector, to bring Bassein into it. When he reached the town on 6 December he learned what had happened the day before. On 5 December about 4,000 inhabitants had gathered in front of the Mamlatdar’s office, and many of them “had thrown down their notices and forms”. [n43; SM-I pp. 22–25, citing Judicial Dept vol. 53 of 1860, pp. 110–113] The Mamlatdar’s office was the seat of the revenue in the town; the building the Gazetteer would later describe was not put up until 1869. [n32; Gazetteer XIV, p. 36]

Hunter called the people to hear him, and a crowd of 1,200 to 1,500 came, “half of whom were Kolies”, the fishermen of the coast. [n43; SM-I] Their spokesman was a man the file calls Govardhan Gangadas in one paragraph and “Sheth Gordhandas”, the leader, in the next; he was probably one man, though the text does not say so. [n43; SM-I; Gaps] “Sheth” is the title of a merchant or a man of substance. He refused the form, and he gave his reasons, which the official file preserves. The salaries of the European officers were so large, he said, that “one-fourth of the salary of one single English officer is enough to cover the expenditure of the whole establishment of his office”; “we poor residents of this country should not be taxed”; and the government wanted them “reduced to utter poverty… as the Red Indians were annihilated in America.” [n43; SM-I] It is a remarkable speech to find in a government file of 1860: a Bassein sheth, three years after the great rising in the north, comparing the fate of his countrymen with that of the native peoples of America.

Hunter insisted, and when he tried to arrest the leaders, in the editors’ words, “confusion and conflict followed”. He seized Gordhandas by the arm, and the crowd rose. On the high road he was stoned and beaten with sticks. He made for the creek. He fled by the back door to catch a country craft, a boat that would carry him away on the tide, but “the kolies and other agriculturists followed him up to the creek, jumped into water, and after stopping the country craft rushed into it and brought it back to the shore.” [n43; SM-I pp. 22–25] By the file’s own account, the crowd anchored his boat and pelted it for about an hour. [n43; SM-I, J.D. vol. 53, pp. 110–113]

What followed was strange and, in its way, orderly. The Kolis did not kill him. They “practically detained” him: they made him sit, and “simply urged him to drop the collection of the tax.” Then the leaders relieved him and let him go. [n43; SM-I] Hunter thought the whole affair “a prearranged plot”. [n43; SM-I] The government answered in the usual way: a criminal case was filed, and “two or three persons were convicted”. Their names are not given. [n43; SM-I; Gaps]

The scene belongs to the island’s whole history: a Deputy Collector running for the tide, fishermen in the water dragging a boat back to the shore, a merchant arguing the economics of empire on the Bassein high road. It was a tax riot, not a nationalist rising, and it should be read as one; the people of Bassein were defending their purses, not demanding self-rule. [n43; Inferences] But the government itself thought it worth a section in its official source book on the history of the freedom movement, under the heading “Bassein (District Thana) resists income-tax, 1860”, and the editors’ summary is that Bassein “stood firmly against the imposition of the Income-tax”. [n43; SM-I contents p. 23] It is the first documented defiance of British rule on this coast. Three years earlier, in 1857, the Thana district had had “no share” in the mutinies, and no Bassein incident of that year has been found. [n43; Section 1; n16]

In 1861 a poet named Haridas composed the Nirmal Mahatmya, the praise-poem of Nirmal, which tells of the demon Vimal, Parashurama and the Vimaleshvar linga, and is still published by the trust of the Shankaracharya temple there. [n16; Thana Gazetteer, History; n02; Shah 2020]

On 8 June 1862, in Rome, Pope Pius IX canonised the twenty-six martyrs of Japan, crucified at Nagasaki in 1597. One of them was Gonsalo Garcia, born in the Bassein area to a Portuguese father and a local mother and schooled by the Jesuits in the fort. He became the first Indian-born saint. The notes say nothing of how the news was received in Bassein that year. But the ruined Jesuit church and college where he had studied stood inside walls that had just been leased to a sugar planter. [n25; Wikipedia: Gonsalo Garcia; n07]

The revenue survey was finished in 1861–62, and in 1862 the Bassein settlement came into force. The surveyors had measured about 250 square miles and 104 villages, of which all but four alienated villages, about a hundred, were assessed. They sorted the villages into four classes, with top rates per acre of Rs 6, Rs 5.5 to 5, Rs 4.5 to 4, and Rs 3.5 to 3. The district’s list of settlements gives “Bassein (1862)”, between Salsette in 1861 and Mahim in 1863. Every garden, every rice field and every salt-rice plot on the island now had a class and a rate. [n28; Gazetteer II, p. 610]

Then, in 1864, came two foundings in one year.

The first was civic. On 15 March 1864, by Government Resolution 621, a municipality was established at Bassein. Its limits took in the old fort and most of the modern town, which stood in the village of Malonde, together with the survey villages of Dhauli, Sandore and Mulgaon, “and many gardens and fields between Bassein and Papdi, about a mile and a half to the east”. The modern town now had a council of its own, though the notes give no names of its members and no account of its first years. By 1880–81 it would be collecting Rs 7,141 and spending Rs 6,637, including £117 on scavenging, £46 on lighting and £77 on roads. [n32; Gazetteer XIV, p. 36; n11]

The second founding was the railway. On 28 November 1864 the Baroda line opened along 95 miles from Bombay to the Surat border, with sixteen stations. Bassein Road, 83¾ miles up the line, was served by the first passenger run on the BB&CI’s route between Grant Road and Ahmedabad, and Virar had its station five miles further on. Bassein Road is today Vasai Road, and its station code, BSR, still remembers the old name. In 1882 the Gazetteer would still often call it the Manikpur station, after the village beside it. [n05; Wikipedia: Vasai Road railway station; n28; Gazetteer I, Communications; n11; Gazetteer XIV, pp. 314, 341]

To reach Bassein from Bombay the line had to cross the Bassein creek, and the same day a bridge opened over it, split in two by Panju island: a southern section of about 1,314 metres in 69 spans and a northern section of about 476 metres in 25 spans. One source places the original bridge in the late 1850s, but most sources give 1864. Local history calls it “Cooley Bridge”, or the Bhayander bridge. [n05; Wikipedia: Vasai Creek Railway Bridge; Wikipedia: Panju Island; memumbai; Vasaiker]

The railway was built partly from the island’s own rock. About a mile north of Achola, near the top of the steep Rakshi Dongar or Tulinj hill, a basalt dyke stood out from the hill as six-sided pillars six to twenty-two feet long; the rock was dark, glistened with mica, and rang like an anvil when struck. “Many of the pillars were used when the railway was made”, the Gazetteer notes, and people were still carrying them away in 1882. [n31; n12; Gazetteer XIV]

The station at Bassein Road drew business at once. On 1 November 1865 the BB&CI started a local service between Grant Road and Bassein Road, one train each way, with Bassein Road as its northern terminus. In December 1865, because two passenger trains were often at the station together, Rs 6,522 was sanctioned for a second roofed platform. Wells were dug at the station. The sources do not agree on when Bombay’s suburban trains began: the station’s history gives 1 November 1865, the Wikipedia article on Vasai gives 1867, and the history of the Western line dates the first regular suburban service, from Back Bay to Virar, to 12 April 1867. All three may describe different services. What is clear is that by 1867 Virar, not Bassein Road, was the end of the line for Bombay’s local trains, and would remain so for well over a century. [n05; Wikipedia: Vasai Road railway station; Wikipedia: Vasai; n28; Wikipedia: Western line; Wikipedia: Virar railway station]

A tourism summary on the Palghar district site speaks of regular trains from 1869 to “Bassein, Nalasopara and Virar”. That claim could not be verified, and it conflicts with the Gazetteer’s 1882 station list, which has no station at Sopara at all. The name Nallasopara does not yet belong in this story. [n11; palghar.gov.in pointer; n28; Gazetteer I]

The rails brought new kinds of visitors, and with them new schemes. From 1865 to 1874, T. C. Hope, Sir Bartle Frere and others tried to turn Tungar hill, north-east of the station, into a health resort. In 1868 a Portuguese opened a hotel with refreshment rooms at Bassein Road station. In 1869 the summit of Tungar was surveyed, fifty-three house sites were marked out and about thirteen miles of foot and bridle paths were cleared. The road from Gokirva to the top was built in 1869–71 for £855. The Gazetteer’s case for the hill was that it might serve “as a sanitarium for poor families, who cannot avail themselves of the expensive accommodation of Matheran; and for the large colony of Europeans employed by the Baroda and Peninsula Railway Companies”, sparing them “the great expense of invaliding to England”. [n28; Gazetteer XIV, Tungar; n12; Gazetteer XIV, pp. 366–368]

The hill already had its own people and its own gods. The path to the old Tungareshvar temple, rebuilt about 1750 by Shankarji Keshav, passed through the Varli hamlet of Dhondvira, and the people of the hill were Kolis and Varlis. In 1872 there were 6,099 Varlis in Bassein taluka, against 45,330 in their Dahanu heartland. The Varlis of Bassein belonged to the Nihiri section, which did not intermarry with the other two. “Their love for the forests is so great”, the Gazetteer wrote, “that… they cannot be induced to go so far from their woods.” It noted too that Varlis who had pledged their land were employed in tillage in the rains, an early glimpse of the debt bondage that would bring them, eighty years later, into open revolt. [n10; Gazetteer XIV, pp. 367–368; n08; Gazetteer I, pp. 182–183]

In 1865 the Christians of Papdi, about two miles north-east of Bassein, built their church of Our Blessed Lady of Grace for Rs 18,000 raised by subscription. By 1881 it had a congregation of 1,294 and a government-supported parish school of 56 pupils with a violin master. More than a century later it would become the cathedral of the Diocese of Vasai. In 1869 the government built a mamlatdar’s office in the town for Rs 35,530. And local history records that a regular steamer service along the west coast began in 1869. [n11; Gazetteer XIV, p. 37; n32; Gazetteer XIV, p. 36; n05; Vasaiker]

The creek that dried

There was one more change in these years, and it was the slowest and the most final.

The “island of Bassein” had been an island because a backwater cut it off from the mainland. It left the Bassein creek above where the railway bridge now stood and wound north to the Vaitarna. People called its southern arm the Manikpur creek, its middle the Sopara creek and its northern arm the Bolinj creek. Before the Portuguese, the whole strip had been called the island of Sopara. [n11; n12; Gazetteer XIV, pp. 314–315]

That backwater had been silting for a long time. As early as 1728 a Portuguese report complained that it could be forded at low water for about 4,000 feet between Sopara and Bolinj, and warned that it would silt up level with the fields unless it was dug out. Nobody dug it out. Then the railway came. In 1882 the Gazetteer described the creek as silting fast, “especially since the long railway embankment has dammed the flow of water from the south”. A modern geographer, B. Arunachalam, writes that the line, built on a high embankment in the second half of the nineteenth century, “dismembered the channel”. [n12; Gazetteer XIV; n31; n02; Shah 2020, citing Arunachalam 2002]

By 1882 boats of ten or twelve tons could still come up from the south as far as Gass, about a mile from Sopara, and from the north as far as Bolinj, about two miles from Dedar, the old Sopara landing. From Gass, Bolinj and Vagholi, small craft still carried molasses, rice and betel leaf to Surat. “At Sopara”, the Gazetteer says simply, “the creek is dry.” [n11; Gazetteer XIV, pp. 315–316]

So the town that had once been the capital of Aparanta, the Suppara of the Greek sailors and the Subara of the Arab geographers, now sat less than a mile from a railway that did not stop for it, beside a creek that no longer reached it. It was still, in the Gazetteer’s words, “a rich country town with a crowded weekly market and a population of about 1700”, with a broad market place and houses of one and two storeys “with rich wood carving”. Its husbandmen were Christians, Naita Muslims descended from Arab and Persian traders, and Hindus, mostly Samvedi Brahmans and Bhandaris; its traders were Naitas and Lad Vanis whose families had come from Cambay about 1760. But in 1882 it was “somewhat badly off for communications”, and the Gazetteer thought “a station at Sopara… would be a boon to its people”. [n31; n11; Gazetteer XIV, pp. 314–316]

The island was joining the mainland, in mud and in iron. The sea that had made Sopara had left it, and the railway that would make the modern city had passed it by. Under Sopara’s fields, though, lay a brick mound that the villagers called Burud Rajache Kot, the fort of the Burud king. Within twenty years of the first train, a scholar from Junagadh would come up the line with a team of diggers and open it.
