Period covered: from the making of the island to November 2026

In this chapter. The oldest place in the book, from the island’s making to the suburb: the pond with Ashoka’s edict, the stupa at Mardes, the port and its merchants, and the Shilahara district.

The Nallasopara part of the book begins here. The main chapters (04, 05, 06 and those after) told Sopara’s story as one thread of the whole coast’s history. This chapter and the next turn round and look at the place itself: the island of Sopara, its creek, its ponds and fields and stones, and the people who lived on it, generation by generation. Where the main chapters have already told an episode at length, this one points back to them and adds what is particular to the ground.

A day’s walk in 1882

In 1882 the editors of the Gazetteer of the Bombay Presidency printed, inside their long entry on Sopara, something unusual for a government volume: a walking route. A visitor, they suggested, should take the train to Manikpur, the station the timetables called Bassein Road, and set out north-west on foot or by cart. The way crossed “a bare flat, formerly flooded during the rains and now being gradually reclaimed for rice”. To the north, across this waste, rose “the raised lands of Gas and Sopara, full of trees and sugarcane gardens”. To the west stood the long wooded hill of Nirmal, and nearer, a low knoll covered in bush and palms that the people called Brahma hill. [n42 §1; Gaz XIV, Sopara, “Objects of Interest”]

From Brahma hill, about four miles from the station, the route went on to Nirmal, then to a mound known as the “Burud-king’s tower”, then half a mile east into Sopara itself, then “about a mile south to Gas”, and finally “about three miles south-east to Manikpur”: about twelve miles in all, a long day. [n42 §1; Gaz XIV, Sopara section] Another version of the circuit sent the visitor back from Sopara by the “stone dam”, the dagdi bandh, a line of great basalt blocks laid across the mud, and home through the village of Chulna, about two miles west of the station. [n11 Q2; Gaz XIV p. 341]

The editors could describe the walk because people had walked it for them. The antiquarian part of the entry, they said in a footnote, was taken from Pandit Bhagwanlal Indraji’s paper “Antiquarian Remains at Sopara and Padana”, printed that same year in the journal of the Bombay branch of the Royal Asiatic Society, and they thanked three men for local help: Mr W. W. Loch, Mr Govind Moreshvar Pandit, and Mr Ramdas Kasidas Modi, “a native of Sopara”. [n42 §2; n11 Q2; Gaz XIV p. 314 n.] Modi is the first person in the whole record of this place who is named as belonging to it and who helped to write its history down. Nothing else is known of him; the notes found no other trace. [n25]

What the visitor saw from Brahma hill was described too. To the north lay “the rich gardens of Sopara and the rugged outline of Jivdhan”; to the east, “the level line of Tungar, ending to the south in the peak of Kamandurg”. [n46 §1; n12 §6; Gaz XIV, Sopara] Within the gardens the lanes ran “four or five feet below the level of the fields”, between high hedgerows, past round ponds and long winding lakes “fringed by high pile-supported Persian wheels”, past tiled houses with wooden walls set on high masonry plinths, plinths often built from older carved stones. [n11 Q1; Gaz XIV p. 315]

Those old stones in the plinths are the subject of this chapter. Sopara is a place where every age built with what the age before had left lying about. A Mauryan edict ended up in a field by a pond. A Buddhist relic mound became, in local speech, the castle of a forgotten king. Temple pillars went into Portuguese walls at Bassein. A Pali-inscribed block served a Gass housewife as a washing stone. A three-headed Brahma, dug out of a field, was worshipped as Dattatraya. A pond where the Portuguese kept a tower and a landing became a Muslim burial ground, then a find-spot for archaeologists, then a name on a map of a megacity suburb. To tell Sopara’s story honestly means walking the same ground again and again, each time in a later century.

The island and its creek

A garden island that was not quite an island

The ground first. The deep history of how the lava was laid down, how the sea rose and fell over it, and how the first people came to this coast belongs to Chapters 01 to 03b. What matters here is the shape the land had taken by the time people built a town on it.

The Gazetteer of 1882 explained it best. Between the Bassein creek, the mouth of the Ulhas river, in the south, and the Vaitarna river in the north ran “the strip of garden land about three miles broad”. Its middle part, “the richly wooded well-watered and highly tilled tract, including Bolinj, Koprad and Umbrala in the north, Nirmal and Vagholi in the west, Gas in the south, and Sopara and Mardesh in the centre, is locally known as the garden or agar of Sopara”. North of it lay the agar of Agashi, south of it the agar of Bassein. “Under the Portuguese the whole of this rich strip, with its patches of salt marsh, was known as the island of Bassein, and, before Portuguese times, as the island of Sopara.” [n11 Q1; n12 §1; Gaz XIV pp. 314–315]

It was an island because “a backwater which leaves the Bassein creek a little above the railway bridge and winds north to the Vaitarna” cut it off from the mainland. That backwater “used to be called the Sopara creek”. By 1882 it had three local names along its length: the Manikpur creek in the south, the Sopara creek in the centre and the Bolinj creek in the north. “Even from the south side,” another volume of the Gazetteer adds, “the whole backwater is still, from the ancient trade centre, known as the Sopara creek.” [n48 §1, §2; Gaz XIV pp. 314–315; Gaz XIII-1, general description]

Why a town here? The Gazetteer’s answer is one of its best sentences. “Its height above the surrounding salt marsh, its rich soil and fresh water, and its nearness to the holy Vaitarna, Jivdhan, Tungar and Nirmal must early have made Sopara a chosen settlement. Its distance from the coast and its broad backwater guarded it from pirate raids or the forays of the wild hill and forest tribes, and its water connection with the Vaitarna on the north and the Ulhas or Bassein river on the south … made it an early centre of trade.” [n37 §6; Gaz XIV p. 316] Another footnote adds a land road: along the Tansa valley, between the Vaitarna and the Ulhas, Pelhar and Mandvi “were once places of importance”, and at Bhatane, a landing north of Tungar, were “a reservoir of dressed stones and quantities of old bricks”. The Gazetteer gave the Tansa route as one more reason Sopara became a centre of trade. That rests on a single footnote, from information given by Loch. [n12 §7; Gaz XIV, Sopara fn.]

The sea was higher

The island was wetter in Sopara’s first age than in 1882. The study of the port by Monish Shah, Siddharth Kale and Jainacharya Bhagyayashsuri (2020), drawing on the geographer B. Arunachalam, puts the sea about half a metre higher around the second and third centuries BCE. [n02 §5; Shah 2020] One radiocarbon date exists from near Nala Sopara: shells from a beach-dune rock called “Karal”, dated to 3250 ± 110 years before the present. The sample’s context is not stated in the notes, and it is a marine date, not a cultural one; it says the shore was there, not that people were. [n24 §1; n42 §1; UNESCO-hosted paper, via search summary] The study by Sila Tripati and A. S. Gaur of the National Institute of Oceanography found that in the Early Historical period Sopara faced Agashi island to the north and Bassein to the south, with anchorages in the backwaters, and that creek navigation continued into the nineteenth century with vessels of about twenty tonnes. [n02 §5; Wikipedia: Sopara, citing Tripati & Gaur 1997]

The silting, in four readings

The creek’s slow death can be read in four dated observations, spread over seven centuries.

In 1153 the Arab geographer al-Idrisi wrote that “Subara” was “situated one-and-a-half mile from the sea”. [n36 §3; E&D I p. 85] That is the first measurement anyone made of the distance between the town and the water, and it shows that by the twelfth century Sopara was already an inland market rather than a harbour on the open shore.

In 1728 a Portuguese officer reporting on the defences complained that the creek between Sopara and Bolinj could be forded at low water for about 4,000 feet, and asked that it be dug out “as deep as the height of a man, otherwise it would soon be filled to a level with the fields, because of the great amount of silt that was brought by the river … from its two mouths”. [n36 §6; n12 §1; Gaz XIV p. 322]

In 1882 the Gazetteer found that the backwater “was once a considerable arm of the sea overflowing a large breadth of land on both banks”, but that “with the mud-laden Vaitarna on the north and the Bassein creek or Ulhas river on the south, silting has been rapid, especially since the long railway embankment has dammed the flow of water from the south”. Boats of ten to twelve tons still came up from the south as far as Gas, and from the north as far as Bolinj. “At Sopara the creek is dry.” Teak rafts had once floated down to the Sopara landing, but “for many years boats have been unable to pass beyond Bolinj”. [n36 §6; n40 A11; Gaz XIV pp. 314–315, 338]

And in 2002 Arunachalam, as summarised by the Shah study, described siltation over about two thousand years, sped up by colonial deforestation inland and by a railway on a high embankment that “dismembered the channel” in the second half of the nineteenth century. Pia Brancaccio, writing in 2023, confirms that the old lagoon “has been filled in by river deposits” and is now mudflats encroached by settlement. [n02 §5; n36 §6; Shah 2020; Brancaccio 2023 p. 11]

Put together, the 1728 report and the 1882 description make a century-and-a-half time series of a silting channel, and the railway embankment is named in a government volume as one of the causes eighteen years after the line was built. [n36 §6 inferences; n31 §2] The Gazetteer also recorded where the stone for the line had come from. On the steep west face of Tulinj hill, which local people called Rakshi Dongar, about a mile north of Achole, stood “a fine basalt dyke”, dark, glistening with mica, ringing “like an anvil” when struck, with six-sided pillars six to twenty-two feet long. “Many of the pillars were used when the railway was made,” and people were still carrying them away. [n31 §2; n12 §3; Gaz XIV] So the railway came to Sopara twice over: once as an embankment that strangled its creek, and once as a quarry on its eastern hill.

The landings

In 1882 three landings served Sopara’s trade by water: Gas, about a mile south of the town; Bolinj, about two miles north of Dedar, “the old Sopara landing”; and Vagholi, from which small boats came “from the west as near as … about two miles”. Molasses, rice and betel leaf went from these three places by sea to Surat, Broach, Jambusar, Dholera, Bhavnagar and Gogha. [n31 §2; n37 §6; n40 A7; Gaz XIV pp. 315–316] The French traveller Anquetil du Perron, who passed in 1760, noticed Vagholi “with a creek of its own”. [n11 Q2; Gaz XIV p. 322] East of Gass, the Shah study reports, a Portuguese landing place has been found. [n02 §6; Shah 2020] A “Kutchi sea manual” of the seventeenth century, the same study says, records a regular cargo route through the Bolinj–Sopara creek. [n02 §5; Shah 2020] Of Dedar, the old landing itself, the notes know nothing beyond its name.

The names of the place

The town has had more names than most cities. The Sanskrit epics and Puranas call it Shurparaka or Surparaka; the Pali texts Supparaka; the Jains Soparaka; Ptolemy, in the second century CE, Soupara; the author of the Periplus, Suppara; the Arab geographers Subara, Subara and Sufara; Friar Jordanus in 1322 Supera; a Gujarat notice of 1500 “Sorab”; the Portuguese Supara and Sopará; the British Supara and Sopara. [n31 §1; n36 §3; n03; Gaz XIV p. 322 n. 3] A Nashik cave inscription spells it Sorparaga, a Naneghat cistern Soparaya, Kanheri Soparaka. [n02 §4; n37 §1] Aggregator sites add Sururaka, Sopare, Sopra, Shurparak Nagari; “Subara”, sometimes quoted, did not turn up in any source the research could check. [n31 §1; n22]

What the name meant is a matter of guesses. Gerson da Cunha in 1876 gave the German scholar Lassen’s reading of Surparaka as “fine shore”, or a derivation from surpa, “winnow”, for the winnow-shaped strip of coast that Parashurama won from the sea. [n37 §6; da Cunha pp. 169–170] Wikipedia glosses it, loosely, as “city of braves”. [n22] A tradition ties the name to the Bodhisattva Supparaka of the Jataka, but that is no settled etymology. [n22] Wikipedia calls the city’s official name today “Shurparak Nagar” and gives no source for it; the notes could not find who coined it. [n31 §1, Gaps]

The name was not only a word. The Gazetteer’s own phrase for Sopara’s antiquity was that it had been “the capital of the Konkan from about B.C. 1500 to A.D. 1300”, a loose formula that mixes the dates of legend with those of record. [n31 §1; n37 §6; Gaz XIV p. 314] The record begins with Ashoka, in the third century BCE. Everything before is epic: Parashurama shooting his arrow into the sea and reclaiming the land, Arjuna and the Pandavas resting at holy Surparaka, the Harivansh, which the Gazetteer itself called “probably modern”. [n37 §1; n02 §2; Gaz XIV pp. 316–317] The Biblical identification with Ophir, the land of gold to which Solomon’s ships sailed, rests on a likeness between the Septuagint’s “Sophir” and Ptolemy’s “Soupara”, and has no archaeological support; Chapter 04 weighs it. [n02 §3; n36 §9]

How “Sopara” came to have “Nala” in front of it is a story of the railway age, and belongs to the next chapter. Here it is enough to note that a village called Nala, or Nila, was there long before the railway, a mile north of Sopara, and that its own stones and records appear at their places below.

Ashoka by the pond

Two fragments

The first datable words on this coast come from Sopara. Chapter 04 tells their story in full; the outline is enough here. In 1882 Bhagwanlal Indraji found “a broken slab of basalt bearing parts of some lines of Rock Edict VIII” near the Bhatela pond, east of the old town. On 1 January 1956 a schoolboy at Bhuigaon, a village west of Nirmal, found a second slab, holding a little over half of Rock Edict IX, and brought it to N. A. Gore, librarian of the Bombay Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society. The epigraphist D. C. Sircar copied it at the Prince of Wales Museum in January 1957. Both slabs are now in that museum, renamed the Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj Vastu Sangrahalaya. [n02 §1; Sircar EI 32; Shah 2020; IAR 1955–56 via n24] Sircar concluded that a complete set of Ashoka’s fourteen Rock Edicts “must have originally existed near Sopara”, cut in groups on separate slabs. [n02 §1; Sircar EI 32]

Two pieces of a set of fourteen. If Sircar was right, the other twelve or so slabs were somewhere on the island, and may still be: in a field, in a plinth, in a pond, under a road. One Wikipedia account places the 1882 find “in an old Muslim graveyard near Ramkund”; Sircar’s primary publication says near the Bhatela pond, and this book follows Sircar. [n02 §1; n22] It happens that both may describe the same spot, because the Bhatela pond had Muslim graves on its banks in 1882, as will appear.

The Bhatela pond through time

The Bhatela pond deserves a short biography of its own, because it is one of the few places on the island where four ages can be seen at once.

The Gazetteer of 1882 measured it at 240 by 146 yards. It lay about fifty yards east of the Khare or Shirmoli pond, which was itself just inside the limits of Gas, south-east of a “Musalman pond” at the southern edge of Sopara. On its north and west banks were Muslim graves. [n31 §2; n42 §1; Gaz XIV, Sopara section, near p. 340] Near it was “the site of a Portuguese tower, an old landing-place”. [n31 §2; Gaz XIV] Wikipedia, and the Shah study, report remains of a Portuguese jetty and customs house near the pond. [n02 §6; n31 §3; Wikipedia: Sopara] And from beside it, in 1882, came the Ashokan slab.

So the Bhatela pond holds, in its banks and bed, a Mauryan edict of the third century BCE, the landing and tower of the Portuguese, perhaps with their customs post, the graves of the island’s Muslims, and in 1882 the excavation of an Indian archaeologist working for a British journal. What the pond looks like in 2026, the notes do not say. A Marathi report of 15 November 2026 warned that Vasai-Virar’s historic lakes were choked with garbage and water hyacinth, but it names no single pond. [n06 §4; eSakal, 15 Nov 2026]

The stupa at Mardes

“The fort of the Burud king”

Half a mile east of Sopara, in the village of Mardes (Mardesh, Madres), stood a mound that the people called Burud Rajacha Kot, “the fort of the Burud king”. By the nineteenth century it had a legend of a lost palace. [n18; n25 §“Burud Raja”; n02 §1] Who the Burud king was supposed to be, the notes do not say, and the legend itself is not recorded in any detail. What is recorded is that the mound turned out to be a stupa, a solid Buddhist relic mound, first built in the Mauryan period, enlarged between the first century BCE and the third century CE, and expanded again in the Rashtrakuta period. [n02 §1; LiveHistoryIndia]

The Gazetteer’s walking route treated it as a landmark between Nirmal and Sopara, and the heritage survey of 2008 lists the stupa among the landmarks of the Madres gram panchayat, with the Madres Talav beside it, “where local Catholic communities gather around the cross for prayers and ritual processions” at Christmas and the feast of the Nativity. [n40 A8; CRIT Vol I items 70–71] That small fact is a picture of the island in one frame: a Mauryan Buddhist mound, a pond, and Kupari Catholics in procession round a cross at its edge.

April 1882, and after

Chapter 04 describes what Indraji found when he opened the mound in April 1882 (some sources say February): a brick chamber; a great stone coffer; nested caskets of copper, silver, stone, crystal and gold; gold flowers; scented powder; jewellery; “fragments of a begging bowl believed to have been used by Gautama Buddha”; a silver coin; and eight bronzes, seven Buddhas and Maitreya, some thousand years younger than the mound’s Mauryan core. [n02 §1; Asiatic Society of Mumbai; Shah 2020] The coin’s attribution is argued over: Gautamiputra Satakarni, Yajnashri Satakarni, the Gazetteer’s combined “Gotamiputra Yajnashri Shatakarni”, or, in Brancaccio’s 2023 reading, a Kshatrapa imitation of a Satavahana coin, which would put the sealing of the relics in the first century CE. [n02 §1; n37 §1; n36 §1; Brancaccio 2023] The Gazetteer drew one lasting conclusion: that Sopara “was then a Buddhist religious centre, and had skilful masons, coppersmiths, and goldsmiths”. [n37 §1; Gaz XIV p. 320]

A tradition Indraji recorded at Sopara adds a story the mound does not tell. The Buddha, it said, converted a Brahmin sage named Vakkali and five hundred widows, and gave them relics of his nails and hair, over which they built “the Widows’ Stupa”. [n02 §2; Shah 2020] It is a local legend, set down in 1882, and nothing ties it to the excavated mound.

The relics went to the Asiatic Society of Bombay, now of Mumbai, which keeps them still; the stone coffer stands in its vestibule. [n02 §1] The mound was dug again in 1939–40 by M. M. Qureshi of the Archaeological Survey, who found stone lintels, two small stupas on its south side and glazed sherds of the Muslim period. [n02 §1; Shah 2020, citing Tripati & Gaur 1997] More than ten votive stupas lie beside the main mound, some brought to light in more recent digging by M. S. Chauhan, along with at least four kichaka (bracket-figure) carvings that point to a temple nearby. No date or report for Chauhan’s work was found. [n02 §6; Shah 2020] On 6 January 2012 workers digging a garden near the stupa uncovered a pillar carved with a Buddha, and the digging was stopped. [n02 §6; TOI via Kerala Mahabodhi blog]

The credit for the 1882 dig is itself not quite settled: a 1910 edition of Fergusson’s history of architecture names James M. Campbell, editor of the Gazetteer, as the excavator. Every other source names Indraji. [n25; Fergusson 1910 via Heidelberg] What happened to the mound after 1940, and what it looks like now, belongs to the end of this chapter.

The port and its merchants

A feeder of the western sea

Chapter 04 follows Sopara’s first great age in detail, and Chapter 39 follows its trade across every generation. The essential shape is this. In the first century CE the Greek Periplus of the Erythraean Sea named “Suppara” as the first market-town south of Barygaza (Bharuch), before “the city of Calliena” (Kalyan), and recorded that under a ruler called Sandanes the coast was restricted and Greek ships landing there were sent under guard to Barygaza. Ptolemy, in the next century, named “Soupara” among the trading towns of the coast. [n02 §3; Periplus, Schoff; Thapar via Wikipedia] The 1993 excavation by the Archaeological Survey with the British Academy found lead and copper coins of the Satavahana and Kshatrapa periods, beads of semi-precious stone, sherds of Northern Black Polished Ware, pieces of Roman amphorae and Islamic blue glazed ware, an earthen wall and a coarse stone wall. [n02 §3; Shah 2020, citing Tripati & Gaur 1997] Goods named in the secondary sources include ivory, shells, silk, elephants and horses; the islands and creeks sheltered the harbour, and local boats piloted ships in. [n02 §3; LiveHistoryIndia] Roads ran from Sopara by Kalyan up to Nashik, Paithan and Ter in the Deccan. [n02 §3; Shah 2020]

The donors of Sopara

Sopara’s merchants are known best not from Sopara but from the caves up the passes, where they paid for cisterns, pillars and rest-houses and had their names cut in the rock. The Kshatrapa governor Ushavadata, son-in-law of Nahapana, recorded at Nashik that he had built a rest-house with four doors and four verandahs at “Sorparaga”, and given coconut trees there. [n02 §4; n18; Shah 2020] At Karle a first-century inscription records the gift of Satimita “of Sopara”, nephew of the monk Dhammutariya. At Naneghat a cistern was cut by Govindadasa “of Soparaya”. At Kanheri a cistern was given by Samika, a merchant of Soparaka, and a jeweller, Nagapalita of Surparaka, appears among the donors. [n02 §4; n36 index; n37 §1] These are the first people of Sopara whose names survive. All of them appear as givers to Buddhist foundations, which is why the Gazetteer and later writers read the town’s early Buddhism as a merchants’ faith, spread by money and prestige along the trade roads. [n37 §1 inferences]

Buddhist tradition supplied a merchant hero to match. The Divyavadana’s tale of Purna, son of a rich Sopara merchant by a slave girl, who went to sea, met the Buddha at Shravasti, built a hall of sandalwood near Surparaka and invited the Buddha, who came “flying through the sky” with five hundred monks, is told in Chapter 04. It is legend, recorded some two thousand years ago; there is no evidence that the Buddha came to Sopara. [n02 §2; n37 §1; 84000; LiveHistoryIndia] A Pali version sets the sandalwood hall in “Sunaparanta”. A painting of the story at Ajanta, in Cave 2, is dated to the fifth century CE. [n02 §2]

Horses, shoes and pearls

The port’s second life came in the centuries after 800, under the Rashtrakuta overlords and the Shilahara kings, and Chapter 05 tells it. Its texture is worth gathering here, because almost all of it concerns Sopara itself.

A Jain novel, the Kuvalayamala of 778 CE, tells of a horse merchant named Lobhadeva from Taxila who came to Sopara to sell horses, and of a local shreshthin, a guild head or banker, who helped him sell them and grow rich. “Sopparaya traders had their own clubs and customs,” the study that summarises it reports, and foreign traders had to share their adventures; the novel describes eighteen breeds of horses, pearl fisheries and a large population. It is a literary source. [n02 §2; n36 §3; Shah 2020, citing Upadhye]

The Arab geographers named the town again and again. Al-Masudi, who visited in 915, put “Subara” with Saimur and Tana as coast towns where a language called Larya was spoken, in the kingdom of the Balhara, the Rashtrakuta king, where “Islam is honoured and protected”. Al-Istakhri, about 951, listed Subara among “the cities of Hind” and said there were Muslims and many mosques in the Balhara’s coast towns. Ibn Haukal, about 970, put Subara among the Indian towns bordering Sind. Al-Biruni, about 1030, gave its distance from Sindan and Tana. [n36 §3; E&D I pp. 24, 34, 66] And al-Idrisi, in 1153: “Subura is situated one-and-a-half mile from the sea. It is a populous, busy town, and is considered one of the entrepots of India. They fish for pearls here. It is in the vicinity of Bara, a small island, on which some cocoa-nut trees and the costus grow.” [n36 §3; E&D I p. 85] The Gazetteer’s notes suggest that Bara, the island of coconuts, was the Sopara–Bassein island itself; that is its reading, not Idrisi’s. [n03]

From the Arab sources the Gazetteer drew a list of the coast’s trade in the Shilahara centuries that names Sopara twice: “a large manufacture of laced shoes in Sopara and Sanjan, and a great export of excellent leather, chiefly to Arabia”, and pearls fished in the creeks near Sopara. Horses came in by the thousand. Dates, Chinese porcelain, copper, cowries, African gold dust and iron, Malayan lead and tin and Indonesian spices were among the imports. [n36 §3; Gaz XIII-II pp. 430–431] The merchants, in the Gazetteer’s summary, were “local Hindu, Musalman, and Parsi traders, and Hindus and Musalmans from Gujarat and from the Malabar coast … foreign Persians and Arabs, Jews, Europeans, and perhaps a chance Chinaman”, with Gujarati the trade tongue and Gujarat Vanis probably the leading traders. That last point is the Gazetteer’s inference from the Lar language, not a record. [n36 §3; Gaz XIII-II pp. 431–432]

One dated document names Sopara’s shipping by its owners. In 1094 the Shilahara king Anantadeva granted “exemption from tolls, at the ports of Sristhanaka [Thana], Nagapura, Surpparaka, and Chemuli” to ships belonging to two brothers, Bhabhana and Dhanama, who were his ministers. [n36 §3; Indraji 1882 p. 278] It is the clearest customs record the island has, and it shows that the king’s ministers were also shipowners. What rate of toll the brothers escaped, no source says. [n36 §3 Gaps]

The silent centuries

Between the two bright ages lies a dark one. Tripati and Gaur found Sopara a main entrepôt “from the pre-Ashokan period to the 3rd century CE and again 9th–13th century CE”, with “no cultural remains from 4th to 9th centuries”. [n02 §5; Wikipedia, citing Tripati & Gaur 1997] Chapter 05 describes what the neighbours were doing in those centuries: the monks of Kanheri carrying on, the Konkan Mauryas ruling from Puri, the Christians of the bishopric at Kalyan, the first Arab sails. The Gazetteer’s guess that Nestorian refugees from Persia strengthened the Christian settlements “at Kalyan and Sopara” in the fifth century is a hedged inference, not a record of Sopara. [n03; n37 §1; Gaz XIII-II p. 432 n. 4] The bronzes inside the stupa show that someone was still repairing and re-consecrating the mound in the seventh to ninth centuries. [n02 §1] Beyond that, for some five hundred years, the island’s own ground has given up nothing that can be dated. That silence may be an accident of where people have dug. It cannot be filled with invention.

The Shilahara district of Sopara

The sixty-six villages

When the record resumes, Sopara is the centre of a district. The Shilahara kings of the North Konkan, who ruled about 1,400 villages from about 800 to about 1260, named a territory called the Shurparaka-shatshashti, “the Shurparaka sixty-six”, a district of sixty-six villages round Sopara. [n03; n02 §4] The copper plates of Mummuniraja, dated by an eclipse to 20 February 1049 and dug up at Thana in 1956, gave produce from five villages in it to Brahmins: the greater and lesser Adane, Khanuvada, Pedhala (Pelhar) and Virara (Virar), with Uppalapallika, a hamlet of Virar. Chapter 27 opens with those plates. [n03; n46 §4; CII VI pp. 115–116] The district reached that far east and north. The city itself, in the Shilahara centuries, covered present-day Sopara, Bolinj, Nirmal and Gass, according to LiveHistoryIndia; that is a modern writer’s summary, not a medieval boundary. [n02 §4]

Customs were levied at Sopara, Thana, Chaul and perhaps Nagothna. The coin was the dramma. A king’s highway ran roughly on the line of the later Bombay–Thana road. [n03]

Stones in the town

Three Shilahara stones bring the district down to the streets of the town. A stone of 1149 of King Haripaladeva was found built into a house plinth at Sopara. [n03; TG-II p. 426 n. 3] Another, dated Shak 1072, AD 1150, found at Agashi and kept in the Collector’s garden at Thana, records that a prince “enjoying the village of Vattaraka in Shurparaka” gave land to a Brahmin, witnessed by “Risi Mhatara, headman of Vattaraka village, Naguji Mhatara, Ananta Nayaka, and Changdev Mhatara”. The Gazetteer identified Vattaraka as “the modern Vatargaon, two miles north-west of Sopara”. [n40 A13; n46 §4; Gaz XIV p. 385 and n. 1] And from a well at Vagholi, about a mile west of Sopara, came a stone of fourteen lines, all illegible, which was also carried off to the Collector’s garden. [n11 Q2; Gaz XIV pp. 342, 419]

These stones are worth a moment. The headmen of Vatar in 1150 bore the name Mhatara, the same name the Gazetteer wrote in 1882 for a Gass householder, Degu Pobria Mhatara, and the name that survives as Mhatre in the villages. The research notes point out the match and say plainly that no source tests it. [n40 A13 inferences; n42 §2] It is one of the few threads that could, in principle, tie a family of the island across seven centuries, and it is still only a resemblance.

A scholar sent to Kashmir

In the twelfth century Sopara also sent a man abroad on the king’s business. The Kashmiri poet Mankha, in his Shrikanthacharita, records that the Shilahara king Aparaditya sent Tejakantha of Shurparaka as his delegate to a literary congress in Kashmir, between about 1135 and 1145. [n03; n36 §3; Indraji 1882 p. 278 n. 8] Nothing is known of Tejakantha except this journey. It is enough to show that twelfth-century Sopara had learned men whom a king could send to the far north to speak for his court.

Was Sopara the capital?

The Shilaharas ruled from a capital called Puri, and nobody knows where Puri was. Chapter 05 weighs the candidates. The Places of Interest volume of the Gazetteer thought “the site of the Silhara capital seems to have been between Bassein and Agashi. Puri may therefore have been Sopara, to which a Bakhar of the eighteenth century seems to refer as Puri.” The History volume of the same Gazetteer preferred Gharapuri, on Elephanta island. The two volumes of one work disagree, and the question is still open. [n03; TG-XIV p. 355 n. 5; TG-II p. 423 n.] A third candidate, a place “said to be Burhanpur” about 500 yards south-west of Manikpur station, named in a Maratha chronicle of the 1738 siege, showed “no signs of old remains”. [n11 Q2; Gaz XIV p. 402]

Chakreshwar: the lake and its gods

The lake in 1882

About 550 yards north-east of the stupa mound lies the lake of Chakreshwar. In 1882 it measured about 180 by 120 yards, shallow and never lined with masonry except for a central cistern. Midway along its west bank stood “the mean modern temple of Chakreshvar”, with a modern lamp pillar, a rest-house and a shrine of Maruti. [n10 §1.3; Gaz XIV pp. 336–337] Even then, in other words, the temple was new. What made the place remarkable was the collection of old stones gathered round it.

The Gazetteer described them one by one. A headless bull, with a carved chain-and-bell necklace, dug from a well in the Gass field called Sonarbhat, “probably of about the same age (A.D. 1060) as the Ambarnath temple”. An eleventh-century Surya riding seven horses. A stone of Shiva and Parvati with Ganpati and Kartikeya. A slab with Shitala Devi and a small Jain Chandraprabha, the eighth Tirthankara. A sati memorial stone. A hero stone in three panels. In a hollow of the great banyan, forty-eight feet round, a six-inch naked Parshvanath under a five-hooded snake, “probably of the tenth century or earlier”. [n10 §1.3; n03; Gaz XIV pp. 337–338]

And the chief piece: a Brahma, six feet four inches tall, “a full length three-headed figure with four arms… The front face has a pointed rishi-like beard.” He holds a ladle, a rosary, the Veda and an oil flask, and his vehicle, the swan, is at his feet. He was “probably carved in the eleventh or twelfth century”, and had been “found about thirty years ago [c. 1850] buried in a field in Sonarbhat”. And then a sentence that sums up the island: “It is still worshipped as Dattatraya.” [n10 §1.3; Gaz XIV pp. 337–338]

There is a small conflict in the Gazetteer over where the Brahma was found. The Chakreshwar passage says a field in Sonarbhat. The description of the ponds says that about fifty yards south of the Khare or Shirmoli pond, inside Gass limits, “was found the statue of Brahma now near the Chakreshvar temple”. [n10 §1.3; n42 §1] Both places are in or by Gass, and the two statements may describe one spot by different landmarks. They cannot be reconciled further from the notes.

What the stones say, and where the temples went

The sculptures are Shilahara-age work of the eleventh and twelfth centuries, with a Jain image perhaps older. Taken together with the eleventh- and twelfth-century temple foundations by the Gass lake, the sun-door stone in Sonarbhat and the carved stones in the plinths of Sopara houses, they say that the island once had stone temples of some richness, Shaiva, Vaishnava, solar and Jain side by side. [n03; n11 Q2; Gaz XIV pp. 336–341] The research notes call them the only hard medieval evidence at any of the island’s sacred sites; everything else standing is eighteenth-century Maratha rebuilding or later. [n03]

Where did the temples go? The Gazetteer gives two answers of unequal weight. “Of stone temples … many are said to have been destroyed by the Musalmans in the beginning of the fourteenth century.” That carries a “said to”, and the footnote it rests on, Jordanus’s Mirabilia, does not obviously support it; it is a tradition. The second answer is firmer: “enough were spared, or repaired, to excite the admiration” of the first Portuguese in the 1530s, and the real stripping came “during the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, [when] the lake banks were stripped and the temples pulled down, and the stones used in building Portuguese churches, forts, and houses” and “the great walls … of Bassein”. [n37 §1; n36 §6; Gaz XIV p. 323] The walls of Bassein fort, which Chapter 07 describes going up from 1536, are in part made of Sopara.

The legends of the lake

Local tradition has its own story of the images. One version says the Portuguese threw the idols into the lake, and they were recovered later; another says the people themselves hid them there to save them. Hindu belief holds that Krishna’s discus, the chakra, dug the tank, which gives it its name. Jain belief links the tank with Chakreshvari, the guardian goddess (yakshini) of Adinath. [n10 §1.3; Inditales; Heritage Chronicles 2014] All of these are traditions. What is certain is that Hindu and Jain sculptures were in fact recovered from around the same water, so the tank’s double memory has a basis in the stones. [n10 §3 inferences]

Chakreshwar now

The present temple has been rebuilt. Inditales says “most historians” date the building to the nineteenth century; a Wikipedia-derived snippet gives the thirteenth to eighteenth; the Gazetteer had already called the main shrine “modern” in 1882. [n10 §1.3] The Shah study found the sculptures kept at a Shri Ram temple that is itself less than a hundred years old, with an old temple plinth alongside, and listed the collection: the bearded three-headed Brahma, a rare Agni riding a ram, Mahishasuramardini, Surya, Ganesha, Uma-Maheshvara, Harihara, Gajalakshmi, a Jain Ambika, Nandi, memorial stones and surasundaris, celestial women. [n02 §6; Shah 2020] Beside the temple stands the Akkalkot Swami Math, which holds the samadhi of Swami Mayuranand; Swami Samarth of Akkalkot is believed to have stayed there, which is tradition. A jogging track now rings the lake. [n10 §1.3; Inditales; Heritage Chronicles 2014] The Wikipedia page on Nallasopara counts Chakreshwar lake as one of the sights of Nallasopara West. [n31 §3]

So the Shilahara Brahma that a Gass farmer’s plough or spade turned up around 1850 now stands by a lake where people jog in the evening, a few hundred yards from a Mauryan relic mound, inside the limits of a municipal corporation of more than a million people. It is the clearest single image of what this chapter means by Sopara through the ages.

The Jain tirtha of Soparaka

Among the eighty-four

For the Jains, Sopara was holy ground. The Gazetteer of 1882 put it plainly: “Jinaprabhasuri mentions Sopara (Soparaka) as one of the eighty-four sacred places of the Jains, and notices that while he was still alive Sopara had an image of Rishabhadev the first Tirthankar. One of the eighty-four gachchhas or Jain sects is called after Sopara.” [n10 §3; n15; n37 §1; Gaz XIV p. 319] Jinaprabhasuri wrote his Vividha Tirtha Kalpa in the fourteenth century. The Shah study adds that the image of Adinath, Rishabhadeva, was believed to be a jivitswami image, one made in the Tirthankara’s lifetime, and that a Shurparaka or “Sopariya” gachha was still active in 1953, citing P. K. Gode. [n02 §2; Shah 2020] A gachha is a lineage of monks; that one should carry Sopara’s name across six or seven centuries, long after the town had shrunk to a market village, is one of the quieter marks the place has left on the wider world.

Jain tradition

Jain tradition tells more, and the research notes keep it apart from evidence. Acharya Yakshadevsuri is said to have held a recitation of the Agamas at Sopara after a twelve-year famine, in Vira Samvat 585, about 58 CE. Pethad Shah, a minister who lived from 1223 to 1283, is said to have built a temple of Parshvanath at Sopara, the fifty-first of his eighty-four. Sopara is called the earliest taleti, the foot-shrine, of the holy mountain Shatrunjaya in Gujarat. The Mauryan prince Samprati is said to have patronised the Jains here, and the merchant-ministers Vastupal and Tejpal to have been born here. [n02 §2; n10 §3; Shah 2020; Inditales] The Shripalcharit tells that the mythical king Shripal married Tilakasundari, daughter of King Mahasena of Soparaka; the Gazetteer recorded that too. [n15; n10 §3; Gaz XIV p. 319] The Paumacariyam has Lava and Kusha conquering the region. [n10 §3] All of this is tradition, and the notes are careful to say that the Samprati and Vastupal–Tejpal claims rest on Jain tradition alone. [n02 §2 Gaps]

Jain stones

The evidence is the stones. The Parshvanath in the banyan at Chakreshwar, “probably of the tenth century or earlier”, and the Chandraprabha on the eleventh-century slab are the oldest Jain images on the island. [n10 §3; Gaz XIV pp. 337–338] At Gass, in the field called Sonarbhat, the Gazetteer saw a broken cross-legged female figure, perhaps Ambika, and the remains of a temple said to be of Parasnath. [n11 Q2; Gaz XIV p. 341] A Surya image from Gass is dated 1412 CE, and seven inscribed Tirthankara bronzes dated between 1458 and 1555 are held by the Agashi (Chalpeth) Jain Sangh. [n02 §2; Shah 2020] In 2019 Siddharth Kale, Monish Shah and two Jain monks found inscriptions of the thirteenth to sixteenth centuries showing a Jain clan of the Modha caste (nyati) at Sopara. [n03; Dalal, LiveHistoryIndia 2019] Some Gass finds are held by the University of Mumbai, others by local temples. [n02 §6; Shah 2020]

The image that rose from the lake

The living Jain centre of the old island is not at Sopara but at Agashi, at Chalpeth, and its founding story points back to Chakreshwar. The 1882 Gazetteer said the Jain temple at Agashi was dedicated to Parasnath and “was built about sixty years ago by Motilal a Vani of Bombay”, that is, about the 1820s. Today its main image is of Munisuvrat Swami, the twentieth Tirthankara, and temple accounts say the image rose out of a pond at Chakreshwar, or out of “the lake of Nalasopara”, about two hundred years ago. In one version a sea merchant, Moti Shah, vowed a temple where his lost ships returned, and they reappeared at Sopara; in another, rival congregations disputed the image until a “bullock-less bullock cart” carried it some nine kilometres to Chalpeth. [n10 §3; n08 §7; n49; jainmandir.org; Indianetzone; Inditales] These are temple traditions. The discrepancy between the Gazetteer’s Parasnath and today’s Munisuvrat Swami may mean the temple was rededicated, or that the image came later; the notes cannot say which. [n10 §3 inferences]

In Nallasopara East today, a Shri Munisuvrat Swami Jain Derasar and Upashray stands on Tulinj Road, at an address given as Shripal Complex; the name echoes the king who married the princess of Soparaka in the old story, though no source says the echo is deliberate. No history of the derasar was found. [n10 §3; n08 §7; jainmandir.org] The Jains of modern Nallasopara are mostly Gujarati and Marwari families of the recent decades, and they belong to the next chapter. [n08 §7]

The Yadava stone from Nila, and the land-grant stones

Nila enters the record

The village that would one day lend half its name to a suburb first appears on a stone. Among the inscribed stones the Gazetteer listed in 1882 was one “brought from Nila about a mile north of Sopara”. It named the Yadava king Ramchandradev, whom the Gazetteer dated to AD 1280–1298 by its reading of the stone (“Shak 1202 or 1220”), and it named Shurparak twice. [n40 A15; Gaz XIV p. 387] The Yadavas of Devagiri had replaced the Shilaharas as overlords of the North Konkan in the 1260s, as Chapter 05 describes; this stone is Sopara’s own witness to their rule.

Two things about the Nila stone matter for this book. First, it means that a place called Nila stood about a mile north of Sopara in the 1880s and had a carved stone worth carrying away. Second, it shows that the name Shurparaka was still in official use under the Yadavas, late in the thirteenth century. An earlier research note, searching the Gazetteer for a village called “Nala”, found nothing and concluded there was no such place in 1882; a later and fuller check found Nila on this stone, and Nala in the Gazetteer’s tables of trade and crops, as the section on the village later in this chapter shows. [n31 §1, §2; n40 A15]

Sun, moon, cow and calf

Medieval land grants on this coast were often marked by a particular kind of stone: a slab carved with the sun and the moon, meaning that the grant should last as long as they did, and sometimes with a cow and calf, a sign of the gift’s sanctity, and a curse on anyone who broke it. The Gazetteer found one near Sopara dated AD 1351, near Pada, a hamlet of Sondavli. [n31 §2; n42 §3; Gaz XIV pp. 313–340] It drew from it a historical point: “after their first conquest in 1290, the Muhammadans left to the local chief the right of making land grants.” [n42 §3; Gaz XIV] So in the middle of the fourteenth century, after the Delhi sultans had reached this coast, a local lord near Sopara was still granting land in the old way, under the old symbols.

A “Gai-Vasru”, a cow-and-calf land-grant stele, stands outside the small Surleshwar temple on the bank of the Nirmal lake today, among broken sculptures that include a three-faced bearded figure like the Chakreshwar Brahma. No date is given for it. [n10 §1.2; Heritage Chronicles 2019]

The Koprad stone

A little further north, at Koprad, half a mile south-west of Bolinj, a fifteenth-century stone lay in 1882 near the house of the headman Bhiku Patil, “a Samvedi Brahman”. Dated Shak 1386 and Hijra 868, that is 1464 CE, it is in Devanagari, mentions Muslim names, and names Mahim, Thana, Koprad and “Bimbstan”, the land of the Bimb kings whose legend Chapter 06 follows. [n03; n11 Q2; Gaz XIV p. 342] A Samvedi Brahmin headman living beside a stone that mixes Hindu and Muslim dating and names the Bimb country is as good a picture as any of the island in the fifteenth century: a Hindu village order under Muslim overlords, with its own memory of an older kingdom.

Jordanus and the church of St Thomas

In the winter of 1321–22 four friars, three Franciscans and a Georgian lay brother, were killed at Thana by order of its Muslim governor. A Dominican, Jordanus of Séverac, who had travelled with them, had gone ahead to preach at Sopara. Hearing of the killings, he came back, took the bodies to Sopara and buried them there. Within a year the Franciscan Odoric of Pordenone came to Thana, visited the tomb and carried the martyrs’ bones away; they ended up in China and Italy. Chapter 05 tells the story in full. [n38 §1; Gaz XIV pp. 322, 357; Yule, Cathay]

What concerns this chapter is what Jordanus found at Sopara itself. He stayed fifteen days at “Supera”. He found there “many Nestorian Christians and a church dedicated to St Thomas the Apostle”. He instructed and baptised “many people”, and gave Holy Communion to about ninety Christians. Between Thana and Supara he made thirty-five converts, and he found the pagans, Parsis and Hindus as he described them, “willing to listen”. [n38 §1; n37 §1; Gaz XIV p. 322; Gaz XIII-I p. 200] The Gazetteer read the Sopara church as “a relic of the old Kalyan bishopric”, the Christian see that Kosmas had recorded in the sixth century. [n38 §1; Gaz XIV p. 322] In his letter Jordanus asked for friars to be stationed at Supera. [n38 §1 inferences]

Two cautions. The dedication to St Thomas is a dedication, not evidence that the apostle came here; the notes found no Thomas tradition specific to Sopara beyond Jordanus’s church. And whether the ninety Christians belonged to the Church of the East, the “Nestorians”, or simply predated the Latins, is a matter of inference. [n38 §1 inferences, Gaps] What is certain is that two centuries before the Portuguese, there was a Christian church in Sopara with a congregation of about ninety, and that Sopara was for a short time the burial place of the first Latin martyrs of India. Where the church stood, and where the grave was, nobody knows.

Under the sultans

Thana rises, Sopara endures

By the fourteenth century the island’s port had been overtaken. “Before the beginning of the fourteenth century,” the Gazetteer wrote, “Thana had become the chief centre of trade. But Sopara was still a place of consequence.” [n03; n36 §3; Gaz XIV p. 321] The Arab geographer Abu-l-Fida, who died in 1331, still mentioned Sopara. [n03] A Gujarat notice of about 1500 named “Sorab” as a Konkan place trading with Gujarat. [n36 §3; Gaz XIV p. 322 n. 3] Indraji, in his 1882 paper, sketched the end: “In the fifteenth century … Sopara seems to have passed to the Musalmán rulers of Ahmadabad. Early in the sixteenth century the building of the Musalmán fort of Bassein, and later … the establishment at Bassein of the Court of the General of the North took from Sopára its former importance.” [n36 §3; Indraji 1882 p. 279] Bassein, six miles south, “can hardly have been a place of consequence so long as Sopara (A.D. 1100) remained a centre of trade”, the Gazetteer observed. [n37 §6; n32; Gaz XIV p. 28] The two towns’ fortunes crossed in these centuries, and the crossing is the hinge on which the book’s whole story of the coast turns.

Muslims of the island

Islam came to Sopara with traders long before it came with armies. The Gazetteer’s Naitas, whom it found at Sopara in 1882, were in its words “descendants of Arab and Persian refugees and traders”; the Konkani Muslims as a whole, it said, owed their origin to Arab and Persian settlers of the eighth to tenth centuries. The Portuguese botanist Garcia d’Orta, in the sixteenth century, wrote of the “Naitas of Bassein” who married local women and were rich traders when the Portuguese came. [n37 §1; n35 §6; Gaz XIII-I pp. 216–217; Gaz XIV p. 315 n.] A note in the Gazetteer cites Reinaud’s edition of Abu-l-Fida for Arabs settled at Sopara “in very early times”. [n08; Gaz XIII-I p. 61 n.] The Shilahara court, the Gazetteer said, had been liberal to Arab merchants, “allowing them to have mosques and a headman to settle disputes”. [n37 §1; Gaz XIII-II p. 432]

Their traces are in the ground. In the Gass field Sonarbhat, beside the Jain and Hindu stones, the Gazetteer saw the tomb of a Muslim saint, Shaikh Akbar-moi-ud-din, bordered with very large old bricks, sixteen by ten by three inches. [n11 Q2; n42 §3; Gaz XIV p. 341] On the banks of the Bhatela pond were Muslim graves. [n31 §2] Qureshi’s dig of 1939–40 found glazed sherds of the Muslim period in the stupa mound, and the 1993 excavation found Islamic blue glazed ware. [n02 §1, §3] The heritage survey of 2008 lists the Rehmatullah Alleya dargah at Sopara with the date 1555 and the note “rebuilt”, and the Hazrat Diwan Shah dargah on the Sopara–Gass road; the 1555 date is the survey’s, and no other source checks it. [n40 A15; CRIT Vol I items 8–11; Vol II] Near the Gass–Taki Pada area, where a stone anchor was found in 2018, stands a mosque called the Bandar Masjid, the “port mosque”. [n02 §6; Shah 2020] Its name is a memory of the landing; its date is not known.

Wikipedia’s account of Nallasopara lists three mohallas, quarters, of the old Sopara village: Nawayat, Kharkhandi and Bazar Peth. The source is a secondary mirror. [n31 §1] The name Nawayat is suggestive, since the Navayats are a Muslim community of Arab descent on the Konkan and Kanara coasts, but one research note found “nothing specific on Nawayat presence at Vasai or Sopara”, and the name of a lane cannot carry more than that. [n03; n35 §6]

The Portuguese island

Stockades, a tower and a landing

On 23 December 1534 Sultan Bahadur of Gujarat ceded Bassein and its district to Portugal. Chapter 07 tells how the fort-city of Baçaim rose. For Sopara the change was decisive and permanent: from now on the island would be called after Bassein, not Sopara. [n11 Q1; Gaz XIV p. 314]

The Portuguese found the land “guarded by stockades and fortified posts”. [n03; TG-II p. 451] In the first years of their rule Sopara was “a fortified post with four wooden stockades”. [n36 §4; n40 A15; da Cunha pp. 158–159; Gaz XIV p. 322] By the Bhatela pond they had a tower and a landing, and perhaps their jetty and customs house. [n31 §2; n02 §6] East of Gass was another Portuguese landing. [n02 §6] In 1728 an officer reporting on the defences complained that the “Nil hill” near Sopara had been fortified without an engineer, with bastions too small to carry a four-pounder gun; the same report described the creek fordable for 4,000 feet and General Louis de Mello Pereira’s bridge across it at Gokirva, guarded by watch-towers of palm stems. [n31 §2; n12 §1; Gaz XIV p. 322 and n., citing O Chronista de Tissuary] At Achole, a mile and a half north of Manikpur, the Portuguese had a fort, which the Gazetteer’s visitors were sent to see. [n31 §2; n12 §3]

The Nil hill is a small mystery. The research notes point out that “Nil” might be connected with Nila, the village a mile north of Sopara, and with the “Neela” of an early railway timetable, but no source makes the link, and it stays a possibility. [n31 §1 inferences]

The island in the Tombo

The Portuguese crown leased the villages of its northern province to settlers, religious orders and noble families, and the register of those leases, the Tombo de Baçaim of 1727–30, survives. It lists 362 titles: 36 in the caçabé (district) of Baçaim, 28 in Agaçaim, and only 4 in Sopará. [n40 §2; Tombo pp. 338–339] Sopara had become a small district in its own province.

Several of the island’s villages can be matched to entries in it, by sound and context; the matching is the research notes’ own, not the register’s. “Mardes Sirgoly”, the village of the stupa, was held by Lucas de Almeida de Andrade, at a rent of 180 pardaus. “Vagoly” was held by Manuel de Abreu de Lima, “Vastal Vagoly” by Fernão da Silveira e Meneses. “Bolinja e terra Belvar” was leased for lives to Dona Maria Henriques Coelho da Silva at 450 pardaus. “Vatar Rajoly” was held by Manuel Varjão da Fonseca at 700 pardaus a year, the highest rent in the list. “Cholna Roba”, Chulne, belonged to the Jesuit Procurador Geral. [n40 §2; n39; Tombo table, fl. 41–54v] And “Nala Magir” was a pacaria, an estate, held by Miguel Francisco Mira de Vasconcelos, with an extra garden, Matarvary, held by Gonçalo Carneiro. [n40 A15; Tombo fl. 43, fl. 40] That entry is the first appearance of Nala by that spelling in any document the research reached. The research found no entry that could be matched with confidence to Gass, or to Sopara village itself; “Gris” is Giriz. [n40 A6; n42 §3]

The register tells us who owned the rents. It does not tell us who tilled the fields. For that, the sources jump forward to the nineteenth century.

Coins of Shah Jahan

One small find shows that the island traded with the Mughal world even under the Portuguese. Indraji found at Sopara about ten square white-metal coins of the Mughal emperor Shah Jahan, who reigned from 1628 to 1658, and thought they had been struck to replace Portuguese white-metal coins. [n36 §3; Indraji 1882 p. 279] Whatever their purpose, they were in Sopara’s soil, and somebody had been spending them.

The converts of Sopara

Chapter 34 tells how and why the people of the Bassein lands became Christian between 1534 and 1739: the Franciscans from 1535 and the Jesuits from the 1540s; the laws that favoured converts; the destruction of temples; the incentives of land and office; the threat of coercion that community traditions remember and Portuguese records deny; and real belief. It weighs both sides. What this chapter can add is what the record says about Sopara.

It says one thing clearly, and says it late. In 1882 the Gazetteer wrote: “The husbandmen of Sopara are Christians, many of them converted Samvedi Brahmans; Musalmans, many of them Naitas …; and Hindus, chiefly Samvedi Brahmans and Bhandaris.” [n34 §1; n11 Q2; Gaz XIV p. 315] That sentence is the strongest single statement in any primary source that the Christian farmers of the Sopara garden villages came from the Samvedi Brahmins. It is backed by a second: at Agashi, Vatar and Koprad, the Gazetteer counted “about seventy-five families of Native Christians originally Samvedis and Pachkalshis” drying plantains beside about eighty-five Hindu Samvedi families. [n34 §1; Gaz XIII-1 p. 395]

The Gazetteer never uses the word “Kupari”. But these are the Kuparis’ ancestors, and Sopara is where the evidence for them begins. The community’s own list of Kupari villages, as given by a community writer in 2009, is Nirmal, Agashi, Nandakhal, Gass, Bhuigaon, Umrale, Naanbhat, Shrilaiwadi, Bolinj, Mardes, Wagholi, Gomes Ali and Rajodi; and the Gazetteer’s list of the villages of “the garden or agar of Sopara”, Bolinj, Koprad, Umbrala, Nirmal, Vagholi, Gas, Sopara and Mardesh, is very nearly the southern half of it. [n40 §1; n34 §1; Rajan D’Mello blog 2009; Gaz XIV p. 314] A sociologist’s account published by Sahapedia says that the first converts to Christianity among the Samvedis were those settled between Agashi and Nalasopara on the Vaitarna side, that the Samvedi Christians were “largely farmers”, once forest contractors, and “the only Bassein group to receive Jesuit education”. It cites no sources in the text the research could see, and should be read as an informed popular synthesis. [n34 §1; n35 §1; Sahapedia]

The other side must be heard too. Gerson da Cunha, writing in 1876 and hostile to the converts’ claims, said there were no high-caste native descendants among the Catholics of Bassein, and modern historians stress that most converts across the province were farmers, fishers and toddy-tappers. The Gazetteer’s own general account names Brahmans, Prabhus, Pachkalshis, Bhandaris, Kolis, Kunbis and many others among the Christians, and says the bulk were Bhandaris, Kolis and Kunbis. [n34 §1; n40 §2; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 199–201] So “converted Samvedi Brahmans” has genuine nineteenth-century support for the Sopara and Agashi villages, and describes part of the community, not all of it. [n34 §1]

What did conversion mean on the ground at Sopara? The notes have no Portuguese record of the Sopara conversions, no baptismal register, no named convert. The parish that served the Sopara garden villages in Portuguese times was, by every later account, Holy Cross at Nirmal, whose church is variously dated to 1557, 1558, 1580 and 1586; Gass, Bhuigaon and Gomes Ali were cut from it only in the twentieth century. [n40 A1] The Hindu shrine at Nirmal was pulled down: “The Portuguese had pulled down the temples and destroyed the ling,” the Gazetteer recorded. [n11 Q2; Gaz XIV p. 293] At Sopara the temples were stripped for stone. [n37 §1] The converts kept their language, their Samvedi speech, later called Kadodi or Kupari, and their crafts of the garden: the sugarcane, the plantains, the betel vine, the Persian wheels over the wells. Chapter 36 describes those ways of life. And they kept, as the Gazetteer’s 1882 sentence shows, their neighbours: Hindu Samvedis in the same villages, sometimes in the same families’ memory.

There is one more trace of that closeness, from 1882, at Gass. In front of the house of “one Degu Pobria Mhatara” lay the six-sided basalt block with the Pali word “Badhuya”; two hundred yards away, at the house of “a Samvedi Brahman, Bab Naik by name”, lay the second inscribed block. [n42 §2; Gaz XIV p. 341] The Gazetteer does not say whether Degu was Christian or Hindu. Chapter 26 tells the story of those two stones. Here it is enough that in one hamlet of the Sopara garden in 1882, a Samvedi Brahmin and a neighbour of the old Mhatara name lived two hundred yards apart, each with a piece of ancient Sopara outside his door.

The Agris of Achole

Not all the converts on the Sopara side were Samvedis. On the eastern edge of the island, the Gazetteer recorded villages of Urap Agris, Christian converts of the salt-and-rice caste who went back to Hinduism in 1820 and 1828 and were known as “Nave Marathas”, new Marathas. Among the Bassein villages it named were Achole and Juchandra, as well as Umele, Navapur, Virar and Kolwad. They kept Portuguese surnames such as Gomes, Soz, Fernan, Frutad and Minez, and Palshe or Golak Brahmins served as their priests. [n40 §1; n49 §1e; n35 §4; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 116–117 and n. 1; Enthoven I] The name Urap, the sources say, may come from Persian urf, from “Europe”, or from the Marathi orapne, to sear with a hot iron, the purification ordeal. [n35 §4] Achole, then, was an Agri village with a Portuguese fort, and its people had passed from Hinduism to Christianity and back again within three centuries. It is now one of the densest neighbourhoods of Nallasopara East, and the next chapter returns to it.

Maratha Sopara, 1739–1818

The Marathas took Arnala in March 1737 and Bassein in May 1739. Chapters 08 and 09 tell the siege and its aftermath. The Gazetteer records almost nothing of Sopara under the Marathas, and the notes add little. What is known comes from the neighbours and from newcomers.

At Nirmal, “on the fall of Bassein (1739) Nirmal was purified, prints of the feet of Shri Dattatraya took the place of the ling, and a reservoir was built”, and about 1750 the Maratha governor Shankarji Keshav built eight temples round the lake, with stones the Portuguese had built into churches “probably used to raise the temples of the older gods”. [n11 Q2; n40 A1; Gaz XIV pp. 292–293] The Holy Cross church at Nirmal was destroyed in the war, and from 1739 to 1856, its plaque says, “the present Cemetery was pro tempore their Church”. [n40 A1; EI Nirmal] So the Christians of the Sopara garden worshipped for a hundred and seventeen years in a graveyard, a few hundred yards from a temple being rebuilt with the stones of their church. Across the Bassein lands, the Gazetteer says, “numbers of the Christians were … purified by Brahmans and admitted into Hinduism” after 1739–40, while others stayed Christian. [n35 §3; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 202–203] Chapter 35 tells how the Kuparis lived through those decades. When Anquetil du Perron reached Agashi on 16 December 1760, the feast day of its church, he found its lanes “full of people going to church as freely as in a Christian country”, under Maratha rule. [n40 A2; Gaz XIV p. 2; da Cunha p. 164]

The newcomers came by sea from Gujarat. “About the middle of the eighteenth century (1760–1766) a considerable number of Cambay Vanis, chiefly of the Lad sub-division, and with them several Gujarat Brahmans settled in Supara, Bassein, and other coast towns to avoid the exactions of Momin Khan II (1748–1783),” the Gazetteer recorded. [n35 §8; Gaz XIII-1 p. 63] Of about 120 families of Lad Vanis in the district in 1882, “about forty are in Thana, thirty-five in Supara, and the rest in Bassein, Agashi, Nala, Papdi, and Dahanu”. The Gazetteer thought Masudi’s tenth-century report of the Lar language on this coast made it “probable that from very early times Lad Vanis had settled along the Thana coast for purposes of trade”, but that most of the families of 1882 were late settlers. They spoke Gujarati among themselves and Marathi with others; they were shopkeepers, moneylenders, landholders and merchants, Vaishnavas of the Vallabhacharya sect who “observe Shaiv fasts and feasts” because of their close connection with Maratha Brahmins. [n35 §8; Gaz XIII-1] Thirty-five families at Sopara made it the second Lad Vani centre in the district after Thana. And some of them were at Nala.

In the last years of Maratha rule, Sopara was briefly on the edge of great events. On 17 December 1802 the Peshwa Baji Rao II, fleeing Holkar, reached Bassein, and on 31 December he signed the Treaty of Bassein with the East India Company, which led in sixteen years to the end of his rule. During those days a stockade of palm trees was built “at the Sopara bridge” to protect the passage to the island of Bassein. [n28; Gaz XIV, Bassein; Gaz XIII-II, History] The bridge is presumably the crossing of the old creek, though the notes do not place it.

British Sopara

A kasba and a post-runner’s station

Company rule came in 1818. Chapter 11 describes the island under the Company. In 1826 Clunes’s gazetteer called Sopara a kasba, a market town, with four hundred houses and a sugar factory, and a post-runner’s station. [n31 §2; n37 §6; Gaz XIV pp. 315, 323] In 1837, the Gazetteer recorded, “very good fishing boats and country vessels were built” at Sopara. [n36 §5; Gaz XIV p. 323] By the 1870s da Cunha could call it “once a famous bandar, but now almost abandoned”, and describe the island as having “about its middle the ancient famous port of Supara”. [n40 A15; da Cunha pp. 118, 157]

The Gazetteer’s Sopara, 1882

The fullest portrait comes from 1882, and it is worth assembling its pieces in one place.

Sopara was “still a rich country town with a crowded weekly market and a population of about 1700”. It lay about three and a half miles north-west of Bassein Road station and three and a half miles south-west of Virar station, not more than three-quarters of a mile from the railway, and yet “somewhat badly off for communications”. From Manikpur the way crossed a salt waste that flooded and became impassable in the rains; from Virar it ran along the Agashi road for a mile and then two and a half miles along a winding lane; and “between Bolinj and Sopara the road is a narrow low lane passable for wheels only in fair weather”. “A station at Sopara or a made road to the town from the Agashi high road would be a boon to its people.” [n31 §1; n12 §3; n40 A11; n46 §2; Gaz XIV pp. 314–316, 382] It had a broad market place lined with one- and two-storey houses carved richly in wood. [n11 Q2; Gaz XIV pp. 314–315] Its weekly market was one of four in the Bassein sub-division, with Agashi, Virar and a place the OCR calls “Dhovh”, each held on a different day and attended by five or six hundred people. [n46 §6; n48 §8; Gaz XIII-1 p. 333]

Its people were the husbandmen already quoted, Christians, many of them converted Samvedi Brahmins, Naita Muslims, and Hindus, chiefly Samvedi Brahmins and Bhandaris; and its traders were Muslims and the Lad Vanis from Cambay. [n11 Q2; Gaz XIV p. 315] Its chief products were molasses, rice, plantains and betel leaf. Molasses, rice and betel went by sea from the landings at Gas, Bolinj and Vagholi to the Gujarat ports; by rail, chiefly from Virar, molasses, rice and betel went north to Ankleshvar, Nadiad, Viramgam and Patan, “and large quantities of plantains and betel-leaves are sent south to Bombay”. Oilcake from the Gujarat ports came in as manure for the sugarcane. [n48 §1; n12 §2; Gaz XIV pp. 315–316] Bills of exchange, hundis, could be cashed at Sopara, as at Nala, Agashi and Navghar, up to sizeable sums: a small credit centre in the old style. [n40 A15; n48 §1; Gaz XIII-1 trade chapter]

Around the town lay its ponds. The Gazetteer counted at least four large lakes in Sopara village, one of them called Sarpala, the “snake pond”, with carved stones. [n48 §5; Gaz XIV pp. 314–316] At the south edge was a “Musalman pond”; within Gass limits, the Khare or Shirmoli pond; east of it, the Bhatela pond with its graves. [n42 §1] There was Rama’s Pool, Ramkund, where pilgrims bathed in the month of Shravan. [n31 §2; Gaz XIV] There was the Chakreshwar lake with its temple and stones. And on the Gass side, the winding Gas lake, 120 by 800 yards, with two cross-dams, fringed by gardens and Persian wheels, with an eleventh- or twelfth-century temple foundation at a spot called “Mala” on its shore. [n11 Q2; Gaz XIV p. 341]

There were other remains. Near the village stood a ruined English dwelling which, the headman said, a Dr Seward had built; nothing else is known of him. [n31 §2; n25] The Gazetteer also recorded a local tale of robbers who troubled Sopara and of the garden of a chemist named Shaikh Amir; the notes record only that the tale is there, as local legend. [n31 §2] Brahma hill, the bush-covered knoll south-west of the gardens, had long been used as a quarry: “large blocks of basalt are piled in places as if into an artificial wall … The number of these blocks, laid as a footway along some of the lowest parts of the road between Gas and Nirmal and built into the plinths of Gas and Sopara houses, shows that Brahma hill has long been used as a quarry.” Under a banyan between Gass and Brahma hill stood a slab carved with a cobra god, a nagoba. [n42 §1; Gaz XIV, Sopara section] Raised footways of old basalt blocks crossed the mud near Bolinj and south of Gass, where the dagdi bandh ran towards Achole. [n42 §1; n12 §3; Gaz XIV pp. 341–342]

That was the island in 1882: a town of 1,700 in the middle of a garden belt, with a dry creek, three working landings, a weekly market, Hindu, Christian, Muslim and Gujarati merchant households, temples and churches in its neighbour villages, and its ancient past lying about in its fields and plinths for anyone with Indraji’s eyes to see.

Plantains and the village of Nala

The Gazetteer’s tables of crops and trade show something its Sopara entry did not. “The well-known Bassein dried-plantains are the fruit of the rajele variety,” it said, and they were prepared only in nine villages: “Agáshi, Vagholi, Vatár, Bolinj, Koprád, Nala, Umrále, Rajodi, and Murdes, all in Bassein.” [n21; n40 §1; Gaz XIII-1 p. 292] Nala is on the list. So it was a garden village of the plantain belt. The Gazetteer described the work only for Agashi, Vatar and Koprad, where the driers worked from October to January and sold the fruit as far as Thana, Bombay, Surat, Poona and Sholapur; Nala’s driers presumably worked the same season for the same markets, though the source does not say so. [n48 §9; Gaz XIII-1 p. 395] It was one of four Bassein villages, with Agashi, Sopara and Navghar, where hundis were cashed. [n40 A15] And it appears, spelled “Nela”, in a list of ten Bassein trade centres (Bassein, Nela, Sopara, Agashi, Kaman, Bolinj, Manikpur, Virar, Bhatane and Kharbhav), though the reading of that page of the OCR is uncertain. [n40 A15; Gaz XIII-1, OCR p. 300] Some of the Lad Vani families lived there. [n35 §8; n05]

So by 1882 Nala, or Nila, was a modest village a mile north of Sopara: a place with a Yadava-era stone, a Portuguese estate, plantain driers, Gujarati merchants and a credit market, but no separate entry of its own in the Places of Interest. [n40 A15; n31 §2] What the village was like, who lived there besides the Lad Vanis, and how large it was, the notes cannot say; the census figures for it were not retrieved. [n31 §2 Gaps] It was about to become, by a historical accident, the more famous half of a name.

The railway at the door

The railway had already come. The Bombay, Baroda and Central India line opened through Bassein Road and Virar in the 1860s, and its embankment ran between Sopara and the mainland. Chapter 12 tells the story; Chapter 28 tells it from Virar. The line had no station at Sopara in 1882, and the Gazetteer’s list of stations that year (Bandra, Andheri, Goregaon, Borivli, Bhayandar, Bassein Road, Virar and on north) has no Nallasopara and no Naigaon. [n28; n11 rails; Gaz XIII-1] “Along the coast,” another passage of the Gazetteer said, “the opening of the Baroda railway has destroyed the old cart traffic, and has reduced the sea-trade with Gujarat and Bombay.” [n36 §5; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 341–342] For Sopara the railway took away the last of the creek, took stone from Tulinj hill, and moved the island’s trade from its landings to Virar. When at last a halt was put on the line near Sopara, it took its name not from the ancient town but from the village of Nala beside it. That is where the next chapter begins.

The freedom years

The record of Sopara in the national movement is a single line. In the autumn of 1930, after a Thana District Liquor Prohibition Mandal was formed on 31 October to work in Bassein taluka, the Bombay police abstracts reported a prohibition drive “in the villages in Bassein taluka from Sopara as centre” (15 November 1930). [n46 §10; SM-XII p. 253] Who led it and what came of it, the notes do not say. Chapter 13 and Chapter 28 tell what is known of the movement in the taluka.

Sopara’s peoples through time

The sections above have met Sopara’s communities one era at a time. Chapter 40 tells how each community of the whole coast came and how its language formed. It is worth drawing the threads together here for the island alone, community by community, saying for each what the record shows at Sopara and where it falls silent.

The Samvedi Brahmins

The Samvedi Brahmins, followers of the Sama Veda, are the island’s oldest community whose presence the documents can follow. A copper plate of 1026 from Bhandup records a Shilahara grant to a Brahmin of “the Chhandoga shakha of the Samaveda”, which shows Samaveda Brahmins receiving land on this coast in the eleventh century, though not yet at Sopara by name. [n03; TG-II p. 425 n.] By 1464 a Samvedi Brahmin family held the headmanship of Koprad, if the stone found beside Bhiku Patil’s house in 1882 was theirs from the start; that is the Gazetteer’s implication, not a record. [n03; Gaz XIV p. 342] In 1882 the Hindu husbandmen of Sopara were “chiefly Samvedi Brahmans and Bhandaris”, a Samvedi Brahmin named Bab Naik lived at Gass, and some eighty-five Samvedi families dried plantains at Agashi, Vatar and Koprad. [n11 Q2; n34 §1; Gaz XIV pp. 315, 341; Gaz XIII-1 p. 395]

Their own tradition, recorded on community websites and in a temple tradition at Nirmal, says they came to Shurparaka from Utkala or Kalinga, Odisha, as singers, that their Vedic practice lapsed under Buddhism and was restored by Shankaracharya at Nirmal, and that under Raja Pratap Bimb they turned to sugarcane and garden farming. The name “Shamedi”, the same tradition says, comes from musicians at the samadhi shrine. [n08; n35 §1; n40 A1] All of that is tradition, unsourced in any document, and the notes treat it so. [n35 §1] What the documents show is a farming Brahmin community of the garden villages round Nirmal lake, Hindu and Christian, sharing a speech that later writers called Samvedi or Kadodi. [n05; n34 §4]

The Kuparis

From the Samvedis came most of the Kuparis, the Samvedi Christians, and Sopara is where their story first becomes visible in an official record. The 1882 sentence about the “converted Samvedi Brahmans” of Sopara is the foundation of every later account. [n34 §1] It is fair to say plainly what this chapter has found about them on the island, because so much of what is written about the community rests on tradition.

They lived, in 1882, in the garden villages that the Gazetteer called the agar of Sopara: Gass, Nirmal, Vagholi, Mardes, Umrale, Bolinj, Koprad, and Sopara itself. [n11 Q1; n40 §1] They were the island’s best farmers. “The most hardworking and skilful husbandmen are the Christians of Bassein,” the Gazetteer wrote, growing sugarcane and plantains. [n36 §5; Gaz XIII-I p. 301] They worked the Persian wheels over the wells, the high-plinthed tiled houses, the sunken lanes and the gardens of betel, plantain and cane that the Gazetteer described as the island’s landscape. [n11 Q1] They dried the rajele plantains in the nine villages named in the Gazetteer, which include Nala, Mardes, Vagholi, Bolinj, Koprad and Umrale, and sent them to Bombay and the Deccan. [n21; n40 §1]

Their parish was Holy Cross at Nirmal, rebuilt by its parishioners in 1856 “at the cost of Rs 20,000 and physical labour”, after more than a century of worship in its cemetery. In 1882 it had 2,184 parishioners, a vicar paid a small British grant, and a private school whose teacher played the violin in church. [n40 A1; Gaz XIV p. 293] The villages nearest Sopara were later cut from it: Gass in 1942, Bhuigaon in 1946, Gomes Ali in 2004; Umrale was cut from Nandakhal in 1970 and Bolinj in 2001. [n40 A1, A3, A9, A11] The procession round the cross at Madres Talav, beside the stupa, belongs to them. [n40 A8] So does the parish of St Gonsalo Garcia at Gass, a mile south of the old town, whose story is told in Chapter 26.

Sopara village itself is described in the research notes as mixed: Kupari, Hindu and Muslim, with Nala the same. [n40 A15] No parish history of a Catholic church at Sopara village itself was found; the Christians of Sopara presumably belonged to Nirmal and later to Gass, but the notes do not say so in terms. In the same way, the notes found no Kupari family history tied to Sopara village by name, no census of Christians at Sopara at any date, and no record of when the Sopara Christians’ land began to be sold for building. Those are gaps. [n40 A15; n42 §4 Gaps] Chapters 33 to K6 tell the Kuparis’ story across the whole coast, and K7 tells it for Gass.

Pachkalshis and Vadvals

The Pachkalshis, the Somvanshi Kshatriya garden caste whose Christian members are the Vadvals, appear in the 1882 record beside the Samvedis in the northern villages: fifteen Pachkalshi families dried plantains at Agashi, Vatar and Koprad, and some of the seventy-five Christian families there were “originally Samvedis and Pachkalshis”. [n34 §1; n40 §1; Gaz XIII-1 p. 395] But the research found no source placing Vadvals or Pachkalshis at Sopara or Nallasopara specifically; the 1882 text describes Sopara as a Samvedi, Christian and Brahmin-farming place, and the Vadval Christian villages lay to the south, round the old fort at Sandor, Papdi, Manickpur and Chulne. [n41; n40 §1] Chulne, through which the 1882 visitor returned from Sopara, was a Vadval village, its Christian carters known as Vadvals or gardeners. [n40 §1; Gaz XIII-1 p. 97] The Pachkalshi presence at Sopara is therefore at its edge, not at its centre, as far as the documents go.

Bhandaris

The Bhandaris, the caste of toddy-tappers and palm-garden workers, were in 1882 one of the two Hindu groups the Gazetteer named at Sopara. [n11 Q2; Gaz XIV p. 315] Across the Bassein lands, the Gazetteer said, many Christians came from among them, and after 1739 some went back: the “Kirpals”, “perhaps Kriyapals”, were probably Bhandaris who “went back to the old religion”. Among some Bhandaris and Agris, it added, “the remembrance of a hurried flight from the south and some traces of Lingayat customs remain”. [n35 §4; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 63, 203] Their palm groves, the brab and coconut that shaded the Sopara gardens, were part of the landscape the Gazetteer described. [n11 Q1] No Bhandari family history of Sopara was found.

Kolis

The Kolis were the coast’s fishing people. In 1882 “the mass of the sea-fishing population” were Son Kolis, and there were Christian Kolis, Uraps among them, in Bassein. [n08; n36 §7] The Kolis of Bassein, the Gazetteer said, took to tillage in the monsoon. [n37 §6] But the research found no Koli settlement at Sopara itself in any source; Sopara was a garden town on a dry creek, and the island’s fishing villages lay on the open coast at Arnala, Pachu Bandar and Vasai Koliwada. [n08; n36 §7] The Kolis came to Sopara’s history through its weekly market and its boats. In 1882 the builders of the coast’s small craft included “Marathas, Christians, Gujarat carpenters, Cutch Musalmans, Kolis, and Pachkalshis”, and the boats of 1837 built at Sopara may have been their work; the notes do not say who built them. [n36 §5; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 342–343; Gaz XIV p. 323]

Agris

The Agris, the salt-and-rice cultivators, belong to the island’s eastern edge. The Urap Agris of Achole, converts who returned to Hinduism in 1820 and 1828, have been described above. [n40 §1; n35 §4] In 1882 salt works and rice flats lay on the low ground between the gardens and the hills; the city plan of the 2000s still recorded salt cultivation on the marshy land south of Nallasopara station. [n48 §2] The Agris of that ground are the community most closely tied to the eastern half of the modern suburb, and the least documented in its history.

Muslims

The Naitas, the Arab- and Persian-descended Muslims of the coast, were in 1882 among both the husbandmen and the traders of Sopara. [n11 Q2; Gaz XIV p. 315] Their saints’ tombs and dargahs, their graves by the Bhatela pond, the Bandar Masjid near the old landing and the Muslim-period sherds in the stupa mound show them on the island for many centuries, though no inscription dates their arrival. [n02; n11; n40 A15] In the whole Bassein sub-division in 1872 there were only 2,025 Muslims, 1,866 Sunni and 159 Shia. [n08] Modern Nallasopara’s much larger Muslim population, including Urdu speakers counted in 2001, came mostly in the last decades and belongs to the next chapter. [n31 §3]

Gujarati merchants and Jains

The Lad Vanis from Cambay, thirty-five families at Supara in 1882, with some at Nala, were Vaishnava merchants who kept Shaiva fasts. [n35 §8] The Jains of medieval Sopara, of the Modha clan whose inscriptions were found in 2019, and of the tradition that made Sopara a tirtha, are a separate and older thread, and the notes found “no source … for a Jain community in Sopara beyond the tradition” for the centuries after the sixteenth. [n03; n37 §1 Gaps] Marwari Vanis came under the British, after 1818, as moneylenders to cultivators. [n35 §8] The Jain derasar on Tulinj Road belongs to the twentieth- and twenty-first-century city.

Buddhists

The Buddhists who built the stupa, gave cisterns at Kanheri and Karle, and kept the relic mound alive for a thousand years left the island with no community that the record can follow. The stupa’s last expansion was in the Rashtrakuta period, and after that the sources fall silent on Buddhism at Sopara. [n02 §1; n37 §1 Gaps] Buddhism returned to Vasai-Virar in the twentieth century: the 2011 census counted 2.5 to 2.65 per cent of the corporation’s people as Buddhist. [n06 §1; n49 §1h] Who they are and where they live, the notes do not record; it would be guessing to say that the modern Buddhists of the city and the stupa at Mardes are connected by more than the state’s plans for a Buddhist circuit. That story belongs to the next chapter.

Who is missing

The record of Sopara’s peoples is a record of farmers, merchants, priests and the occasional named householder. It has almost nothing on the island’s labourers, its women, its servants, its Mahars and others of the castes the Gazetteer names elsewhere in the taluka. The Warlis and Kolis came to the Saturday market at Virar; whether they came to Sopara’s, the Gazetteer does not say. [n12 §2] These silences are not evidence that such people were absent. They are evidence of who wrote the documents and whom they talked to.

The twentieth century: diggers and a quiet village

Between the wars

For most of the twentieth century Sopara drops out of the written record. No census figure for the village after 1882 was found, and no account of its life under late British rule. [n31 §2 Gaps; n21] The one event the notes record for the island between the wars is archaeological. In 1939–40 M. M. Qureshi of the Archaeological Survey re-excavated the stupa mound, finding stone lintels, two small stupas on the south side and Muslim-period glazed sherds. [n02 §1; n25; Shah 2020] What prompted the dig, the notes do not say.

1956: the schoolboy’s stone

The next find came by accident. On 1 January 1956 a schoolboy at Bhuigaon found the broken slab with Ashoka’s Ninth Edict, and brought it to N. A. Gore. The Archaeological Survey’s review of the year called it “the earliest” of the epigraphical discoveries of 1955–56, and concluded that “it is now almost certain that Sopara contained a complete set of the Rock-edicts of Asoka”. [n24 §3; n02 §1; IAR 1955–56] Bhuigaon lies about two kilometres south-west of Nirmal, on the far side of Nirmal from the stupa. That a piece of the edicts turned up there suggests the slabs were scattered widely across the island, or carried off as building stone, as so much of Sopara was. That is an inference.

1972, 1993 and the coastal surveys

In 1972 Anwar Munshi found Satavahana lead coins at Sopara. [n02 §4; Shah 2020] In 1993 the Archaeological Survey and the British Academy dug jointly and found the coins, beads, Northern Black Polished Ware, Roman amphora pieces, Islamic glazed ware and walls described above. [n02 §3] In 1994 exploration near the stupa yielded red polished ware and glazed ware, and a Portuguese landing place east of Gass was identified. [n02 §6; Shah 2020] Between 1994 and 1997 Sila Tripati and A. S. Gaur of the National Institute of Oceanography surveyed the coast onshore and nearshore, and their 1997 paper gave the account of Sopara’s two peaks and its silent centuries that every later writer uses. [n02 §5; Tripati & Gaur 1997] Some time after, M. S. Chauhan dug round the stupa and uncovered votive stupas; no date or report was found. [n02 §6]

2012: a pillar in a garden

On 6 January 2012 workers digging a garden near the stupa found a pillar carved with an image of the Buddha, and the digging was halted. The Times of India reported it three days later, and reported that the state and the Vasai-Virar Municipal Corporation planned a meditation centre, a museum and a mini-theatre to make the stupa an “international tourist spot”. [n02 §6; TOI via Kerala Mahabodhi blog] No later report of the pillar’s fate was found.

2018: an anchor near Taki Pada

In 2018 local surveyors found a stone anchor near Gass and Taki Pada, close to the Bandar Masjid. The Marathi daily Loksatta reported it in a piece on the ancient villages of Nallasopara being ignored, and the Shah study cites that report. [n02 §6; n01; Shah 2020; Loksatta] The anchor is of the historic period, not prehistoric. [n01] Its date, its present location and who holds it are not recorded in the notes. [n42 §2 Gaps] It is the only physical relic of Sopara’s shipping found on the island, and it was found not at the stupa but by the old southern landing, near a mosque named for the port.

2019: the Jain inscriptions

In 2019 Siddharth Kale, Monish Shah and two Jain monks found the thirteenth- to sixteenth-century inscriptions of the Modha Jains at Sopara. [n03] The same group published, in 2020, the study of “Sopara: An Ancient Port, Trade Center and a Teertha” on which much of this chapter rests. [n02]

Scattered treasure

Sopara’s past is now held in many places. The edict slabs are in the Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj Vastu Sangrahalaya in Mumbai. The relics of the stupa are with the Asiatic Society of Mumbai. Some finds from Gass are with the University of Mumbai; others are with local temples. The Jain bronzes are with the Agashi Jain Sangh. The Shilahara stones from Agashi and Vagholi went to the Collector’s garden at Thana; where they are now, the notes do not say. The sculptures of Chakreshwar are at the lake. The two inscribed blocks of Gass have not been traced since 1882. [n02 §6 inferences; n42 §2 Gaps; n11 Q2] No museum at Sopara brings them together. [n02 §6 Gaps]

The stupa: protection and neglect

The stupa is protected by the Archaeological Survey of India. The research could not find its monument number or notification date in a current Survey list. [n02 §1 Gaps] It is the island’s one nationally protected monument.

Protection has not meant attention. In 2014 the state government decided to make the stupa the hub of Maharashtra’s Buddhist circuit. A later report, by Buddhistdoor citing the Indian Express, said it “still lies largely neglected”, and that a nine-foot statue of the Buddha, donated by a trust in Pune, had been installed about 200 metres away. [n02 §1; Buddhistdoor] Outlook Traveller called the site a candidate for “India’s least visited tourist sight”. [n02 §1] The DNA newspaper wrote: “While the Stupa has been looked after, Nalasopara’s historical significance is nowhere to be seen.” [n02 §1; DNA] LiveHistoryIndia: “no traces of the ancient port remain”, only a few protected mounds and a dried-up lake. [n02 §1, §5]

Encroachment is the danger everywhere in Nallasopara, as the next chapter shows, but the notes found no report tying encroachment to the protected mound itself. The demolitions of 2024–25 and the mob that halted a demolition drive in February 2026 concerned municipal land elsewhere. [n02 §1 Gaps, §6 inferences] The danger to Sopara’s past is less to the mound, which the law protects, than to everything around it that the law does not: the ponds, the fields where Brahmas and bulls were dug up, the plinths of old houses, the places where the other twelve edict slabs may lie.

Sopara gaon today

What the surveys list

The village of Sopara, Sopara gaon, is today a locality of Nallasopara West, inside the Vasai-Virar City Municipal Corporation. LiveHistoryIndia’s phrase is that Sopara “has returned to a cluster of villages”. [n02 §6; n31 §1] Wikipedia lists Sopara Gaon and Nale Gaon among the localities of Nallasopara West. [n31 §1] The 2011 census has no separate figure for it, because the villages inside the corporation were counted as one urban unit. [n11 Q1; n40 §2]

The heritage survey of the Vasai plantation zone made in 2008 for the corporation lists the landmarks of the Sopara gram panchayat as it then was: the market precinct of Burhanpur Chowk, named, the survey says, after a nineteenth-century Mr Burhanpur Harris; the Rehmatullah Alleya dargah, dated 1555 and rebuilt; the Chakreshwar Talav; the Sopare Zilla Parishad school; talavs along the Sopara–Gass road; and the Hazrat Diwan Shah dargah. [n40 A15; CRIT Vol I items 8–11; Vol II] Next door, the Madres gram panchayat’s list has the stupa and the Madres Talav with its cross. [n40 A8] The survey’s explanation of the name Burhanpur Chowk cannot be checked from the notes, and it sits oddly beside the “Burhanpur” that a Maratha chronicle placed near Manikpur in 1738. [n11 Q2; n40 A15] The dates of the dargahs are the survey’s.

A user-compiled listing names the Hazrat Kutti Shah Baba dargah, the “Pahad wali dargah”, the dargah on the hill, near Nallasopara West station, with a mosque on the hill. It is a low-reliability source, and no history, date or urs calendar was found for any of the Nallasopara dargahs. [n10 §5]

What the notes cannot see

That is all the documents say about Sopara village in the twenty-first century. The notes found no account of who lives in Sopara gaon now, how many of its people are Kupari, Hindu Samvedi, Muslim or newcomers, whether the old two-storey carved houses round the market place survive, whether Ramkund still draws bathers in Shravan, whether the Bhatela pond still holds water, or what has happened to the fields of Sonarbhat. [n31 §3 Gaps; n42 §6 Gaps] A Marathi report of 15 November 2026 warned of water scarcity and of the region’s historic lakes choked with garbage and water hyacinth; it does not name Sopara’s ponds, and it would be wrong to assume it means them. [n06 §4; eSakal]

What can be said is where Sopara gaon sits. It is in Nallasopara West, a short way from the station, on the old garden ground between Nirmal and the railway. To the south is Gass, still a largely Catholic garden village whose people fought off a dumping ground in 2025. To the north-west is Nirmal, with its temples, its church and its November fair. To the east, across the line, is the dense new city of Nallasopara East. The ground the 1882 visitor walked from Manikpur, “a bare flat, formerly flooded during the rains”, is built over. [n42 §1, §6; n31 §3]

What is still unknown

A chapter that has tried to be honest about its evidence should end by listing what it could not find.

  • The edicts. Where the other slabs of Ashoka’s set may lie; the exact findspot of the 1882 slab (Bhatela pond, or a Muslim graveyard by Ramkund, or both).
  • The stupa. Indraji’s own reading of the coin; the date and report of Chauhan’s excavation; the Survey’s monument number; what became of the 2012 pillar.
  • The port. Where Dedar, “the old Sopara landing”, lay; when large ships stopped coming; the date and present home of the 2018 anchor.
  • The silent centuries. Any find of the fourth to ninth centuries on the island.
  • The church of St Thomas. Where it stood; where the four martyrs were buried before Odoric took their bones.
  • The temples. Which of the Shilahara temples stood where, and whether the Muslim destruction of the early fourteenth century, which the Gazetteer reports only as “said”, happened at all.
  • The Portuguese island. A conversion record for Sopara or Nala; which Tombo entries, if any, cover Sopara village and Gass.
  • The village of Nala. Its population in any census; its people besides the Lad Vanis; how it relates to the “Nil hill” of 1728 and the “Neela” of the railway.
  • The twentieth century. Any census of Sopara village after 1882; when its gardens began to be built over; the life of its Christian, Hindu and Muslim households.
  • Today. The state of its ponds, its old houses and its fields in 2026.

These are not small gaps. Some could be filled by records that exist but were out of reach: Indraji’s journal paper, the Archaeological Survey’s files, the Thana District Gazetteer of 1982, the census village tables, the Marathi press, and the parish books of Nirmal and Gass. Others could be filled only by the people of Sopara themselves.

Into the halt

In the 1880s the trains went past Sopara without stopping, on an embankment that was drying out its creek, and the island’s plantains and betel leaf went south to Bombay from Virar. In 1882 the Gazetteer wished for a station there. When one came, at a date nobody has yet pinned down, its name was not Sopara. An early timetable, as a railway magazine of 1923 remembered it, called the halt “Neela”. The halt stood by the village of Nala. Over the next century, the two names would join, and the village of 1,700 people would become a city of hundreds of thousands. That is the next chapter.
