Period covered: 15 August 1947 to November 2026

In this chapter. The town in the republic: councils, a corporation, the creek, the green belt and the townships of the east.

On 20 February 2024 a ferry left the old fort jetty at Vasai, the Killa Bandar of the fishing boats, and crossed the creek to Bhayandar. It was a roll-on, roll-off vessel, a Ro-Ro, built to carry about a hundred passengers and thirty-three vehicles, run by Suvarnadurg Shipping & Marine Services under the Maharashtra Maritime Board, and it was the second such service in the metropolitan region after the one between Ferry Wharf and Mandwa. The member of parliament for Thane, Rajan Vichare, inaugurated it. The tender had been floated seven years earlier, in 2017, at about Rs 6.2 crore, as part of the national Sagarmala programme of coastal works. [n52 §3; Latestly/PTI; Lokmat Times] Five days later, on 25 February, the service was halted: one of the vessels had struck the concrete jetty at Vasai. [n52 §3; Lokmat Times]

It is a fitting scene with which to begin the modern history of the town, because it joins the oldest Vasai to the newest. The fort jetty is where the Portuguese city of Baçaim met the sea, where the customs house of British Bassein stood, and where, through the twentieth century, the buses to the fort still ended. [A1 §XIV] The creek it faces had been crossed by a railway bridge since 1864 and by a road bridge since sometime in the twentieth century, both a long way upstream to the east. For eighty years after independence, the quickest road from the old town to the city of Bombay, and later Mumbai, was a detour inland, round the head of the creek and back down the highway. The Ro-Ro was an attempt to go straight across the water again, as the boats of the Bassein ports had gone for centuries; its bump against the jetty, and the patient revival that followed, are a small picture of how Vasai has met modern times. By the autumn of 2026 the ferry was running on a new timetable, a road-and-metro bridge across the same water had been proposed in detail, and a metro line that would cross the creek had been approved by the state government. [n52 §3]

This chapter follows Vasai, the old taluka town and its railway twin on the east side of the line, from independence to November 2026. It tells the town as it stood in 1947; the two towns into which it grew, Vasai on the side of the fort and Navghar-Manikpur on the side of the station; the councils that governed them and the corporation that swallowed them in 2009; the elections from the Vartak years to the vote-count argument of 2024; the railway, the creek bridges, the ferry and the metro; the townships and industrial estates of Vasai East; the schools and colleges; the festivals; the floods; the police; the communities of the town and of the Kupari and Vadval parishes around it; and the town as it stood in November 2026, under a new mayor. Chapter 17 has told the town and the fort from their beginnings, and its last sections (XV to XIX) give the twentieth-century town in outline; Chapter 21 has told the villages one by one; Chapters 14, 14 and 15 tell the history of the whole taluka in these decades; Chapter 29 tells Virar’s. Where those chapters have told an episode at length, this one points to them and adds what is particular to Vasai.

A warning first, of the kind every chapter of this part of the book has had to give. For the modern town the record is thin in exactly the places where a town’s history usually begins. The research behind this book found no population figure for Vasai town or for Navghar-Manikpur for 1951, 1961, 1971 or 1991; no founding year for either municipal council in its modern form; no list of either council’s presidents; no account of either council’s last election before 2009; no founding dates for most schools and hospitals; and no history of the town’s Muslim, Parsi, Jain or Gujarati families after 1882. Searches in English and in Marathi, including for नगराध्यक्ष, “council president”, returned nothing usable. [n52 §1 Gaps, §2 Gaps, §5 Gaps; n28] Where the record is silent this chapter says so, and says what the record holds around the silence.

1947: the taluka town

What did Vasai look like on 15 August 1947? No document describes it on that day, and no local account of independence was found. [n28; n52] The nearest fixed points are a census taken sixteen years before and one taken four years after.

The census of 1931 counted 12,689 people in the Bassein municipality, of whom 7,392 were Hindus and 1,148 Muslims; the Christian figure is illegible in the scan that survives. [A1 §XIII; n21] Fifty years earlier, in 1881, the town had held 10,356. It had grown by only about a fifth in half a century. The municipality itself was old: it had been set up by a Government Resolution of 15 March 1864, the same year the railway came, and took in the old fort, the modern town in the village of Malonde, the survey villages of Dhauli (the Remedy parish), Sandor and Mulgaon, and the gardens between Bassein and Papdi. [A1 §XII; n21; Gaz XIV p. 36] Its buildings were those of a British taluka headquarters: the mamlatdar’s office of 1869, the dispensary of 1872, the school-house of 1878, the public garden of 1877 and the Robertson markets with the municipal office above them. [A1 §XII]

The census of 1951 gives the taluka. Bassein taluka held 133,523 people, of whom 41,130 were counted as urban, “with Bassein town as the 41,130 urban part” in the reading of the researchers who opened the table; how that urban figure was bounded, and whether it took in the railway settlement at Manikpur, is not stated. [n21] Between 1911 and 1951 the taluka had grown by about two-thirds, from 79,978, though the researchers warn that its boundaries and area had changed in between. [n21] So the Vasai of 1947 was a taluka town of perhaps a few tens of thousands, by far the largest place on this coast, larger than the station town of Virar and much larger than the village of Nala beside ancient Sopara.

It was a town in two pieces. The old town stood in Malonde, half a mile inland from the fort walls, with its market, offices and parishes; four and a half miles to the north-east, on the far side of the old channel that had once made Bassein an island, stood the railway station at Manikpur, “Bassein Road”, with its own small settlement. A metalled road joined them. [A1 §XII, §XIV; n32] Around both lay the garden belt: the Christian Vadval villages immediately around the fort, the Brahmin and Vani quarter of Papdi, the Kupari villages to the north and north-west, the Koli shore, the salt pans on the north bank of the creek, and to the east the paddy and the hills of Tungar. [A1 §XIII; A2] Electric local trains had run through Bassein Road since 1936, when the lines from Borivali to Virar were electrified. [A1 §XV; n28] On 5 November 1951 the railway company that had built the line through Bassein, the Bombay, Baroda and Central India, became the Western Railway. [n28; Wikipedia: Western line]

Of the town’s own government in these years almost nothing is known. Bassein remained the headquarters of a taluka of Thana district, and its seat in the state legislature was called “Bassein” until 1972. [n28; n52 §2] When the Bassein municipality became the Vasai municipal council, under what law and in what year, is not recorded in any note; nor is the list of its presidents. [A1 §XVI; n52 §1 Gaps] On 1 May 1960 the state of Maharashtra was formed, with Thana as one of its twenty-six districts; in the division of the old Bombay State forty-seven villages and three towns of Umbargaon taluka, far to the north of Vasai, went to Gujarat. [n28; Wikipedia: Thane district] Nothing in the notes records how the Samyukta Maharashtra movement, which won that state, was felt in Vasai. [n28]

The quiet decades

The history of the Tata Institute of Fundamental Research describes the whole Vasai–Virar region as “mainly agricultural until the late 1980s”. [n52 §1; TIFR] Chapter 14 tells those decades for the taluka: the socialist members of the 1950s, the Vartak years, the parishes and the schools, the kawadis on the afternoon trains, the young people leaving for Bombay and the Gulf. For the town itself the dated threads are few, and most of them run through politics, the church and the schools, told below.

One number marks the end of the period. In 1981 the census counted 34,900 people in Vasai town, against 23,303 in Virar, and found that the whole region held fewer than 250,000; Agashi, with more than ten thousand, was the only village over that size. [n52 §1; TIFR] Between 1931 and 1981 the town had nearly tripled, most of that growth after independence. [A1 §XV] And the census of 1981 recorded something stranger, which Chapter 14 discusses: many towns and villages of the region had actually lost people in the decade before. [n06 §1; TIFR] Whether Vasai was among them is not stated.

The town in the 1980s, in a Marathi encyclopedia

A Marathi encyclopedia entry written in the 1980s, the Marathi Vishwakosh, is a rare snapshot in the notes of Vasai town as a place to live between independence and the corporation. [n62] It gives the town 52,398 people in 1981 and describes the municipality as old, founded in 1864, and responsible for water, public health, drainage and roads. The taluka, it says, led in medical services: about 70 per cent of the rural area had medical care. The town had seven hospitals, a family-planning centre and seventeen public dispensaries, as well as private ones; twelve primary schools, five higher secondary schools, two junior colleges, two vocational institutions, a college and a training college, and a government ashram school for tribal children; three cinemas, three auditoriums and two public libraries. Its sights were the Lokmanya Tilak Udyan (a park), the Narveer Chimaji Appa Maidan and the Tamtalav market. [n62]

Its economy, the entry says, rested on fishing; salt pans on 1,200 hectares; the red-yellow laterite clay used for bricks, pipes, tiles and pots and sent to Mumbai and other cities; oil that had recently been found in the sea off Vasai; bauxite in the Tungar range; the Bassein Electric Supply Company, “a major power producer”; and bananas, with dried-banana (sukeli) gardens at Agashi. It also records “a vast recent growth of small industries”. [n62] These are the figures of a town before the corporation of 2009 and the high-rise townships described below.

Two towns: Vasai and Navghar-Manikpur

The two halves of 1947 grew apart in the decades that followed, and the numbers show it more clearly than anything else in the record.

Vasai on the fort side

The census figures for Vasai town are two. It had 34,900 people in 1981 and 49,346 in 2001, the second figure coming from an appraisal document of the Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs. [n52 §1; TIFR; MoHUA appraisal] Wikipedia’s Vasai page gives 49,337, labelled “2007”; the figure is so close to the census count that the researchers judged it probably the 2001 figure mislabelled, though that is unverified. [n52 §1; Wikipedia: Vasai] On these figures the old town grew by about forty-one per cent in twenty years. In the same twenty years Virar grew roughly five-fold, from 23,303 to 118,945. [n52 §1 Inferences] The 1991 figure for Vasai town was not found. [n52 §1 Gaps]

Forty-one per cent in two decades is growth, but it is the growth of a town keeping its old shape. The fort side of Vasai was hemmed in: by the creek to the south, the sea to the west, and on its landward sides by the parishes and garden villages of Sandor, Papdi, Remedy, Merces, Giriz, Pali and Chulne, whose land was still largely wadi and paddy. [A2 Part One] It was in those villages, and in the Kupari villages beyond them, that the green-belt movement of 1989 was strongest (below). The research found no planning document saying so directly, but the result is plain in the census: the old town grew, slowly, and the new city went elsewhere.

Navghar-Manikpur on the station side

It went east, to the railway. The settlement round Bassein Road station had grown into a town of its own, Navghar-Manikpur, named after two villages: Navghar, the “new houses”, and Manikpur, the old Jesuit parish of St Michael, whose Portuguese name had been Porim or Purim. [A1 §XIV; A2] By 2001 Navghar-Manikpur had about 116,700 people, more than twice the population of Vasai town itself. [n52 §1; Wikipedia: Navghar-Manikpur]

The Wikipedia page for Navghar-Manikpur, whose figures the researchers took as given, describes that population: fifty-three per cent male and forty-seven per cent female, a literacy rate of about eighty-three per cent, about eleven per cent of the population under six years old, and among its speakers about 23.78 per cent Gujarati and 18.24 per cent Hindi. [n52 §1; Wikipedia: Navghar-Manikpur] Each of those figures says something. A town with more men than women and a large share of small children is a town of young migrant families and of men who came first to work. A town where nearly a quarter of the people speak Gujarati and nearly a fifth Hindi is a town that has filled up from outside the Marathi coast. The figures are not from the census tables themselves, which were not reached, and they should be read as indications rather than measurements. [n52 §1]

Put the two towns side by side and the shape of modern Vasai appears. The old town of the fort and the parishes took a modest share of the growth that followed 1988. The town at the station, close to the trains, the national highway and the new industrial estates, took the migrants. [n52 §1 Inferences] The PIN codes still divide Vasai West from Vasai East, and so, to a degree no census measures, do language, building and work. [A1 §XIX]

The corporation’s numbers

For the area that became the city corporation, the census series runs 219,868 in 1981, 365,480 in 1991, 693,350 in 2001 and, in 2011, either 1,343,402 or 1,222,390, depending on whether the wider urban agglomeration or the corporation itself is counted. [n06 §1; Wikipedia: VVCMC; citypopulation.de] The growth rates that follow from these totals, about sixty-six per cent in 1981–91 and about ninety per cent in 1991–2001, are the researchers’ own arithmetic. [n06 §1 Inferences] A Business Standard article of 2014 gave a 1971 regional figure of 194,262 and decadal growth rates that do not match the census totals, and should be treated with caution. [n06 §1; Business Standard, 11 Apr 2014] No census was held in 2021, and no reliable count of Vasai or of the city exists for 2026. [n06 §1]

The census of 2011 gives the religious make-up of Vasai taluka, which by then was more than nine-tenths urban: about 78.05 per cent Hindu, 8.45 per cent Muslim, 8.25 per cent Christian (about 110,800 people), 2.53 per cent Buddhist and 1.75 per cent Jain. [n34 §8; Wikipedia: Vasai taluka; CityPopulation.de] The Christian share, high for the Mumbai region, is the inheritance of the Portuguese centuries; but in absolute terms the Christians, who had been a fifth of the sub-division in 1881, were now a twelfth. [A1 §XIII; n34 §8] The census does not count castes or the communities this book follows: there is no figure for Kuparis, Vadvals, Samvedi Brahmins, Pachkalshis, Kolis, Agris or Bhandaris in 2011, in Vasai or anywhere. [n34 §8; n41 §13]

The councils

Through the boom years Vasai was governed, locally, by two municipal councils, nagar parishads: the Vasai Municipal Council on the fort side and the Navghar-Manikpur Municipal Council on the station side. With the Virar and Nallasopara councils they were the four that would be merged into the Vasai-Virar City Municipal Corporation. [n52 §1; n06 §1; VVCMC About] That much is certain.

Almost everything else is not. The Vasai council was the successor of the municipality of 1864, one of the oldest civic bodies on the north Konkan coast; but the year in which it was reconstituted as a modern municipal council, the law under which that was done, and how its boundaries changed between 1882 and 2009 are not recorded in any note. [A1 §XVI; n52 §1 Gaps; n28] The founding year of the Navghar-Manikpur council was not found at all. [n52 §1 Gaps] Neither council’s list of presidents, nagaradhyakshas, was found, in English or in Marathi; nor were the results of either council’s last election before the merger. [n52 §1 Gaps, §2 Gaps] The researchers looked specifically for a link between the Vartak family, which dominated Vasai’s politics in the 1960s and 1970s, and the council presidency, and found none. [n52 §2 Inferences] For Virar’s council two presidents’ names survive in later sources; for Vasai’s and Navghar-Manikpur’s, not one. [V3]

Two leads exist and were not followed to the end. A doctoral thesis on local self-government in Vasai, by T. T. Pereira (2018), and another on education in Vasai from 1947 to 1987, by R. J. Gonsalves (2016), were identified in India’s national thesis repository but not opened. [n34 §8 Gaps] They are the first place a future historian of the Vasai councils, and of the town’s schools, should look, together with the Maharashtra Government Gazette and the council records themselves.

So the bodies that governed Vasai through the years in which the town changed most, that approved, or failed to stop, the building of Vasai East, and that dealt with the water, the drains and the roads of the 1990s, exist in this book only as names and an ending. [n52 §1]

2006–2009: the merger

The ending is dated, and it was resisted. As early as 28 September 2006, a United News of India report said that the four councils would be merged with “nearly 45 villages” into a Vasai-Virar corporation; the Shiv Sena asked that an administrator be appointed to the four councils and that the villages not be pressured into joining. [n52 §9; OneIndia/UNI, 28 Sep 2006] Three years later it happened. By the corporation’s own account the Vasai-Virar City Municipal Corporation was established on 3 July 2009, from the four councils and fifty-three villages. [n52 §1; n29 §2, §5; VVCMC About] Wikipedia’s mirrors and two environmental-clearance documents of 2014 and 2017 give 2010; the researchers’ best reading, offered as an inference, is that 3 July 2009 is the notification and 2010 the first elected body. [n29 §5; n52 §1] The first mayor, Rajeev Patil, took office on 28 March 2010 by one table, on 1 June 2010 by another; Chapter 29 sets out the conflicting lists of mayors, and Chapter 16 tells the corporation’s first years. [V3; 15; n26 §3]

The fifty-three villages were the ones that objected. By Wikipedia’s account, forty-nine of them opposed inclusion, led by Vivek Pandit’s Gao Bachao Andolan Samiti, the “save the villages” committee; the same page alleges that Hitendra Thakur “allegedly influenced police” to arrest and assault Pandit during the protest, an allegation without a finding. [n29 §2; n26 §2; Wikipedia: Hitendra Thakur] Many of those villages were the Christian and Hindu garden villages around Vasai town, the Kupari and Vadval parishes among them, which had fought the green-belt rezoning twenty years before. [A2; K5] In 2011 a state notification excluded twenty-nine of the fifty-three villages, followed by a court case, according to a blog of that September. [n29 §3; vasairoad blog] A later search summary says the state reversed the exclusion in August 2025, and that four petitions over the inclusion of villages had reached the Bombay High Court, threatening to delay the civic polls; that summary has not been verified. [n52 §9; FPJ (search summary)] Chapter 15 tells the protest of 2009.

For Vasai town, the merger meant the end of 145 years of a civic body of its own. The Bassein municipality of 1864 became a set of wards in a corporation of more than a million people, whose headquarters, by the reports of the 2026 floods, lay where floodwater could reach it. [A1 §XVI; n52 §7]

The seat of Bassein and Vasai, 1952–2024

For the whole period the town gave its name to a seat in the state legislature: “Bassein” from 1952 to 1972, and from 1978 the Vasai constituency, number 133. [n52 §2; n26 §1; Wikipedia: Vasai Assembly constituency] Its history falls into three movements: the Vartak years, a short unsettled middle, and the long Thakur era, which ended in November 2024 by a few thousand votes whose exact number is itself disputed.

The socialists and the Vartaks

The first two elections of the republic went to a socialist. Sadanand G. Warty won Bassein in 1952 for the Socialist Party and in 1957 for the Praja Socialist Party; the names come from the results sections of a Wikipedia page whose table is incomplete, and the party labels need checking. [n26 §1; Wikipedia: Bassein Assembly constituency] Of Warty himself nothing more is recorded. Chapter 14 sets the socialist decade in its setting.

In 1962 the seat passed to the man who would define Vasai’s politics for a generation. Hari Govindrao Vartak, “Bhausaheb”, born on 9 February 1914, won for the Congress with 36,498 votes, 59 per cent, against 20,430 for Warty, now of the PSP. The 1967 winner appears in the mirrored table only as “B. Vartak (INC)”; in 1972 Vartak won with 40,589 votes, 58.65 per cent, against 28,617 for Pandharinath R. Choudhari of the Samyukta Socialist Party. [n52 §2; Wikipedia: Bassein Assembly constituency (search summary)] These figures rest on a single mirrored page, never opened directly, whose infobox oddly calls the constituency abolished in 1972; they need checking against the Election Commission’s statistical reports. [n52 §2] Vartak became a cabinet minister in Vasantrao Naik’s government, holding public health, salt land and fishing, and later revenue, and received the Padma Shri in 1991. He died on 7 October 1998. [n52 §2; n26 §2; Wikipedia: Hari Govindrao Vartak] A Congress publication of 1963 listed him among freedom fighters, though no details of his part in the national movement were found. [n26 §2]

His lasting monument is not a building of government but a campus: the Vidyavardhini education society, which he founded in 1970, and its colleges at Vasai Road (below). [n52 §5; n41 §12; avcjr.ac.in]

The Vartak name continued through Tarabai Vartak, born at Bordi on 19 August 1926, the daughter of Atmarampant Save, who married Narsinh Vartak in 1944. In 1972 she became president of the Thane Zilla Parishad, the first woman to head a zilla parishad anywhere in India. [n26 §2; n41 §12; Wikipedia: Tarabai Vartak] Her relationship to Bhausaheb Vartak, beyond the shared family name, is not stated in the sources reached. [n52 §2 Gaps]

The unsettled middle, 1978–1985

When the renamed Vasai constituency first voted in 1978, it went against the Congress. Pandharinath Choudhari, Vartak’s opponent of 1972, won for the Janata Party, beating Tarabai Vartak by 17,751 votes. In 1980 she turned the result round, beating Choudhari by 6,295 for the Congress (Indira), and became Minister of State for Social Welfare. In 1985 Dominic Gonsalves of the Janata Party beat her by 3,937. [n52 §2; n26 §1–2; Wikipedia: Vasai Assembly constituency] Neither Choudhari nor Gonsalves has a biography in the notes; Gonsalves’s name, in a seat where about a fifth of the voters had once been Christian, is suggestive, but the sources say nothing about his community or career. [n26 §1 Gaps] Tarabai Vartak founded schools and colleges in Vasai–Virar and died on 29 May 2008. [n26 §2; n41 §12]

Four members in twelve years, from three parties: the researchers read it as the politics of a still-agrarian seat, Christian and Hindu, before one man took hold of it. [n28 Inferences]

The Thakur era, 1990–2024

In March 1990 a twenty-nine-year-old Congress candidate from Virar, Hitendra Thakur, a Vartak College graduate and president of the Vasai Taluka Youth Congress since 1988, won Vasai by 46,468 votes over Dominik John Gonsalvis of the Janata Dal. [n52 §2; n29 §1; Wikipedia: Hitendra Thakur] He won again in 1995, 1999 and 2004 as an independent, by 33,575 over Deepak Gawhankar of the Shiv Sena, by 50,428 over the same man (spelled Gavankar), and by 67,917 over Vivek Pandit, then the Shiv Sena’s candidate. [n52 §2; n29 §1–2] In 2004, by Wikipedia’s account, he helped the film star Govinda, another Vartak College man, win the Mumbai North parliamentary seat. [n29 §2] His local party, the Vasai Vikas Mandal, became the Bahujan Vikas Aghadi, whose president he became in 2008. [n29 §2] Why he left the Congress between 1990 and 1995 is not explained. [n29 §1 Gaps]

Chapter 15 tells how the Thakurs rose and Chapter 29 tells Virar as their base; neither story needs repeating here. What must be repeated, because it concerns the man who represented Vasai for most of thirty-four years, is the terms in which the sources allow it to be told. His brother Jayendra “Bhai” Thakur was tried for thirty-four years in the TADA case over the murder of the builder Suresh Dube on Nallasopara station on 9 October 1989, and acquitted by a special court in Pune on 24 May 2023; Dube’s family said it would appeal. [n26 §2; n29 §4; FPJ, 24 May 2023] A search summary of the Supreme Court judgment in the earlier stage of that case lists Hitendra Thakur among the accused, but his final status in it is not clear in the sources; his later career shows he was not convicted. [n29 §1] His 2014 election affidavit listed eight criminal cases, with charges framed in seven, and no convictions. [n26 §2; n29 §3] In 1995 UCA News reported the green-belt movement’s charge that its main work was opposing “Thakur’s land acquisitions, unauthorized, ill-planned construction and illegal land transactions”, which was the movement’s claim as reported. [n29 §1] In 2015 he declared assets of Rs 158 crore, the highest of any candidate in that year’s civic polls. [n26 §2; ADR] The record, in short, is of long electoral dominance, persistent accusation, and no conviction of a member of the family in any case where a court outcome is stated.

2009: Vivek Pandit

The one break in the Thakur run came in October 2009, months after the merger fight. Vivek Raghunath Pandit, “Bhau”, standing as an independent with Shiv Sena support, beat the BVA’s candidate, Narayan Mankar, by 16,798 votes. [n52 §2; n29 §2; n26 §1] (Some accounts describe the result as Thakur’s own defeat; the constituency table names Mankar as the BVA candidate, and Chapter 29 follows the accounts that speak of Thakur losing the seat. What is not in doubt is that the BVA lost Vasai.)

Pandit was an outsider of a particular kind. Born at Dahanu on 23 August 1957, he had left Mumbai for rural Vasai and begun work at Dahisar village, and in 1982, with his wife Vidyullata, founded the Shramajeevi Sanghatana, an organisation to free bonded labourers in the Adivasi villages of the district. Wikipedia credits him with an International Anti-Slavery Award in 1999 and books including Bandhmukthi; at some point not dated in the sources he chaired a state committee reviewing tribal areas, with the status of a minister. [n26 §2; Wikipedia: Vivek Raghunath Pandit; Indian Express, 1998] He was the man who had led the villages against the corporation that summer. Chapter 15 tells the election and Chapter 21 the Adivasi villages where his organisation worked.

In 2014 Thakur took the seat back from Pandit by 31,896 votes, 97,291 to 65,395, and in 2019 he beat Vijay Govind Patil of the Shiv Sena by 25,995. [n52 §2; n29 §3] In the same years his son Kshitij held the new Nallasopara seat, created in the delimitation of 2008, and at the parliamentary level the BVA had once, in 2009, won the Palghar Lok Sabha seat, into which Vasai now fell with Dahanu, Vikramgad, Palghar, Boisar and Nallasopara. [n26 §1; n52 §2]

23 November 2024: Sneha Dube Pandit, and how many votes

In the assembly election counted on 23 November 2024, Vasai went to the Bharatiya Janata Party for the first time. Its candidate, Sneha Dube Pandit, beat Hitendra Thakur. [n52 §2; n26 §1; ECI] The Deccan Herald’s headline across the region was “saffron wave hits bastion of Thakurs”. [n06 §3]

By how much she won, and with how many votes, is less simple than it should be, and the conflict is worth setting out because a book should not quietly choose. The Election Commission’s round-wise table gives her margin as 3,153 votes, with 77,553 votes polled for her. [n06 §3; n26 §1; ECI AC 133, round-wise] Wikipedia’s constituency page gives the margin as 3,275; Lokmat reported “over 3,700”; and her own Wikipedia stub quotes an India Today headline beginning “93745 defeats BVA’s Hitendra Vishnu Thakur”, which, if it is a vote total, conflicts with the 77,553 of the round-wise table. [n52 §2; n29 §4; Lokmat Times; Wikipedia: Sneha Pandit] The researchers suggest that the 93,745 may be a final total and the 77,553 a figure from an interim round of counting; that is a guess, and the conflict was not resolved against the Election Commission’s final result, which was not opened. [n52 §2] This book therefore gives the margin as 3,153 on the Election Commission’s round-wise count, the figure the notes advise using, and leaves her vote total as unsettled between 77,553 and 93,745. [n06 §3; n52 §2]

Whatever the exact number, the margin was the closest in Vasai since Dominic Gonsalves’s 3,937 in 1985. [n52 §2 Inferences] Of the new member herself the sources say little: her 2024 affidavit describes her as a graduate; her assets, cases and career before 2024 were not read; her relationship, if any, to Vivek Pandit is not established, and should not be assumed; and her surname suggests a north Indian origin, which is an inference and not a sourced fact. [n26 §2] The research notes point to a possible link between her and the family of the murdered builder Suresh Dube, but the note they refer to does not in fact state one (it names only Dube’s daughter, Neha), so no link is asserted here. [n52 §2 Gaps; n29 §4]

No source explains the result. The researchers observe that the BJP’s gains came with a statewide wave, with a larger vote among the region’s north Indian migrants (visible in the names of the BJP’s winners in 2024 and 2026), and with the demolitions and investigations of 2024–25 told in Chapter 16; and they say plainly that no source establishes a causal link. [n06 §1 Gaps, §3 Inferences] Fourteen months later the BVA held the corporation (below), and Vasai found itself with a BJP member of the assembly and a BVA mayor.

Community and party

One question hangs over all this political history: whose politics were they? The sources do not say. A Wikipedia list of the Pachkalshi or Somvanshi Kshatriya Pathare community names both Bhausaheb Vartak and Hitendra Thakur among its members; neither man’s own page states his community, and the researchers declined to assert it. [n41 §12] The community composition of the BVA’s support “is not documented”. [n41 §13] What can be said is that the two longest arcs of Vasai’s modern politics, the Vartak years and the Thakur years, were built on organisations (a college society, a youth congress, a local party) rooted in the taluka itself, and that the shifts against them, in 1978, in 2009 and in 2024, came when outside forces, Janata, a bonded-labour movement with Shiv Sena backing, and the BJP, combined with local grievance. [n26; n28; n06]

Bassein Road to Vasai Road

The railway made Vasai East, and to tell the modern town without it would be to tell half the story. Its beginnings are told in Chapter 17 and Chapter 12; here they are needed only as the foundation.

The station of 1864 and the first local train

The station already existed in 1864, on the first passenger service of the Bombay, Baroda and Central India Railway, from Grant Road to Ahmedabad, on 28 November of that year. It was called Bassein Road, and the code it still carries, BSR, keeps the old name. [n52 §3; Wikipedia: Vasai Road railway station] On 1 November 1865, by Wikipedia’s account, it became the northern terminus of the railway’s first local service: two coach services between Grant Road and “Bassein Road”. In December 1865 the railway sanctioned Rs 6,522 for a second roofed platform. [n52 §3] Other sources date the first regular suburban service to April 1867, from Back Bay to Virar, and the dates conflict; if the 1865 account is right, Bassein Road was the northern end of Bombay’s first commuter train, more than a century before Vasai became a commuter suburb. [A1 §XII; V3; n28]

For the first century the station stood apart from the town, at Manikpur, where the line crossed the old channel. It was the place where the old town’s people, the Vadvals of the cart villages and the Kuparis of the north, came to catch the trains to Bombay, and where the kawadis, the farmer-vendors with their shoulder poles and baskets of plantains and vegetables, boarded the afternoon trains for the city markets. [A1 §XII; K5; 13]

1983: the line to Diva

The next date of consequence came in 1983, and it explains a puzzle in the record. Vasai Road is today a junction: from it a line runs east and then south through Juchandra and Kaman Road, round the head of the creek, to Diva on the Central Railway, and on to Panvel and Roha. The Diva–Vasai section opened in 1983; it was electrified in 1982–83 at 1,500 volts direct current and converted to 25,000 volts alternating current early in 2004, to carry container freight to and from the Jawaharlal Nehru Port. The rest of the line was older or later: Diva to Panvel opened in 1964, Panvel to Apta in 1966, Apta to Roha in 1986. [n52 §3; Wikipedia: Vasai Road–Roha line] The research found no date for the start of passenger service on the line, or for when Vasai Road became a junction for Diva trains. [n52 §3 Gaps]

The same year saw a change on the creek. By Wikipedia’s article on the Vasai Creek railway bridge, the original steel railway bridge was replaced in 1983 by the first concrete bridge, built to its east. [n28; Wikipedia: Vasai Creek Railway Bridge] The two items of 1983, the new line and the new bridge, are both recorded; the researchers could not tie a creek bridge to the Diva line, and advise that the “1983 railway” sometimes mentioned in briefs means the Diva line. [n52 §3 Inferences]

The Diva line made Vasai Road a crossing point between the two suburban systems of Mumbai, the Western and the Central. It also, for most purposes, did not join them: in November 2026 Lokmat reported an effort to ease travel between Panvel and Vasai–Virar, which still needed a change of trains at Kurla or Dadar. [n52 §3; Lokmat (search summary)]

The busiest years

Through the boom the station grew with the town round it. Twelve-coach trains began running between Dadar and Virar on 28 October 1986; the first air-conditioned local trains reached the line in 2018, when regular Churchgate–Virar AC services began on 1 January, eight of twelve of them fast, with Vasai Road and Bhayandar among their stops. [n28; n29 §3; PIB] From 16 April 2013 suburban trains ran on beyond Virar to Dahanu Road; in 2026 the first fifteen-coach services ran on the line, and the fifth and sixth tracks from Borivali, meant to separate suburban from long-distance trains, were about a third built, with completion targeted for 2029. [n29 §3; n06 §2] Chapter 29 tells those works from Virar’s end.

Wikipedia gives Vasai Road seven platforms and nine tracks, and 215,296 passengers in 2012–13, a figure the researchers flag for checking, since it seems low for a junction of this size; whether it is daily, annual or something else is not clear. [n52 §3] No current ridership figure was found. [n52 §3 Gaps]

The station that had been built for the old town now faced the new one. The works of 2025 and 2026 were nearly all on its east side. In March 2025 Western Railway approved a new coaching terminal on the east side of the yard. Under the third phase of the Mumbai Urban Transport Project, the Mumbai Railway Vikas Corporation built an elevated deck on the east side, of which fifty metres of a planned 250, ten metres wide, were done by December 2025, and a foot overbridge forty-two metres long and six wide, completed about January 2026 and reported on 13 February, linking the east side to platforms 4–5 and 6–7. [n52 §3, §4; Curly Tales, 13 Feb 2026; PIB (title only)] The direction of the work is the direction of the town: east, towards the townships and the industrial estates.

When the trains stop

The line through Vasai floods. In August 2025, when red alerts were issued for Mumbai, Thane and Palghar, waterlogging between Nallasopara and Vasai stations and point failures at Vasai junction disrupted the Western Railway. [n29 §4; Deccan Herald, 20 Aug 2025] In July 2026, when services between Vasai Road and Virar were suspended because water covered the tracks near Nallasopara, angry passengers at Vasai station surrounded a loco pilot, in a scene filmed and reported by the Free Press Journal; the article’s date is inferred from an age stamp. [n29 §4; FPJ, Vasai station chaos] Western Railway’s General Manager, Ramashray Pandey, ordered a survey of whether the tracks through Vasai, Nallasopara and Virar could be raised, and the railway blamed the topography of the region, lower than Palghar, its urbanisation and its blocked outlets. [n52 §7; FPJ, 8 Jul 2026] And in November 2026 a Marathi report counted five deaths in twenty-four hours on the Vasai–Vaitarna stretch, four of them in accidents. [n29 §4; Aapla Mahanagar] The railway that had given the town its second half also took its toll.

The creek

Vasai’s southern edge is the creek, and the story of the town’s modern links to Mumbai is the story of its crossings. There were three kinds: the railway bridges, the road bridges of the national highway, and, newest and oldest, the boats.

The rail bridges

The first bridge was the railway’s. The Vasai Creek railway bridge between Bhayandar and Naigaon “originally opened on 28 November 1864”, by Wikipedia’s uncited account, the same day as the first passenger train; it was rebuilt in the 1920s and survives as two steel-girder sections with Panju Island between them, no longer in use, a heritage structure, with two newer parallel railway bridges to its east. [n52 §3; Wikipedia: Vasai Creek] Chapter 17 dates the rebuilding more closely: strengthening between 1896 and 1902, rebuilding from 1920, with the main work from 1923 and pile-driving trials at Panju on 11 September 1923, the new bridges well advanced by August 1925. [A1 §XV; n28] In 1983 the first concrete railway bridge was built to the east (above). [n28] So the creek carries, side by side, three generations of railway bridge: the colonial girders of the 1920s, the concrete of 1983, and a later span.

The road bridges

The road bridges are harder to date. Wikipedia gives no date for the first road bridge between Ghodbunder and Naigaon. An infrastructure site claims a four-lane bridge of the colonial era, of 1928, which the researchers judged weak. [n52 §3; infralens] Earlier chapters of this book, relying on another research note, date the first road bridge on the Mumbai–Ahmedabad highway to 1968; the research for this chapter could not confirm that date in any source and advises against stating it as fact. [13; 15; n52 §3 Inferences; n28] The honest position is that the opening year of the old road bridge is not established; 1968 is a reported date, 1928 a weak one.

What is well documented is its failure. In December 2013 cracks appeared on a girder of the old bridge, and the section was closed; it reopened in mid-2014 with a fifteen-tonne limit, and heavy vehicles were sent the long way round, via Kaman, Kharbao and Bhiwandi. Work on a new bridge began with a foundation in January 2018. It was built by the National Highways Authority of India for about Rs 247 crore: a 918-metre balanced-cantilever four-lane span with 1.33 kilometres of approach roads, 2.25 kilometres in all. It opened to vehicles on 27 March 2023, announced by the Union minister for road transport, Nitin Gadkari. [n52 §3; FPJ, 28 Mar 2023] The Free Press Journal’s headline said it had opened “after a decade of misery”; in those years, the paper said, getting through the Fountain Inn junction at the head of the creek could take up to ninety minutes. [n52 §3; FPJ] An infrastructure database gives 2020 for the new bridge, which conflicts; the newspaper is the better source. [n52 §3] Chapter 16 tells the bridge in the setting of the taluka.

Ninety minutes at a junction, for a decade, was a large part of a commuter’s day, and it was felt most by the industrial estates of Vasai East, whose lorries were the heavy vehicles sent round by Bhiwandi.

The Ro-Ro

The ferry of February 2024, with which this chapter opened, was meant to cut the creek another way: from the fort jetty at Vasai straight across to Bhayandar, about twenty minutes on the water. Its first week ended against the jetty. [n52 §3; Latestly/PTI; Lokmat Times] By 2026 it was running again: a new timetable from 8 August 2026 had sailings from Vasai between 7.30 in the morning and 8.30 at night, and from Bhayandar between 8.00 and 9.15, and in early September extra trips were added for the Velankanni festival. [n52 §3; Vasai Virar Infra on X; Urban Acres] The sources do not record how many passengers or vehicles it carried.

The ferry put Killa Bandar back on a working route for the first time in the modern record. The fishing life of the jetty itself, its boats, catch and families, remains one of the gaps Chapter 17 lists; but from 2024 cars and passengers used the jetty again. [A1 §XIV; n52 §3]

The double-deck bridge

The ferry was a stopgap for a bridge that had been promised for a quarter of a century. A road bridge between Bhayandar and Vasai was first planned in 2000 and approved by the state in 2013. [n52 §3; Mumbai Live] Earlier reports had it as a six-lane crossing costing Rs 1,100 crore. [n29 §3 Gaps; FPJ, 25 Feb 2020] On 4 May 2026 the Mumbai Metropolitan Region Development Authority sent a revised proposal to the state’s urban development department: a double-deck bridge, with Metro Line 13 on the lower deck and the road above, costing about Rs 2,500 crore. It would cut the journey between Bhayandar and Vasai from thirty-nine kilometres by the highway, about ninety minutes, to about five kilometres, about ten minutes. Approvals from the Maritime Board, the Inland Waterways Authority and the coastal zone authority were done; the forest department’s and the salt commissioner’s were pending; and ten families, 119 people, claimed the salt-pan land on which part of it would stand. [n52 §3; Mumbai Live] The salt pans of the creek’s north bank, worked by Agris in the 1880s, were still, in 2026, a question of title. [A1 §XII]

Metro Line 13

On 17 August 2026 the state’s Cabinet Infrastructure Committee, under Chief Minister Devendra Fadnavis, approved Metro Line 13, from Mira-Bhayandar to Virar. It is to run 25.03 kilometres with sixteen stations, 21.78 kilometres elevated and 3.25 underground, at a cost of Rs 17,724.99 crore, which newspapers rounded to Rs 17,725 crore. It would cross the creek on a 4.92-kilometre road-cum-metro bridge by way of Panju Island, with its depot at Chikhal Dongri in Virar West, and was to be built in five years, carrying about 4.49 lakh riders a day by 2031. [n52 §3; FPJ, 18 Aug 2026] The route of the line through Vasai, and the names of its stations, are not in the notes. [n52 §3]

The researchers drew the arc: the railway bridge of 1864 and the road bridge of 2023 are 159 years apart, and the approval of Metro Line 13 in 2026 revived a bridge idea of 2000, twenty-six years old, with the Ro-Ro as the stopgap in between. [n52 §3 Inferences] In November 2026 the bridge was a proposal and the metro an approval. Neither had been begun.

1988: the green belt and the town

The decision that remade Vasai was taken in 1988. In August of that year the state converted the green belt of Vasai–Virar to residential use, as the TIFR history puts it, “contrary to the recommendation of the MMRDA”, and without the roads, drains and water a city would need; builders moved in at once. [n52 §1; n29 §1; TIFR; Sabrang India] In May 1990 the City and Industrial Development Corporation, CIDCO, was appointed planning authority for the sub-region (14 May 1990 is the best-documented date; other sources say 1991). [n29 §1, §5] By 1991 residents had power two days a week, no piped water, no central drainage, and motorable roads on under a tenth of the area. [n52 §1; TIFR]

Chapter 15 tells the response, and it was Vasai’s above all. On 1 April 1989 Fr Francis D’Britto, editor of the Catholic Marathi periodical Suvarta, called a meeting of public figures, and from it came the Harit Vasai Saurakshan Samiti, the “Save Green Vasai” committee; on 1 October 1989 it held a rally of about 35,000, with the playwright Vijay Tendulkar and Kisan Mahta among the speakers; on 26 January 1992 about 100,000 people gathered on the Vasai grounds, and about 100,000 signatures went to the Governor. [n29 §1; Sabrang India] In May 1991 the Times of India, as quoted by Sabrang, described violence against the area’s Christian residents linked to the agitation over tankers drawing well water from the green belt; after talks in which the committee took part, tankers were banned from drawing water there. [n29 §1] CIDCO later revised its plans to keep the green belt. The state, an advocate said at D’Britto’s funeral in 2024, had limited development to 1.5 kilometres on either side of the railway line: “The greenery that you see now is because of the movement.” [n29 §4; FPJ, 25 Jul 2024]

Read against the census, that limit is the whole story of the two towns. The green-belt movement was strongest in the parishes round the old town and in the Kupari villages to the north-west; the zone it could not hold was the strip along the railway. Vasai West grew by forty per cent in twenty years; the station side grew into a town of more than a hundred thousand. [n52 §1] The researchers were careful to call the link an inference; but it is the inference the numbers invite.

Vasai East: Evershine City and the townships

East of the station, on the low ground between the railway and the national highway, the new town of the 1990s went up. The record of how it was built is almost entirely the record of the companies that built it, and it must be read as such.

Evershine City

The largest and best-known of the townships is Evershine City, in Vasai East, built by Evershine Builders. A property portal lists the launch of its project as September 1999 and its possession as May 2011; the developer’s own marketing speaks of a “legacy dating back to 1960”; and listings describe Evershine City as “the biggest and first ever township of Mumbai”, which is promotional language and not a historical claim. [n52 §4; Housing.com; Square Yards; Ghar.tv] A review of the developer calls it a pioneer of gated communities and of “comprehensive townships such as Evershine City in Vasai”. [n52 §4; Ghar.tv] No independent source for the township’s launch year was found, and so this book says “about 1999”, on the portal’s evidence, and no more. [n52 §4 Gaps] (The same developer, with Rustomjee, is named on some pages as a builder of Global City in Virar West; Chapter 29 tells that township.) [V3]

The sectors of Evershine City, Evershine Road and the neighbourhoods named after other developments, Vasant Nagari among them, are the Vasai East that appears in the news. They appear most often under water (below). [n52 §4, §7] Of the people who live there, the census figures for Navghar-Manikpur in 2001, with their large Gujarati and Hindi-speaking shares, are the only indication. [n52 §1] How many people the township holds, what the land was before it, and who first moved in are not recorded. [n52 §4 Gaps] Chapter 15 describes the ground of 1990 east of the line as paddy, salt pans and mangrove-fringed creeks round the low hamlets of Achole, Tulinj, Gokhivare, Waliv and Sativali, and Harit Vasai later said that about half of Vasai’s land is low-lying or marshy; that is a fact about the ground beneath the townships worth keeping in mind. [14; n06 §4; CAT; The Quint]

The neighbourhoods round the townships have names that the old maps do not: Dhumal Nagar, where on 9 October 2026 the corporation’s inspectors seized nearly three tonnes of banned plastic, and where a shopkeeper threatened to set his shop on fire; Bhoidapada and Rajavali, the low settlements on the eastern edge that the rescue teams know best; and the roads, Evershine Road, Sativali Road, the Kaman–Bhiwandi Road, Ambadi Road and Gokhivare Naka, that the flood reports and the victory rally of the Vijayotsav (below) both list. [n52 §6, §7; FPJ, 9 Oct 2026] The old villages are there too, under the new names: Gokhivare, one of the five Christian Vadval cart villages of 1882, and Navghar and Manikpur themselves. [A2; n41 §7]

The industrial east: Navghar, Gokhivare, Waliv and Sativali

Vasai East is not only a dormitory. It is, by the account of the people who run its factories, one of the largest concentrations of small industry in the metropolitan region, and almost none of it was planned by the state.

The fullest account is a Free Press Journal report of 29 May 2019. Over four decades, it said, hundreds of industrial units had moved from cramped premises in Mumbai to larger plots in Vasai; some had been there for more than fifty years. Navghar, Gokhivare, Waliv and Sativali were “large industrial areas”, and the industrialists estimated about 2,500 units and more than 50,000 jobs. The occasion of the report was a meeting of more than two hundred industrialists at Kapur Automobiles in Navghar, to oppose the proposed Virar–Alibaug corridor, whose route threatened their land; the president of the Vasai Industries Association was Anil Ambardekar. [n52 §4; FPJ, 29 May 2019] Another count, quoted on Wikipedia from the Deccan Herald of 12 July 2018, puts about 12,000 industrial units across eastern Vasai. [n52 §1; Wikipedia: Vasai] The researchers suggest the two figures differ because one counts four villages and the other the whole of the east; they could not reconcile them. [n52 §4 Inferences]

These are not estates of the Maharashtra Industrial Development Corporation. The state Pollution Control Board lists Vasai’s units under “Non MIDC area”, and the estates have the names of private developers: the Tara Industrial Estate at Waliv, the Shivam Industrial Estate on Sativali Road, and others whose galas, small units for sale or rent, appear in property listings. [n52 §4; MPCB list; Ghar.tv; A2] No official count of units was found. [n52 §4] “Over four decades” from 2019 would put the first wave of relocation in the late 1970s, before the green belt was opened; if so, the industrial east is older than the residential east. That is arithmetic from a newspaper phrase, not a dated fact. [n52 §4] When the industrial belt began, and the histories of Waliv and Sativali as villages, are not recorded. [n52 §4 Gaps; A2]

The record holds glimpses of the life of the estates in 2025 and 2026, nearly all of them through the police and the power company. On 7 April 2025 the police’s anti-narcotics cell arrested a Nigerian national, aged thirty-seven, at Mahesh Apartment in Vasai East, alleging that he was manufacturing mephedrone; the police said they seized 22.86 kilograms of the drug with raw materials, valued at Rs 11.08 crore, and forty-eight grams of cocaine, and passed the case to Waliv police. No court outcome is reported, and the case is an allegation. [n52 §8; FPJ, 8 Apr 2025] On 3 June 2026 Manikpur police booked three youths after two staff of the state electricity company were allegedly assaulted while restoring power at a failed transformer in Vasai East; this too is an allegation. [n52 §8; FPJ] In September 2026, by a Hindi local outlet’s account, a cross-party agitation in Vasai–Virar protested against smart electricity meters and higher bills. [n06 §5; Metro City Samachar] And in October 2026 Naigaon police arrested four people for allegedly planning about two thousand illegal rooms on land acquired for the Virar–Alibaug corridor, in order to claim compensation, under the supervision of Deputy Commissioner Ashok Virkar; the report was read only as a search summary, and the charge is an allegation. [n52 §8; Lokmat Times (search summary)] The corridor the industrialists of 2019 had opposed was, by then, worth building on in order to be paid to leave.

The broader shift of the economy, from fishing, salt, handloom weaving and orchards to construction, real estate and small industry, is described for the whole region in Chapter 16 and, for the land, in Chapter 30. [n06 §5]

Schools, colleges and hospitals

Before 1970 there was no degree college in Vasai. The researchers read the gap as a sign of how rural the taluka still was. [n52 §5 Inferences] The schools that existed were mostly the Church’s.

The church schools

The oldest dated school in the notes is the Thomas Baptista Primary School, for which one directory gives 1889; the date is unverified. [n52 §5] The Carmelite Convent School at Sandor dates its history from 1939, by its own account read only in summary. [A1 §XV; n28] St Augustine’s High School in Vasai West was founded by the Christian Brothers of India; their own page gives 1972, Wikipedia’s infobox 1970. [n52 §5; CCBI; n28] Thomas Baptista Junior College is dated 1975 by a search summary that was not opened. [n52 §5] Wikipedia’s Vasai page lists other schools without years: the Carmelite Convent at Sandor, Holy Family Convent, J. B. Ludhani High School, G. J. Vartak Vidyalaya, Mount Carmel, Nazareth Convent, Sheth Vidya Mandir, St Anne’s, St Xavier’s, Vidya Vikasini and Podar International. [n52 §5; Wikipedia: Vasai] The list is a map of the town’s communities in itself: convents of the Catholic orders, a school bearing the Vartak name, schools of Gujarati trading families, and a branch of a national private chain. When most of them began is not recorded. [n52 §5 Gaps]

On 9 February 1986 Pope John Paul II, on his Indian journey, made Vasai the first stop of his Bombay visit and spoke at St Augustine’s High School. Vasai, he said, was “where the Christian faith was first proclaimed and Christian communities were first established” in the region, and he named “Saint Gonsalo Garcia, the young native Franciscan of Vasai, who was martyred together with his confreres in Japan”. The Vatican keeps the text. [n38; Vatican, Address at Vasai, 9 Feb 1986] Chapter 14 tells the visit.

Vidyavardhini, 1970

The first colleges were not the Church’s but the Vartaks’. The Vidyavardhini society was founded in 1970 by “the late Padmashri H. G. ‘Bhausaheb’ Vartak”, in its own words, “to offer affordable quality education”. In 1971 it opened three colleges, affiliated to the University of Mumbai, on a campus at K. T. Marg in Vasai Road West: the Annasaheb Vartak College of Arts, the Kedarnath Malhotra College of Commerce and the E. S. Andrades College of Science. [n52 §5; avcjr.ac.in] The names record a partnership: the founder’s family, a Hindu commercial donor and a Christian one. Vidyavardhini’s College of Engineering and Technology received the approval of the All India Council for Technical Education with effect from July 1994; the Bhausaheb Vartak Polytechnic stands on the same campus, its year not found. [n52 §5; vcet.edu.in]

“Vartak College” became the ladder of the taluka. Two of its most famous students came from Virar: the film star Govinda, who took a commerce degree there, and Hitendra Thakur, the politician who would end the Vartaks’ political line. [n27; n26 §2] Chapter 29 tells what that ladder meant for Virar, and how the Thakurs later built a rung of their own.

St Gonsalo Garcia College, 1984

The Church’s college came fourteen years later. St Gonsalo Garcia College was established in 1984, affiliated to the University of Mumbai, and named after the Vasai-born Franciscan martyr of Nagasaki (1556 to 5 February 1597). [n52 §5; n38; Wikipedia: St Gonsalo Garcia College] A search summary, not opened, adds that its trust is the Our Lady of Grace Trust of Papdi, the cathedral parish; that Pope John Paul II laid a foundation stone for it in 1986, presumably on his visit to Vasai; and that the campus moved in 1996. These details are unverified, and the college’s self-study report of 2015 is the place to check them. [n52 §5] Its courses in 2026 ran from commerce and the arts to management, banking, accounting and information technology at bachelor’s and master’s level. [n52 §5] In 2020, in the first wave of the pandemic, the college was turned into a 750-bed COVID care centre. [n52 §5; n38; Wikipedia] Chapter 16 tells the pandemic.

By 2009, Vatican Radio reported, the Diocese of Vasai ran four hospitals, three senior and two junior colleges, twenty-three high schools and fifty-six primary schools, with orphanages, homes for the aged, dispensaries and leprosy homes; another directory, undated, lists more. [n38; Vatican Radio archive; Catholics India] Not all of these are in the town, and the notes do not say which are.

Hospitals

The history of Vasai’s hospitals is almost wholly missing. The dispensary of 1872 is the last hospital whose founding the notes record. [A1 §XII] The Cardinal Gracias Memorial Hospital at Bangli, Sandor, on the Bassein Road in Vasai West, is a Catholic hospital of 147 beds; D’Souza Hospital stands at Padrikhan Wadi; both are known only from directory entries, without founding years. [n52 §5; n38] No history was found of the sub-district hospital at Vasai or of the corporation’s own hospitals. [n52 §5 Gaps] On 6 May 2020, by one report, the hospitals of the whole corporation had seven ventilators and two intensive-care beds. [n29 §4] The corporation’s budget for 2026–27 proposed a 400-bed hospital at Achole, in Nallasopara East. [V3]

The town’s calendar

When the councils disappeared into the corporation in 2009, the researchers observed, the identity of the old town survived chiefly in its festivals: the Kala Krida in December, the Vijayotsav in May, and the church feasts through the year. [n52 §9 Inferences] They are worth telling one by one, because each belongs to a different Vasai, and because two of them remember the same year, 1739, from opposite sides.

The Kala Krida Mahotsav

The largest is the youngest. The Vasai Taluka Kala Krida Mahotsav, the arts and sports festival of the taluka, held its thirty-sixth edition from 26 to 31 December 2025. Its organisers were the Young Stars Trust and the Vasai Taluka Kala Krida Vikas Mandal, its executive president Prakash Vanmali. It held eighty-five competitions, thirty-two in the arts and fifty-three in sport, with more than sixty thousand contestants, and record entries of 245 kabaddi teams and 138 kho-kho teams; kick volleyball and arm wrestling were new that year. Because the model code of conduct for the civic elections was in force, there was no state aid. Its shields were given in memory of the late Mukesh Save. [n52 §6; FPJ, 24 Dec 2025]

Save, who died in Dadar in late November 2025 aged seventy-three, was remembered by the Marathi daily Sakal as the festival’s “architect”, and its main pillar for thirty-seven years; he was described as a former council president of Virar, and his show Mahi Vasai, on the traditions and past of Vasai, had been staged at the twenty-fifth edition. [V3; n20; eSakal, 30 Nov 2025] Who founded the festival, and in what year, were not found. [n52 §6 Gaps] A thirty-sixth edition in December 2025 implies a first edition about 1990; an earlier note made the same calculation and warned that, if the article it read was a year older, the date would be 1989; and a report of a thirty-seventh edition in preparation in October 2026 is consistent with either. [n52 §6; n28 Inferences; V3] This book therefore says that the festival began about 1989 or 1990, by inference from its numbering, and that the founding year needs the organisers’ confirmation. Attribution of its founding to the Thakur family is common in conversation, Chapter 15 notes, but unsourced. [14]

If the inference is right, the festival was born in the same years as the green-belt movement and the Thakur era: the years when the old taluka, its villages filling with strangers, began to stage itself as a single community of the arts and sport.

The Vijayotsav

In May the corporation keeps the anniversary of the Maratha capture of Vasai fort, on 16 May 1739. The 2025 celebration was billed as the “287th” Vasai Vijayotsav, the “victory festival”, a count that runs from 1739 inclusively. It was planned for 10 to 12 May and cut by rain to a single day, 12 May, Buddha Purnima. A torch was lit at the temple of Vajreshwari, the goddess in the hills to the east; a bike rally ran from Vasai Parnaka to the fort by way of Shirsad Phata, the national highway, Vasai Phata, Waliv Naka, Gokhivare Naka and Ambadi Road; and floral tributes were laid at the memorial to Narveer Chimaji Appa, the Peshwa’s brother who commanded the siege. [n52 §6; FPJ, 9 May 2025; Marathi Wikipedia: Vasai] When the festival in its modern civic form was first held is not known; the numbering counts from the event, not from the first celebration. [n52 §6 Inferences, Gaps]

The rally’s route is itself a description of modern Vasai: it begins at the junction of the old town, runs out to the highway and the industrial nakas of the east, and comes back along the road to the fort. The torch is lit at Vajreshwari for a reason the sources give in a secondary form: after the capture, by Wikipedia’s account, Chimaji Appa dedicated the bells of the fort’s churches to the Vajreshwari temple. [n41 §6; Wikipedia: Chimaji Appa (secondary)] Chapter 10 tells the siege and its aftermath, and Chapter 17 the fall of the walled city.

Killa cha Sann

The other memory of 1739 is kept in January, inside the same walls. The feast of St Gonsalo Garcia in the roofless Jesuit church of the fort, Killa cha Sann, “the fort’s festival”, is held on “the first Sunday nearest the neap tide after Christmas”; in 2026 it fell on 11 January, with Fr Peter Fernandez presiding before hundreds of worshippers, while across the creek the church of St Bonaventure at Erangal kept its own feast. Families arrive the evening before, camp in the enclosures, and cook East Indian dishes; once, a man of Erangal told a reporter, people came from Vasai and Versova by boat. [n52 §6; n38; FPJ, 11 Jan 2026; DNA, 12 Jan 2014] Chapter 17 tells the feast in full, and how the Koliwada youth of the fort shore decorate the church, the Vadval parishes round the fort fill it, and the Kupari villages of the north come south for the day. [A1 §XVIII]

So the old town keeps, four months apart, two festivals of the same ruins. In May the corporation, its rally of motorcycles and its torch from Vajreshwari honour the Maratha victory; in January the Catholic parishes honour the Vasai boy who became a saint, in a church the victors left roofless. No source records friction between the two, and none records them being joined. Both are municipal Vasai: the Vijayotsav is organised by the corporation, and the corporation’s deputy mayor in 2026 was a Catholic, Marshal Dominic Lopes. [n52 §6, §9]

Christmas, Ganpati and the other feasts

At Christmas the East Indian Catholic gaothans, the old villages, of Vasai hold crib-making contests and keep their own food and village traditions, among them the fugias, the fried dough balls of the season, by a food writer’s account of Christmas 2025. [n52 §6; Slurrp, 2025] Chapter 21 and Chapter 43 tell the food.

For the Hindu public festivals of the town the notes are thin. “Vasai cha Maharaja”, the Ganesh image of the Anand Nagar Mitra Mandal, is called one of the oldest public Ganesh mandals of Vasai–Virar; and in September 2026, 1,709 Gauri images, the goddess installed beside Ganpati in many Marathi homes, were counted in the city. [V3; n20] The history of Ganeshotsav in Vasai was not found. [n52 §6 Gaps]

The fort on screen and in repair

From the 1990s the fort became a film set, and its ruins are now known to many who have never been to Vasai. Scenes of Josh, with Shah Rukh Khan, of Ram Gopal Varma’s Aag, of Khamoshi: The Musical and of Love Ke Liye Kuch Bhi Karega were shot there; so were the video for “Lean On”, by Major Lazer and DJ Snake, in March 2015, and Coldplay’s “Hymn for the Weekend”, shot in October 2015 and released on 29 January 2016. Bhoomi was shot in April 2017 at Parnaka, and scenes of Munna Michael, Thackeray and Zero on the Madhuban road. [n52 §6; Wikipedia: Vasai] Chapter 17 adds other songs and the friction of shooting in a protected monument. [A1 §XVII]

The fort’s conservation has been argued over since the Archaeological Survey’s work in the early twenty-first century. Conservationists criticised the restoration in 2008 and 2010, and a wall collapsed during restoration in August 2010. [n52 §6; Wikipedia: Vasai] A local history group, Kille Vasai Mohim, the “Vasai fort campaign”, held a sit-in opposite the ASI’s office at the fort over pre-wedding shoots and graffiti, by an undated Deccan Herald report. [n52 §6; Deccan Herald] On 4 June 2026 the ASI’s Mumbai Circle announced the conservation of the ruins of three churches, which it named St Baptist, St Dominic and St Augustine, with a steel gate, wooden gates and fencing, in answer to a grievance filed on the Prime Minister’s portal by Vinod Roshan D’Souza; the Survey said the Franciscan church, the St Gonsalo Garcia church and the Balle Killa, the citadel, had already been done. [n52 §6; FPJ, 4 Jun 2026] Chapter 17 records a conservation tender of 2026 worth Rs 72.65 lakh, opened on 29 May. [A1 §XVII]

Dance, sport and the marathon

Two other kinds of fame came to modern Vasai, and both began in the town’s own halls and grounds. The choreographer Suresh Mukund co-founded the dance group Fictitious with Vernon Monteiro in 2009; renamed the SNV Group, it won the third season of India’s Got Talent in 2011. He then founded Kings United, the Vasai crew that won a bronze at the World Hip Hop Dance Championship in 2015, the first Indian medal there, and the third season of NBC’s World of Dance in 2019, with a prize of a million US dollars. His academy, Kingdom of Art, opened in Vasai in January 2016. [n27; Wikipedia: Kings United; The Week] A Free Press Journal feature of 2019 called Vasai–Virar “India’s dance capital”. [n27] Chapter 16 tells the dance boom.

In sport, the mountaineer and triathlete Harshvardhan Joshi, born in Vasai in 1995, climbed Everest on 23 May 2021 on his first attempt, in a carbon-neutral expedition, days after recovering from COVID-19. [n27; Wikipedia: Harshvardhan Joshi; FPJ] The corporation says it runs twelve grounds, eight developed and four in progress, among them the Hitendra Thakur ground, “Appa Maidan”, at Waliv and a ground at Vasai Road East, with a fee of Rs 1,000 a day for professional matches; a private Chimaji Appa Cricket Ground stands in Vasai West, and the city’s swimming-pool complex has its own Wikipedia page. [n52 §9; VVCMC sports department] The Vasai-Virar Mayor’s Marathon, begun in 2011 with about seven hundred runners, drew about fifteen thousand to its twelfth edition on 8 December 2024, and its thirteenth was set for 13 December 2026, run by the corporation with the Kala Krida Vikas Mandal. [n29 §3–4] The names on the grounds are a political map of their own: a Peshwa general on one, a sitting party leader on another.

The floods

The Vasai of the twenty-first century floods almost every heavy monsoon, and Vasai East floods worst. The record of the floods is one of the fuller records of the modern town, though it is a record of newspaper reports, with figures that rarely agree.

2005 to 2019

Of 26 July 2005, the day of the great Mumbai deluge, the sources found cover Mumbai, where Santacruz recorded 944 millimetres. A Vasai resident quoted later by the Deccan Herald said Vasai had not seen such a deluge then; residents still call it the benchmark. No Vasai toll was found. [n52 §7; n29 §2; Wikipedia: 2005 Mumbai floods; Deccan Herald] Chapter 15 tells the day.

The first Vasai flood with numbers is that of about 8 to 11 July 2018. Western Railway suspended services between Vasai Road and Virar; the National Disaster Response Force rescued twenty-three people at Rajavali, in Vasai East, and 120 were rescued from Bhoidapada; the Deccan Herald reported more than 500 millimetres in thirty-six hours; services on all four lines were restored by 11 July. The rainfall figures conflict between reports. [n52 §7; DNA India and others (search summary)] For the floods of 2019 no Vasai-specific source was found. [n52 §7]

2023 to 2025

In about 21 July 2023, Lokmat reported, Vasai recorded 204 millimetres in twenty-four hours, with rescues by the disaster force. [n52 §7; Lokmat Times (search summary)] On 7 and 8 July 2024 heavy rain across the region flooded Evershine City and Vasant Nagari, by a Deccan Herald report headlined “Welcome to Venice”, whose page could not be opened and whose date was not displayed. [n52 §7; n06 §4]

On 18 and 19 August 2025 Vasai recorded 93 millimetres in twenty-four hours under a red alert, and its urban roads went under. Nallasopara station and the Viva College in Virar were flooded; in Vasai East, Bhoidapada, Rajavali, Evershine Road, Gokhivare, the Kaman–Bhiwandi Road and Sativali Road were worst hit. The corporation rescued about five hundred people, and tractors charged thirty to fifty rupees a ride through the water. [n52 §7; FPJ, 19 Aug 2025] The Deccan Herald put the number temporarily shifted at 497. [n29 §4] In late September 2025 another red alert brought heavy waterlogging. [n29 §4]

The list of places repeats from year to year, and the researchers drew the conclusion: Vasai East floods because both its townships and its industrial estates sit on low, built-over ground, and the railway track at Nallasopara is the single point that stops the western line. [n52 §7 Inferences]

July 2026

The worst flood in the record came in the first week of July 2026, and its numbers conflict more than any.

Water entered homes in Nallasopara East from about six in the morning of 4 July, and waterlogging reached the corporation’s headquarters. [n52 §7; FPJ, 4 Jul 2026] By the evening of 7 July, 125 suburban train services had been cancelled. [n52 §7; FPJ, 8 Jul 2026] Palghar’s Collector, Dr Indu Rani Jakhar, reported an average of 203 millimetres in twenty-four hours on 7 July; ten people had died in rain-related incidents in the district since 1 July; 389 families, 1,261 people, had been moved to relief centres in Palghar, Vasai and Wada talukas; and national and state disaster teams were working in Vasai and Virar. [n29 §4; FPJ, 7 Jul 2026]

The Khaleej Times, a Dubai newspaper, reported on 8 July a much larger picture: 750 millimetres of rain in Vasai–Virar from Saturday to Monday, seventy per cent of the urban area under water, the main Vasai substation submerged and power cut by the electricity company, about 250 people rescued by the disaster force and the fire brigade, and, in its headline, two million residents without power, water or telephones. It quoted Kalash Patil of the Agri Sena, an organisation of the Agri community, blaming unauthorised buildings. The same piece named “Ashwini Bhide, Mumbai’s municipal commissioner”, a role the researchers judged mis-attributed. [n52 §7; Khaleej Times, 8 Jul 2026] The Quint, on 13 July, put the rain-related toll in Vasai–Virar at nine, with bodies found in the pond at Waliv in Vasai East, a man of thirty-two, behind the crematorium in Vasai West, and two at Virar; its own count is internally inconsistent, six plus four more. [n52 §7; n29 §4; The Quint, 13 Jul 2026] Other reports gave 690 millimetres in forty-eight hours, and a Lokmat report more than a thousand millimetres in the first week of July. [n29 §4]

These figures measure different things over different periods, and they should not be combined: an average for a district in a day, a total for a city over a weekend, a total for a week. [n29 §4] Nor can the tolls be compared with earlier floods, since no Vasai toll was found for 2005, 2018, 2019 or 2024. [n29 §4 Inferences; n52 §7 Inferences] What can be said is that July 2026 is the worst flood in the sources reached for this book, that it killed people in Vasai West and Vasai East alike, and that it closed the railway. Western Railway blamed the flooding on construction over salt pans, mangroves and waterholes, on choked culverts and on overflow from the Pelhar dam; recommendations made by NEERI and IIT Bombay in 2018 remained unimplemented. [n06 §2] Chapter 16 tells the flood across the city.

Water and waste

The town that floods in July runs short of water in April. The corporation area needed about 372 million litres a day and received about 230, by a report of 2023; its allocation from the Surya regional scheme, whose water reached Vasai–Virar in November 2023, was 185 million litres a day, of which about 170 arrived. A transformer failure once cut 140 million litres a day. From 4 to 10 April 2026 rotational cuts were imposed across the city, with Navghar in Vasai among the zones; the Kholsapada-2 dam, costing Rs 135 crore and expected to add 22 million litres a day, was eighty per cent complete, and a Kholsapada-1 scheme was being pursued for 48 million litres more. [n52 §7; n06 §4; FPJ; Mumbai Live] On 15 November 2026 a Marathi daily warned that reservoir levels were falling and that the city’s historic lakes were choked with garbage and water hyacinth. [n06 §4; eSakal, 15 Nov 2026]

Waste was the other burden. The corporation sought bids for the bio-mining of more than a million tonnes of rubbish at the Gokhivare dumping ground in Vasai East; whether the work began is not known. [n52 §7; VVCMC tender notice] Eight contractors challenged in the Bombay High Court the cancellation of a three-year waste tender; at a hearing on 9 June 2025 no stay was granted. [n52 §7; FPJ] And in October 2025, the Hindustan Times reported, the corporation withdrew a plan to shift the dumping-ground and sewage-plant reservation from Agarwal Nagar to a plot of about 1,500 acres at Gass, after about nine thousand villagers objected. [n52 §7; HT, Oct 2025] Gass is a Kupari village, the author’s birthplace, and Chapter 26 tells that fight from inside it.

The police

For most of the period Vasai was policed from Thane, and after 2014 from the new Palghar district. On 1 October 2020 the state created the Mira-Bhayandar Vasai-Virar Police Commissionerate, MBVV, bringing the corporation area and its southern neighbour under a police commissioner. The Free Press Journal’s report of its creation lists twenty police stations, among them, from Palghar district, Vasai, Virar, Nallasopara, Manikpur, Waliv, Arnala and Tulinj; another report cited in Chapter 29 says thirteen, and the difference was not resolved. Its first commissioner was Sadanand Date, followed by Madhukar Pandey, and its headquarters sat provisionally in a corporation-owned building at Ram Nagar, Mira Road. [n52 §8; FPJ; V3] In May 2022 the force reported a conviction rate of 89.63 per cent, the highest in the state, which is the force’s own figure. [n52 §8; FPJ, May 2022]

The town’s own stations appear in the ordinary news of 2026. On 23 August 2026 Manikpur police arrested two men over an alleged chain-snatching near Manikpur Talao, the pond of the old Jesuit parish, and recovered a mangalsutra, a marriage necklace, worth about Rs 2.5 lakh. In October 2026 Mumbai’s Crime Branch arrested a thirty-one-year-old tailor from Vasai East for allegedly filming women on trains and morphing the images with artificial intelligence. Around 21 October, police arrested two builders over an eight-storey building in Virar East allegedly put up on forged approvals, affecting 113 flat buyers. [n52 §7, §8; Tripti Praman; NewsBytes; FPJ] All of these are allegations, and no outcome is recorded.

Above the stations, the biggest case in the corporation’s history concerned its own head. On 13 August 2025 the Enforcement Directorate arrested the corporation’s former commissioner, Anil Pawar, and three others in a money-laundering case over the forty-one illegal buildings of Agarwal Nagari in Nallasopara East; in October 2025 the Bombay High Court held the arrest illegal, calling the Directorate’s stand “speculative and based on hazy facts”, and ordered his release. [n52 §8; n29 §4; Law Trend] That is a court outcome on the arrest, not on the allegations themselves. The case is Nallasopara’s, and Chapter 16 tells it.

Of crime and policing in Vasai town between 1947 and 1990, nothing was found. [n52 §8 Gaps]

The peoples of the town

The town of 1881 had been about two-thirds Hindu, a quarter Christian and a little under a tenth Muslim, with a handful of Parsis and Jews; around it lay villages of Christian and Hindu gardeners, Brahmins and Vanis, Kolis, Agris, Bhandaris and, in the eastern hills, Warlis and Katkaris. Chapter 17 describes each of them as the record of the nineteenth century shows them. [A1 §XIII] After 1947 the census stopped counting castes, and after 1988 the town filled with people from everywhere; so what follows is less a census than a gathering of what the notes record about each people of Vasai in the modern period. For most of them it is not much. For two, the Christian Vadvals of the parishes round the fort and the Kuparis of the villages to the north and north-west, it is more, because their parishes kept registers and their priests became bishops.

The Christian Vadvals of the fort parishes

The Christians of the parishes immediately around the town, Sandor, Papdi, Remedy, Merces, Pali, Manickpur and Chulne, are mainly Vadvals, the Christian descendants of the Pachkalshi and Chaukalshi gardening castes, whom the Gazetteer of 1882 called “among the most skilful cultivators in the district” and whose cartmen of Chulna, Manikpur, Gokhivra, Sandor and Barhanpur were “the best axemen in the district”. [A1 §XIII; n41 §3, §7; Gaz XIII-I pp. 97–98, 205] Two of the 1864 municipality’s survey villages were theirs. They were, from the start, citizens of the town as well as its neighbours.

In the modern period the Vadval parishes gave the Church a remarkable line of leaders. Felix Anthony Machado, born at Remedy in 1948, served for nearly a decade in the Roman Curia as under-secretary of the Pontifical Council for Interreligious Dialogue, from 1999 to 2008, helped organise the inter-religious prayer at Assisi in 2002, became Bishop of Nashik in 2008, and from December 2009 to June 2024 was the second Bishop of Vasai, with the personal title of archbishop. [n38; n26 §4; Wikipedia: Felix Anthony Machado; GCatholic] Thomas D’Souza, born at Chulne in 1970, was rector of the shrine of Our Lady of Remedy from 2013 to 2019, was named the third Bishop of Vasai on 9 November 2024 and consecrated on 15 December. [n38; n26 §4; Fides] A Wikipedia list of the corporation’s “notable personalities”, not individually verified, adds Archbishop Elias Gonsalves of Nagpur, also from Chulne; Bishop Malcolm Sequeira of Amravati, born at Giriz in 1961; and Bishop Ivan Pereira of Jammu and Kashmir, from Papdi. [n27; n38; Wikipedia: VVCMC] For two centuries after 1739, the Gazetteer had observed, the parish priests of this coast were mostly Goans or Salsette men educated at Goa. [n34 §8] By 2026 the bishop’s chair of Vasai had been held for seventeen years by sons of the Vadval parishes round the old town.

Their speech has fared worse than their clergy. Vadvali, the Marathi dialect with Gujarati forms that Grierson recorded in 1905 as the speech of about 3,500 people, was put at about 2,500 native speakers around 2000, and in 2026 the Glottolog catalogue listed it, under the name Phudagi, as “threatened”. [n41 §8; Glottolog; Wikipedia: Phudagi language] Wikipedia says, in a passage marked as needing a citation, that it is used less and less as a first language by the young. A relay of the 2017 People’s Linguistic Survey says the community is recording and printing its oral folk songs because it fears the language could disappear. [n41 §8] Whether Hindu and Christian Vadvals speak the same variety is not documented. [n41 §8 Gaps]

Their identity has institutions, though not dated ones: the Vadval Samaj, the Somvanshi Kshatriya Samaj Sangh, a Chaukalshi Vadval community website, and, for the East Indian Christians as a whole, the Mobai Gaothan Panchayat. [n41 §13] An East Indian band, the Ghumat Gang, played at a Vadval East Indian wedding in Vasai in 2018. [n27; East Indian Memory Co.] An opinion piece in Swarajya in 2019, polemical in tone, described the villages as “highly segregated socially and communally”, dominated by Hindu Vadvals, affluent Catholics and Samvedi Brahmins. [n41 §13] No census has counted the Vadvals since about 1920, when Enthoven put the division at about eight thousand; every modern figure is an unreliable estimate. [n41 §13] Chapter 21 tells their villages and Chapter 40 their origins.

The Kuparis: the town as market, school and cathedral

The Kuparis, the Samvedi Christians, were never a people of the town in the way the Vadvals and the Kolis were. Their villages lay to the north and north-west, in the old garden belt of Sopara and Agashi: Nirmal, Bhuigaon, Gass, Nandakhal, Wagholi, Mardes, Gomes Ali and the rest. [A2 Part Two; K6] But the modern history of the Kuparis runs through Vasai town at almost every turn, and it deserves its own telling here.

The town was where they left the land. The community’s own histories, uncited and to be read as tradition, say that farming dominated Kupari life until the late 1960s and early 1970s, and then gave way to engineering, medicine, banking, teaching and later information technology, and to emigration to the Gulf, Europe, the United States and Australia. [n34 §8; Vasai Kupari blog, 2007; Post in Mumbai, 2015] The ladders out of farming stood in and near Vasai: the station at Manikpur and its morning trains; the parish and convent schools; and, from 1971 and 1984, the Vartak colleges and St Gonsalo Garcia College, the second named after the saint whose great shrine stands at Gass, in the heart of the Kupari belt. [n34 §8; n38; n52 §5] The doctoral thesis on education in Vasai from 1947 to 1987, identified but not read, would be the place to test that story against the registers. [n34 §8 Gaps]

The town was also where they fought for the land they still held. The Harit Vasai movement of 1989 was led by a Kupari priest, Fr Francis D’Britto, born on 4 December 1943 at Jeladi, a hamlet of Nandakhal parish, editor of Suvarta from 1983 to 2007. Its great rallies were held at Vasai, and its strength lay in the garden villages that ring the old town, the Kupari villages among them. [n29 §1; n38; K5] D’Britto went on to translate the Bible into Marathi, the Subodh Bible of 2010, which won the state translation award in 2013 and the Sahitya Akademi Award in 2014, reported as the first Bible translation to win a national award; in January 2020 he presided over the 93rd Akhil Bharatiya Marathi Sahitya Sammelan at Osmanabad, the first Christian elected to the post. He died on 25 July 2024, and about three thousand people came to his funeral at Nandakhal. [n38; n29 §4; FPJ, 25 Jul 2024] Chapter 37 tells his life in full.

The town became, in 1998, their cathedral city. When John Paul II erected the Diocese of Vasai on 22 May 1998, carved from the Archdiocese of Bombay, its first bishop was Thomas Dabre, born on 23 October 1945 at Bhuigaon, a village on the community’s own list of Kupari villages; he was ordained on 31 October 1971, became auxiliary bishop of Bombay (the sources give 1988 and 1990, and one says he had charge of Vasai from 1990), and was installed on 15 August 1998 at Our Lady of Grace, Papdi, which became the cathedral, before moving to Poona in 2009. [n34 §8; n38; n26 §4; Catholic-Hierarchy] No source states Bishop Dabre’s community; the link is through his birthplace, and it is an inference. [n34 §8] The same Wikipedia list of notable personalities names Sr Nandita Pereira of Nandakhal, Superior General of the Missionary Sisters of the Queen of the Apostles in Vienna, and Bishop Simon Almeida, named Bishop of Poona on 1 November 2025, whose birthplace is given as Gass by one local source, not confirmed. [n27; n38] Chapter 37 tells these priests and nuns.

The Kuparis’ place in the modern civic order is that of East Indians, and it has been argued over in Vasai. East Indians appear at serial number 341 of Maharashtra’s list of Other Backward Classes, under a government resolution issued in 2006 after a campaign led by Oneil Kinny, Prem Moraes and the Maharashtra Christi Boomputra Bandhav Sangathana; the central list still excluded them in October 2026, when a proposal to add thirty-three Maharashtra groups, “including East Indian Catholics”, awaited the Union Cabinet. Neither source names Kuparis or Samvedi Christians, and a Kupari family, on the researchers’ reading, obtains a certificate as an East Indian. [n34 §8; east-indians.com; FPJ, Oct 2026]

Their speech, Kadodi, has begun to be written down. In 2024 three scholars, Dabre, Dabre and Pereira, published “Suman”, a Kadodi–Marathi–English dictionary of 949 entries and 942 sentence triples gathered from native speakers. [n34 §4; ACL Anthology 2024] Their dress has not fared so well: an article of 29 June 2026 on a local site said the red cap of the men and the red lugda of the women have “largely disappeared from everyday life”, through urbanisation, education, migration and the media, while remaining part of the community’s identity. [n34 §5, §8; All About Vasai Virar, 29 Jun 2026] No census counts the Kuparis; community estimates run from 40,000 to more than 60,000, and the researchers’ defensible statement is “tens of thousands”. [n34 §8]

Three events of the 2020s tie the Kupari villages to the town’s civic life. On 11 August 2023 the church of St Gonsalo Garcia at Gass, built in 1942 and called the diocese’s “major shrine”, was desecrated, and Vatican News reported it. [n38] In October 2025 the corporation withdrew its plan for a dumping ground and sewage plant on about 1,500 acres at Gass after about nine thousand villagers objected. [n52 §7] And on 7 June 2026 the diocese made St Joseph’s Church at Umrale, another Kupari parish, a pilgrim centre for the Year of St Francis declared by Pope Leo XIV, with a plenary indulgence. [n38] In each case the decision, or the protest, ran between the villages and the institutions seated in Vasai: the corporation, the police, the diocese.

The wider Christian town

Not every Christian of Vasai is a Vadval, a Kupari or a Koli. By the twentieth century the town held families from Goa and Mangalore, and after 1988 Catholic migrants from elsewhere, whom the notes do not count. [A1 §XIII] The Diocese of Vasai counted 117,026 Catholics at the end of 2022, about 2.6 per cent of the 4.4 million people of its territory, in forty-nine parishes and four missions, with 185 priests and 458 religious brothers and sisters; other counts give between 122,000 and 137,600, and the researchers warn that the apparent fall from about 136,000 in 2010 reflects different sources and methods rather than a real decline. [n38; n34 §8; GCatholic] The diocese opened the Jubilee year of 2025 on 29 December 2024 at the cathedral in Papdi, and on 4 March 2025 Bishop D’Souza blessed “Mini Jubilee Crosses” for each parish, so that people who could not travel far could make their pilgrimage at home. [n38] In 2025 the parish community’s restoration of the cathedral won a UNESCO Asia-Pacific Award of Merit. [A1 §XIV]

The Christians of Vasai also kept a presence in civic office. The corporation’s fourth deputy mayor was Prakash Rodrigues and its fifth, from 3 February 2026, Marshal Dominic Lopes. [n26 §3] The sources say nothing of either man’s parish or community.

The Kolis of the shore

The Koliwada beside the fort and the fishing village of Killa Bandar are the town’s fishing quarters, and they are Christian, Hindu and Muslim at once: the Koliwada youth decorate the Jesuit church for the fort feast, a church of St Peter with a wayside cross stands in the Koliwada, and so does the Gausia Masjid. [A1 §XIV; n38] The modern record of their fishing is almost empty; the Marathi press names Arnala, Pachu Bandar and Naigaon, not Killa Bandar, as the main fishing harbours of the Vasai coast. [A1 §XIV] The fishers’ struggles of these years, against the Vadhvan port in Dahanu taluka to the north, whose foundation stone was laid on 30 August 2024 and which drew protests of thousands of fishermen, are told in Chapter 16. [n29 §4]

The Hindu communities

The Hindus of Vasai, about four-fifths of the taluka in 2011, are many peoples, and the modern record barely separates them.

The Pachkalshis, the Somvanshi Kshatriya Pathares whose Hindu and Christian branches share the Vadval name, are the community a Wikipedia list attaches to Bhausaheb Vartak and Hitendra Thakur, without confirmation from either man’s own page. [n41 §12] Their remembered part in the Maratha siege of 1739, for which the Peshwa rewarded families with patilships and inam villages, is the deepest local root of the Vijayotsav (above). [n41 §6] Chapter 40 and Chapter 31 tell them.

The Brahmin families of the town, the Palshes, the old priestly community, and the Chitpavan, Karhada and Deshastha Brahmins who came with the Marathas and settled at Papdi, have no modern history in the notes. [A1 §XIII] The Samvedi Brahmins, kin of the Kuparis, lived in the northern garden villages, not in the town; Chapter 31 tells them. [A1 §XIII]

The Agris, who worked the salt pans on the north bank of the creek in the 1880s, appear in the modern record by name only once, through the Agri Sena, whose Kalash Patil blamed unauthorised building for the July 2026 floods. The ten families who in 2026 claimed the salt-pan land on the line of the double-deck bridge are not identified by community in the report, and this book does not guess. [n52 §3, §7] The Bhandaris, the palm-tappers of the nineteenth century, do not appear at all.

In the hills to the east, the Warli, Katkari and Thakur Adivasis of the Tungar foothills lived through these decades a history of their own, of bonded labour and its abolition, brick kilns and land. It was there that Vivek Pandit’s Shramajeevi Sanghatana worked from 1982, and from there that he came to the assembly in 2009. Chapter 21 and Chapter 31 tell it. [n26 §2; A2]

Muslims

The Muslims were 8.45 per cent of the taluka in 2011. [n34 §8] Their history in the town is old: the Konkani Muslims who descended from the Arab and Persian settlers of the early centuries, the Naitas who had been rich traders when the Portuguese came, the 885 Muslims of the 1881 town. [A1 §XIII] In the modern period the record holds two things. In the Koliwada, the Gausia Masjid began as a prayer shed; the foundation stone of its present two-storey building was laid in 1982, and it runs an Arabic school with free lodging for about fifty students. Near Pali, the heritage survey records a sixteenth-century dargah with a step well and an eighteenth-century mosque whose architecture mixes Islamic, Portuguese and Maratha features. [A1 §XIII] And the corporation’s first deputy mayor was Sagir Ahmed Dange. [n26 §3] No modern history of Vasai’s Muslim families was found. [A1; n52]

Parsis, Jains and Gujaratis

Fourteen Parsis lived in the town in 1881, the heirs of two Parsi rest-house builders of 1780 and 1836, and five Jews. [A1 §XIII] Nothing was found about either community in Vasai after independence.

The Jains were 1.75 per cent of the taluka in 2011, and the Gujarati language far more: about 23.78 per cent of the people of Navghar-Manikpur in 2001 by its Wikipedia page. [n34 §8; n52 §1] The Gujarati Vanis had held the shops of Bassein in 1837 and the betel-nut trade of Papdi in 1882; in modern Vasai the Gujarati and Marwari trading families, Jain and Hindu, are part of the town’s commercial life, but the notes give neither figures nor names. [A1 §XIII] Some of the schools in Wikipedia’s list carry names that suggest Gujarati founders, but that is an inference from names. [n52 §5]

The newcomers

And then there are the people who, by number, are most of modern Vasai East: the migrants who came after 1988. The sources do not say in numbers where they came from; the census migration tables were not reached. [n06 §1 Gaps] The language figures of Navghar-Manikpur in 2001, a quarter Gujarati and a fifth Hindi, are the nearest measure. [n52 §1] In politics they are visible by inference: the names of the BJP’s winning candidates in 2024 and 2026, Sneha Dube Pandit, Jayprakash Singh, Ravi Purohit, are names from beyond the Marathi coast, and the researchers read in them the political weight of north Indian voters, while saying plainly that this is an inference and not a migration statistic. [n06 §1 Gaps, §3] They came to work in the estates of Waliv and Sativali, to live in the flats of Evershine City and its neighbours, and to ride the trains to Mumbai, and they made the station side of Vasai the larger town. Their own histories of arrival have not been written.

3 February 2026: Ajiv Patil

The corporation had gone without an elected house since mid-2020, when civic elections across Maharashtra were delayed. [n06 §3; n26 §3] Who governed it in the interval is not stated in the notes; an administrator is likely, but unsourced. [n26 §3 Gaps]

On 15 January 2026 the city voted, and on 16 January the votes were counted. The BVA won 71 of 115 seats, the BJP 43 and the Shinde faction of the Shiv Sena one, on a turnout of 57.12 per cent, one of the highest in the state; no other party won a seat. The BJP had risen from about one seat to forty-three. [n06 §3; FPJ, 16 Jan 2026; India TV] The Deccan Herald headlined it “whistle blows lotus off Vasai fort”. [n06 §3] Among the BVA’s losses were Hitendra Thakur’s cousin Pankaj Thakur, beaten by the BJP’s Mehul Shah; the former mayor Rupesh Jadhav, who lost Ward 21 to the BJP’s Vishal Jadhav; Kiran Thakur, beaten by Ravi Purohit; Hardik Raut, beaten by Gaurav Raut; and Dhananjay Gawade, beaten by Jayprakash Singh. [n06 §3; FPJ] Chapters 29 and 15 tell the election across the city.

The mayor was chosen on 3 February 2026. By the account of Navbharat Live, Ajiv Yashwant Patil of the BVA won with seventy-one votes, the BVA’s seventy and one Congress member, against forty-four for the Mahayuti alliance’s candidate, Adv. Darshana Anil Tripathi; Marshal Lopes (the paper spells it Lopis) of the BVA was elected deputy mayor, also with seventy-one. The Collector of Palghar, Indurani Jakhar, and the corporation’s commissioner, Manoj Kumar Suryavanshi, presided. [n52 §9; Navbharat Live, 4 Feb 2026] An earlier Lokmat headline had given his name as “Ajeev Patil”. [n52 §2]

Navbharat called him the city’s seventh mayor. The two tables of mayors described in Chapter 29 list Rajeev Patil, Narayan Mankar, Pravina Thakur, Rupesh Jadhav, Narayan Mankar again for twenty-eight days in 2019, and Pravin Shetty: five people, six tenures. [n26 §3; V3] Counting Mankar’s two tenures separately makes Ajiv Patil the seventh, which may be how the paper counted; that is this book’s arithmetic, and the researchers left the conflict unreconciled. [n52 §9 Gaps] Whether Ajiv Patil is related to Rajeev Patil, the first mayor, could not be confirmed. [n26 §3 Gaps]

In May 2026 Mayor Patil attended the opening of the Naringi flyover in Virar, with the Guardian Minister of Palghar district, Ganesh Naik. [V3; n29 §4] In July his city flooded, and the water reached his own headquarters. [n52 §7]

Vasai in November 2026

In the autumn of 2026 Vasai was two towns in one city. On the fort side, the old town of Vasai Gaon, Papdi, Parnaka and the Koliwada lay between the creek, the sea and the railway, ringed by the Vadval parishes and, beyond them, the Kupari villages, with the cathedral at Papdi and the ruins at the water’s edge. On the station side, Vasai East spread across the low ground to the highway: the townships, the industrial estates of Navghar, Gokhivare, Waliv and Sativali, and the new neighbourhoods whose names the old registers do not hold. [A1 §XIX; n52 §1, §4] No one had counted either since 2011. [n06 §1]

Its member of the assembly, since November 2024, was Sneha Dube Pandit of the BJP, the first of her party to hold the seat; its mayor, since February 2026, was Ajiv Patil of the BVA, the party that had held the seat for most of thirty-four years. [n52 §2, §9] Its police answered to a commissioner at Mira Road; its district headquarters, since 1 August 2014, lay fifty kilometres north at Palghar. [n52 §8; n29 §3]

Its links to the city were being remade. The new highway bridge over the creek had been open for three and a half years. The Ro-Ro ran from the fort jetty to Bhayandar on its August timetable. The double-deck bridge waited for the forest department, the salt commissioner and ten families’ claims; Metro Line 13 had its approval and its money on paper; the east side of Vasai Road station had a new deck and a new footbridge, and a coaching terminal was coming. Lokmat reported, on 17 November, a new effort to spare travellers between Panvel and Vasai–Virar the change at Kurla or Dadar. [n52 §3]

The water was short again. Reservoir levels were falling, the historic lakes were choked, and the Kholsapada-2 dam was not yet finished. [n06 §4; n52 §7] The July floods had killed people in Vasai West and East and drowned the main substation; the railway was surveying whether to raise its tracks. [n52 §7] The Gokhivare dump waited for its bio-mining; Gass had kept its fields. [n52 §7]

The fort, its three churches under conservation, had seen its January feast and its May victory festival. The Kala Krida Vikas Mandal was preparing the thirty-seventh festival, the first without Mukesh Save, and the thirteenth Mayor’s Marathon was set for 13 December. [n52 §6; n29 §4; V3] At Umrale, a Kupari parish to the north, pilgrims came for the Year of St Francis; at Papdi, a bishop born at Chulne sat in a cathedral whose restoration had won a UNESCO award. [n38; A1 §XIV] In Dhumal Nagar the corporation was seizing plastic; at Manikpur Talao, the old Jesuit pond, the police were chasing chain-snatchers; in the estates of Waliv the lorries came and went. [n52 §7, §8]

Seventy-nine years earlier the town had been a taluka headquarters of perhaps a few tens of thousands, with a fort in ruins, a dozen parishes, a market and a station four miles away. In November 2026 it was the southern end of a city of well over a million, and the station had become the larger town. Whether the old Vasai of the fort, the parishes and the garden villages would hold its shape against the new one, as the green-belt movement had made it hold since 1989, was a question its history had not yet answered.

What the record does not hold

The gaps in Vasai’s modern history are wide, and most of them sit where a civic history would normally begin.

  • Population. No figure for Vasai town or Navghar-Manikpur for 1951, 1961, 1971 or 1991, or for either town in 2011 (both are folded into the corporation). The Census of India’s district handbooks for Thana would hold them; they were not found online. [n52 §1 Gaps; n21]
  • The councils. The year the Vasai municipality became a modern municipal council; the founding year of the Navghar-Manikpur council; any list of either council’s presidents; either council’s elections, budgets and boundaries before 2009. The theses of T. T. Pereira (2018, local self-government) and R. J. Gonsalves (2016, education 1947–87) were identified and not opened. [n52 §1–2 Gaps; n34 §8 Gaps]
  • Elections. Runners-up and margins for Bassein in 1952, 1957 and 1967; biographies of Sadanand Warty, Pandharinath Choudhari and Dominic Gonsalves; Tarabai Vartak’s relationship to Bhausaheb Vartak; Sneha Dube Pandit’s biography and her final vote total (77,553 or 93,745). [n52 §2 Gaps; n26 §1 Gaps]
  • The road bridge. The opening year of the first road bridge over the creek (1968 is reported in other chapters and could not be confirmed here; 1928 is weak). [n52 §3 Gaps]
  • The railway. Vasai Road’s current ridership; when passenger trains began on the Diva line and when Vasai Road became a junction for them. [n52 §3 Gaps]
  • Metro Line 13. Its route and stations through Vasai. [n52 §3]
  • Vasai East. An independent launch date for Evershine City; the histories of Vasant Nagari and the other townships; the founding of the industrial belt; any history of Waliv or Sativali as villages; an official count of industrial units. [n52 §4 Gaps]
  • Schools and hospitals. Founding years for Thomas Baptista (1889 and 1975 unverified), St Xavier’s, Nazareth, Mount Carmel, Holy Family and the others; the details of St Gonsalo Garcia College’s foundation stone and campus move; the founding of any Vasai hospital after 1872, including the sub-district and civic hospitals. [n52 §5 Gaps]
  • Festivals. The Kala Krida Mahotsav’s founder and first year (about 1989 or 1990 by inference); the first modern Vijayotsav; the history of Ganeshotsav in Vasai. [n52 §6 Gaps]
  • Floods. Vasai tolls and rainfall for 2005, 2019, 2021 (Cyclone Tauktae) and 2024; a reconciled figure for July 2026. [n52 §7 Gaps]
  • Police. Crime and policing in the town from 1947 to 1990; the number of MBVV stations (twenty or thirteen); outcomes of the 2025–26 arrests. [n52 §8 Gaps]
  • The communities. No count of Kuparis, Vadvals, Samvedi Brahmins, Pachkalshis, Kolis, Agris or Bhandaris at any date since independence; no modern history of the town’s Muslim, Parsi, Jain or Gujarati families; no Kupari organisation with a verifiable founding date; no census migration tables for the newcomers. [n34 §8 Gaps; n41 §13 Gaps; n06 §1 Gaps]
  • The interval. Who governed the corporation between July 2020 and February 2026. [n26 §3 Gaps]

These are the places where the council and corporation archives, the parish and diocesan registers, the Maharashtra Government Gazette, the Census of India’s printed tables, the Election Commission’s statistical reports and the Marathi newspapers would add most.

From the fort jetty the Ro-Ro still pushes off across the creek towards Bhayandar, past the girders of 1864’s successors and under the line of a bridge not yet built. Behind it lie the walls of 1554, the parishes of the Vadvals and, to the north, the villages of the Kuparis, whose own modern history Part Seven of this book tells, beginning with how they came to be.
