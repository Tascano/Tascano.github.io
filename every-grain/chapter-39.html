Period covered: from the first evidence of seafaring on this coast to the Vadhvan port fight, 2024–2026

In this chapter. Thread one: the sea, from the first evidence of boats on this coast to the Vadhvan port fight of 2024–2026.

A stone in the mud at Gass

In 2018 a local researcher named Siddharth Kale reported a stone anchor lying near Gass and Taki Pada, at the southern edge of the old Sopara island. It is a plain object: a worked block of stone with a hole bored through it for a rope, the kind of weight that sailors on the Indian Ocean dropped over the side for centuries before iron anchors were cheap. It was not found on a beach. It lay inland, in ground that is now fields, house plots and lanes, close to a small mosque that people of the neighbourhood call the Bandar Masjid, the “Port Mosque”. [n36; Shah et al. 2020; Brancaccio 2023 p. 12 n. 30] [n02; Shah 2020, citing Loksatta]

Nobody has dated the anchor. Stone anchors are notoriously hard to date, because the same simple shapes were made and used for two thousand years and more. But the place where it lay says something by itself. Two years later, in 2020, a survey team from the University of Warsaw and the Indian National Heritage Conservation and Research Foundation mapped the buried paleochannels of this ground, the beds of old waterways that now sit about half a metre below present sea level. The anchor, the “Port Mosque” and the buried channels all point the same way: the sea once came in here, and boats once tied up where houses now stand. [n36; Shah et al. 2020; Brancaccio 2023 p. 12]

This thread of the book follows that sea, and the people who made a living on it, through every generation for which any record survives. Other chapters tell the coast’s story era by era, with kings, churches and villages. Here the story is told along one line only: the ports, the routes, the goods, the ships and the men who built them, the guilds and customs houses, the named traders and the trading communities, the pirates and the navies, and the slow choking of the creeks that ended it all. Along the way it tracks the other things the sea gave this coast: fish, salt, defence, its weather and the people it carried here.

It is a long thread with large holes in it. Some generations left a copper plate or a traveller’s sentence and nothing else. Some left nothing at all. Where the record is silent, this chapter says so, tells what the neighbouring ports were doing, and names the sources that tell us. A sea thread that pretended to be continuous would be a work of fiction. This one is a chain of evidence, with its missing links marked.

Before the record: a shore without a port

The sea here was not always where it is now. At the height of the last Ice Age, about 20,000 years ago, global sea level stood some 125 to 130 metres lower than today, and the coast off Bombay lay far out on what is now the drowned continental shelf, which runs about 280 kilometres wide from Bombay to Ratnagiri. As the ice melted, the sea rose, flooded the river valleys and turned them into creeks: the Vasai creek at the mouth of the Ulhas, the Vaitarna estuary at Arnala, and the coast-parallel channel that cut off the “island of Sopara”. Mangrove swamps spread along their edges; geologists date west-coast mangrove layers from the latest Pleistocene to the mid-Holocene, between about 10,760 and 4,540 radiocarbon years before the present. [n23; Yokoyama et al. 2018] [n01; Kumaran et al.] Chapters 2 and 3 tell that story in full. For this thread it matters for one reason: the waterways that later carried ships to Sopara were made by the rising sea, and they would be unmade by silt.

The first people of the coast lived on and from that shore. Near Vasai the clearest early evidence is Mesolithic: scatters of small stone tools, microliths, on the low coastal hills of Manori, Marve and Salsette, a few kilometres south. People who lived on this kind of coast ate a great deal of marine food. But no boat, no paddle and no fishing gear of that age has been found anywhere near Vasai, and “evidence for actual Palaeolithic boats is absent”. [n14; INFLIBNET; UNESCO Silk Roads paper]

The one radiocarbon date from near Sopara itself is a shell. A survey of Quaternary sea levels on the western coast reported shells near Nala Sopara with an age of 3250 ± 110 years before present, roughly the middle of the second millennium BCE. The context of the shells is not stated, and a single date is not a sea-level record, let alone a sign of a harbour. [n23; UNESCO Silk Roads paper]

Further north, the Bronze Age certainly knew the sea. At Lothal in Gujarat a brick basin of about 2350 BCE is conventionally read as a dockyard; a 2024 study by IIT Gandhinagar traced an old course of the Sabarmati past it, though “absolute proof of the dock reading is elusive”. A Persian Gulf seal of Dilmun type was found at Lothal, and Indus beads, seals and weights turn up in Mesopotamia, the Gulf and Elam. Inland, across the Ghats, the Jorwe farmers of Maharashtra (about 1400 to 700 BCE) are said by one source to have traded with Gujarat and the Konkan for fish, conch shell and haematite, but “no such evidence is yet found from the coastal Konkan area”. No Harappan site is known at Vasai, and no Neolithic, Chalcolithic or Iron Age site has been excavated inside the taluka. [n14; IndianCulture.gov.in; Harappa.com] [n23; Jorwe culture; Live History India] For the two thousand years before the Mauryas, the honest description of this coast’s seafaring is: probable, regional, and unrecorded.

That silence has been filled, for a century and a half, by stories. The Bombay Gazetteer of 1882 opened its history of the Thana coast with a sweeping sentence: “From B.C. 2500 to B.C. 500 there are signs of trade with Egypt, Phoenicia, and Babylon”. The “signs” it then listed are literary, not physical: Rawlinson on Gerrha, a Babylonian tradition of teachers who came from the southern sea, Ezekiel on the ivory of Dedan. None is a find on this coast. [n36; Gaz XIII-II p. 400] The same volume called the identification of Sopara with Solomon’s Ophir “though not unlikely, … doubtful”, and the Places volume laid out four arguments for it: the likeness of the name to Josephus’s “Sophir” and the Coptic “Sophir” for India; the three-year round trip of Solomon’s fleet; the Indian character of its cargo of gold, gems, sandalwood, ivory, peacocks and apes; and the Indian origin of the Hebrew words for those goods. It concluded that “the site of Ophir is unsettled”. [n36; Gaz XIII-II pp. 400–401; Gaz XIV pp. 316–317] Bhagwanlal Indraji, who dug at Sopara in 1882, thought his finds “strengthen” the Ophir case, but he offered no object of the tenth century BCE to support it. [n36; Indraji 1882 p. 277] The Jewish Encyclopedia’s verdict is the soberest: the Septuagint and Josephus rendering “Ophir” as “Sophir” “merely proves that Josephus and others identified Ophir with India”, because India was then the land of gold. Older Bible dictionaries list “Supara” among Indian candidates and still conclude that southeastern Arabia “has on the whole more to support it”. [n02; Jewish Encyclopedia; BibleStudyTools; ISBE] Ophir is a legend about Sopara, not evidence from it.

The epics give Shurparaka a holy antiquity rather than a commercial one. The Mahabharata (Vana Parva, chapter 118) makes it a place where the Pandavas rested; the Harivamsha calls it a city “500 bows” broad, built on land Parashurama won back from the sea with his arrow; the Buddhist tradition makes the Bodhisattva, in a former birth, a mariner named Supparaka; and the Sri Lankan chronicle has the prince Vijaya, mythical first king of the island, sail from Supparaka. The Gazetteer’s phrase about “a place whose importance as a religious and trade centre lasted for over two thousand five hundred years (B.C. 1400–A.D. 1300)” rests on a Mahabharata date that no historian now accepts. [n36; Gaz XIV pp. 316–317, 323] [n02; Wikipedia: Sopara, citing Thapar 1997] There is a strange fitness in the Parashurama story, all the same. A coast that really did rise out of tidal mud as the Holocene sea fell back and the creeks silted is exactly the coast a myth of “land won from the sea” would describe. That is resonance, not proof. [n01; inference]

Ashoka’s slabs: the first certain fact

The story of this coast as a place of record begins with a king’s words on basalt. In 1882 Indraji found “a broken slab of basalt bearing parts of some lines of Rock Edict VIII” of Ashoka near the Bhatela pond at Sopara; on 1 January 1956 N. A. Gore of the Bombay Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society found a fragment of Rock Edict IX at Bhuigaon nearby. Both are now in Mumbai’s CSMVS museum. [n02; Sircar EI 32] The Gazetteer itself, for all its talk of Egypt and Ophir, said plainly that the carving of the edicts (which it dated B.C. 225) “remains the earliest known fact in the history of the Thana coast”. [n36; Gaz XIII-II pp. 400–401] On that basis the scholar Pia Brancaccio concludes that Sopara was “probably the oldest of the Ulhas ports”. [n36; Brancaccio 2023 p. 11, citing Falk 2006]

The edicts are not trade documents. But a Mauryan emperor did not cut his edicts in a backwater. The slabs mark Sopara as a place where people gathered and passed through, and the small sherds of Northern Black Polished Ware, the fine glossy pottery of the Gangetic and Mauryan world, reported from the 1993 excavation tie it to that sphere by about the third century BCE. [n01; Shah et al. 2020] The Sri Lankan chronicle tradition adds that after the Third Buddhist Council Ashoka sent a Yavana (Greek) monk, Dharmarakshita, to Aparanta, the western coast; Indraji believed he made Sopara his base. That is chronicle tradition. The edicts are stone. [n36; Gaz XIII-II p. 400] [n02; Shah 2020]

Why here? The Gazetteer of 1882 gave an answer that still reads well. Sopara stood a little above the salt marsh, with rich soil and fresh water. “Its distance from the coast and its broad backwater guarded it from pirate raids or the forays of the wild hill and forest tribes, and its water connection with the Vaitarna on the north and the Ulhas or Bassein river on the south, and … the easy land route along the Tansa valley made it an early centre of trade.” [n37; Gaz XIV p. 316] [n36; Gaz XIV p. 316] The site was, in other words, a sheltered inner harbour with two mouths to the sea, and a road inland. That road ran from Sopara up through the passes to the Deccan: via Kalyan and the Naneghat pass to Junnar, Nashik, Paithan and Ter. [n02; Shah 2020] [n36; Indraji 1882 p. 277 n. 3] An 1882 footnote records that along the Tansa valley Pelhar and Mandvi “were once places of importance”, and that at Bhatana, “a landing station to the north of Tungar”, lay a reservoir of dressed stones and quantities of old bricks. [n12; Gaz XIV, Sopara fn., information from W. W. Loch] Those three villages, now on the eastern edge of the municipal corporation, may mark the first miles of the road from the sea to the plateau.

The port in the Periplus

The first time a foreign sailor wrote Sopara’s name, he did it in one line, in Greek. The Periplus of the Erythraean Sea, a merchant’s handbook to the ports of the Red Sea and the Indian Ocean, was written in the first century CE; the 1882 Gazetteer dated it to AD 247, a date now abandoned, and Yule put it at AD 80–90. [n36; Gaz XIV p. 320; Schoff pp. 197–200] [n15; Hobson-Jobson] Its author works his way south along the Indian coast from Barygaza (Bharuch), the great port of the Narmada, and in section 52 he writes:

“The market-towns of this region are, in order, after Barygaza: Suppara, and the city of Calliena, which in the time of the elder Saraganus became a lawful market-town; but since it came into the possession of Sandares the port is much obstructed, and Greek ships landing there may chance to be taken to Barygaza under guard.” [n36; Schoff 1912 §52, p. 43]

That is all. Suppara is a “market-town”, one of the local marts of the coast below Barygaza. The Periplus names no goods for it. What it describes instead is a political tangle: Calliena (Kalyan), deep in the estuary, had been a lawful port under “the elder Saraganus”, but under a ruler called Sandares (or Sandanes) foreign ships putting in there could be seized and marched north to Barygaza under guard. Scholars have spent a century arguing about these names. Schoff wondered whether “Sandares” was a Sundara Satakarni; the Gazetteer’s footnote wondered whether “Sandanes” was a tribal name or a garbled “Kshatrap”; a later edition calls “Sandares” an unjustified conjecture for the manuscript’s “Sandanes”. The Gazetteer read the passage as showing that the coast “had passed to a king named Sandanes, who stopped all foreign trade”. [n36; Gaz XIII-II p. 418; Schoff pp. 197–200] [n02; ToposText] Most modern readers see behind it the rivalry of the Western Kshatrapas of Gujarat and the Satavahanas of the Deccan, whose frontier seems to have run along this coast. [n02; LiveHistoryIndia]

Past Calliena the Periplus lists Semylla (Chaul), Mandagora, Palaepatmae, Melizigara, Byzantium, Togarum and Aurannoboas, then a string of islands, “and in these places there are pirates”, before Naura, Tyndis, Muziris and Nelcynda on the Malabar coast. [n36; Schoff §53, pp. 43–44] Piracy was already part of this sea’s description in the first century.

Because the Periplus gives Sopara no goods, the cargoes of the coast have to be inferred from Barygaza, one stage north, to which the Konkan marts were tied. Barygaza exported spikenard, costus, bdellium, ivory, agate and carnelian, lycium, cotton cloth, silk cloth, mallow cloth, yarn and long pepper. It imported Italian, Laodicean and Arabian wine, copper, tin and lead, coral and topaz, thin clothing, storax, sweet clover, flint glass, realgar and antimony, gold and silver coin “on which there is a profit when exchanged for the money of the country”, silver vessels, and for the king, singing boys and “beautiful maidens for the harem”. Ships bound from Egypt made the voyage “favorably about the month of July, that is Epiphi”. [n36; Schoff §§48–49, pp. 41–42] The 1882 Gazetteer drew up its own long list of what the Konkan ports sent to Egypt (sesamum oil, sugar, cotton, silk thread, spikenard, costus, bdellium, long pepper, lac, indigo, diamonds, opals, onyx, iron and steel) and what came back (wines, metals, coin, coral, silver plate, glass, slaves). Those lists are largely the Periplus’s Barygaza and Muziris lists transposed to the Thana coast, and should be read as such. [n36; Gaz XIII-II p. 416] The safest statement is the one this book’s research notes settled on: Sopara was one of the Aparanta ports that fed the Barygaza–Paithan–Tagara network, and its cargoes were the Deccan and Gujarat goods of the Barygaza lists, plus the Konkan’s own: timber, coconuts, cloth, pearls, gem stones and sandalwood brought up from further south. [n36; inference]

Two local goods do stand out. “In very early times (A.D. 500) the Sopara stone was famous”, the Gazetteer notes, referring to a gem named after the town in the old astronomical-astrological literature. And pearls were found in the creeks; that comes from a twelfth-century Arab writer, but there is no reason to think it began then. [n36; Gaz XIII-II p. 416 n. 1]

The Gazetteer itself admitted that this was not the centre of the Egyptian trade. Ashoka’s reign may have opened “a direct sea-trade with Egypt”, it wrote, “But the direct trade with Egypt was never large, and it centred at Broach, not at Sopara”; it was “doubtful which of the Konkan ports was the centre of the Egyptian trade; the references seem to point to Simulla or Chaul and to Zizerus”. It cited Strabo for Indian fleets in the Red Sea growing to 120 sail by 25 BCE. [n36; Gaz XIII-II pp. 409–410] It also imagined coasting vessels running south to meet the Egyptian ships at Muziris and Nelcynda, or further to Ceylon and the Coromandel coast for the fine cloths of Masulipatam, and a trade with Gedrosia and Apologos (Obollah, near Basra) in teak, squared timber and ebony blocks against wine, dates, cloth, purple and gold. That is the Gazetteer’s inference from McCrindle; the Periplus supports teak and those routes at other ports, not at Sopara by name. [n36; Gaz XIII-II p. 418]

Where did a Roman-era ship actually lie at Sopara? Brancaccio’s reading of the geography is the best modern account. Sopara sat “on a large coastal island right at the river mouth, with its lagoon and coastal canals”, the “most convenient and accessible location for docking transoceanic ships within the Ulhas’ estuary system”. Kalyan lay deep inside the estuary. Goods moved between the two on small craft, “likely on wooden canoes comparable to the one unearthed at … Pattanam” in Kerala. In her model the estuary had a division of labour: Sopara the deep-water mouth, Kalyan the inland entrepot. [n36; Brancaccio 2023 p. 10] That is an informed reconstruction, not an excavated harbour. But one modest excavation supports it. In 1993 the Archaeological Survey of India and the British Academy dug trial trenches southeast of the stupa. They found Satavahana and Kshatrapa coins of lead and copper, semi-precious stone beads, fragments of Roman amphorae (the tall wine and oil jars of the Mediterranean), Islamic blue glazed ware from a much later age, an earthen wall and traces of a coarse stone wall that has been read as a small docking structure. The sequences were disturbed, and there is no secure stratigraphy; Brancaccio concedes that excavation at Sopara is “especially difficult” because the site is built over. [n36; Brancaccio 2023 p. 11] [n02; Shah 2020, citing Tripati & Gaur 1997] Those amphora sherds are the only physical trace yet found of the Mediterranean at Sopara.

The pilot boats of this sea are known from Barygaza, not Sopara. At the Narmada mouth, the Periplus says, “native fishermen in the King’s service … in well-manned large boats called trappaga and cotymba” came out to meet incoming ships and tow them to fixed stations, because of the tidal bore and the shoals. [n36; Schoff §44, pp. 40–41] Brancaccio uses this, with the Purna story below, to argue that “escort charges” at estuary ports like Sopara were a pilotage fee. That transfer is her inference. [n36; Brancaccio 2023 p. 13] A popular account adds that “islands and creeks protected the harbour, and local boats piloted ships in”, and lists ivory, shells, silk, elephants and horses among the goods; it gives no primary source. [n02; LiveHistoryIndia]

What did the ships look like? No hull has been found on this coast. Schoff’s notes describe “ships of the country” as double-canoe sangara and plank-sewn craft, mentions an Andhra (Satavahana) coin showing a two-masted ship, and suggests the Gujarati ship carved at Borobudur around 600 CE “was probably not different from those of the 1st century”. That is Schoff’s speculation about Gujarat-type ships, not a Sopara find. [n36; Schoff pp. 243–245]

The sailing year was set by the monsoon, then as for the next two thousand years. Egypt-bound ships left in July. Pliny’s route, via the cape of Fartak in Arabia, reached the Konkan “near the end of September” and left again in December or January. [n36; Gaz XIII-II p. 410 n. 5, citing Vincent and Pliny] The Gazetteer found no direct mention of Sopara in Pliny, only a “Perimula” that may be Chaul. [n15; Gaz XIV] But a generation after the Periplus, the geographer Ptolemy (about AD 150) put “Soupara” on his map of India between Nusaripa (Navsari) and Simylla (Chaul), with the mouths of rivers he called Goaris and Binda nearby, perhaps the Vaitarna and the Bhiwandi or Bassein creek. Romila Thapar reads him as treating it as a major commercial centre. [n15; Hobson-Jobson; Gaz XIV p. 320] [n02; Wikipedia, citing Thapar 1997]

The merchants who paid for caves

If the Greek and Roman writers give Sopara a single line, the people of Sopara wrote their own names, on stone, in the Buddhist monasteries of the hills. The donor inscriptions of Kanheri, Nashik, Karle and Naneghat are the nearest thing this coast has to a list of its ancient traders. They are not ledgers. They are gifts, recorded by people who wanted the gift remembered. But they say who had money, where they came from and what trade they followed.

The best documented is a man called Samika. At Kanheri, the great cave monastery in the hills of Salsette across the creek, inscription XII records a water cistern beside Cave VII given “by the worshipper Samika, a merchant of Sopáraka”. Indraji dated it to the middle of the first century CE; another reading of the Gazetteer puts it in the second. A modern digest calls him Samika or Svamika, a negama (merchant) and lay worshipper “from Soparaga”. [n36; Indraji 1882 p. 277; Puratattva digest] [n37; Gaz XIV pp. 319–320] In a country where water had to be stored through eight dry months, a cistern was a practical gift to the monks and to every traveller who climbed to the caves.

Beside him in the same digest stands Nagapalita, a manikara (jeweller) of Surparaka, donor at Cave 86. [n36; Puratattva digest] At Naneghat, the pass on the trade road to the Deccan, inscription VIII records “a cistern by one Govindadása of Sopáraya”. At Karle, the grand chaitya on the road between the passes and the plateau, a pillar containing relics was “the gift of … Sátimita, nephew of the reverend Dhamutariya from Sopáraka”, of the Dharmottariya school. [n36; Indraji 1882 p. 277] [n02; Shah 2020, citing Poonam Kumari 2019] The dating of the Karle gift varies between sources, from the first century BCE to the first century CE. [n37; Gaz XIV p. 319] [n02; Shah 2020] Another Kanheri record, inscription V in Cave III, is a gift made “in the district or dhára of Soparaka” at the end of the second century. [n36; Indraji 1882 p. 277]

The neighbours were richer, or at least more generous. Among the Kanheri donors are Samidata (Svamidatta), a goldsmith of Kaliyana; Nanda, a kamara (blacksmith) of Kalyana; Dhama, son of Sivamita, a merchant of Kalyana; Aparenu son of Ananda, a merchant at Kalyan; Rishipala son of Golanaka; four men, Nagakunda, Ananda, Gajasena and Gajamitra, described as “merchants (vanijaka)” who built a chaitya together; Kanaka, a brazier of Nasika; Sulasadata of Chemula (Chaul), a goldsmith or treasurer’s son; and a third-century “merchant householder”, son of Vishnunandi of Kalyan, at Cave 74. [n36; Puratattva digest] Brancaccio, counting from Gokhale’s 1991 edition of the Kanheri inscriptions, found that among 42 Prakrit inscriptions of the second and third centuries CE, Kalyan is named in thirteen and Sopara in five. [n36; Brancaccio 2023 p. 10 n. 22] Kalyan’s donors outnumbered Sopara’s by more than two to one. That fits her picture of a two-port estuary, Sopara at the mouth and Kalyan inland, and it fits the Periplus, which records Kalyan’s restriction under Sandares and may explain why foreign skippers still made for Sopara first. [n36; inference]

Two names in the same digest need a warning. An 1850s reading by Stevenson produced a “Sateraka”, minister of “the Bassein province” under a Satrap, and a “Dharmanaka, son of Sayana Sarvamata of Vas[ai]”. The Gazetteer itself reports that Indraji judged a supposed “Vasai” reading at Kanheri to be a mistake. Until Lüders’s list is checked, these lines are not evidence that the name Vasai existed in the second century. [n36; Puratattva] [n03; Gaz XIV p. 28 n. 4]

The occupations in these records are revealing. Merchants (negama, vanijaka), goldsmiths, a jeweller, a blacksmith, a brazier: the craftsmen of a port that dealt in metal, bullion and luxury goods. The Periplus had said that Roman coin brought a profit when changed at Barygaza. The relic stupa at Sopara points the same way. When Indraji opened the mound called Burud Rajacha Kot in April 1882, he found a stone coffer holding nested caskets of copper, silver, stone, crystal and gold, gold flowers, and a silver coin of a Satavahana king; Brancaccio dates the gold casket by its ornament to the first century CE. The Gazetteer read the workmanship as proof that Sopara around AD 160 “had skilful masons, coppersmiths, and goldsmiths”. [n36; Brancaccio 2023 p. 11 n. 28; Gaz XIII-II p. 409] [n02; Asiatic Society of Mumbai]

What the donor records do not contain is just as important. A modern summary of the Kanheri inscriptions notes that no guild and no ship is named in them. There is a “granary and store-house” (Cave 3) and “an establishment for the distribution of money” (Cave 21), but no shipowner, no captain, no fleet. [n36; Puratattva] The historian Himanshu Prabha Ray argued in Monastery and Guild (1986) that the monasteries and the merchants formed one commercial network, with the caves commanding the trade routes from the ports to the plateau; this book’s notes could see only catalogue records of the book, so her argument is a framework here, not a quotation. [n36; ZENON/DAI catalogue] The 1882 Gazetteer made the same point in its own words: “The merchants of the Thana ports were Hindus, Buddhism favouring trade, and owing many of its finest monuments to the [merchants],” and the monasteries “commanded the main lines of traffic”. [n37; Gaz XIII-II, history chapter] Buddhism’s rise on this coast followed merchant money and royal patronage. Almost every inscription is a gift, which is exactly what one would expect if the new faith travelled along the trade network and drew prestige from it. [n37; inference]

Ushavadata’s ferries and the guild banks of Govardhana

The grandest gift involving Sopara was made by a governor, not a merchant. In Cave 10 of the Pandavleni caves at Nashik, an inscription of Ushavadata, son-in-law of the Western Kshatrapa ruler Nahapana, lists his works of merit across his father-in-law’s lands. He built rest-houses at “Bharukachha, Dedapura, Govardhana and Sorparaga”, that is at Bharuch, at an unidentified Dedapura, at Govardhana near Nashik, and at Sopara. He set up free ferries by boat on the rivers Iba, Parada, Damana, Tapi, Karabena and Dahanuka, the chain of rivers that a traveller crossed on the coastal road from Gujarat down to the Konkan. And he gave 32,000 coconut stems at a village called Nanamgola to the ascetics of the Charaka order at the Ramatirtha in Sorparaga. [n36; Indraji 1882 p. 277; Wikipedia: Nasik inscription of Ushavadata] A second summary describes the Sopara rest-house as having four doors and four verandahs. [n02; Shah 2020, citing Indraji 1882] Indraji dated the inscription to “the beginning of the first century after Christ”; the Gazetteer said “A.D. 100”; the dating of Nahapana’s era is not settled in these sources. [n36; Indraji 1882 p. 277; Gaz XIII-II p. 411 n. 3]

The ferries matter for this thread. They show the coast road as a working route that a ruler thought worth subsidising, and the rest-house at Sopara shows the town as a stage on it, a place where travellers and caravans were expected to arrive and stay. Ushavadata’s gift at Sopara also implies that the Kshatrapas held the port in Nahapana’s time, before the Satavahana king Gautamiputra Satakarni won the region back. [n02; inference]

The same inscription preserves the best evidence anywhere in the region for how guilds handled money. Ushavadata deposited permanent endowments, akshaya nivi, with two weavers’ guilds at Govardhana, in local kārṣāpaṇa coins: “one guild paid at the rate of 12 per cent. per annum and the other 9 per cent.” [n36; Indian Antiquary vol. 48 p. 86] That is Nashik, not Sopara, and this book’s notes read only a single page of a secondary edition; Senart’s edition in Epigraphia Indica VIII is the primary text. But it shows that in the Satavahana–Kshatrapa age, guilds (shreni, nikaya) took deposits and paid fixed interest, exactly the kind of body that, in the story of Purna, ruled the trade of Sopara.

Purna and the guild of Sopara

The only picture of how a Sopara merchant guild worked comes from a Buddhist saint’s legend. It must be told as legend. It is also too detailed, and too consistent with the inscriptions, to leave out.

Purna (Punna, Purna Maitrayaniputra) is, in the Sanskrit and Tibetan versions summarised by the French scholar Burnouf, “the son of a rich Sopara merchant by a slave girl”. Denied a share of the family property, he rose by his own wits to be one of the leading merchants of Sopara; he met Buddhist sandalwood traders, became a monk, and “turned the people of the Konkan from their old faith to Buddhism”. [n36; Gaz XIV p. 320; Gaz XIII-II pp. 407–408] [n37; Gaz XIV pp. 320–321]

The Sopara of this story is a great city: “the seat of a king, a city with several hundred thousand inhabitants, with eighteen gates, and a temple of Buddha adorned with friezes of carved sandalwood”. The Gazetteer’s summary continues: “It was a great place of trade. Caravans of merchants came from Shravasti in Oude, and great ships with 500 … merchants … traded to distant lands. There was much risk … Two or three successful voyages made a merchant a man of mark.” One merchant in the story asks who, “that has made six safe voyages, has ever been known to tempt Providence by trying a seventh?” The chief goods were cloth, “blue, yellow, red, and white”, and sandalwood of the kind called goshirsha, “cow’s head”, “brought apparently from the Kanara or Malabar coast”. “The coinage was gold, and many of the merchants had huge fortunes. A strong merchant-guild ruled the trade of the city.” [n36; Gaz XIV pp. 320–321]

Brancaccio’s translation of the relevant passage of the Purnavadana (from Tatelman’s edition) adds the guild’s rules. Ships arrive from across the great ocean. Purna goes out to them without entering the city and negotiates for their whole cargo, valued at 1,800,000 gold coins, with 300,000 down as a deposit. This breaks the guild’s law, which the story states plainly: “No one of us … may approach these [visiting] merchants independently. Only the guild as a body may purchase their goods. Anyone who deals with those merchants on his own shall be fined sixty silver coins.” The guild owned warehouses. It fixed resale margins at double the cost. And the text speaks of “customs, duties, escort-charges and freight fees”. [n36; Brancaccio 2023 pp. 12–13] In another episode, merchants whom Purna saves in a storm at sea build a Buddhist temple at Sopara. [n36; Gaz XIII-II p. 408]

The Pali tradition tells a related story set in “Sunaparanta”. There, merchants give Punna part of their red sandalwood cargo after he saves them from a storm; with it he builds a hall, the Candanasala, and the Buddha comes with 500 arahants, spends one night and leaves before dawn. [n02; DPPN; palikanon.com] In the Sanskrit Divyavadana, the Buddha comes “flying through the sky” with his monks to Purna’s sandalwood temple near Surparaka. [n02; 84000 Knowledgebase] Ajanta Cave 2 has a painting of the Purnavadana, dated to the fifth century. [n02; LiveHistoryIndia] An anonymous web essay mentions a merchant brother pair, Punna and Chulla Punna, in the Pali tradition; it is low-reliability and is noted only for completeness. [n36; MHS India blog]

How old is this picture? The Gazetteer put the text “apparently of the fourth or fifth century after Christ”; Brancaccio takes it to reflect “the beginning of the Common Era”. It is hagiography, preserved in Sanskrit and Tibetan recensions. The fairest reading is that its guild rules are a literary description of how a port guild was imagined to work, consistent with the Arthashastra and with the Nashik guild-deposit inscriptions, not Sopara’s actual bylaws. [n36; Gaz XIV p. 320 n. 1; Brancaccio 2023] No historical evidence shows the Buddha came to Sopara. [n02; LiveHistoryIndia] But the legend gives the ancient port a figure the inscriptions never do: a self-made merchant of mixed birth who made his fortune on the sea and brought a new faith home by it.

The quiet centuries, c. 300–800

Then the port almost disappears.

A survey by the National Institute of Oceanography, as relayed by later writers, found Sopara active from a pre-Ashokan phase to the third century CE and again from about the ninth to the thirteenth century, with “no cultural remains from the 4th to 9th centuries”. [n36; Shah et al. 2020; Wikipedia: Sopara] This book’s notes could not open that survey itself, and one check could not confirm the exact attribution, so the claim stays secondary. But no other source fills the gap with an object. For about five hundred years, this coast’s seafaring is known almost entirely from its neighbours.

What the neighbours show is that the sea did not go quiet. In the sixth century the Greek merchant-monk Kosmas Indikopleustes described Kalyan as a port with a Christian bishop “appointed from Persia”, trading in “brass, blackwood logs, and articles of clothing”; he also mentions a “Sibor”, “a place of great trade on the coast near Kalyan”, which the Gazetteer calls only “a doubtful reference” to Sopara. [n36; Schoff p. 197] [n15; Gaz XIV pp. 320, 322] The Gazetteer thought the Christian settlements at Kalyan and Sopara were probably strengthened by Nestorian refugees from Persia and Mesopotamia in the fifth century; that is its inference. [n03; Gaz XIII-II p. 420 n. 4]

In the early seventh century the sea brought war. The Aihole inscription of the Chalukya king Pulakeshin II (Gazetteer dates 610–640) says that his general, “with hundreds of ships, attacked the Maurya capital Puri, the goddess of the fortunes of the western ocean”. Where Puri stood is unknown: Mirashi left it for further research; one volume of the Gazetteer thought it “may therefore have been Sopara”, another preferred Elephanta. [n03; Gaz XIII-II p. 420; CII-VI p. 2; Gaz XIV p. 355 n. 5] Whatever its site, the inscription is the first record of a fleet in these waters, and it describes the capital of the North Konkan as a sea city.

Arab fleets came next. In 636 or 637 an expedition under the governor of Bahrain and Oman plundered the coast near Thana, and the place was sacked again around 660; Caliph Umar disapproved of the venture and forbade more. [n03; Gaz XIII-II p. 421; Gaz XIV p. 355] Then the record goes silent. “No further notice of the North Konkan has been traced till the rise of the Silaharas,” the Gazetteer says, and that leaves a documentary blank for about 700 to 800. [n03; Gaz XIII-II p. 421]

The relic stupa at Sopara offers one hint that the town was not abandoned. Among the relics Indraji found in 1882 were eight bronze images, the past Buddhas and Maitreya, dated by style to the seventh to ninth centuries, some 1,000 years younger than the stupa’s Mauryan core. They were probably put in when the stupa was enlarged or repaired, perhaps under the Rashtrakutas. [n02; Asiatic Society of Mumbai; LiveHistoryIndia; Shah 2020] Someone in Sopara had the means to refurbish a great monument at the very end of the “gap”. Whether that wealth came from the sea, the record does not say.

The horse merchant of 778

The first trader to reappear is a horse dealer, and he comes from a novel.

The Kuvalayamala, a Jain prose romance written by Uddyotana Suri in 778 CE, has a merchant named Lobhadeva, from Takshashila (Taxila) in the far north-west, who travels to Dakshinapatha, the southern country, and comes to Sopara to sell horses. There he meets a shreshthin, a guild head or banker, who helps him sell them; Lobhadeva grows rich. The novel, as relayed by modern writers, remarks that “Sopparaya traders had their own clubs and customs and foreign traders had to share their adventures”; it describes eighteen breeds of horses; and it mentions Sopara’s pearl fisheries and its large population. [n36; Shah et al. 2020, citing Upadhye 1970] [n02; LiveHistoryIndia] This book’s notes did not read Upadhye’s edition, and the novel is a literary source, not a customs record. But it is the first text to make Sopara a horse market, and horses turn out to be the longest-running single import of this whole coast: here in 778, at Thana around 1290, and written into the Portuguese treaty of 1534. [n36; inference]

The shreshthin who helps Lobhadeva is, like Purna’s guild, a figure of a port where local merchants controlled access to the market and a visiting trader needed a local partner. The same structure will reappear eight centuries later, when visiting Gujarat traders spend the dry season at Bassein and Agashi.

Subara among the cities of Hind

From the tenth century the Arab geographers take up the story. They knew Sopara as Subara, Sufara or Surbaraha, one of “the cities of Hind”.

Al-Masudi, who visited the coast in 915 or 916, names Subara with Thana and Saimur (Chaul): “On the coast, as in Saimur, Subara, Tana, and other towns, a language called Lárya is spoken.” The country belonged to the Balhara, the Rashtrakuta king of Mankir, “the most friendly to Musalmans of all Indian kings”: “In his kingdom Islam is honoured and protected.” He found 10,000 Persian and Arab settlers at Chaul. He also says that Cambay, Sanjan and Sopara were famous for leather and slippers. [n36; E&D I p. 24; Gaz XIII-II p. 432] [n03; Gaz XIV p. 321] [n15; Gaz XIV p. 321, citing Prairies d’Or] Al-Istakhri (about 951) lists Subara among the cities of Hind with Kambaya and Sindan (Sanjan) and adds: “From Kambaya to Saimur is the land of the Balhara … there are Musulmans in its cities, and none but Musulmans rule over them on the part of the Balhara. There are many mosques in these places.” Ibn Haukal (about 970) puts “Subara” among the Indian towns bordering Sind. The Gazetteer thought both had confused it with a different place in Surat. Al-Biruni (about 1030) “restores it to its right place”, 48 miles south of Sanjan and 40 north of Thana. [n36; E&D I pp. 34, ~38, 66; Gaz XIV p. 321] [n15; Gaz XIV p. 321]

Then, in 1153, al-Idrisi, writing at the Norman court of Sicily, gave the fullest description: “Subura is situated one-and-a-half mile from the sea. It is a populous, busy town, and is considered one of the entrepots of India. They fish for pearls here. It is in the vicinity of Bara, a small island, on which some cocoa-nut trees and the costus grow. From Subara to Sindan is considered five days.” [n36; E&D I p. 85] Bara is taken to be the island of Sopara itself. [n15; Gaz XIV p. 321] Idrisi’s mile and a half is the first measure, in any source, of the distance between Sopara and the open sea, and so the first measure of the silting that would end the port. [n36; inference]

The Arab sources show what the trade of the Thana coast looked like in the Silahara age, and the 1882 Gazetteer assembled them into a list. Exports included tabashir (bamboo manna), brown incense (guggul), “a large manufacture of laced shoes in Sopara and Sanjan, and a great export of excellent leather, chiefly to Arabia”, pearls from the creeks near Sopara, Sanjan’s emeralds, and teak and bamboos sent from Sanjan to the Persian Gulf for house building. Imports included horses, of which as many as 10,000 a year were said to come in (“No ships came to Thana without horses”), dates, Chinese porcelain and copper carried as ballast, cowries, gold dust and iron from Africa, lead and tin from Malaya, and spices from Indonesia. [n36; Gaz XIII-II pp. 430–431] A second summary of the same chapter adds rice, salt sent inland to Devagiri, coconuts and betel, and cotton from Khandesh, and names partner ports from Quilon and Calicut through Broach, Cambay, Somnath, Daybul, Siraf, Hormuz, Aden and Socotra to Zeila, Mogadishu and Mombasa, and on to Kalah, Java and China. [n03; Gaz XIII-II pp. 429–430] Sanjan, Masudi says, exported a hard, bright emerald known as the “Mecca emerald”. [n03; Gaz XIV, Sanjan]

Who carried all this? The Gazetteer’s answer is a crowd: “local Hindu, Musalman, and Parsi traders, and Hindus and Musalmans from Gujarat and from the Malabar coast … foreign Persians and Arabs, Jews, Europeans, and perhaps a chance Chinaman”. From Masudi’s Lar language it inferred that “the trade tongue of the Thana ports was Gujarati, and the leading traders were probably Gujarat Vanis”. By the beginning of the tenth century, “the Parsis seem to have risen to wealth in Sanjan”. [n36; Gaz XIII-II pp. 431–432] The great carriers between the Mediterranean and India in the early tenth century, according to Ibn Khurdadhbih, were Jewish merchants, the Radhanites. [n03; Gaz XIII-II p. 432]

Some of these communities are documented on the coast by their own records. The Chinchani copper plates (926–1053), from a village north of the Vaitarna, name a Tajika (Arab) governor of Sanjan, “Madhumati Sugatipa”, that is Muhammad, appointed under the Rashtrakuta king Krishna II and named in a grant of 926; they also mention Parsis as subjects and the “hamyamana”, the anjuman or community council, in all five grants. [n03; Dalal 2019, LiveHistoryIndia] The Silahara and Balhara rulers allowed the Arabs mosques and a headman of their own to settle disputes. [n03; Gaz XIII-II p. 432] At Kanheri, Pahlavi inscriptions of the Yazdgard years 378 and 390 (AD 1009 and 1021) were left by Parsis “from their names fresh arrivals from Persia”, and a Kanheri inscription of 854 names a donor, Gomin Avighnakar, from Gauda in Bengal. [n36; Puratattva] [n35; Enthoven III pp. 192–195] [n02; LiveHistoryIndia] None of these visitors gives his trade. But they show the sea routes still delivering newcomers to this coast.

The communities that grew from those merchants were still here when the British counted them. The 1882 Gazetteer traced the Konkani Muslims of Thana to “the Arab and Persian refugees, merchants, and adventurers, who settled along the coast in the eighth, ninth, and tenth centuries”; at Sopara it called many of the Muslims “Naitas”, “descendants of Arab and Persian refugees”, and it quoted the sixteenth-century Portuguese physician Garcia da Orta on “Naitas of Bassein” who had married local women and were rich traders when the Portuguese came. [n35; Gaz XIII-I pp. 64, 216] [n37; Gaz XIII-I pp. 216–217; Gaz XIV p. 315 n.] Their own tradition, recorded by Enthoven, says that the bulk of them fled to India from the Euphrates valley about the year 700 to escape the governor Hajjaj bin Yusuf, and that later waves came as soldiers; “As late as the Marathas the services of Arab seamen were still in demand.” [n35; Enthoven III, Musalmans] The Gazetteer is firm on one point that matters for how this coast changed faith: “unlike Gujarat and the Deccan, Thana seems never to have been the scene of any forcible conversion of Hindus to Islam”; Muslim presence came from “traders, refugees and adventurers from Africa, Arabia and Persia” from 640 onward. [n37; Gaz XIII-I p. 216] Islam reached Sopara by sea, with the merchants.

Bhabhana and Dhanama: the ships of 1094

The clearest dated customs record in Sopara’s whole history is a royal grant on copper. In 1094 the Silahara king Anantadeva issued a set of plates, later found at Kharepatan, granting “exemption from tolls, at the ports of Sristhanaka [Thana], Nagapura, Surpparaka, and Chemuli, … to ships belonging to two brothers, Bhabhana and Dhanama, the ministers of Anantadeva”. [n36; Indraji 1882 p. 278, reading Indian Antiquary IX 38] Yule’s reading of the same plate has the tolls released at “the ports, Sri Sthanaka (Tana), as well as Nagapur, Surparaka, Chemuli (Chaul) and others, included within the Konkan Fourteen Hundred”. [n15; Hobson-Jobson]

Bhabhana and Dhanama are the only individuals in the record who owned ships at Sopara before the Portuguese. They were not simple merchants. They were the king’s ministers, and the plate shows that at the end of the eleventh century the men who governed the Konkan also owned its shipping, and could have their own vessels freed from the tolls that everyone else paid. It shows, too, that Sopara was still a toll port of the Silahara state, one of a chain of four named harbours. In the same reign Anantadeva boasted of having expelled “wicked” enemies who had ravaged the Konkan, perhaps a civil war. [n03; Gaz XIII-II pp. 425–426; Gaz XIV p. 321]

Around this one plate the Silahara record fills out. Customs were levied at Sopara, Thana, Chaul and perhaps Nagothna. The coin was the dramma, of a Sasanian type the Gazetteer calls “gadhiya”. A king’s highway ran roughly on the line of the later Bombay–Thana road. The administration had a great minister, a minister for peace and war and lords of the treasury, and many ministers’ names are Kannada or Telugu in form. [n03; Gaz XIII-II p. 428] A Silahara grant of 1049 places villages around Virar, Pelhar and Adane in the “Shurparaka-shatshashti vishaya”, the district of sixty-six villages around Sopara, so Sopara was a district headquarters as well as a port. [n03; CII-VI pp. 115–118] In the 1130s or 1140s King Aparaditya sent a scholar named Tejakantha from Sopara to a literary congress in Kashmir, recorded in Mankha’s Shrikanthacharita; the town still produced men of learning fit to represent the kingdom. [n36; Indraji 1882 p. 278 n. 8] [n03; Gaz XIII-II p. 426 n.] A stone of King Haripaladeva dated 1149 was found built into a house plinth at Sopara; a stone of Mallikarjuna from Bassein is dated 1160; another of Keshideva, dated 1203, came from near Mandvi. [n03; Gaz XIII-II pp. 426–427] A Silahara grant of 1255 found at Sandor, three miles north of Bassein, gives an astrologer a garden “within the limits of Sandor village” and names a “head-quarter station Nagapur” and a high road. The Gazetteer guesses that Nagapur was Nagaon, perhaps in Alibag; there is no evidence it was Naigaon in Vasai. [n37; Gaz XIV p. 402] The Nagapura of the 1094 plate is equally unidentified.

Jain merchants were part of this world too. Research in 2019 by Siddharth Kale, Monish Shah and two Jain monks found inscriptions of the thirteenth to sixteenth centuries showing a Jain Modha clan at Sopara; dated Jain bronzes of 1458–1555 are held by the Jain congregation at Agashi and Chalpeth, and a Surya image from Gass is dated 1412. [n03; Dalal 2019] [n02; Shah 2020] Jain tradition says that Pethad Shah (1223–1283), a famous minister and merchant, built a Parshvanath temple at Sopara, and the fourteenth-century writer Jinaprabhasuri listed Sopara among the eighty-four great Jain tirthas. Those are tradition, not documents. [n02; Shah 2020]

Thana’s corsairs and the passing of Sopara

By about 1300 the centre of trade had moved. “Before the beginning of the fourteenth century Thana had become the chief centre of trade. But Sopara was still a place of consequence,” the Gazetteer says. [n36; Gaz XIV pp. 321–322] Thana, on the Ulhas further inside the estuary, had what Idrisi called “a great gulf where vessels anchor”; Abulfeda, in the early fourteenth century, called it the best city of al-Lar, famous for tanasi cloth and tabashir. Of Sopara the same Abulfeda wrote, in a Latin translation: “I have found nothing worth recording about it.” [n03; Gaz XIV pp. 355–356] [n15; Hobson-Jobson]

Thana’s trade had a dark side, and the sea thread here acquires its first named pirates. Marco Polo, around 1290, described a “kingdom of Tana” whose king was tributary to no one and was “in league with corsairs, who plundered merchants and gave him all the horses they caught. No ships came without horses.” The kingdom imported gold, silver and copper and exported brown incense, cotton cloth and leather. [n36; Gaz XIV pp. 354–355] Two centuries later the Portuguese writer Duarte Barbosa (1514) complained of the same pirate “watch-boats” at Thana. [n36; Gaz XIII-II p. 438 n. 2] Ibn Battuta, in 1344, said that “from it ships of large burden went to Aden”. [n36; Gaz XIV pp. 356–357]

Sopara was not yet empty. In 1321 or 1322 the Dominican friar Jordanus Catalani went from Thana to Broach by way of “Supera”, carrying the bodies of four Franciscan companions executed at Thana. At Sopara he found “many Nestorian Christians and a church dedicated to St. Thomas the Apostle”, buried the four friars, and in a fifteen-day stay baptised many people and gave communion to about ninety Christians. He recommended Supera as a place where two friars might be stationed, and as “a good starting point for missionaries to Ethiopia”. [n15; Gaz XIV pp. 321–322; Hobson-Jobson, quoting Yule’s Cathay] To a Catalan friar in 1321, Sopara still looked like a port from which one could take ship for Africa.

The political map then shifted the trade further. When the Bahmani sultans moved their capital to Kulbarga in 1347, the coast trade went south to Chaul and Dabhol. The rise of Ahmadnagar after 1496 raised Chaul again. Horses from Ormuz and Aden kept a link to eastern Arabia. In the fifteenth century the conquests of the sultans of Ahmadabad reduced the North Konkan ports. The chief Thana ports of 1300–1500, in the Gazetteer’s list, were Thana, Chaul, Mahim and “Sopara a place of consequence”. [n36; Gaz XIII-II p. 444] Indraji summed up the ending: “In the fifteenth century … Sopara seems to have passed to the Musalmán rulers of Ahmadabad. Early in the sixteenth century the building of the Musalmán fort of Bassein, and later … the establishment at Bassein of the Court of the General of the North took from Sopára its former importance.” [n36; Indraji 1882 p. 279] A Persian notice of 1500 in the Mirat-i-Ahmadi still lists “Sorab”, perhaps Sopara, as a Konkan place trading with Gujarat. [n36; Gaz XIV p. 322 n. 3]

The Gazetteer offers one more picture of how trade on this coast worked, borrowed from Barbosa’s account of Chaul: a seasonal fair of four months, December to March, with “great caravans of oxen” stopping a league from the city to exchange the goods of the plateau for the goods of the sea. It thought this “probably true of the trade of the Thana ports from the earliest times”. That extrapolation backwards is speculation, but the season fits everything else in this thread: ships came with the end of the monsoon and left before it returned. [n36; Gaz XIII-II p. 446]

Gujarat’s harbour: Baxay

Bassein itself enters the record late. “As Bassein lies only six miles south of Sopara it can hardly have been a place of consequence so long as Sopara (A.D. 1100) remained a centre of trade,” the Gazetteer observes. A doubtful reference makes it the seat of a district under the Devagiri Yadavas, whose viceroys in the late thirteenth century had headquarters at Kalyan and Bassein and left stones near Bassein dated 1280 and 1288; but “there is no certain notice till 1507”. [n15; Gaz XIV p. 28] [n03; Gaz XIII-II p. 437; Gaz XIV p. 28] The Muslims called it Basai and the Portuguese Baçaim. [n03; Gaz XIV p. 28 n. 2]

Its rise came with the Gujarat sultanate and with war at sea. From about 1390 Gujarat held the coast through garrisons and tributary Hindu chiefs. In 1429, when a Bahmani minister seized Mahim and Salsette, Sultan Ahmad Shah of Gujarat sent seventeen ships from Diu, Gogha and Cambay, with an army by land, and retook Thana by land and sea. Around 1490 the rebel Bahadur Khan Gilani sent an Abyssinian commander, Yaqut, with twenty ships to waste Mahim, and a Gujarat fleet was lost in a storm. [n03; Gaz XIII-II pp. 441–443] The coast’s war fleets now had names and numbers. Mahmud Begada, the greatest of the Gujarat sultans, “effected his designs against Bassein and Bombay” in 1503, according to the Mirat-i-Ahmadi, or in 1507, the date of the Gazetteer’s first “certain notice” of Bassein. [n03; Gaz XIII-II pp. 442–443; Gaz XIV p. 28] At almost the same moment, around 1505–1507, timber from Bassein was being shipped to Egypt, by way of the Red Sea, to build the Mamluk fleet that the Sultan of Cairo was sending against the Portuguese. [n03; Gaz XIII-II p. 449] Before the Portuguese ever held Bassein, its teak was being cut to fight them.

Barbosa, writing in 1514, is the first European to describe the port. In the Gazetteer’s paraphrase, “Baxay” was “a town of Moors and Gentiles, a good seaport belonging to the king of Gujarat. Much merchandise was exchanged, and there was a great movement of shipping from all parts and many boats came from Malabar laden with arecas, cocoa, and spices.” [n15; Gaz XIV p. 28, citing Stanley’s Barbosa 68] Elsewhere the Gazetteer quotes him calling Bassein “a great place of trade”. [n36; Gaz XIII-II p. 465 n. 4] The government of the port was mixed: the governor of Chaul in 1503 and of Surat in 1514 were Hindus, the “lord of Thana” who became tributary to the Portuguese in 1529 was apparently a Hindu chief, and a “Gujarat and Bassein minister” corresponded with Albuquerque in 1510. [n03; Gaz XIII-II pp. 449–450 nn.]

Arnala, the island at the mouth of the Vaitarna, was already fortified. When the Portuguese arrived around 1530 they found “a strong fort in the hands of the Gujarat Musalmans with domes and Saracenic arches”. A modern summary attributes it to Mahmud Begada in 1516; the Gazetteer does not say who built it. [n15; Gaz XIV pp. 10–11] [n37; Gaz XIV pp. 10–11] [n13; Wikipedia: Arnala fort] Agashi, on the bay just south of Arnala, was “probably an old timber trade and ship-building centre”. [n37; Gaz XIV p. 1]

Two inscriptions from the eve of the Portuguese age show that the old landing places still lived. The Koprad stone, near Bolinj on the Sopara island, dated 1464, carries Muslim names and a Hijri date and names Mahim, Thana, Koprad and “Bimbstan”; it lay near the house of a Samvedi Brahmin headman. [n03; Gaz XIV, Koprad and Sopara] A stone at Sandor dated Hijra 966 (AD 1558) names a “Nayak Laro, lord of the western ocean”. The title is grand and the man is otherwise unknown. [n37; Gaz XIV p. 402]

The Portuguese arrive by fire

The Portuguese came to Bassein as raiders before they came as rulers. They had a trading factory there by 1526 (one later source says 1518). In 1528 or 1529 Heitor da Silveira destroyed the Gujarat fleet of a commander named Alishah near Bandra and Mahim, taking seventy-three prizes, then entered “the difficult Bassein river” by night, stormed its gun-defended works, beat Alishah’s 3,500 men, and plundered and burnt the town. [n03; Gaz XIII-II pp. 450–451; Gaz XIV p. 28 n. 7] [n04; Military history of Bassein] In 1530–31 Daman and Agashi were destroyed; the Portuguese burnt 300 ships at Agashi, and Antonio de Saldanha burnt Bulsar, Tarapur, Kelve-Mahim and Agashi on his way back from a failed attack on Diu. [n03; Gaz XIII-II p. 451] The Agashi burning is dated 1530 in most of the Gazetteer passages this book’s notes read, and 1536 in one; the 1530–31 date fits the campaign. [n36; Gaz XIV pp. 1–2] [n15; Gaz XIV p. 1] Three hundred boats is a measure of how busy that small bay was.

In 1532, “to put a stop to these raids”, Sultan Bahadur Shah ordered Malik Tokan, governor of Diu, to fortify Bassein. He built a citadel with ramparts and a salt-water ditch and garrisoned it with 12,000 or 15,000 men. Nuno da Cunha came with 150 sail and about 4,000 men, the walls were scaled, the defenders fled, and the Portuguese took 400 guns, razed the citadel and sailed away. [n03; Gaz XIV pp. 28–29; Gaz XIII-II p. 451]

Then the sultan, at war with the Mughal emperor Humayun as well as with the Portuguese and the Rajputs, bought peace at sea with a port. By the treaty signed on 23 December 1534 aboard the galleon São Mateus, Bahadur gave Bassein “with all its revenues” to Portugal. [n03; Wikipedia: Treaty of Bassein (1534)] [n04; Military history of Bassein] The 1882 Gazetteer dates the treaty to 1533; a separate peace treaty of 25 October 1535 confirmed the cession. [n03; Gaz XIII-II p. 451] The text, as printed by Danvers, is a sea document:

“provided that all the vessels from the kingdom of Guzerat, bound for the Straits, should first call at Bassein for passes, and on the return voyage call there again in order to pay the customary dues, under penalty and risk of seizure. No ships of war were to be built at any of the ports in the kingdom of Guzerat, but any number of trading ships might be constructed.” [n36; Danvers vol. I p. 416]

Horses were written in too: “All horses from the Straits of Mecca, and from other parts of Arabia, during the first three years after the completion of the fortress of Bassein, were to proceed thither, in order that Sultan Bedur and his vassals might purchase them there on payment of the customary dues to the King of Portugal, and they were not to be taken to the Deccan, Canara, or Malabar.” And the 5,000 larins formerly paid from Bassein’s revenues to mosques “was to continue to be so applied”. [n36; Danvers vol. I p. 416] [n37; Danvers p. 416]

This is the moment Bassein became a customs gate. Under the Portuguese pass system, the cartaz, every non-Portuguese merchant vessel had to buy a pass; the 1534 treaty made Gujarat’s Red Sea shipping take its pass at Bassein and pay dues there on the way home. Coastal trade, in the general summary, needed no cartaz. That summary comes from secondary web pages this book’s notes could not open; the treaty wording itself is the firmer evidence. [n36; GKToday; Gateway House] The horse clause turned the old Arab horse trade, which had run through Thana and Chaul for six centuries, into a Portuguese monopoly for three years, and kept the horses away from the Deccan sultanates the Portuguese feared. The treaty also explains why the Portuguese chose Bassein as their northern capital: the Sultan’s own clause had already made it the customs post for Gujarat’s Red Sea trade. [n37; Danvers p. 416; inference] Camões put the capture into the Lusíadas: “Bassein, though so strong in verity, / Shall yield thy guns to him through smoke and flame.” [n15; Gaz XIV p. 29 n.]

Counting the revenue: fedeas, xerafins and Agashi’s custom house

The Portuguese were meticulous accountants, and their accounts give this coast its first revenue figures.

In 1535 they took the first half-year’s revenue of the Bassein district: 158,475 fedeas, each worth 12 reis. It came from taxes and duties on coconut oil, opium, cotton, toddy, vegetables, fish, sugarcane and betel, and on the professions of butchers, dyers, Koli fishermen and shepherds. [n36; Da Cunha pp. 158–159] [n03; Gaz XIII-II p. 451] So the Koli fishermen of the coast paid a tax for their trade from the first year of Portuguese rule. A Tombo, a land register, drawn up by Simão Botelho in 1538, records the “Rent of the caçabe (Cusbah) of Çupara … 14,122 fedeas”, the revenue of Sopara town, now a minor item in someone else’s province. [n15; Hobson-Jobson] Sopara had four wooden stockades. [n36; Da Cunha p. 159]

Agashi had its own fortress and, importantly, “a custom-house”. Its captain drew 30,000 reis a year and its customs clerk 20,000. [n36; Da Cunha pp. 158–159] And Agashi preserves the one run of customs figures for any port on this coast before the nineteenth century. Its customs and town yielded, in fedeas: 281,249 in 1636 (about £234 by the Gazetteer’s reckoning); 313,874 in 1637; 162,822 in 1638; 176,876 in 1640; 199,400 in 1641; 216,400 in 1642; and 38,800 in 1643, about £32. [n36; Gaz XIV p. 1 n. 4, citing Colleção de Monumentos Ineditos V-2, 142] The series shows a port at its peak in 1636–37, falling by almost half in 1638, recovering slowly, and then collapsing to an eighth of its peak value in 1643. The research notes link the collapse to the damage of the 1630s: a Mughal (Gujarat) raid of 1634 that destroyed a Dominican monastery at Agashi, and an earlier burning. [n36; Gaz XIV p. 1 n. 4] [n37; Gaz XIV pp. 1–2] Converting fedeas reliably into anything modern is unsafe; the run is best read as a rough index of the port’s fortunes. [n36; inference]

Bassein’s own revenue climbed over the following century. Da Cunha gives 172,920 xerafins in 1680, 194,748 in 1709 and 310,779 in 1718–19, with OCR doubts on some digits; the Gazetteer converts the revenue to £8,646 in 1686, £9,737 in 1709, £15,639 in 1718 and £45,706 in 1729. [n36; Da Cunha pp. 145–146] [n32; Gaz XIV p. 34 n. 8] The figures are for the whole district, land as well as sea; no separate total for the customs of Bassein town survives in the sources used here. [n36; gap]

The paperwork of the port is visible in a list of officials around 1634. Bassein had a sea bailiff on £2 10s, a customs storekeeper (almoxarife dos armazens), and a “master builder” on £3 16s. [n36; Gaz XIV p. 31] Strangely for a customs gate, it had no royal customs house: “there being no mandovim or custom-house built at Bassein belonging to the king, a private house was rented for that purpose, paying 84 pardaos a year.” The ferry boatmen of the Bassein river were paid 200 réis a month. [n36; Da Cunha, Bassein officials] [n32; DC 222–223] The Captain of Bassein, who served a three-year term drawn from certain families, earned 600,000 réis a year around 1550. [n32; Gaz XIV p. 30; DC 218–219]

Teak, basalt and ships for Europe

What Bassein sold, above all, was what grew and lay in its hinterland: timber and stone.

Cesare de’ Federici, a Venetian merchant who travelled these coasts in the 1560s, was blunt: “After Daman comes Basain with many towns … from which nothing is drawn but rice, wheat, and much timber”, and compared with Daman it was “a filthy place”. The English merchant Ralph Fitch in 1583 named it among the places of most trade in corn and rice. Akbar’s chronicler Abul Fazl, in the Ain-i-Akbari (1586), listed “Bussy” as an emporium like Daman, Sanjan, Mahim and Tarapur, “all five of them being in the possession of the Europeans through the negligence of the Moghal officers”. [n15; Hobson-Jobson; Gaz XIV pp. 29–30] In the 1580s, the Gazetteer says, Bassein traded in rice, corn and “timber for ship building”. [n36; Gaz XIV pp. 30–31]

The French traveller Pyrard de Laval, who saw it in 1607, gave the classic description: “From Bassein is exported the best timber for the building of houses and ships, the greater part of which latter are built here.” “No place had better timber than Bassein.” In the Gazetteer’s words, “In the beginning of the seventeenth century (1607) Bassein was a great place for ship-building and had a large trade in timber and building stone, which was as fine and hard as granite, and was used in all the Goa churches and palaces.” [n36; Gaz XIV pp. 30–31; Da Cunha pp. ~143–144; Gaz XIII-II p. 468] Dom João de Castro in 1538 noticed the great basalt columns across the water from Bassein. [n36; Gaz XIII-II pp. 465–468] When the visitor to Old Goa today looks up at its churches, some of that stone may have been cut on this coast and carried south by sea.

The teak went further than Goa. “Great quantities of the finest teak” went “to Goa, Gujarat, Sindh, and occasionally to the Persian Gulf and the Red Sea”. [n36; Gaz XIII-II pp. 465–468] Some of that stone, at least, came from closer to home than any quarry. During the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, the Gazetteer records, the banks of Sopara’s lakes “were stripped and the temples pulled down, and the stones used in building Portuguese churches, forts, and houses”. [n36; Gaz XIV p. 323] The old port was quarried to build the new one.

And the shipyards were famous. “Between 1550 and 1600 great ships built at Agashi and Bassein made many voyages to Europe.” [n36; Gaz XIII-II pp. 468–469] Agashi in 1630, “though poor in buildings, … had a rich timber trade, and built ships as good as Portuguese ships, able to make the voyage to Europe.” [n36; Gaz XIV pp. 1–2] The Gazetteer took these claims from the Portuguese chroniclers De Couto and Barros, and they have not been independently checked. [n36; gap] A modern geographer, B. Arunachalam, writing in 2002, says that Agashi built ships of up to 500 tons for both Marathas and Portuguese, and that a seventeenth-century Kutchi sea manual describes a regular cargo route through the Bolinj–Sopara creek. These are reported second-hand, through Shah and his colleagues; neither the manual nor Arunachalam’s paper was read for this book. [n02; Shah 2020, citing Arunachalam 2002] [n01; Shah et al. 2020] If the Kutchi manual is correct, then Kutchi navigators, the same seafaring community whose Musalman carpenters were still building boats here in 1882, knew the inner channel of the Sopara island well enough to write it into their pilot books.

The Gazetteer preserves the Portuguese chronicler Gaspar Correa’s description of the local craft of the Konkan. Their planks were either sewn together with coir thread, for keeled boats, or nailed, for flat-bottomed ones. Pitched cloth and cane mats kept out the sea. Cargo chambers were roofed with woven palm leaf. A boat carried one large mast and a long-sprit lateen sail and was steered close to the wind. [n36; Gaz XIII-II pp. 468–469] This is the oldest description of the working boats of this coast, and the lateen-rigged craft of the 1880s, described later in this chapter, are its direct descendants.

The English, too, came to Bassein for ships. “In 1634, the English had four pinnaces built for the coast trade, two at Daman and two at Bassein,” the Gazetteer records from Bruce’s Annals. Da Cunha dates the order to 1636: “two pinnaces were built for the Surat Factory at Bassein, which with two others built at Damaun formed the nucleus of what afterwards developed into the Bombay Marine.” [n36; Gaz XIII-II pp. 468–469; Da Cunha] [n32; DC 221 n.] The East India Company’s navy, which would one day patrol this whole coast, began in part with two small hulls from Bassein’s slipways.

Captains who traded on their own account

Who profited from this timber? Not, it seems, the king of Portugal.

“Under the Portuguese, foreign trade was a monopoly of the king,” the Gazetteer explains. “Most of the local sea trade was in the hands of free-traders or interlopers, whom the Portuguese government tried to put down.” [n36; Gaz XIII-II p. 465] And the timber trade was in the hands of the government’s own men. “The Bassein timber trade was chiefly carried on by the captains of forts and other government officers.” [n36; Gaz XIII-II p. 465] A royal letter of 13 March 1587 complained that the captains of Bassein and Daman supplied timber for the king’s own armada “from captains themselves, who charged far higher prices than their value in the market”, because they traded on their own account. [n36; Da Cunha p. 144, citing Archivo Portuguez Oriental fasc. III p. 106] The captains who commanded the forts were also the timber merchants who sold to the crown, and they overcharged it.

This is a pattern the copper plate of 1094 has already shown: the men who governed the coast also owned its trade. The names of the captains of Bassein survive, from Garcia de Sá in 1535 through Francisco de Sá in 1554, a long gap that “cannot be easily filled up”, and then a continuous run from Nuno Vaz de Castelo Branco in 1611 to Caetano de Sousa Pereira in 1739. [n32; DC 219 n.] No surviving document in the sources used here ties a particular timber deal to a particular captain. The royal letter names the practice, not the men.

The research brief for this book asked about the casados, the married Portuguese settlers who, across Portuguese Asia, often lived by private trade. The term does not appear in any of the sources this book’s notes read for Bassein. What the sources do record is about 400 Portuguese families, 200 Indian Christian families and 1,800 slaves living in Bassein in 1634, the “free-traders or interlopers” the government tried to suppress, and a nobility rich enough to be proverbial. [n04; Vasai Fort, Wikipedia] [n36; Gaz XIII-II p. 465] Who among those 400 families traded at sea, and in what, is a question the record here does not answer.

The rich of Bassein were very rich. There were, the Gazetteer says, “more than 100 families of the highest in India and proverbially rich”, and the ladies of Bassein once subscribed £10,000 (200,000 xerafins) to build a nunnery in Goa. [n36; Gaz XIV p. 29 n. 6, citing Da Cunha p. 246] [n15; Gaz XIV pp. 30, 33] Dr John Fryer, an English surgeon who visited in 1675, saw the city at its height: the fidalgos had “stately Dwellings, graced with covered Balconies, and large Windows two Stories high, with Panes of Oister shell”, and “few Artisans are tolerated within the Walls”. He also noticed who was not allowed in: “None but Christians lodge within the City, the Banyans repairing to the Suburbs upon Tattoo,” that is, the Hindu merchants, the banias, had to leave the walled city when the evening drum beat. On his way in he passed at Ghodbandar the estate of “the Richest Don on this side Goa”. [n15; Fryer/Crooke I pp. 190–193] The Portuguese called the high-class Hindus of Bassein “Parabus”, Prabhus. [n36; Da Cunha p. 144 n.] Much later, Bishop Heber, walking through the ruins in 1825, heard that one of the churches had been built by a man who made his fortune in the slipper trade, an echo across six centuries of Masudi’s Sopara slippers. [n15; Gaz XIV pp. 31 n., 35]

The port’s reach can be measured by the list of places it traded with. After the middle of the sixteenth century, the Gazetteer says, the North Konkan ports’ old share of the trade with Europe “left … for Goa and for Diu”. But “Bassein, Mahim, Thana, and Chaul maintained a large coasting traffic with the Malabar, Gujarat, and Sindh ports, and a considerable foreign trade with the Persian Gulf, the Arabian and African coasts”. The connected ports it lists run from Cambay, Diu, Surat and Diul-Sindhi; through Goa, Calicut, Cochin and Quilon; to Chittagong; to Ormuz, Maskat, Shehr, Julfar and Kalat in the Gulf; to Socotra, Aden, Mocha and Jidda; to Zaila, Quiloa, Brava, Mombasa, Melinda, Mogadishu and Sofala on the African coast; to Colombo; and to Malacca and Achin. [n36; Gaz XIII-II p. 465] The cargoes outward were rice, pulse, vegetables, coconuts and betelnuts, teak and basalt, local cotton cloth and muslins to Malabar, Diu, Persia, Arabia and Africa, local blankets and silks, and sandals. Inward came dates and raisins, Spanish wine, silks, gold from Sofala, ivory from Abyssinia, spices from Malabar, Sumatra, Ceylon, Borneo and the Moluccas, horses from the Persian Gulf and the Arab coast, elephants from Ceylon, slaves from Abyssinia. Pilgrims went out to Mecca. [n36; Gaz XIII-II pp. 465–468] The merchants changed: the Gazetteer notes “the disappearance of the Chinese, and the decrease of Arabs and Turks”, and new arrivals, Portuguese, English, Dutch, French and Danes; Hindu traders from this coast were met in the Persian Gulf, at Mocha, in African ports, at Malacca and Achin. [n36; Gaz XIII-II p. 468]

The slaves in that list are not a figure of speech. The 1,800 slaves of Bassein in 1634 were a large share of its people; house slaves from Africa were “made Christians and sometimes raised to be priests”, and the Gazetteer found “African blood” among Salsette Christians and traces of it among Konkani Muslims, some Katkaris and Thakurs. [n37; Gaz XIII-II p. 460 n. 3; Gaz XIII-I p. 200 n. 3] [n35; Gaz XIII-I p. 64] The sea brought people to this coast who had not chosen to come.

Even so, the Gazetteer’s overall verdict on Portuguese Bassein’s commerce is restrained. Bassein was “never so busy a trade centre as Chaul”, it wrote, though it “was famous for its ship building”. [n36; Gaz XIV pp. 30–31] Portuguese Bassein was an administrative, military and timber-and-stone city more than an entrepot, living off its villages and its nobility rather than off merchant shipping. [n36; inference]

Passes, horses and storms: the long seventeenth century

The treaty of 1534 had made Bassein a gate on the sea road from Gujarat to the Red Sea. What the records do not show is the gate at work. No register of the cartazes issued at Bassein, no list of the Gujarat ships that called there for passes, and no account of the dues they paid on the way home survives in the sources this book could use. The treaty clause is the whole of the evidence: ships “bound for the Straits, should first call at Bassein for passes, and on the return voyage call there again in order to pay the customary dues, under penalty and risk of seizure”. [n36; Danvers vol. I p. 416] How often the penalty was imposed, and how many ships slipped past, the record does not say. [n36; gap]

The horse clause has the same silence after it. For three years after the fortress was finished, horses from the Straits of Mecca and from Arabia were to go to Bassein, where the Sultan and his vassals could buy them after paying dues to the King of Portugal. [n36; Danvers vol. I p. 416] What happened when the three years ran out is not recorded here. Horses “from the Persian Gulf and the Arab coast” stay on the Gazetteer’s list of what the North Konkan ports imported under the Portuguese, with elephants from Ceylon beside them, but without a number, a buyer or a date. [n36; Gaz XIII-II pp. 465–468] It is an odd end for the oldest import of this coast. The horse dealer Lobhadeva came to Sopara in a novel of 778; Thana took in, by one Arab report, as many as 10,000 horses a year; Marco Polo was told that no ship came to Thana without horses; and a sultan wrote horses into the treaty that gave Bassein away. [n36; Shah et al. 2020; Gaz XIII-II pp. 430–431; Gaz XIV pp. 354–355] After 1534 the trade fades into a line in a list. By 1881 the whole Bassein sub-division counted 128 horses among its livestock, a figure read from a damaged table. [n21; Gaz XIII-II p. 680, OCR uncertain]

The wider ocean was slipping away from the Portuguese, and from Bassein with them. In the seventeenth century the European trade of western India passed to Surat, and the Portuguese lost Ormuz in 1622 and Maskat in 1650, the two Gulf ports that had anchored their horse and pass system at its western end. [n36; Gaz XIII-II p. 465] Closer to home, the seven islands of Bombay, which had been a minor dependency of Baçaim since 1534, passed to England in the marriage treaty of 1661 as part of the dowry of Catherine of Braganza; one secondary page gives both 1661 and 1665 for the transfer. [n04; Wikipedia: Vasai Fort; Bombay East Indians] For 127 years Bombay had been an outlying piece of the province governed from Bassein. Within two centuries the relation would be reversed for good.

The sea also brought disasters, and the Portuguese recorded them. In 1618 the province suffered plague, then a cyclone on 15 May, then famine. [n04; Wikipedia: Vasai Fort] At Agashi the storm blew a boat in from the sea and into a house, killing a woman and her child; at Bassein it wrecked the “provision boats” that carried the city’s food. [n36; Gaz XIV pp. 1, 31] [n11; Gaz XIV p. 2] In 1634 raiders from Mughal Gujarat destroyed a “handsome Dominican monastery” at Agashi, and the customs run quoted earlier in this chapter shows Agashi’s yield halving in 1638 and collapsing in 1643. [n11; Gaz XIV p. 2] [n36; Gaz XIV p. 1 n. 4] One caution belongs here. The research notes read the dates of that customs series in two ways: as 1636–1643 in the trade note and as 1536–1543 in the village note, from what seems to be the same Gazetteer footnote. This book has followed 1636–43, which fits the raid of 1634; the printed page should be checked before anyone builds an argument on the dates. [n36; n11; gap]

Pirates came back too. The Gazetteer records that “Maskat pirates” and Pendharis harassed Bassein in the later seventeenth century. [n36; Gaz XIII-II p. 484] A secondary page, whose sourcing it flags as unreliable, says that in 1674 some 600 Muscat Arab raiders plundered the churches outside the walls; in the same year More Pandit forced the Portuguese to pay the Marathas a quarter of Baçaim’s revenues, the chauth. [n04; Wikipedia: Vasai Fort] Maskat had been lost by the Portuguese in 1650; a generation later raiders from it were on the Bassein coast. The sea that had once brought Arab horse ships to Thana now brought Arab war boats to Bassein.

Then disease. About 1690 the city was “stricken by a pestilential fever or plague, ‘exactly like a bubo’”, which the Portuguese called the carazzo; it came back at intervals for several years and “robbed the city of about one-third of its people”. [n21; Gaz XIV p. 33] [n36; Gaz XIII-II p. 484] The early eighteenth-century return of the district’s population was 60,499, almost all of them native Christians; the notes read that count as 58,131 in Da Cunha and 58,181 in the Gazetteer. [n36; Da Cunha pp. 145–146] [n21; Gaz XIV p. 33] The traveller Hamilton, writing about 1700, summed up the port in one sentence the Gazetteer preserved: “Bassein was a place of small trade, its riches dead and buried in the churches.” [n36; Gaz XIII-II p. 485]

The fort itself still faced the water. In the late 1590s description that the Gazetteer takes from a Portuguese chronicle, its walls carried ninety pieces of artillery and seventy mortars and the city was “defended by twenty-one gunboats” of sixteen to eighteen guns each. [n32; Gaz XIV pp. 30, 34 n. 8] Its Sea Gate, the Porta do Mar, still has teak doors cased with iron bars and spikes, and on one of them, half hidden by a bar, the date 20 November 1720. [n32; Gaz XIV pp. 38–39; DC 213–214] Teak from the hinterland, the wood that had built ships for Europe, ended as the city’s last door to the sea.

The final Portuguese land register, the Tombo de Baçaim of 1727–30, shows how the profits of the sea and the shore were parcelled out in the last years. The coloria of Agashi and of “Ilha das Vacas”, Arnala island, was held by a widow, D. Domingas Pereira de Azevedo; the urracas, the toddy rents of Baçaim, by Francisco Pereira Pinto. [n39; Teixeira and Pires 2007, tombo fls. 26, 41–42] The notes take the coloria to be a revenue lease, perhaps on fisheries or boats; the word is not explained in the source they read, so that reading is an inference. [n39; inference] What is plain is that in 1727 the island at the mouth of the Vaitarna, and whatever income came off its water, belonged to a Portuguese lady of Bassein. Ten years later it belonged to the Marathas.

The Angres and the fall of Arnala (c. 1650–1739)

The power that took the sea from the Portuguese had grown up on the same coast, a little to the south. Its admirals were the Angres, and the 1882 Gazetteer preserves a strange story about where they came from. In a footnote it quotes the writer Grose: Kanhoji Angre’s grandfather was “a Musalman negro from the Persian Gulf”, who became a Hindu after two sea fights about 1650. [n36; Gaz XIII-II p. 412 n. 2] This is a single colonial-era report, repeated without comment, and no other source in the notes supports or tests it. It is worth recording for one reason only: it shows how a European writer could imagine the greatest Indian admiral of the coast, as the grandson of a Gulf sailor. That fits a remark in the community self-account of the Konkani Muslims that Enthoven recorded: “As late as the Marathas the services of Arab seamen were still in demand.” [n35; Enthoven III, Musalmans]

Kanhoji Angre died in 1729, and his fleet split. According to secondary sources, the Peshwa’s navy came to have its headquarters at Vasai, which can only mean after 1739, and most Maratha vessels were built of teak from the Bombay region. [n36; Wikipedia: Kanhoji Angre; Wikipedia: Arnala fort] No source in the notes describes Peshwa-era shipbuilding at Vasai itself; the nearest evidence is the Gazetteer’s note that good fishing boats and coasting craft were still being built at Agashi and Sopara in 1837. [n36; Gaz XIV pp. 2, 323; gap]

The first Maratha target on this coast was the island at the mouth of the Vaitarna. The Gazetteer describes Arnala fort, “also called Janjira or The Island”, as guarding “the southern main entrance of the Vaitarna, the most extensive inlet in the north Konkan”. [n11; Gaz XIV p. 10] When the Portuguese arrived about 1530 they had found a strong Gujarat fort there, destroyed it, and handed the place over to a Portuguese gentleman of Bassein. [n11; Gaz XIV pp. 10–11] Whoever held Arnala held the mouth of the river that ran up past Sopara and Bolinj into the hinterland.

The Marathas understood that. Secondary accounts say that Shankarji Pant urged Chimaji Appa to attack Arnala for its naval value. A first assault, made with Govindji Kasar and the navy of Manaji Angre, failed. A second, under Chimaji, succeeded on 28 March 1737. [n13; Wikipedia: Arnala fort] [n36; Wikipedia: Arnala fort] Da Cunha, writing in 1876, adds the grim detail that the Marathas “put its commandant and garrison to the sword”. [n32; DC 147–148] Baji Rao I then ordered the fort rebuilt. A Marathi inscription over the north gate records the rebuilding in 1737 (Shaka 1659) by an architect named Baji Tulaji; one local source gives the completion date as 23 May 1737, a date that still needs a primary check. [n11; Gaz XIV p. 11] [n13; Durg Bharari] The rebuilt fort was called Janjire Arnala, the island fort, and also Jaldurg, the water fort. [n39; Incredible India] On it stood, in 1882, temples of Trimbakeshvar and Bhavani and the tomb of two Muslim saints, Shahali and Hajiali, each with a small yearly grant from the government, and a stone-lined eight-cornered pond. [n11; Gaz XIV p. 11]

Arnala was the first link to break. In the next two years the Marathas seized the islands of Versova and Dharavi and the creeks around Salsette. [n32; DC 147] In March 1739, by the secondary account of the campaign, Manaji Angre took Uran, Bandra, Versova and Dharavi and then joined Chimaji at Bassein in April. [n04; Wikipedia: Battle of Vasai] The Portuguese governor of Salsette, Luis de Botelho, withdrew to Karanja. Bombay, the English port that had once belonged to Bassein, refused help despite the appeals of João de Sousa Terras. [n32; DC 147–148] Bassein was cut off from its creeks and from the sea that had always been its road to Goa.

The siege began on 17 February 1739. It was won by miners, not ships: some twelve or thirteen mines were sprung, two exploded and opened a great breach, a third blew up “hundreds of the assailants”, and the final breach came beneath the tower of São Sebastião. [n32; DC 148; Gaz XIV p. 34] The garrison surrendered on 16 May 1739, about 800 Portuguese having died; the figure of about 12,000 Marathas killed and wounded is in both Da Cunha and the Gazetteer, as a round number repeated from an older review. [n32; DC 148; Gaz XIV p. 34] The terms let “all the garrison, as well regulars as auxiliaries” march out “with all the honours of war”, and gave the inhabitants eight days to leave with their movable goods. [n32; DC 148–149] The secondary account says the garrison went by sea to Bombay. [n04; Wikipedia: Battle of Vasai, citing Monteiro and da Silva 1996 p. 154] The Portuguese had arrived at Bassein in 1529 by boat, at night, up the difficult river. Two hundred and ten years later they left it the same way.

Bajipur: the Maratha port and the Peshwa’s admiral (1739–1818)

The Marathas renamed the city Bajipur, “Baji Rao’s city”, and made it the headquarters of a governor whose charge ran along the coast from the Bankot river to Daman. [n32; Gaz XIV p. 34; DC 149] [n36; Gaz XIII-II pp. 498–499] The first governor, or subhedar, was Shankarji Keshav Phadke, the commander for the Agashi–Arnala country. [n04; Wikipedia: Vajreshwari temple] A local source credits him with the Bhavani Shankar temple and lake at Agashi, built between 1739 and 1750; the same tradition says large stones and old images were found when the lake was dug. [n11; Durg Bharari, single source]

What happened to Bassein’s sea trade under the Peshwas is almost unrecorded in the notes. The fort was a naval as well as a civil seat. Around 1780, the Gazetteer says, “The governor of Bassein, one of the Peshwa’s admirals”, “used to attack English ships”. [n36; Gaz XIII-II pp. 498–499, 504 n. 3] That single line is the only sign in the sources of Bassein as a Maratha naval base in action. The names of the admirals, the number and size of their ships, and whether any were built here, the records used for this book do not give. [n36; gap]

What the record does show is people arriving by sea. “About the middle of the eighteenth century (1760–1766) a considerable number of Cambay Vanis, chiefly of the Lad sub-division, and with them several Gujarat Brahmans settled in Supara, Bassein, and other coast towns to avoid the exactions of Momin Khan II (1748–1783)” of Cambay. [n35; Gaz XIII-I p. 63] The notes infer, reasonably, that they came by the old Cambay–Bassein sea route, more than a century before the railway. [n35; inference] The Lad Vanis will be met again below as the leading shopkeepers and moneylenders of the British coast.

The French scholar Anquetil du Perron passed through in 1760. At Agashi he found “the lanes full of people going to church as freely as in a Christian country”, under Maratha rule; at Bassein he described the fort and noted that the Marathas had “condemned the south-west gate”. [n11; Gaz XIV p. 2] [n32; Gaz XIV p. 34 n. 2] The Christian farmers and boat-builders of the coast had stayed.

The English came next, by land and sea. General Goddard besieged Bassein from 13 November to 11 December 1780 and took it for the East India Company. [n04; FIBIS, citing Grant Duff] At Arnala, in January 1781, the Maratha commandant refused to give up the island until the British had prepared a bombardment “from Agashi”; a secondary source gives the date of the British capture as 18 January 1781. [n11; Gaz XIV p. 11; Wikipedia: Arnala Fort] The Treaty of Salbai, signed on 17 May 1782, gave Bassein and Arnala back to the Marathas. [n04; GKToday] Twenty years later the place gave its name to a turning point of Indian history: on 31 December 1802 the fleeing Peshwa Baji Rao II signed here the Treaty of Bassein, accepting a permanent force of about 6,000 Company troops and ceding districts worth Rs 26 lakh a year to pay for them. [n04; Wikipedia: Treaty of Bassein (1802)] [n28; Gaz XIII-II] A treaty signed at sea off Bassein in 1534 had begun Portuguese rule on this coast; a treaty signed at Bassein in 1802 began the end of Maratha rule. The Company took the coast for good in 1817–18, and in 1818 Arnala ranked next to Bassein among the forts of the North Konkan coast. [n11; Gaz XIV pp. 10–11] [n04; Wikipedia: Treaty of Bassein (1802)]

Under the Company the trade figures begin again, and they show a working port. The Bombay returns put the trade of the Bassein ports with Bombay and Surat at “about three and a half lakhs” of rupees in 1802, two lakhs of exports and one and a half of imports; at nine lakhs in 1805; and at an average of “about nine lakhs of rupees … of which about five lakhs were exports” for the five years to 1806, a figure the Gazetteer took from the trade writer Milburn. In 1805 Bassein exported piece-goods, grain, iron, sugar, coconuts, betelnut, dates, pepper, turmeric and treasure, and imported ivory, oil, timber, hemp, piece-goods and betelnut. The Gazetteer thought the iron and ivory odd, perhaps the sign of a direct trade with Africa, perhaps a quirk of the recording clerk. [n36; Gaz XIII-II pp. 512–513] It is the only glimpse in the sources of Bassein’s trade between the Portuguese and the 1870s.

By then the real centre of the coast had moved. “Almost the whole of the foreign trade” of western India, the Gazetteer says, passed through Bombay by 1800. [n36; Gaz XIII-II p. 513 n. 3] The Bombay Dockyard, founded in 1735 under Parsi master builders of the Wadia family from Surat, built 159 ships of over 100 tons between 1736 and 1821, of Malabar teak; this book’s notes have that from a search summary only. [n36; Wikipedia: Lovji Nusserwanjee Wadia, search summary] The shipbuilding that had made Bassein and Agashi famous in 1600 now belonged to the island the Portuguese had let go.

A market town of Gujarati shops (1818–1860)

The first British descriptions of Bassein are of a ruin beside a market. In 1825 Bishop Heber found the fort “perfectly uninhabited”, its houses and churches under brushwood. [n28; Gaz XIV] In 1837 Vaupell called Bassein “the chief market town of a petty division”, its shops “mostly held by Gujarati Vanis and a few Muslims”. [n28; Gaz XIV] [n32; Gaz XIV pp. 35–36] In 1838 the traveller Mrs Postans described it as long forsaken, the haunt of a few fishermen and hunters. [n28; Gaz XIV] The same year’s account of Agashi is livelier: in 1837 it was “the chief town on Bassein island”, with a large Christian and Hindu population and a brisk trade in grain, garden produce and salt to Gujarat and Bombay, and “very good fishing boats and coasting craft were built” there. [n11; Gaz XIV p. 2] [n36; Gaz XIV pp. 1–2]

The Company’s officials tried to make the old fort pay. In 1829 a Mr Lingard was given a forty-year rent-free lease of about 83 acres on the fort esplanade and an advance of £2,300 to grow Mauritius cane and build a sugar mill; he died in 1832. A Hindu lessee, Narayan Krishna, failed with it in 1833–36; McGregor Brownrigg and Company took a perpetual lease in 1841 and reported in 1843 that cane did not thrive; in 1852 the ruined church of Nossa Senhora da Vida inside the fort was turned into a sugar factory; and in 1860 Macfarlane and Littlewood formed a “Bassein Sugar Company”, sold the next year. By 1881 the company “has long ceased to exist”. [n21; Gaz XIII-I pp. 394–395] [n28; Gaz XIII-I] A road to the landing place was built in 1856. [n32; Gaz XIV p. 36] The interior of the fort was leased in 1860 for thirty years to Major Littlewood, whose widow’s tenancy the Collector blamed in 1882 for making “the proper preservation of the ruins almost impossible”. [n32; Gaz XIV pp. 37–38]

The traders who ran this market are the best-documented merchant community in the sources, and the first who can be followed from one generation to the next.

The traders of the Company’s coast

The Lad Vanis. In 1882 the Gazetteer counted about 120 families of Lad Vanis in the district: “about forty are in Thana, thirty-five in Supara, and the rest in Bassein, Agashi, Nala, Papdi, and Dahanu”. [n35; Gaz XIII-I pp. 112–113] Their name comes from Lat-desh, the old name of South Gujarat; they are divided into Visa and Dasa sections, worship the family goddess Ashapuri of Ashmai near Petlad, and employ Khedawal Brahmans as priests. [n35; Enthoven III, “Vania: Lad”, p. 428 approx.] The Gazetteer gave them an old pedigree on this coast: “Masudi’s statement that when he wrote (915) the Lar language was spoken in the coast towns as far south as Chaul makes it probable that from very early times Lad Vanis had settled along the Thana coast for purposes of trade.” But “most of the present families are late settlers”, the Cambay refugees of the 1760s. [n35; Gaz XIII-I pp. 112–113] That is an argument from a language name, not proof of continuity across eight centuries, and it should be read as the Gazetteer’s inference.

In 1882 the Lad Vanis were shopkeepers, moneylenders, superior landholders and merchants. They spoke “Gujarati among themselves and Marathi with others”. They were Vaishnavas of the Vallabhacharya sect who also observed Shaiva fasts and feasts, which the Gazetteer put down to “close connection with Maratha Brahmans”, and they took brides from Cambay, Jambusar and Bombay rather than from the Thana Lad families. [n35; Gaz XIII-I pp. 112–113] A trading people of Gujarat, then, settled for a century on a Marathi coast, bilingual at the shop counter, still marrying back into the old home ports. At Sopara itself, the Gazetteer says, the traders in 1882 were mainly Muslims and Gujarati Vanis. [n05; Gaz XIV, Sopara entry, search snippet]

The Vanis of Papdi. One branch of the trade had a village of its own. Betel-nut (areca) palms grew “chiefly in garden lands at Bassein and Bombay-Mahim”, tended partly by Christian growers (converted Malis) and partly by Pachkalshi Hindus. The growers sold their nuts wholesale to “the Vanis of Papdi”, about two miles from Bassein, who sorted them into six grades, phulbardi, tambdi, chikni, lavangchuri, pandhri and dagdi or kapkadi. “Bassein is famous for its phulbardi betelnuts.” At Papdi they fetched Rs 21 to Rs 36 for a maund of 80 pounds, and they went out to Bombay, Poona and Gujarat. [n21; Gaz XIII-I pp. 298–300] Papdi’s Vanis were middlemen in the classic sense: they stood between a garden belt of small Christian and Hindu growers and the markets of three regions.

The Marwari Vanis. A second wave of traders came by a different road. Marwari Vanis, the Gazetteer says, “are believed to have come to Thana from Rajputana or Marwar, almost entirely since the British conquest. Their usual route has been through Gujarat and Bombay, and since the railway has been opened, they have come in great numbers.” Their first movement “followed the very liberal and general reduction of rent … between 1835 and 1838”, which left the landholder a margin; “the Marvadis came, advanced money at from 100 to 200 per cent to the husbandmen, and sold them up”. In 1846 the Collector, Mr Law, observed that “the thrifty avaricious Marvadi had begun to settle even in the remotest villages”. They were Jains, kept weekly fasts in Bhadrapad, and in the Gazetteer’s words “carried their gains to their own country, the Konkan benefiting nothing”. [n35; Gaz XIII-I pp. 64, 113–114] The adjectives are the colonial author’s, and the book records them as his judgement. What lies under them is a real shift: the trade of this coast was moving from the sea to the countryside, from shipping cargo to lending against land and crops, and the newcomers came overland.

Lohanas, Bhatias, Bohoras, Khojas and Memons. Lohanas and Bhatias from Cutch and North Gujarat were “grain and cloth merchants in most of the leading towns” of the district. Bohora and Memon traders came “from Gujarat through Bombay”; Musalman and Khoja merchants carried hides and bones. [n35; Gaz XIII-I pp. 64, 113–114] [n36; Gaz XIII-I p. 301] The Konkani Muslims, the descendants of the old Arab and Persian settlers, were described by Enthoven as landholders, traders in rice, timber and salt, and sailors. [n35; Enthoven III, Musalmans] Some of these were the owners of the boats described below.

The Parsis. The Parsis had a longer history on this coast than any of these Gujarati groups, as the Pahlavi inscriptions at Kanheri and the Chinchani plates show, and a tradition recorded by Enthoven says they left Portuguese Thana for Kalyan about 1533 and came back under British patronage around 1774. [n35; Enthoven III pp. 192–195] By 1881 there were only 27 Parsis in the whole Bassein sub-division, 14 of them in the town. [n21; Gaz XIII-II p. 680; Gaz XIV p. 28] The Gazetteer described the Parsis of the district as “Gujarat Parsi liquor-contractors and Government servants”. [n35; Gaz XIII-I p. 65] They left two buildings at Bassein: a rest-house built in 1780 by Lavji Sorabji Lakdawala, and another built in 1836 by Dadabhai and Maneckji Pestonji Wadia. [n32; Gaz XIV p. 37] The donors’ trades are not recorded. Wadia is also the name of the Parsi master builders of the Bombay dockyard, but no source in the notes ties these two donors to that family or to the sea. [n32; n36; gap]

The Jains. The Jain traders of the twentieth century are mostly Gujarati and Marwari. Their living centre on this coast is the Munisuvrat Swami tirth at Agashi (Chalpeth), whose image, temple tradition says, came out of the pond at Chakreshwar about two hundred years ago, with the temple linked to a merchant named Motisha Seth. That is temple and blog tradition, not a dated record. [n08; temple and blog claims] The older Jain bronzes held by the congregations at Agashi and Chalpeth, dated 1458–1555, are mentioned earlier in this chapter.

Machvas, mhangiris and tandels: the boats of the 1880s

The 1882 Gazetteer gives the fullest description of this coast’s working boats that exists before modern times, and it reads like a portrait of a living tradition.

The Thana coast, it says, had ten varieties of lateen-rigged craft. The local coasters were the balav, used for fishing; the machva (also called suval); the padav, a cotton boat; and the mhangiri, a large padav. “Some are built in Bombay, Versova, Dantivra, Mahim, Karanja, and Daman, but most in the Bassein sub-division, chiefly at Bassein.” [n36; Gaz XIII-I pp. 342–343] For the boats of its own three ports the Gazetteer adds batelas, and gives their burden as “from fifty to seventy tons”. [n36; Gaz XIII-I pp. 358–359]

The builders were “Marathas, Christians, Gujarat carpenters, Cutch Musalmans, Kolis, and Pachkalshis”. The timber was “teak and ain from Bombay, Bassein, and Jawhar”. [n36; Gaz XIII-I pp. 342–343] For the Bassein boats specifically, the builders were “Marathas and Native Christians”. [n36; Gaz XIII-I pp. 358–359] The Panchkalshi community’s own account, recorded by Enthoven, says that “under the Portuguese many of them became skilled mechanics and carpenters (house and boat builders)”. [n35; Enthoven III p. 163] Here, in one list, is the shipbuilding tradition of Portuguese Bassein surviving into the age of steam: carried on by the Christian and Panchkalshi descendants of the Portuguese-era carpenters, alongside Cutchi Muslim and Gujarati shipwrights from the north, using the same teak.

The owners were a crowd of communities: “Gujarat and Marwar Vanis, Brahmans, Agris, Bhatias, Lingayats, Bhandaris, Machhis, Kolis, Khojas”. A boat lasted twenty to thirty years. The sailors were paid by a share of the earnings, and the captain, the tandel, took a double share. They steered “almost entirely by land-marks, and … by the stars”. [n36; Gaz XIII-I pp. 342–343] No compass is mentioned. The crews of the Bassein boats came from Bassein and the villages around it. [n21; Gaz XIII-I pp. 358–359]

The sailing year was the monsoon’s year, as it had been in the Periplus. Local craft were hauled ashore from early June until Coconut Day, the full moon of Shravan, when the sea was thought safe again. [n36; Gaz XIII-I p. 342] The festival the fishing villages still keep as Narali Purnima, offering a coconut to the sea, marked the opening of the sailing season for the coasting trade as well as for fishing. Whether it was celebrated at Arnala or Pachu Bandar in this form in 1882 is not recorded in the notes; the Gazetteer’s reference is to the whole coast. [n36; Gaz XIII-I p. 342] [n13; gap]

Some of these boats were old designs. Gaspar Correa had described the Konkan’s craft in the sixteenth century: planks sewn with coir or nailed, one large mast, a long-sprit lateen sail, steered close to the wind. The Gazetteer’s lateen-rigged machvas and padavs of 1882 belong to the same family. [n36; Gaz XIII-II pp. 468–469; inference] Some change was coming. “Along the coast as far as Bassein,” the Gazetteer noted, “fishermen have taken to use jolly-boats of from 14 to 25 feet long”. [n21; Gaz XIII-I p. 351 approx.]

How many boats were there? For the whole Thana district the Gazetteer gives a count. In 1880–81, 19,959 vessels of 199,361 tons entered its ports with cargoes, and 34,717 vessels of 375,915 tons cleared with cargoes, against 13,487 vessels of 129,294 tons entering and 25,113 of 302,279 tons clearing in 1871–72. About 1,100 vessels were locally owned: 767 registered at Thana ports, and 325 registered at Bombay but working the Thana coast trade. [n36; Gaz XIII-I p. 343] The counts rose sharply in a decade, by about half for entries. They are for all 33 ports of the district together. No count of vessels for Bassein, Agashi or Navghar alone survives in the sources. [n36; gap]

Three small ports: £108,868

By the 1880s the sea trade of the old island of Sopara and Bassein ran through three small harbours: Agashi in the north, Bassein in the south, and Navghar, which the Gazetteer calls “a small port about six miles east of Bassein”, Sassu Navghar on the creek. [n36; Gaz XIII-I pp. 358–359] [n11; Gaz XIV p. 305] They formed the Bassein customs division, one of seven divisions covering the district’s 33 ports. [n21; Gaz XIII-I p. 354]

In 1881–82 their trade was estimated at £108,868: imports of £27,127 and exports of £81,741. [n36; Gaz XIII-I pp. 358–359] [n21; Gaz XIII-I p. 358] For the year before, 1880–81, the figures for the Bassein customs division read £28,267 of imports and £74,072 of exports, a total of £102,329; the printed parts of that line do not add up, a fault of the scan or the print. [n21; Gaz XIII-I p. 355 approx., OCR uncertain] Against the district, it was a small share. The sea trade of all Thana averaged £1,779,315 over the eight years to 1881, and stood at £2,043,241 in 1880–81 and £2,002,697 in 1881–82. [n36; Gaz XIII-I p. 358] The three Bassein ports carried about 5.4 per cent of it in 1881–82. The Trombay division, which included Thana and Kalyan, did £659,927 of trade that year and the Ghodbandar division £210,777. [n36; Gaz XIII-I pp. 358–359; derived] The estuary that had held Sopara and Kalyan as its twin ports two thousand years earlier still did most of the district’s business, but at its inner end.

The goods tell the story of a garden coast. The Bassein ports sent husked rice, molasses, coconuts and plantains to Bombay and Gujarat, and firewood and tiles to Bombay. They took in wheat, pulse and ghee from Bombay and Surat, timber from Bombay, piece-goods, oil and oil-cake, and lime. [n36; Gaz XIII-I pp. 358–359] The exports were bulky, cheap and often perishable; the imports were the staples and building materials of a farming population. The coast that had once sent teak and basalt to Goa now bought its timber from Bombay.

The traders of the three ports, in 1882, were “Musalmans, Marathas, local Vanis, and a few Brahmans”. About half were natives of Bassein; the rest came from Kalyan, Bhiwndi, Bombay and Gujarat and stayed “from December to June”, the fair season between the end of one monsoon and the start of the next. [n36; Gaz XIII-I pp. 358–359] Visiting vessels came from Gujarat, Cutch, Kathiawar, Diu, Daman and the south Konkan, “anchoring at twenty-five to 200 feet from the landing at Bassein, and 100 to 200 feet at Agashi”. [n36; Gaz XIII-I pp. 358–359] That December-to-June pattern is the oldest rhythm in this whole thread. The Periplus had its Egyptian ships arrive after the monsoon; the Gazetteer imagined a four-month fair at Chaul from December to March; and now, in the records of a British customs clerk, Gujarati and Cutchi traders arrived after the rains and left before them. [n36; Schoff §49; Gaz XIII-II p. 446; inference]

Bassein town. For the five years to 1878–79 the port of Bassein averaged exports of £51,414 and imports of £22,520 a year. The yearly figures swung wildly. Exports were £78,004 in 1874–75, £97,480 in 1875–76, then only £20,710 in 1876–77, £33,868 in 1877–78 and £27,007 in 1878–79; imports ran from £17,205 to £33,547, though the text and the footnote disagree on one of those figures. [n21; Gaz XIV p. 36 and footnote] The collapse of 1876–77 coincides with the great famine of 1876–77 across the Deccan and Thana, which the Gazetteer mentions without giving its effect on Bassein. [n28; Gaz XIII-I; inference] The town had “a good landing place and custom house” in the 1880s. [n32; Gaz XIV p. 36] A modern secondary page claims that the Bhatela pond at Nallasopara holds the remains of a Portuguese jetty and customs house; the Gazetteer, which describes Sopara in detail, says nothing of either, and the claim is unverified. [n31; Wikipedia: Nallasopara]

Agashi. The smaller port in the north had a sharper specialty. Over the five years to 1879 it averaged exports of £31,847 and imports of £8,565, exports ranging from £24,643 in 1875 to £48,181 in 1879. “It drives a great trade with Bombay in plantains and betel leaves, its dried plantains being the best in the district.” [n21; Gaz XIV p. 2] [n36; Gaz XIV p. 2] The Bassein dried plantains were made from the rajeli variety, and only in Agashi, Vagholi, Vatar, Bolinj, Koprad, Nala, Umrale, Rajodi and Murdes, the Kupari and Samvedi villages of the old Sopara garden. About 85 families of Samvedi Brahmans, 15 of Pachkalshis and about 75 of Native Christians dried ripe plantains at Agashi, Vatar and Koprad, in a season from October to January. At Agashi the yearly yield was about 160 tons, worth about £2,700. [n21; Gaz XIII-I pp. 292, 395] Local dealers sent the dried fruit to Thana, Bombay, Surat, Poona and Sholapur. [n05; Gaz XIII-I, crafts, search snippet] Agashi’s molasses was prized in Gujarat for gudaku, a paste of tobacco and molasses for smoking. [n36; Gaz XIII-I p. 338] About 750,000 sugarcanes a year went out of Bassein, chiefly to Bombay, Surat and Broach. [n21; Gaz XIII-I pp. 291, 338] In 1881 Agashi had 6,823 people: 5,168 Hindus, 1,500 Christians and 155 Muslims. [n36; Gaz XIV p. 2]

The plantain trade’s own figures are the Gazetteer’s best measure of what the garden belt produced. Nine varieties were grown: basrai, mutheli, tambdi, rajeli, lokhandi, sonkeli, bankeli, karanjeli and narsingi. A footnote from the civil servant K. J. Ebden gives the bunches usually sold in a year: about 220,000 of green basrai, 50,000 of red tambdi, 12,000 of mutheli, and between 200 and 5,000 of the others, some 300,000 bunches in all. [n21; Gaz XIII-I pp. 291–292] The plants were manured with oil-cake and then with fish, which was cheaper and gave the best return, though it bred worms. [n21; Gaz XIII-I p. 292] The sea fed the gardens as well as the gardeners.

The “plantain and salt traders” of nineteenth-century Bassein, then, cannot be named as individuals. The sources give categories only: Christian, Samvedi and Pachkalshi growers and dryers; Agri, Koli and Dubla salt and boat workers; Vani, Khoja and Muslim shippers; Gujarat traders visiting for the dry season. No named plantain-trade family, salt merchant or shipowner of Bassein in this century appears in the records this book could use. [n36; gap]

The railway, 1864

The Bombay, Baroda and Central India Railway was begun in May 1858 and opened for traffic on 28 November 1864. It ran 95 miles from Bombay to the Gujarat border, with stations at Bhayandar, Bassein Road, Virar and Saphala among others, and crossed the Bassein creek on two long girder bridges of 69 and 25 spans, divided by Panju island. [n21; Gaz XIII-I pp. 328–329] [n05; Wikipedia: Vasai Creek Railway Bridge] Bassein Road (now Vasai Road, station code BSR) was on the first passenger run between Grant Road and Ahmedabad. From 1 November 1865, by one account, it was the northern end of a local service from Grant Road, one train each way; another account dates the first regular suburban service, Back Bay to Virar, to 12 April 1867. [n05; Wikipedia: Vasai Road railway station] [n28; Wikipedia: Western line] The Bassein creek, which had carried ships for two thousand years, was now crossed by iron.

The Gazetteer’s verdict on what the railway did to the sea trade is one sentence: “Along the coast, the opening of the Baroda railway has destroyed the old cart traffic, and has reduced the sea-trade with Gujarat and Bombay.” [n36; Gaz XIII-I pp. 341–342] Goods that had gone by cart to the creek landings and then by machva to Bombay or Surat now went straight onto the train.

The station figures show the shift, though in an unexpected way. At Bassein Road the passengers rose from 86,478 in 1873 to 140,837 in 1880, but goods fell from 5,292 tons to 3,278. [n21; Gaz XIII-I p. 340; Gaz XIV p. 36] The goods that grew were at Bhayandar, just south of the creek, where traffic rose from 2,627 tons in 1873 to 19,770 tons in 1880, “mostly salt” from the Rai Murdha pans about three miles west. [n21; Gaz XIV p. 45; Gaz XIII-I p. 340] Salt, the bulkiest and cheapest product of the coast, was the first cargo the railway took from the boats.

Sopara’s trade went by rail from Virar. The Gazetteer lists “molasses, rice and betel-leaves” sent north to Ankleshvar, Nadiad, Viramgam and Patan in Gujarat, and “large quantities of plantains and betel-leaves” sent south to Bombay. [n21; Gaz XIV p. 316] Virar station was three and a half miles from Sopara, and the Gazetteer’s author added a hope: “A station at Sopara or a made road to the town … would be a boon.” [n21; Gaz XIV p. 316] The station did come, under the name that the old port would carry into the twentieth century. An early timetable of the Back Bay–Virar service is said to have included a halt at “Neela or (Nalasopara)”; that clue comes from a newspaper retelling of a 1923 railway magazine, and the year the halt opened is not known. [n31; Free Press Journal, 12 Apr 2022] Sopara had been named first by the sea; now it was named by the railway.

The train suited the garden belt. Perishables reached the Bombay markets the same day, and the plantains, betel leaves and vegetables of Agashi, Nirmal, Vatar and Sopara could now be sold fresh. [n05; inference] The Marwari moneylenders, too, “since the railway has been opened … have come in great numbers”. [n35; Gaz XIII-I p. 64] The railway did not kill the sea trade at once; the three ports still did more than £100,000 of business in 1881–82, mostly in goods too bulky or cheap for the railway’s rates. [n36; inference] But it took the profitable, fast and perishable trade, and it brought a new kind of trader overland.

Silt: how the ports ended

The railway was the last blow, not the first. The deeper cause of the end of this coast’s ports was the slow work of the rivers, and for once the record lets us watch it across centuries.

The first measure is Idrisi’s, in 1153: Sopara stood “one-and-a-half mile from the sea”. [n36; E&D I p. 85] Whatever the shoreline had been in the Periplus’s day, by the twelfth century the town was already some way inland, reached by its creek.

The second is Portuguese. In 1728 a Portuguese writer wanted the creek between Sopara and Bolinj dug out “as deep as the height of a man, otherwise it would soon be filled to a level with the fields, because of the great amount of silt that was brought by the river … from its two mouths”. At that time the channel was “fordable at low water” for about 4,000 feet. [n36; Gaz XIV p. 322] This is a dated eyewitness account of silting, made more than a century before any railway, and it names the cause: the river silt coming in through the creek’s two mouths, one on the Vaitarna and one on the Bassein river.

The third is the Gazetteer of 1882, which describes the same backwater as it then was. The garden strip between the Bassein creek and the Vaitarna, about three miles broad, “was known as the island of Bassein” under the Portuguese and “before Portuguese times as the island of Sopara”. It is cut off from the mainland by a backwater that “leaves the Bassein creek a little above the railway bridge and winds north to the Vaitarna”, locally called the Manikpur creek in the south, the Sopara creek in the middle and the Bolinj creek in the north. [n39; Gaz XIV pp. 314–315] That creek “was once a considerable arm of the sea overflowing a large breadth of land … But, with the mud-laden Vaitarna on the north and the Bassein creek or Ulhas river on the south, silting has been rapid, especially since the long railway embankment has dammed the flow of water from the south. Small boats of ten or twelve tons (30–40 khandis) still come from the south as far as Gas … and from the north as far as Bolinj … At Sopara the creek is dry.” [n36; Gaz XIV pp. 314–315]

Three readings, then: a mile and a half from the sea in 1153; fordable at low tide and filling fast in 1728; dry at Sopara in 1882, with ten-ton boats creeping up from either end as far as Gass and Bolinj. Together they make a seven-century record of a channel closing. [n36; inference] The railway embankment, built across the southern mouth of the backwater in the 1860s, made it worse by cutting off the tidal flush from the Bassein side. [n36; Gaz XIV pp. 314–315]

Modern scholarship adds its own reasons. Shah and his colleagues, drawing on the geographer Arunachalam, describe siltation over about two thousand years, sped up by deforestation in the hinterland and by a railway line on a high embankment in the later nineteenth century, and a sea level about half a metre higher around the second or third century BCE. [n36; Shah et al. 2020, citing Arunachalam 2002] Brancaccio confirms that the old lagoon “has been filled in by river deposits”, leaving mudflats now being built over. [n36; Brancaccio 2023 p. 11] No core sample or radiocarbon series measuring the silting rate of the Sopara creek itself was found in the sources; the “two thousand years” is a general statement. [n36; gap] The date at which large ships last reached Sopara is not known. [n36; gap]

The harbour at Agashi had its own obstacle. “Except one channel the mouth of the bay on which the town stands is closed by a reef, and the bay is full of shoals and navigable only by boats and small vessels,” Horsburgh wrote, as quoted in the Gazetteer. [n36; Gaz XIV p. 1] A bay that had sheltered 300 boats in 1530 and built ships for Europe in 1630 could, by the nineteenth century, take only small craft.

Silt was half the story. The other half was political and commercial, and the Gazetteer’s own chapters set out the stages. Thana overtook Sopara by 1300. The Bahmani move of 1347 drew trade south to Chaul and Dabhol. The Gujarat sultans gave Bassein to Portugal in 1534, and the Portuguese court at Bassein, in Indraji’s words, “took from Sopára its former importance”. The European trade moved to Goa, Diu and then Surat. Bassein fell to the Marathas in 1739. Bombay rose after 1661 and by 1800 took “almost the whole of the foreign trade”. The Baroda railway arrived in 1864. The Bassein ports were left as local feeders. [n36; Gaz XIII-II pp. 444, 465, 485–486, 512–513; Gaz XIII-I pp. 341–342; Indraji 1882 p. 279] Da Cunha, writing in 1876, put the end in five words. Sopara, he said, was “once a famous bandar, now almost abandoned”. [n32; DC 156–157] Of the Maratha-era divisions he listed, only Khani Vadem was still described as having “a small bandar”. [n32; DC 156–157]

There is one more irony in the record. In 1840 the Court of Directors sanctioned an embankment across the creek to join Bassein island to the mainland, and by 1876 an iron railway bridge connected Bassein with Bombay. [n32; DC 157] The water that had made the island of Sopara an island, and Sopara a port, was being built over from both sides.

The silent century (1883 to the 1980s)

After the Gazetteer of 1882, the sea trade of this coast vanishes from the record.

The research for this book found no port statistics at all for Agashi, Bassein or Navghar after 1882. The revised Thana District Gazetteer of 1982 could not be located; the District Census Handbooks for Thana of 1961, 1971 and 1981 were not found; and the census volumes of 1901 to 1951 that were read give population, not trade. [n36; gap] [n21; gap] Whether the three ports kept their £100,000 of business into the twentieth century, when the last machva sailed from Bassein for Gujarat, and when the custom house at the landing closed, the record used here does not say. This is a real silence, not a lack of effort, and it should be filled from the Bombay Presidency’s annual sea-trade reports and the 1982 Gazetteer. [n36; gap]

What the record does show is the railway becoming the coast’s road, and Bombay its market. The 1921 census report excluded Bassein, with North Salsette and Kalyan, from its analysis of rural tracts because these talukas “have come under the influence of suburbanisation”. [n21; Census 1921 Vol VIII Pt I] Electric trains reached Virar in 1936, when the lines from Borivali to Virar were electrified, by the one dated source the notes found. [n28; Wikipedia: Western line] The creek bridge was rebuilt in the 1920s. [n05; Wikipedia: Vasai Creek Railway Bridge]

The garden produce that had once gone by sea from Agashi now went by train. Until the early 1980s, the villages of the Vasai-Virar sub-region supplied vegetables to Mumbai’s suburbs; farmers carried their produce on a kawad, a shoulder pole with a basket at each end, onto the four o’clock train to Victoria Terminus, and sold at Bora Bazaar and near the Dadar Kabutarkhana, where customers called them “Vasaiwale mama” and “Vasaiwale maushi”. [n17; India Food Network 2015; Vikalp Sangam 2019] One account says that the betel leaf of the area was exported to Pakistan, presumably after 1947; the claim comes from a single secondary source and has no date or figure. [n17; Vikalp Sangam 2019] The kawadis faded as families moved to office jobs, contractors tied farmers to supermarket chains, and produce from Nashik and Pune took over the Bombay markets. [n17; India Food Network 2015] The trade route that had begun with machvas sailing from Agashi to Bombay ended with farmers on the local train, and then ended altogether.

A local web history says that a regular steamer service along the west coast began in 1869. It gives no source, and whether the steamers called at Bassein is not stated. [n05; Vasaiker] The notes record no Vasai incident in the Salt Satyagraha of 1930, though the salt pans of Bassein lay within reach of the movement’s coastal campaign. [n28; gap] [n05; gap]

The Kolis and the fishing fleets

Through all of this, the one sea trade that never stopped was fishing.

Its people are among the oldest on the coast. Enthoven’s survey of 1922 called “Koli” “a term of vague meaning covering a number of tribes”, probably used “for centuries to describe the greater part of the original inhabitants of the Presidency”; Dr John Wilson counted the Son Koli “as the original inhabitant of Bombay Island”. [n35; Enthoven II pp. 243–246] The Son Kolis, found “along the North Konkan coast from Bassein to Ratnagiri”, had a tradition that most of them descended from hill people who had come down to the coast some four hundred years earlier; Enthoven’s contributor doubted it, because of “the difficulty of understanding the sudden conversion of hillmen into fishers”. [n35; Enthoven II p. 259] Their women sold all the fish and “wield great power, manage household affairs”. [n35; Enthoven II p. 260] Their home tongue was “a patois of Marathi gradually obsolescent under the grinding of the Local Board school and the newspaper”. [n35; Enthoven II pp. 259–260] They said they had been taught “the gods properly” by Kalu Bhagat, a Koli of Varsoli near Alibag, “in the times of the Peshwas some two centuries ago”, and directed to Khandoba of Jejuri; before that, they said, was “ignorance”. [n35; Enthoven II p. 260] The same Jejuri temple, by a modern report, holds one of the bells taken from the churches of Bassein in 1739. [n04; Wikipedia: Battle of Vasai, citing Hindustan Times 2018]

Other fishing peoples had come down the coast from Gujarat. The Gazetteer lists Machhis, Mitne Machhis, Mangelas, Kharvis and Kharpatils among the Gujarat arrivals who “dress in Gujarat fashion and speak Gujarati at home”, and notes that Mangela speech has “many Gujarati words”. [n35; Gaz XIII-I p. 64] In 1881 the Bassein sub-division had 2,375 Mangelas, 77 Machhis and 16 Bhois, river fishers. [n21; Gaz XIII-II p. 680] The Vaitis lived in north Bassein and south Mahim. [n08; Gaz XIII-I] And there were Christian fishermen: “The Uraps and other Christian Kolis in Bassein … are professional fishermen, but the mass of the sea-fishing population are Son Kolis.” [n21; Gaz XIII-I p. 55] The Koli Christian bridegroom, by a modern account, traditionally wears an old Portuguese admiral’s uniform, a strange survival of the age when the Portuguese fleet ruled these waters. [n04; Wikipedia: Bombay East Indians]

The Kolis had always paid for the sea. In the first half-year of Portuguese rule, 1535, the revenue of the Bassein district included a tax on “Koli fishermen” as a profession. [n36; Da Cunha pp. 158–159] Under the British, until Act XIX of 1844, Kolis, Mangelas and Vaitis paid a poll tax called day-dene, Rs 1¼ a head, “for the privilege of fishing”. [n21; Gaz XIII-I p. 54 n.] The Collector’s letters of 1850–51 counted 129 salt-water fisheries in the district, 22 of them in Bassein, and 101 fresh-water fisheries, 18 of them in Bassein; the fresh-water fisheries of Kalyan and Bassein together paid about £13. [n21; Gaz XIII-I p. 54 n.] “Good oysters are found along the Bassein, Mahim and Dahanu coasts,” the Gazetteer adds. [n21; Gaz XIII-I p. 56] No output is recorded for the pearl fishery of the Sopara creeks that Idrisi mentioned in 1153, or for the mangrove timber of the creeks. [n36; gap]

The sea’s dangers came ashore with the fishermen. In 1881 cholera “began among the fishermen of Kelva Mahim in April and prevailed in Bassein from May to July”. [n21; Gaz XIII-II pp. 665–667]

The Gazetteer gives no count of fishing boats or catches for Bassein. [n21; gap] For most of the twentieth century there is no record in the notes at all. The modern story begins with cooperatives. Up the coast at Satpati, in Palghar, the fishermen’s co-operative built the first co-operative ice factory, of seven tons, in 1950–51, which let catches be kept and sent to market. Vasai taluka has eleven primary fishermen’s co-operatives. [n36; ICSF, search summary] A study cited by search summary counted 5,007 active fishermen in Vasai taluka in a 1997 census. [n36; secondary] Fishing stops in the monsoon, from June to August, under the modern ban, as the boats were once hauled ashore from June to Coconut Day. [n36; secondary]

The traditional gear of this coast is the dol, a bag net. Arnala is “one of the major dol net landing centres in Thane district”; the main catch of the dol nets is Bombay duck, the bombil. Pomfret was taken in dol nets until the mid-1960s, and after that with nylon gill nets. [n36; ICSF, search summary] The bombil catch in the Satpati–Dahanu zone reached a record of 40,065 tons in 1979; for the whole state, the catch was 16,576 tons in 2018. At Satpati the boats fishing for bombil fell from thirty or thirty-five in the 1980s to twelve by the middle of 2019. [n36; ICSF; PARI, search summaries]

The fishers of Vasai tell the same story of decline. “A few decades ago, Vasai’s fisherfolk used to catch a variety of fish with their traditional dol nets,” one report says; now “Vasai has become largely a one-fish town”, depending largely on pomfret, and the pomfret too is falling. [n08; ICSF, search summary] [n17; Citizen Matters; Goya] The blame falls on overfishing by trawlers and purse-seiners, on creek pollution choking the breeding grounds, and on the number of boats: the dol-net boats of Vasai are said to have doubled to about a hundred in a decade. Educated young people are leaving the trade. [n17; Citizen Matters] [n08; ICSF; Outlook] Arnala still holds daily fish auctions and keeps traditional boats, by a travel guide’s account, and an Arnala Fishermen’s Association helps with rescues at sea; one recent report describes a fishing boat towed back after its engine failed off the Arnala coast. [n08; Traveldojo] [n11; Free Press Journal] Across the creek, about 40,000 Koli women work as fish vendors in Mumbai and handle about 70 per cent of the post-harvest work; in August 2024, 1,210 Koli fishers from seven Koliwadas went to the Bombay High Court over an alleged 60 per cent drop in their catch caused by the construction of the Mumbai Trans Harbour Link. [n17; Earth Island Journal] That is Mumbai, not Vasai, but it is the same sea and the same people.

The modern sources do not give the registered boat count or the landing centres of Vasai taluka, a figure that the Central Marine Fisheries Research Institute’s fisheries censuses would hold. [n17; gap] [n36; gap] Nor do they describe the fishing at Pachu Bandar, Naigaon or the fort jetty of Killa Bandar, the bombil-drying yards, or the Koli festivals of the Vasai villages in particular. [n32; gap] [n13; gap]

Salt

The second gift the sea gave every year was salt. It was made by letting tide water into shallow pans on the creek flats and leaving the sun to dry it, and the coast’s salt makers worked the same mudflats that silted the old ports.

The Portuguese salt revenue of Bassein was not found in the sources, nor the Maratha. [n36; gap] The first figures are British. In 1880–81, “the seventeen Bassein works are arranged in six groups with 11,374 pans, an area of 1,439 acres, and thirty owners. The Bassein works are widely scattered on the north bank of the Bassein creek; a few are in the interior on the banks of rivers.” [n21; Gaz XIII-I p. 364] That made Bassein third in the district after Uran (3,241 acres) and Ghodbandar (1,616 acres), with about 18 per cent of the district’s pan area. [n21; Gaz XIII-I p. 364; derived] The salt tax was Rs 2 for each Bengal maund of 82½ pounds, and the ground rent in Bassein Rs 1½ an acre. [n21; Gaz XIII-I p. 365 n.] The figures in the district salt table for Bassein’s own output and revenue are too garbled in the scan to read. [n21; gap]

The workers were Agris in Bassein, Ghodbandar, Panvel and Uran; Kharvas in Bassein and the southern divisions; and Dublas from Gujarat, who “work only on the salt-works near Bassein Road station”. Bassein workmen were paid by the unit of 80 Bengal maunds, at Rs 5 to Rs 8. [n21; Gaz XIII-I pp. 365–366] “Since 1876, when the salt trade was thrown open to private enterprise”, the Gazetteer says, the quality of the salt had improved, with Gujarat Dubla workers at Bassein. Bassein and Ghodbandar salt together “command about three-quarters of the Uran trade”, and Bassein salt competed in local markets and along the railways. [n21; Gaz XIII-I p. 370] Salt was the chief cargo of the small boats of Bombay harbour, and the rise of Bhayandar’s goods traffic from 2,627 to 19,770 tons between 1873 and 1880 was mostly salt. [n36; Gaz XIII-I pp. 342–343] [n21; Gaz XIV p. 45]

The salt pans outlasted the ports. Today about 2,000 acres of salt pans are cited for Vasai, as against about 5,379 acres in Mumbai, though sources differ. [n17; Asian Age; Business Standard 2007] Their future became the largest land-use fight of the twenty-first century on this coast when the draft Regional Plan of the Mumbai Metropolitan Region Development Authority for 2016–36 proposed to reclassify 1,560 acres of former salt pans, until then a no-development zone, as a “growth centre” with housing, sports, education and commerce. Residents pointed out that the land floods every monsoon. The Quint reported at least 40,000 objections from Vasai-Virar and over 62,000 across the region; the activist Samir Vartak put the local figure at about 63,000. [n17; The Quint via ICSF] The final status of the growth centre in the sanctioned plan was not confirmed in the sources. [n17; gap] In 2025 the Western Railway blamed waterlogging on the Vasai line partly on buildings put up on salt-pan land and on mangroves and waterholes in Nallasopara and Virar. [n17; PropNewsTime] The pans that had once held the sea back in measured squares were being filled, and the sea’s water now stood on the tracks.

The other gifts of the sea

Trade, fish and salt were the sea’s income. It gave this coast other things that never appear in a customs ledger.

Defence. The first reason the Gazetteer gave for Sopara’s rise was that “its distance from the coast and its broad backwater guarded it from pirate raids or the forays of the wild hill and forest tribes”. [n36; Gaz XIV p. 316] The creeks that made the island of Sopara an island were its moat. The Portuguese built on the same principle with stone and guns: Bassein’s wall faced the river and the sea, defended in the 1590s by twenty-one gunboats; Arnala’s island fort and the fort at Agashi watched the Vaitarna mouth; the “Ilha de vaccas” commanded the southern entrance of the river. [n36; Gaz XIV p. 30; Da Cunha p. 159] In 1882 the Bassein sub-division still counted three sea forts, Arnala, Bassein and Kharlio, and two inland forts. [n39; Gaz XIV, forts list] The sea that protected the coast also exposed it. From the Periplus’s “pirates” south of Chaul, through Marco Polo’s corsairs of Thana and the Maskat raiders of the seventeenth century, to the Maratha fleet that took Arnala in 1737, every serious attack on this coast came by water or depended on command of it. [n36; Schoff §53; Gaz XIV pp. 354–355; Gaz XIII-II p. 484; inference]

Climate and season. The sea also made the weather. The south-west monsoon set the rhythm of every generation in this chapter: Egyptian ships sailing in July, Pliny’s ships reaching the Konkan near the end of September and leaving in December or January, the Portuguese monção, the machvas hauled up in June and launched on Coconut Day, the Gujarat traders arriving in December and leaving in June, and the fishing ban from June to August. [n36; Schoff §49; Gaz XIII-II p. 410 n. 5; Gaz XIII-I p. 342; Gaz XIII-I pp. 358–359] The same monsoon watered the garden belt. Bassein’s rainfall averaged 71.87 inches in the ten years to 1881; a modern figure gives about 1,586 millimetres a year. [n21; Gaz XIII-II p. 678] [n17; 101Reporters 2023] The sea’s weather could also destroy: the cyclone of 15 May 1618; the famine years when the rain failed, as in 1824–25, 1837 and 1838–39; the “salt rice” lands near the creeks whose crop failed in 1848 and was partly ruined by worms in 1854. [n04; Wikipedia: Vasai Fort] [n21; Gaz XIII-I p. 303] In December 2022 the humid air of Cyclone Mandous brought fungus that roughly halved the yield of the sonchafa flower growers of Arnala and Agashi. [n17; 101Reporters 2023] In July 2026 the Palghar Collector reported an average of 203 millimetres of rain in twenty-four hours, and by 13 July nine rain-related deaths had been reported in Vasai-Virar. [n29; Free Press Journal, 7 Jul 2026; The Quint, 13 Jul 2026]

The shore itself is moving. Around 2018 residents said that mangroves had been cleared from more than a hundred acres for building, and that “the sea moves further onto land every day”. [n17; The Quint via ICSF] Activists argue that the filling of wetlands, salt pans and mangroves on these floodplains has made the floods worse; the argument is made by campaigners and by the railway, not by a published study the notes found. [n17; Asian Age via CAT 2018; inference] Projections of sea-level rise for this coast were not searched. [n17; gap] The coast that rose from the Holocene sea, and whose creeks silted until its ports died, now faces a sea that may come back.

Migration. Above all, the sea brought people. Almost every community in this book’s other chapters arrived, or is said to have arrived, by water. The Parsis landed at Sanjan, north of the Vaitarna, according to their tradition after a long stay at Diu; the Kisseh-i-Sanjan has their ruler require them to “give up the language of their mother-land of Persia” and adopt that of the new country. [n35; Enthoven III p. 193] Arab and Persian merchants and refugees settled along the coast from the eighth century, 10,000 of them at Chaul by Masudi’s count, and became the Konkani Muslims and the “Naitas” of Sopara and Bassein. [n36; E&D I p. 24] [n35; Gaz XIII-I pp. 64, 216] Nestorian Christians were at Sopara when Jordanus came in 1321. [n15; Gaz XIV pp. 321–322] The Bene Israel tradition of a shipwreck centres on Navgaon in Kolaba, not on Vasai, and only five Jews lived in Bassein town in 1881. [n35; Enthoven I pp. 69–71] [n21; Gaz XIV p. 28] The Portuguese came by sea in 1529 and left by sea in 1739. Enslaved Africans were brought by sea to Bassein, where they were 1,800 of its people in 1634, and left traces the Gazetteer recorded among Christians, Konkani Muslims, Katkaris and Thakurs. [n04; Wikipedia: Vasai Fort] [n35; Gaz XIII-I p. 64] The Lad Vanis and Gujarat Brahmans came by sea from Cambay in the 1760s, and the Gujarat fishing castes, Mangelas and Machhis, down the coast. [n35; Gaz XIII-I pp. 63–64]

After 1864 the road changed. The Marwari Vanis came “through Gujarat and Bombay, and since the railway has been opened … in great numbers”. [n35; Gaz XIII-I p. 64] In the twentieth century the migrants came by train: commuters from Bombay, whose arrival made the population of Vasai, Virar, Sopara and Sandor grow by 290 per cent in the decade to 1991, and workers from Uttar Pradesh and Bihar, whose languages made Hindi the first language of about a quarter of Vasai taluka by 2011. [n17; TIFR] [n35; Census 2011 via Wikipedia] For two thousand years newcomers had sailed into the creeks of Sopara and Bassein. Since the nineteenth century they have arrived on the platform at Vasai Road.

A ferry, and a port at Vadhvan (2024–2026)

In the last years of this story the sea came back into the plans of the state, in two very different forms.

The first was small. On 20 February 2024 a roll-on, roll-off ferry began a three-month trial across the Vasai creek between Bhayandar and Vasai, a crossing of about 3.5 kilometres by water, with a vessel carrying a hundred passengers and thirty-three vehicles. On 25 February it was halted after hitting the concrete jetty at Vasai. What became of the service after the trial is not confirmed in the sources. [n36; ThePrint; Lokmat Times] The creek that had carried small craft between Sopara and Kalyan in the first century, and the ferry boatmen of the Portuguese captain at 200 réis a month, was again carrying a ferry, briefly. [n36; inference] [n32; DC 222–223]

The second was enormous. On 19 June 2024 the Union Cabinet approved a new deep-water port at Vadhvan (Vadhavan), on the Dahanu coast in the same Palghar district, some way north of Virar, at a cost of ₹76,220 crore including land. As described in the government’s release, it was to handle about 298 million tonnes of cargo a year, including 23.2 million TEU of containers, with nine container terminals, four multipurpose berths, four liquid-cargo berths, a Ro-Ro berth and a Coast Guard berth, built on 1,448 hectares of land reclaimed from the sea. It would be built by Vadhavan Port Project Ltd, owned 74 per cent by the Jawaharlal Nehru Port Authority and 26 per cent by the Maharashtra Maritime Board. [n36; PIB release 2026695, as returned in search results] The Prime Minister laid the foundation stone at Palghar on 30 August 2024. [n36; Free Press Journal; Mumbai Live] [n29; All India Radio]

The fishing villages opposed it. At one gathering more than 8,000 villagers protested; one newspaper headline spoke of 25,000 fishermen; and the Vadhavan Bandar Virodhi Sangharsh Samiti brought fishermen, adivasis and farmers, about 10,000 of them, to a rally at Azad Maidan in Mumbai. [n29; Free Press Journal] [n36; The Quint; The Logical Indian] Fishers protested at the groundbreaking itself. [n17; Free Press Journal] A fishing leader said the port would reclaim about 5,000 acres of sea and close off about 30,000 acres more as a restricted area; research cited in the press said it would directly hit sixteen fishing villages in Palghar. [n17; Free Press Journal] The Dahanu Taluka Environment Protection Authority was reported to have rejected the proposal as “wholly impermissible” and “illegal”, in a ruling the sources do not date, and activists said no legal procedure had been followed. [n29; Free Press Journal; Behanbox] After the protests the site was moved four to six kilometres out to sea. [n36; The Quint; The Logical Indian] The women of the fishing villages, by one report, refused to give up the fight. [n29; Behanbox]

The work went ahead. The near-shore reclamation was awarded in December 2024; a letter of award for dredging and reclamation went to a joint venture of DEME and Jan De Nul with ITD Cementation in March 2025; the access expressway was put at ₹2,360 crore in April 2026; and a breakwater contract of ₹5,301 crore was reported in June 2026. The largest package, offshore dredging and the reclamation bund, valued at about ₹22,300 crore by some reports and ₹19,238 crore by others, had a bid deadline of 23 September 2026, with bids reported from DP World and a joint venture of Boskalis and NMDC. These details come from news aggregators that disagree in places. [n36; Maritime News; Construction World; GlobeNewswire] In December 2025 the Palghar Collector, Dr Indu Rani Jakhar, called for a full social and livelihood survey of the fishers affected by the port. [n17; Free Press Journal] In 2026 the Bombay High Court cleared the removal of mangroves for the project, and the port’s chairman, Gaurav Dayal, reviewed land acquisition, road links and fishermen’s concerns. [n06; Free Press Journal] The National Alliance of People’s Movements asked for the work to be halted pending an independent impact assessment. [n36; Maritime News] In November 2026 the port signed agreements worth ₹5,700 crore, with its first phase targeted for 2029. [n06; India Sea Trade News, Nov 2026]

Vadhvan was not the only plan. A separate ₹19,000-crore port proposal at Murbe, near Boisar, drew protests of its own, and a 2026 news-agency report quoted the state’s minister Ganesh Naik on plans for an “international-level port at Satpati”. [n36; Free Press Journal; ThePrint] The coast where Sopara’s creek had silted shut was again being mapped for harbours, this time not in the sheltered lagoons but in deep water far offshore, built by reclamation. [n36; inference]

Vadhvan lies outside Vasai taluka, and the sources do not say how its traffic or its rail and road links will touch the old island of Sopara. [n06; gap] The notes suggest that the port, with the Virar–Alibaug corridor and the bullet train, could turn Virar from a commuter town at the end of the line into a junction of freight corridors; that is a projection, not a fact. [n06; inference] What is certain is the argument it has started, and it is an old one on this coast: whether the sea belongs to the people who fish it, or to the trade that passes over it.

What the record does not say

This thread has many gaps, and they should be stated plainly at the end of it.

For the ancient port, no hull, harbour wall or cargo has been excavated at Sopara beyond the Roman amphora sherds and a possible docking wall of the 1993 trenches, and the Periplus names no Sopara goods. For about 300 to 800, the port leaves no remains at all. For the Silahara age there is one customs document, the copper plate of 1094. [n36; gaps]

For the Portuguese age there is no register of passes issued at Bassein, no count of ships per year, no customs total for Bassein town apart from the district revenue lines, and no separate salt revenue; Bocarro’s description of Bassein and Agashi of 1634–35 was not read. Whether Agashi’s ships really reached Europe rests on the Portuguese chroniclers as relayed by the Gazetteer. [n36; gaps]

For the Maratha century there is a single line on the governor-admiral of Bassein attacking English ships, and nothing on the ships themselves, their building, or the port’s trade, apart from the Company’s returns of 1802–06. [n36; gaps]

For the British age, the record is rich up to 1882 and then stops. No port statistics after 1882 were found, no named plantain trader, salt merchant or shipowner of Bassein, and no fishing-boat or catch figures for Vasai before the late twentieth century. [n36; n21; gaps]

For the present, the registered boats and landing centres of Vasai taluka, the fishing life of Killa Bandar and Pachu Bandar, and the official construction state of Vadhvan beyond news aggregators all remain to be checked. [n17; n32; n36; gaps]

These holes are as much a part of the coast’s history as the documents. A sea thread with every link in place would be a sign that someone had filled the gaps with guesses.

Coda: from the anchor to the breakwater

The stone anchor near the Port Mosque at Gass is undated, and may stay undated. It lies, now, a long way from any water a boat could float in. Two thousand years ago Greek-speaking captains wrote down the name of the market-town that stood near it. A thousand years ago two brothers who were ministers of a Silahara king sailed their own ships into its creek free of toll. Five hundred years ago a Portuguese fleet burnt 300 boats in the bay at Agashi, and a century later the same bay built ships fit for Europe. In 1882 ten-ton boats still crept up the silting creek as far as Gass. Today the boats are gone from that ground, and the nearest new harbour on this coast is being poured into the sea at Vadhvan.

Along the way, every generation was carried here by the same water: the merchants of Purna’s guild, the Arab settlers, the Parsis, the Portuguese, the Africans brought against their will, the Lad Vanis from Cambay, the Mangela fishers from Gujarat. Each brought a language and, sooner or later, gave most of it up for the speech of the coast, keeping a few words and a prayer. How each of those communities came, and how its speech was formed on this shore, is the next thread of this book.
