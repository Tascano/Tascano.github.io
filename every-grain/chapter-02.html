Period covered: 60 million years ago to c. 20,000 years ago (with a glance ahead)

In this chapter. Once the lava had cooled, the coast was shaped by water: weathering, the arrival of the monsoon and the long ice ages. This chapter carries the story to the eve of the first people.

On the low hills of the Konkan, the rock is red. Where the black basalt of the lava floods has been exposed for long enough to hot sun and heavy rain, it rots in place. The rain carries away the silica and the more soluble elements, and what is left behind is rich in iron and aluminium oxides: a hard, rust-coloured crust called laterite. It is the colour of much of the Konkan’s high ground, and it is the first sign, written on the landscape, that the age of fire had ended and the age of weathering had begun.

This chapter covers sixty million years, from the end of the Mumbai-coast eruptions to the approach of the coldest point of the last ice age, about twenty thousand years ago. It is the age in which India crashed into Asia, the Western Ghats retreated eastward and left a coastal plain in front of them, the world’s climate cooled from hothouse to icehouse, the monsoon began to blow, the ice ages came and went, and the sea fell and rose again and again by more than a hundred metres. Toward the end of it, people arrived: first archaic humans who made hand-axes, then modern humans, whose descendants still live on this coast. The chapter ends with the sea at its lowest, far out on what is now the floor of the Arabian Sea, and with a brief glance at what came after, which the next chapter tells in full.

The evidence in this chapter is of four kinds, and each statement is labelled. Vasai-specific evidence, found inside the taluka, barely exists for this age. Regional evidence comes from India, Maharashtra, Mumbai or the Konkan. Inference is the book’s own reasoning from that evidence. And general knowledge is the common ground of the earth sciences, such as the timing of the India–Asia collision or the cycles of the ice ages, which the research notes flag as background they did not source [n23; Gaps]. General knowledge is given because the local story needs it, and it is never presented as a finding about Vasai.

As in the last chapter, almost all the evidence is regional. There is no published fossil site and no published Stone Age site inside Vasai–Virar taluka [n14; Scope note]. The nearest finds lie across Vasai Creek on Salsette Island, at Kandivli, Worli, Marve, Manori and the hills of Sanjay Gandhi National Park, between about 15 and 35 kilometres south of Sopara on the same coastal plain [n01; Inferences]. Until the sea rose at the end of this chapter, there was no creek in the way. Those sites are fair stand-ins for Vasai’s own deep past, provided we remember that they are stand-ins.

The long weathering

Once the lavas stopped erupting, the tropical climate went to work on them. The evidence for how long ago this began comes from the laterites themselves.

Inside some laterite profiles in the Western Ghats there are potassium-rich manganese oxides, a mineral called cryptomelane, which can be dated by the argon method. Gunnell and colleagues, in 2003, used those dates to show that three lateritic surfaces formed one after another, between about 53 and 23 million years ago [n23; Gunnell et al. 2003; EGU2017 abstracts]. Palaeomagnetic dating of ferricretes, iron-cemented crusts, in the coastal lowland gave about 52 million years. The authors read this as a sign that the coastal lowland had been worn down very little since then, and that the Ghats escarpment had been broadly stable since at least 50 million years ago [n23; EGU/Copernicus abstracts]. A source that gives the escarpment a much older age, about 84 million years, conflicts with that view [n23; Kale blog summary].

A geomorphologist’s summary of the literature describes the Konkan coastal plain as an erosional surface that widened as the Western Ghats escarpment retreated eastward. On this account, the upland laterites formed between about 60 and 50 million years ago, soon after the volcanism ended, while the lowland Konkan laterites belong to a younger phase and are only a few metres thick [n01; Kale blog; Open University]. The laterites near Vasai are most likely of that lowland, younger kind. But that, the notes stress, is a regional statement: no laterite near Vasai has been dated [n23; Inferences and Gaps].

What does this mean for the land? For tens of millions of years after it was made, the basalt of the coast lay under a warm, wet sky, and slowly rusted and crumbled. The red crusts that later builders would cut into blocks, and that later farmers would find on the slopes above their rice fields, are the product of that long patience.

What laterite is

General knowledge in this subsection except where cited.

The word was coined on the other side of the peninsula. In 1807 the Scottish surveyor Francis Buchanan, travelling through Malabar, saw villagers cutting a soft, reddish, iron-rich earth into blocks, which hardened like brick once exposed to the air, and he named it from the Latin later, a brick. The name stuck, and laterite has been the building stone of the whole western coast ever since, from Kerala to the Konkan.

Laterite forms where three conditions meet: rock rich in iron and aluminium, a hot climate, and heavy rain alternating with dry seasons. Rainwater, made slightly acidic by soil and plants, soaks into the rock and dissolves the more mobile elements, silica, calcium, sodium, potassium and magnesium, and carries them away. The iron and aluminium stay behind, oxidised to red and yellow minerals. Over very long periods the weathered zone deepens, and a hard crust of iron oxides, a ferricrete, may form near the top where the water table rises and falls. Basalt, rich in iron, is ideal parent rock. The Deccan lavas, laid under a tropical sky, were waiting for exactly this process.

What makes the Konkan laterites valuable to science is that they can be dated, at least in places. The cryptomelane in the Ghats profiles crystallised inside the weathering zone as it formed, and its potassium decays to argon at a known rate; the ferricretes of the lowland locked in the direction of the Earth’s magnetic field when their iron minerals grew [n23; Gunnell et al. 2003; EGU2017 abstracts] [general knowledge for the methods]. Together they give the laterites of the Ghats and the coastal lowland ages in the range of about 53 to 23 million years, with about 52 million years for the lowland ferricretes [n23; Gunnell et al. 2003; EGU/Copernicus abstracts]. In other words, the red skin of the Konkan is not young. Much of it is older than the Himalaya.

That age carries a climatic message. If the lowland laterite surface was forming around 52 million years ago, then the Konkan, which then lay near the equator, already had a hot, wet, seasonal climate in the early Eocene, long before the modern monsoon is securely dated [inference, from n23; Gunnell et al. 2003 and n23; Takeaway on the monsoon].

The retreating wall

To the east of Vasai, beyond the Tungareshwar and Kamandurg hills, the Western Ghats rise in a great wall. That wall did not always stand where it does. As the edge of India rifted from the Seychelles and sagged toward the new ocean, the land behind it was left standing high, and its seaward face began to erode.

How the escarpment evolved is still argued. A preprint using beryllium-10, an isotope made by cosmic rays in surface minerals and used to measure erosion rates, infers that the escarpment has retreated at hundreds to thousands of metres per million years, at a nearly constant rate since rifting began around 62 million years ago. Other models propose a down-warped margin, a wall that wore down rather than back, or a parallel retreat [n23; Wang and Willett preprint, ESurf 2021]. The notes treat all of these as live options.

It is worth seeing that the two lines of evidence pull against each other. The ferricrete dates from the coastal lowland, about 52 million years, imply that the lowland surface has been worn down very little since then, and the authors read that as a sign that the escarpment has been broadly stable since at least 50 million years ago [n23; EGU/Copernicus abstracts]. The beryllium-10 preprint implies steady retreat, hundreds to thousands of metres per million years, all the way from rifting to the present [n23; Wang and Willett preprint]. If the wall had retreated at a thousand metres per million years for sixty million years, it would have moved sixty kilometres, which is roughly the width of the Konkan plain at this latitude; if it has been stable for fifty million years, most of the plain must have been cut very early [inference, simple arithmetic on the notes’ figures]. The two models can be partly reconciled if the retreat was fast at first and slow afterwards, or if different stretches of the coast behaved differently, but the research found no source that reconciles them, and a third source, giving the escarpment an age of about 84 million years, conflicts with both [n23; Kale blog summary]. The plain on which Vasai stands was made by the wall’s retreat in every model. When, and how fast, is open.

On the retreat model, the Ghats wall that now stands well inland once stood much closer to the sea, and the coastal plain on which Vasai lies was opened up as the wall wore back over tens of millions of years [n23; Wang and Willett preprint]. The Tungareshwar–Kamandurg hills east of Vasai are part of the lava flows, outliers of the old volcanic pile left standing in front of the main escarpment [n01; Takeaway]. The research found no source on their specific geology, their individual flow units, dykes or ages [n01; Gaps].

The Sahyadri

General knowledge in this paragraph and the next except where cited.

The wall that resulted is one of the great mountain fronts of Asia, though it is not a mountain range in the usual sense. The Western Ghats, the Sahyadri of Marathi and Sanskrit, run for about 1,600 kilometres from the Tapi valley in the north to the southern tip of the peninsula. In Maharashtra they are a great step rather than a chain of peaks: on the east the Deccan plateau rolls gently away toward the Bay of Bengal, while on the west the land drops a thousand metres or more, in cliffs and forested slopes, to the Konkan. The highest point in Maharashtra, Kalsubai, at about 1,650 metres, stands on this escarpment north-east of Vasai. The rivers of the eastern side, the Godavari, the Bhima and the Krishna, rise within sight of the Arabian Sea and flow across the whole peninsula to the Bay of Bengal; the rivers of the western side, the Vaitarna, the Ulhas and their neighbours, are short, steep and fast, falling to the sea within a hundred kilometres.

That asymmetry is itself a legacy of the rifting described in the last chapter. In general accounts, when the western margin of India broke away and sagged, the whole peninsula was tilted gently east, so that its great rivers drain toward the Bay of Bengal, and the new western edge stood high and steep above the opening ocean. The escarpment is what is left of that edge after tens of millions of years of erosion. Its slopes are now a UNESCO World Heritage Site, inscribed in 2012 for their biodiversity.

The geography that would govern all of Vasai’s later history was therefore taking shape in this age: a narrow plain between hills and sea, the hills themselves a fragment of the lava plateau, and behind them the wall of the Ghats, which every later trader, army and pilgrim would have to cross to reach the Deccan.

India meets Asia

Meanwhile, India kept moving north. At some point it struck the southern edge of Asia, and the collision began to raise the Himalaya and the Tibetan plateau.

The date of that collision is one of the great questions of Asian geology, and the research notes for this book did not retrieve a source for it. They record only that textbook accounts put the onset at about 50 million years ago, with estimates ranging from about 60 to 35 million years, and that the role of the Himalaya in creating the monsoon is likewise background knowledge not sourced here [n23; Gaps]. This book gives those figures as general knowledge only.

The closing of an ocean

General knowledge throughout this subsection; not sourced in the research notes [n23; Gaps].

The textbook account runs as follows. As India moved north, the ocean in front of it, which geologists call the Neo-Tethys, was consumed: its floor slid down beneath the southern edge of Asia at a deep-sea trench, feeding a long line of volcanoes on the Asian margin, in what is now southern Tibet. When the last of that ocean floor had gone, continental India, too light to sink, ran into Asia. The line where the two met, the Indus–Tsangpo suture, can still be traced across southern Tibet and Ladakh as a belt of crushed ocean-floor rocks and ancient volcanic arcs. The first contact may have been made by an island arc or a smaller continental fragment, which is one reason estimates of the collision date range so widely.

The collision did not stop India. It slowed it. From the very high speeds of the late Cretaceous and Paleocene, up to about sixteen centimetres a year in the notes’ source [n14; VLIZ], India’s motion dropped, in general accounts, to around five centimetres a year, roughly the rate it still moves north-north-east today. Every year since, India has pushed a few centimetres farther into Asia. The crust in front of it buckled and stacked up into the Himalaya; behind them, the crust of Asia thickened and rose to make the Tibetan plateau, the largest high plateau on Earth, about five kilometres above the sea. Some of the push was taken up by squeezing parts of Asia out sideways, to the east. Earthquakes all along the Himalayan front, and in the peninsula’s interior, record the strain that this slow collision still puts on the Indian plate.

For the Konkan, the consequences were indirect but immense. The collision built the mountains and the plateau that, in most accounts, help to drive and shape the Asian monsoon, as the following sections describe. It changed the circulation of the oceans around India. And the continuing stress of the collision may play a part in the slow rising and sinking of the coast that geologists detect in some places, a subject the notes touch on later in this chapter.

India’s own journey was, by then, mostly done. The ground of Vasai, which had erupted in the southern tropics, crossed the equator, in general reconstructions, around the time of the collision, and has crept north since to its present latitude of about nineteen and a half degrees north.

Travellers and survivors

The living world, however, left a clearer record of India’s approach to Asia than the research found for the rocks. Before the collision was complete, possible land bridges may have let frogs and other animals move between India and Southeast Asia. A study of microhylid frogs argues for several such exchange events during the Eocene [n23; BMC Evol. Biol. 9:131]. In the Late Oligocene to Early Miocene, a group of toads that had dispersed from Asia diversified in the Western Ghats and Sri Lanka [n23; Sci. Rep. s41598-018-38133-x]. At the same time, some of the oldest creatures of the Ghats appear to be survivors from Gondwana: centipedes of the scolopendrid family, scorpions of the Heterometrinae and the limbless amphibians called caecilians of the genus Gegeneophis are described as having Gondwanan origins [n23; BMC Evol. Biol. 2019].

The Western Ghats’ famous wealth of endemic species, the notes conclude, is a mixture: ancient relicts that rode north on the island continent, and later arrivals from Asia [n23; Takeaway]. Between about 11 and 3 million years ago, according to a news summary of recent work, peninsular Indian lineages diversified in an “idiosyncratic” way, some of them shaped by changes in temperature and climate [n23; Science Chronicle summary]. The notes found no direct source on the plants or animals of Vasai itself before the Pleistocene, and none on Palaeogene or Neogene fossils from the Konkan coast [n23; Gaps].

From hothouse to icehouse

General knowledge throughout this section except where cited; the research notes did not source the global climate history of the Cenozoic [n23; Gaps].

The sixty million years of this chapter saw the Earth’s climate change more than at any time since the dinosaurs, and every change was felt on this coast, even if no record of it here survives.

The age began hot. The world after the extinction was warm, with no permanent ice at either pole. About 56 million years ago, at the boundary between the Paleocene and Eocene epochs, a sudden release of carbon into the atmosphere and oceans, from a source still debated, drove global temperatures up by several degrees within a few thousand years. Geologists call it the Paleocene–Eocene Thermal Maximum. It acidified the oceans, shifted rainfall belts and caused a burst of change among land mammals, including the first appearance of primates, hoofed animals and horses across the northern continents. Some of the earliest known members of those mammal groups have been found in India, in lignite mines of Gujarat, which were laid down when India was an island continent close to the equator.

For several million years after that, in the Early Eocene Climatic Optimum around fifty million years ago, the Earth was warmer than at any time since. Forests grew near the poles; crocodiles lived in the Arctic. It was in this hothouse world, by the dates in the notes, that the Konkan’s lowland laterite surface was forming, at about 52 million years ago, in a climate hot and wet enough to rot basalt to a red crust [n23; EGU/Copernicus abstracts; Gunnell et al. 2003]. The laterites of the Ghats continued to form in successive surfaces down to about 23 million years ago, through the cooling that followed [n23; Gunnell et al. 2003].

From about fifty million years ago, the long cooling began. Carbon dioxide levels fell, perhaps partly because the rising Himalaya exposed vast amounts of fresh rock to weathering, which draws carbon dioxide out of the air; that idea is one of several, and it remains debated. About 34 million years ago, at the boundary between the Eocene and Oligocene, temperatures dropped sharply and a permanent ice sheet formed on Antarctica, as that continent became isolated around the South Pole by the opening of ocean gateways. The Earth entered an “icehouse” state that it has never left.

There were warm interludes. Around 17 to 15 million years ago, in the Miocene Climatic Optimum, temperatures rose again for a time. Then the cooling resumed. Grasslands spread across much of the world, including the Indian subcontinent, in the later Miocene, as the climate became more seasonal and in places drier; one review the notes cite reads the period from about 8 to 6 million years ago as a shift to more arid conditions in the region [n23; Molnar 2005] [general knowledge for the global spread of grasslands]. In the Pliocene, from about five million years ago, the world was still a little warmer than today, and the sea stood somewhat higher. Then, around 2.7 to 2.6 million years ago, large ice sheets began to grow on the northern continents for the first time, and the ice ages proper began.

On the Konkan coast, nothing of this global story has been read directly. No sediment of Palaeogene or Neogene age from the coast itself was searched for in this research, and the notes found no source on Konkan fossils of these ages, marine or terrestrial [n23; Gaps]. The laterites are the one local record, and they speak of heat and rain early in the age [n23; Gunnell et al. 2003]. For the rest, the Konkan must be imagined through the global record: a coast near the equator, hot and wet in the Eocene, drifting slowly north into a cooling world.

The coming of the monsoon

If one force has shaped life on this coast more than any other, it is the monsoon: the season of torrential rain from the southwest that arrives each June, fills the creeks, floods the rice fields, closes the sea to small boats, and ends in September. Every later chapter of this book is timed to it. When did it begin?

How the monsoon works

General knowledge in this subsection; not sourced in the research notes.

Before the question of dates, the mechanism. “Monsoon” comes, through Portuguese, from the Arabic mawsim, a season, the word the sailors of the Arabian Sea used for the seasonal winds that governed their voyages; the sailors of Sopara and Bassein would have known the thing, if not that word. In the simplest account, the monsoon is a vast sea breeze. In the northern summer the land of South Asia heats up far more than the ocean to its south. Warm air rises over the land, pressure falls, and moist air from the Indian Ocean is drawn in to replace it. The belt of rising air and heavy rain that girdles the tropics, the Intertropical Convergence Zone, swings far north over India in summer and back south in winter. As the moist air crosses the equator it is turned by the Earth’s rotation, so that it arrives on India’s west coast as a strong south-westerly wind, concentrated over the Arabian Sea into a low-level jet that blows past the Horn of Africa toward the Konkan and Malabar.

When that wind reaches the coast, it meets the Western Ghats. Forced up the escarpment, the air cools and drops its water. That is why the Konkan and the Ghats receive two to three metres of rain a year or more, most of it between June and September, while the Deccan plateau just beyond the crest lies in a rain shadow and receives a fraction as much. Vasai’s whole agricultural year, its rice and its gardens, depends on that collision of wind and mountain. In winter the wind reverses, blowing dry and steady from the north-east, and the sea is calm enough for sailing.

The monsoon, then, needs three things: a hot continent, a warm ocean, and the right shape of land. The geology of the last two chapters made all three on this coast: India’s drift into the northern tropics, the ocean opened as the Seychelles left, and the wall of the Ghats left standing at the edge.

The answer is contested, and the notes are careful about it. A 2022 study of winds over the equatorial Indian Ocean concluded that South Asian monsoon winds were already established by 9 million years ago, with no apparent intensification in the late Miocene [n23; Climate of the Past 18:713 (2022)]. An older line of evidence from the Arabian Sea, at an ocean-drilling site called Site 722, showed a shift in upwelling at about 8 to 8.5 million years ago, which was long read as the monsoon strengthening. A 2014 study argues that this signal reflects the uplift of the Owen Ridge and better preservation of carbonate, not the monsoon, so that interpretation is disputed. Another review reads the period from 8 to 6 million years ago as a shift to more arid conditions [n23; Rodriguez et al. 2014; Molnar 2005]. A conference abstract argues that the topography needed for a monsoon was already in place by the late Early Miocene, and points to temperature gradients in the Southern Hemisphere as a possible pacemaker [n23; EGU2020-22410].

The defensible line, as the notes put it, is that the monsoon was in place by roughly 9 million years ago, and probably earlier, but that its exact onset is debated [n23; Inferences]. The book will not say “the monsoon began 8 million years ago” as a fact.

Why the dates disagree

The disagreement is not carelessness; it follows from what each kind of evidence can measure. Laid out in order, the claims in the notes run like this:

  • By the late Early Miocene, roughly 16 million years ago or more: the topography needed for a monsoon was already in place, according to an EGU conference abstract, which suggests that temperature gradients in the Southern Hemisphere may have paced the monsoon’s strength [n23; EGU2020-22410]. This is an argument about the conditions for a monsoon, not a direct record of its rain.
  • By about 9 million years ago: monsoon winds over the equatorial Indian Ocean were already established, with no apparent intensification afterwards in the late Miocene, according to a 2022 study in Climate of the Past [n23; Climate of the Past 18:713 (2022)]. This is a record of wind.
  • About 8 to 8.5 million years ago: a shift in upwelling at Ocean Drilling Program Site 722 in the Arabian Sea was long read as the monsoon strengthening. A 2014 study argued the signal reflected the uplift of the Owen Ridge, on which the site sits, and better preservation of carbonate, not a change in the monsoon [n23; Rodriguez et al. 2014]. This is a record of ocean chemistry and plankton, which the monsoon affects but which other things affect too.
  • About 8 to 6 million years ago: another review reads this interval as a shift to more arid conditions [n23; Molnar 2005].

Outside the research notes, the wider scientific literature contains still older claims, arguing from sediments and fossils in China and Myanmar that monsoon-like seasonal rains already affected parts of Asia in the Eocene, some forty million years ago or more [general knowledge; not sourced in the notes]. If so, the “onset” of the monsoon may be less a single event than a long story of strengthening and weakening, with different parts of the system (the winds, the rains, the Indian and East Asian branches) changing at different times.

The mountains and the rain

General knowledge in this subsection; the notes flag the Himalaya’s role in the monsoon as not sourced [n23; Gaps].

For a long time the textbook explanation linked the monsoon directly to the rise of the Himalaya and Tibet. The high plateau, heated by the summer sun, was thought to act as an elevated heat source, drawing the moist ocean air in. Later work suggested that the Himalaya matter at least as much as a wall, keeping cold, dry air from the north out of India and allowing the hot, moist air over the subcontinent to build up. Both effects may operate. The notes’ conference abstract points to yet another factor, the temperature difference between the hemispheres [n23; EGU2020-22410]. On this question, as on the dates, the honest statement is that the mountains and the monsoon grew up together, and that how much each owes to the other is still argued.

What is not argued is the local consequence. Whatever its age, the monsoon has been, for millions of years, the master of the Konkan’s landscape: the force that rots its basalt to laterite, cuts its streams, swells the Vaitarna and the Ulhas each summer, and carries the Ghats grain by grain to the sea.

What can be said is what the monsoon did to this ground. Heavy seasonal rain, falling for millions of years on basalt in a hot climate, is exactly the condition that produces laterite, deepens weathering, feeds rivers and drives erosion. The Vaitarna in the north and the Ulhas in the south, which would one day frame the island of Sopara, carried the Ghats down to the sea grain by grain. The laterite on the Konkan hills, the notes say, reflects hot, wet weathering from early in this age [n23; Gunnell et al. 2003].

The age of ice

About two and a half million years ago, in the general account that the notes give as background rather than sourced fact, the Earth entered the Pleistocene, the age of ice [n23; Gaps]. Great ice sheets grew and shrank over the northern continents in repeated cycles. When they grew, they locked up so much water that the level of the world’s oceans fell; when they melted, the sea rose again. The notes did not source the timing of these cycles, or the height of the sea during the last interglacial, about 125,000 years ago, and treat them as background [n23; Gaps].

The rhythm of the ice

General knowledge in this subsection; not sourced in the research notes [n23; Gaps].

The ice ages were not random. Their timing is set, in the theory associated with the Serbian scientist Milutin Milanković, by slow, regular changes in the Earth’s orbit and tilt: the shape of the orbit stretches and relaxes over about 100,000 years, the tilt of the axis rocks back and forth over about 41,000 years, and the axis itself wobbles over about 23,000 years. Together they change how much summer sunshine falls on the high northern latitudes, where the great ice sheets grow. When northern summers are cool enough for winter snow to survive, ice builds up year after year; when they warm, it melts.

For the first part of the Pleistocene, the ice sheets grew and shrank roughly every 41,000 years. Then, in a change geologists call the Mid-Pleistocene Transition, between about 1.2 million and 700,000 years ago, the cycles lengthened to about 100,000 years and became much deeper. Each later cycle followed a similar pattern: a long, irregular slide into cold, with the ice sheets growing over tens of thousands of years, then a rapid collapse into a short warm interval, an interglacial, like the one we live in now. Scientists number these warm and cold stages from the chemistry of deep-sea shells, as Marine Isotope Stages: odd numbers for warm, even for cold, counting back from the present. The last interglacial, Marine Isotope Stage 5e, peaked around 125,000 years ago; in general accounts global sea level then stood several metres higher than today, perhaps around six. The last glacial period followed, and it reached its coldest point, the Last Glacial Maximum, a little over twenty thousand years ago.

In each cold stage, the sea fell by up to about 120 metres or more, and in each warm stage it rose back to about its present level. The coast of the north Konkan, then, did not simply sit where it is now and wait. Over the last million years it has swung back and forth across the wide continental shelf perhaps ten times, out to the shelf edge in each glacial and back to the foot of the hills in each interglacial. The creeks, beaches and mangroves of today are only the latest version of a coast that has been drowned and laid bare again and again.

In India the ice ages were felt not as ice but as changes in rain. In general accounts drawn from Arabian Sea cores and Asian caves, the summer monsoon was weaker during the cold stages and stronger during the warm ones, so that glacial India was, broadly, drier and cooler, with grasslands and dry forest spreading where wetter forest stands now. That pattern is general; no Konkan record of it was found.

What the Konkan coast records

A few regional records do reach back into the last glacial period on this coast, and they are worth gathering, because they are the nearest thing to a local history of the late Pleistocene.

The nearest named Late Pleistocene coastal site to Mumbai and Palghar is Uran, on the eastern rim of Bombay Harbour. In 1984 Kale, Kshirsagar and Rajaguru described a Late Pleistocene beach rock there, a beach deposit cemented into stone, in a short paper in Current Science [n24; Gawali et al. 2022, citing Kale, Kshirsagar & Rajaguru 1984]. A 2022 review of Konkan Quaternary studies summarises the sequence that work and others inferred for the Maharashtra coast: a low sea level around 30,000 to 35,000 years ago, a rise after 30,000 years, a regression again, a rise back around 15,000 years ago, and a maximum near the middle of the Holocene [n24; Gawali et al. 2022, pp. 146–147]. The 1984 paper itself was not read for this book, and its dates are not known in detail [n24; Gaps].

That local sequence does not fit easily with the global curve, which, as described below, shows sea level falling steadily toward the glacial maximum after about 30,000 years ago, not rising [n23; Clark and Tarasov commentary]. The difference may reflect the dating methods of the 1980s, local movement of the coast, or a beach deposit formed during a short-lived rise within the overall fall. The notes do not resolve it, and the book gives both.

Farther south, the Velas lignite, a layer of compressed plant matter more than 40,000 years old, now lies about a hundred metres from the sea; Rajshekhar and Kumaran, in 1998, read its position as a sign of neotectonic uplift or subsidence, that is, of the coast itself moving up or down in geologically recent times [n24; Gawali et al. 2022, p. 146]. On the outer shelf, Rao and Veerayya inferred about forty metres of subsidence of a terrace once at 130 metres since about 12,000 years ago [n24; IAS/ICAR catalogue listings]. Both observations carry the same warning: on this margin, the height of an old shoreline does not depend on global sea level alone. The land moves too, and any reconstruction of the coast at a given moment has to allow for it [n24; Inferences].

And one specialist’s verdict hangs over all of it. In 2001, Karlekar wrote that the relative chronology of Konkan sea-level changes “is not comprehensive” [n24; Gawali et al. 2022, p. 146]. The local history of the coast in the ice age remains, in the words of the people who study it, incomplete.

India was never under the ice sheets, but it felt their rhythm in its climate and its coastline. And inland, in the river valleys of the peninsula, it was home to a world of large animals.

The great straight-tusked elephant Palaeoloxodon namadicus was named in 1846 from a skull found in the Godavari valley. Bos namadicus, the Indian aurochs, a wild ancestor of cattle, lived on into the Holocene. A hippopotamus of the genus Hexaprotodon lived in Indian rivers. Up to four species-level extinctions of large mammals took place in India in the Late Pleistocene, though the taxonomy is confused. Their fossils have been reported from the valleys of the Narmada, the Purna and the Manjra, near Latur in Maharashtra [n23; Turvey et al. 2021; Latur radiocarbon paper; cavac.at mirror].

None of those fossils comes from Vasai. The notes found no Pleistocene fossil site in Mumbai, Thane or Palghar, apart from Todd’s disputed fossils from Khandivli [n23; Gaps]. Elephants and aurochs on the Konkan plain are plausible, not documented [n23; Cited Findings]. They belong in this story the way the dinosaurs of Gujarat did in the last chapter: as the world next door, which very probably reached here, though the ground has not yet shown it.

The animals of the river valleys

General knowledge in this subsection except where cited.

The giant elephant deserves a closer look. Palaeoloxodon, the straight-tusked elephants, were among the largest land mammals that have ever lived, and the Indian species, Palaeoloxodon namadicus, named from the Godavari skull in 1846 [n23; cavac.at mirror], has been estimated in some studies to have stood taller than any living elephant. Its name comes from the Narmada, whose old gravels and clays have been one of the richest sources of Indian Pleistocene fossils since British geologists began collecting there in the nineteenth century. In general accounts, the Narmada beds have also yielded wild cattle and buffalo, hippopotamus, horses, deer, pigs, rhinoceros and, in some layers, stegodonts, elephant relatives with low ridged teeth.

Bos namadicus, the Indian aurochs, was the wild ox from which, in most accounts, the humped zebu cattle of South Asia were domesticated; it survived into the Holocene [n23; Turvey et al. 2021] [general knowledge for the zebu link]. The Hexaprotodon hippopotamus of the Indian rivers was a relative of the living hippos of Africa. All three appear in the notes’ sources as part of a Late Pleistocene Indian fauna in which up to four species-level extinctions took place, though the taxonomy is confused [n23; Turvey et al. 2021].

In Maharashtra itself the Purna valley and the Manjra valley near Latur have produced such fossils, and a radiocarbon study has worked on the chronology of the fossil localities in the Manjra [n23; Latur radiocarbon paper]. Those valleys lie on the plateau beyond the Ghats, several hundred kilometres east of Vasai.

What lived on the Konkan plain itself in the Pleistocene can only be inferred. In general terms, the coast was then a wet, forested strip below the Ghats in the interglacials, and probably more open and drier in the glacials, when the monsoon weakened and the sea withdrew across the shelf. Elephants, wild cattle, deer, pigs and big cats, the kinds of animals found in the river valleys, would have found food and water on such a plain [inference; n23; Cited Findings calls them “plausible, not documented”]. When the sea fell in each glacial, the exposed shelf west of Vasai would have offered a broad lowland of river channels, grassland and swamp, a landscape that, in other parts of the world, supported great herds. Whatever bones lay on that lowland now lie under the Arabian Sea.

One measured clue from the plateau comes close to the end of this chapter. At Inamgaon, on the Ghod river in Pune district, freshwater shells from a deposit two metres above the riverbed, found together with a few Middle Stone Age tools, gave a radiocarbon age of 19,290 ± 360 years before present [n24; IAR 1971-72, p. 84, TF-1177]. That is almost exactly the Last Glacial Maximum. On the dry plateau beyond the Ghats, at the coldest point of the ice age, a river still ran, shellfish still lived in it, and people who made stone tools had been there. It is regional evidence, from well over a hundred kilometres away across the Ghats, and the shells date the deposit, not the tools with certainty. But it is a lab date, which the coast itself does not yet have for this age.

The first toolmakers of India

Somewhere in the Pleistocene, the first humans came.

Out of Africa, the first time

General knowledge in this subsection; not sourced in the research notes.

The human story, in the general account, begins in Africa. The earliest members of our genus, Homo, appeared there between about 2.8 and 2 million years ago, and by around 1.8 million years ago a long-legged, large-brained species, usually called Homo erectus, had spread out of Africa into Asia: its fossils are found at Dmanisi in Georgia by about 1.8 million years ago, and in Java and China not long after. Homo erectus was a walker and a generalist, able to live in many kinds of country, and it was the first human to make the Acheulean hand-axe, named after the French site of Saint-Acheul where such tools were first described in the nineteenth century. The hand-axe, a symmetrical, teardrop-shaped stone worked on both faces, was made with very little change in design for over a million years across Africa, western Asia, Europe and India. It is the longest-lasting tool tradition in human history.

Later archaic humans followed in their turn: the people often called Homo heidelbergensis, the Neanderthals of western Eurasia, the Denisovans known mainly from DNA and a few fossils in Asia, and others. Which of these lineages reached India, and when, is one of the great open questions of Asian prehistory, because India has so few human fossils. What India does have is stone tools, in enormous numbers, from river valleys and plateaus across the peninsula.

They were not modern humans. They were archaic people, of the species usually called Homo erectus or of related lineages, who made a distinctive kind of stone tool: the Acheulean hand-axe, a teardrop-shaped stone worked on both faces, and the cleaver, its square-edged cousin. The earliest well-dated Acheulean site in India is at Attirampakkam, in Tamil Nadu. In 2011 Shanti Pappu and colleagues reported in Science that palaeomagnetism and burial dating gave its tools a mean age of about 1.51 million years, with a minimum of 1.07 million. More than 3,500 artefacts were found, including 76 hand-axes and cleavers. The find pushed the South Asian Acheulean back from the previously accepted half a million years or so [n14; Phys.org; Science News; Shaastra (IIT Madras)] [n23; Phys.org]. Attirampakkam also has Middle Palaeolithic tools dated to about 385,000 years ago, reported in Nature in 2018; the notes flag that this detail came from the researcher’s background knowledge and should be checked against the article [n14; National Geographic].

Maharashtra has its own early sites, but they are disputed. At Bori, in the Kukdi valley of Pune district, hand-axes, flakes and choppers lie in gravels cut into a layer of volcanic ash. The age of that ash has been argued over for decades. A potassium–argon date of 1.38 ± 0.24 million years was published by Korisettar and colleagues in 1988–89; later work gave 0.67 ± 0.03 million years; another potassium–argon date gave 538,000 years. Then trace-element analysis matched the ash to the Youngest Toba Tuff, from an eruption only about 75,000 years ago [n14; Bori (Wikipedia); Scroll 2020; Mongabay India; Quaternary Research comment]. A difference of more than a million years in the age of one ash layer is a fair measure of how hard Indian Palaeolithic dating can be.

The Bori story is worth following step by step, because it shows how such a dispute unfolds. The first dates came from the potassium–argon method, which measures argon built up from the decay of potassium in volcanic minerals. Applied to the Bori ash, it gave an age of about 1.38 million years, which, if right, would have made the Bori tools among the oldest in Asia [n14; Bori (Wikipedia)]. But potassium–argon dating of an ash assumes that the minerals measured crystallised in the eruption that laid the ash, and that no older grains were mixed in. A volcanic ash that has been washed into a river and re-deposited may carry older crystals from other rocks, which make it look older than it is [general knowledge]. Later measurements gave 670,000 years, and then 538,000 [n14; Bori (Wikipedia); Scroll 2020]. Finally, geochemists compared the ash’s trace elements, its fingerprint of rare elements, with ashes of known origin, and found that it matched the Youngest Toba Tuff from Sumatra, about 75,000 years old [n14; Quaternary Research comment; Mongabay India].

If the Toba match is right, the ash at Bori is not a local eruption at all, and it gives the tools beneath it only a minimum age, far younger than the first claim. A 2020 article on the site carried the title “Story of a river bed: a geological marvel and community pride” [n14; Mongabay India]. Its tools are real. Their age, beyond being older than the ash, remains uncertain.

Only one hominin fossil has been found in India: the Narmada calvarium, the top of a skull found at Hathnora in Madhya Pradesh in 1982. It was first put at about 500,000 to 600,000 years old, from the animals found with it. A 2009 reassessment in the Journal of Human Evolution gave a maximum age above 236,000 years and noted it could be much younger; another analysis suggests a minimum of about 49,000 years, with reworking after about 160,000 years. Its species is unresolved: it has been called Homo erectus, archaic Homo sapiens, Homo heidelbergensis, or a separate “Homo narmadensis” [n14; Narmada Human (Wikipedia); IAS repository]. No hominin fossil has been found in Maharashtra [n14; Gaps].

The calvarium was found, in general accounts, by the Geological Survey of India geologist Arun Sonakia in the Narmada gravels at Hathnora, in Sehore district, while he was mapping the Quaternary deposits of the valley [general knowledge for the finder’s name; the find-place and year are from n14; Narmada Human (Wikipedia)]. It is a skull cap, without face or jaw, and the shape of such a fragment allows more than one reading. Its features have been compared with Homo erectus and with later archaic humans, which is why the names proposed for it span so wide a range [n14; Narmada Human (Wikipedia)]. The dating problem is that river gravels mix materials of different ages: a fossil can be washed out of one deposit and reburied in a younger one, which is what “reworking” means [general knowledge]. The 2009 reassessment and the later analysis both struggle with that problem, and both leave the skull’s age wide open, somewhere between more than 236,000 years and as little as about 49,000 [n14; Narmada Human (Wikipedia); IAS repository].

What can be said with confidence is modest but important. Archaic humans, people who were not Homo sapiens, lived in central India in the Middle or Late Pleistocene, in a river valley full of elephants, wild cattle and hippopotamus [n14; Narmada Human (Wikipedia)] [n23; Turvey et al. 2021]. The Narmada lies roughly four hundred kilometres north-east of Vasai. It is the nearest place where a human of that age has been found as bone rather than as stone.

Commander Todd’s coast

The nearest evidence of those early toolmakers to Vasai was found by a naval officer with a passion for prehistory.

Commander K. R. U. Todd surveyed the Bombay coast in the 1930s. Around Worli, in 1932, he found what he called “Chellean” hand-axes, using the old French term for the earliest hand-axe industries. At Khandivli (Kandivli), on the northern part of Salsette, he described a continuous stratified sequence: bedrock, then lower, middle and upper clays alternating with lower and upper gravels, containing hand-axes, a cleaver, a rostro-carinate (a beaked tool type), and fossils. He reported “Mousterian” tools at Colaba and “Aurignacian” tools at Worli, using European names for Middle and Upper Palaeolithic industries, and fossils at Kasu Shoal [n01; Ruikar, Antiquity]. His papers set out a prehistoric sequence for Bombay: “Prehistoric man round Bombay” (1932), “Palaeolithic industries of Bombay” (1939), and “The microlithic industries of Bombay” (1950) [n01; Antiquity]. In the 1939 paper he illustrated a hand-axe and a cleaver from Khandivli [n14; Ruikar, Antiquity; Cambridge core reader].

Mortimer Wheeler, writing in 1959, reported an Upper Palaeolithic horizon found by Todd in a clay and gravel pit at Khandivli, “21 miles north of Bombay” [n01; Wheeler, erenow excerpt]. Kalapesi, in 1946, also reported Palaeolithic implements from Kandivli [n01; Antiquity] [n14; Antiquity].

Todd’s work did not go unchallenged. The archaeologist Malik, in 1959, accepted his stratigraphy but could not find some of his industries, including his “Clactonian” and Late Acheulean tools. H. D. Sankalia, the leading Indian prehistorian of the day, disputed Todd’s Khandivli stratigraphy and his classification of the tools in 1959–60, and found no hand-axes there, although Todd’s own illustrations show bifaces [n01; Antiquity; Cambridge, Proc. Prehistoric Society]. A survey of the Lower Palaeolithic notes that Todd’s surface collections ran from Worli Point and Kandivli south as far as Jaygad, and that only a few convincing Palaeolithic sites are known from the Konkan, coastal Karnataka and Kerala as a whole [n14; INFLIBNET e-PG Pathshala; UNESCO Silk Road paper].

What the Archaeological Survey’s own reviews say

Newer research for this book recovered the official record of that dispute, from the Archaeological Survey of India’s annual Indian Archaeology – A Review, by running optical character recognition over scans whose text layer was unreadable. Two entries matter here, and they change the balance of the story. The next chapter quotes them at length, for what they say about the later Stone Age; here they belong for what they say about the hand-axes [n24; Method note; section 3].

In the review for 1957–58, B. B. Lal reported a preliminary survey of the country between the coast and the Ghats, from the Ulhas river in the north to Bombay in the south. Khandivli, the entry says, “has long been known to yield palaeoliths and later Stone Age tools, but the exact chronological positions of these industries in terms of the different epochs of the Pleistocene yet remain to be determined.” Lal’s aim was to see whether the industries could be assigned to river terraces linked to ancient sea levels. His findings were that the tools Todd had found in the nullah section behind Padan Hill near Khandivli “were not all in situ”, that is, not all in the layers where they were first deposited; that the older ones might belong to a higher terrace to the east, where palaeoliths were collected from weathered reddish earth and gravel; and that in the Ulhas valley between the Ghats and the coast “as many as four terraces were observed, not counting the youngest one”, which is flooded at high tide. A similar set of terraces lay in the Dahisar valley between Kanheri Hill and Manori Creek. The Ulhas between Thane and Ghodbunder, Lal wrote, “provides an ideal link for correlating the different terraces with ancient sea-levels” [n24; IAR 1957-58, p. 20].

That last sentence matters for Vasai. The Ulhas reaches the sea through Vasai Creek. The river whose terraces Lal thought might hold the key to the Stone Age sequence of the whole coast is the river that forms the southern edge of the island of Sopara. Nobody, in anything the research found, followed his suggestion with dating [n24; Inferences].

In the review for 1960–61, H. D. Sankalia, assisted by G. C. Mohapatra and V. N. Misra, reported his re-examination of Kandivli. He found two sections, “assignable respectively to a higher and a lower terrace”, the lower one apparently “the result of re-deposition by a small stream”. In both, chert and jasper tools were found, “which, however, did not include any handaxe or cleaver”. The section that Todd and Malik had divided into many sub-groups “could not really be done so”; at most there were two divisions, and both yielded tools of what the reviews called “Series II”, the later Stone Age, flake and microlithic kind. The geologist F. E. Zeuner also examined the sections and held that there were two deposits, not several [n24; IAR 1960-61, p. 22].

This is the ASI-published position, and it should be stated plainly: the Survey’s own re-examination of Kandivli found a later Stone Age record on two terraces, not the long Acheulean-to-Mesolithic sequence Todd described, and found no hand-axe or cleaver [n24; Inferences]. Against it stand Todd’s own published drawings of a hand-axe and a cleaver from Khandivli, Kalapesi’s report of Palaeolithic implements there, Wheeler’s acceptance of an Upper Palaeolithic horizon, and Malik’s acceptance of the stratigraphy [n14; Ruikar, Antiquity] [n01; Wheeler, erenow excerpt]. Lal’s observation that the older tools may have come from a higher terrace to the east offers one way to reconcile the two sides: Todd may have collected genuine early tools that had been washed down out of their original layers, and Sankalia, digging the sections themselves, may have found only the later material that truly belonged there [inference, from n24; IAR 1957-58]. The research found no modern study that settles the matter, and no absolute date for any of these tools [n24; section 2 Takeaway].

A third source adds the setting. Vishnu-Mittre and Statira Guzder, in a study of the stratigraphy and pollen of the mangrove swamps of Bombay, described the swamps as up to five metres of dark and brown mud filling undulating rocky basins. “Middle and Late Stone Age tools,” they wrote, had been found by Todd, Malik, Sankalia and Guzder herself, in the nullah sections at Borivli and Kandivli and on the surface of the coastal headlands at Erangal, Manori and Gorai. If the stratum with the Middle Stone Age tools could be tied to the base of the raised-beach deposits, they noted, the tools could be placed in a known chronological horizon; but the lack of organic matter and pollen prevented a radiocarbon-dated pollen sequence [n24; Journal of Palaeosciences record]. Gorai and Erangal lie on the Bhayandar–Manori peninsula, facing Vasai across the creek. If the authors’ hypothesis is right, the Middle Stone Age tools there are older than the raised beaches of the Holocene, and so belong to the ice age world this chapter describes [n24; Inferences].

Farther down the coast, at Susrondi in Ratnagiri, Ashok Marathe’s work from 2001 is described as the first coastal Acheulean cave site in India, with Late Acheulean artefacts [n14; Academia.edu, “Peopling the Koṁkaṇ Coast”; Garge et al. 2023].

A recent paper by Garge and colleagues goes further, in passages quoted in search results: evidence for the transition from the late Acheulean to the Early Historic in the Konkan “was either missing or not reported earlier”, and the region’s hunter-gatherers “entered in Historical period without attending any technological transition” of the kind seen inland [n24; Garge et al. 2023 (search-result quotations; full paper not read)]. The next chapter returns to that claim.

So the Acheulean of the Konkan is sparse, and the Bombay evidence is old surface and section work without modern absolute dates [n14; Gaps]. Kandivli is about 20 to 25 kilometres south of Vasai across Vasai Creek, and it is the nearest reported hand-axe find spot [n14; Inferences]. The notes draw the careful conclusion: hand-axe makers, presumably Homo erectus or a related archaic human, very likely ranged over the Vasai area too, but no site there is recorded [n14; Inferences]. The hills of Jivdani at Virar, of Tungareshwar and of Kamandurg have not, in any published survey the research found, been searched for such tools. That may reflect unpublished or uncatalogued work rather than true absence [n01; Gaps] [n14; Gaps].

Ash from Sumatra

About 74,000 years ago, a volcano on the island of Sumatra, Toba, erupted with a violence unmatched in recent geological time. Its ash, the Youngest Toba Tuff, fell across South Asia.

At Jwalapuram, in Andhra Pradesh, Michael Petraglia and colleagues reported in Science in 2007 that similar Middle Palaeolithic stone tools lay both below and above the Toba ash, which implied that people were present by about 78,000 years ago and survived the eruption. The authors saw affinities with the Middle Stone Age of Africa, and so suggested these might be the tools of modern humans. There are no human fossils at the site, and critics, notably Stanley Ambrose, dispute both the attribution to modern humans and the reading that the eruption had little impact [n14; Live Science; Archaeology magazine; UOW, Petraglia et al. 2009].

Toba ash is reported inland in Maharashtra, in the Kukdi valley, about 150 kilometres from Vasai; that is the same ash that was matched to the gravels at Bori [n14; Quaternary Research comment; Inferences] [n23; Quaternary Research comment]. The notes infer that the ash fall probably reached the north Konkan too. There is no Vasai record of it [n14; Inferences]. It is one more silence: a layer of grey volcanic dust from four thousand kilometres away that probably lies somewhere in the sediments of this coast, unrecognised.

The coming of modern humans

The people whose descendants live on this coast today were modern humans, Homo sapiens, and they came out of Africa.

The notes summarise the most widely held view: that modern humans probably reached India along a southern route out of Africa, about 65,000 to 50,000 years ago, following the coasts of southern Asia, and that early travellers along that route reached Australia by about 50,000 years ago [n14; Peopling of India (Wikipedia); PBS NOVA timeline]. The settlement of Australia matters to this coast’s story for one reason: reaching it required crossing open sea, so people of that age could build some kind of craft capable of sea crossings, even though no boat of that age survives anywhere [n14; Inferences].

The southern route

General knowledge in this subsection except where cited.

Homo sapiens itself evolved in Africa; the oldest fossils usually assigned to our species, from Jebel Irhoud in Morocco, are about 300,000 years old. Small groups left Africa more than once. Some early ventures into the Levant and Arabia, more than 100,000 years ago, seem to have left little trace in the genes of living people. The main dispersal, the one from which nearly all people outside Africa descend, is usually dated, on genetic grounds, to roughly 70,000 to 50,000 years ago, which agrees with the notes’ window of about 65,000 to 50,000 years for the arrival in India [n14; Peopling of India (Wikipedia); PBS NOVA timeline].

The “southern route” hypothesis holds that these people crossed from the Horn of Africa into southern Arabia, at the Bab-el-Mandeb strait, perhaps at a time of low sea level when the crossing was narrowest, and then moved east along the coasts of Arabia, Iran and the Indian subcontinent, living largely on the resources of the shore: shellfish, fish, turtles, and the plants and animals of coastal plains and river mouths. A coast is, in one sense, an easy road. It offers fresh water where rivers enter the sea, food that does not run away, and a landscape whose kind does not change much from one bay to the next, so that people adapted to one stretch of shore can move to the next without learning a new way of life. On that reckoning, the first modern humans in India would have arrived along the coast of Gujarat and the Konkan, or on the plains then exposed in front of them, and spread inland up the river valleys [general knowledge for the hypothesis; n14; Inferences for its application here].

That hypothesis is attractive and unproven. Its weakness is the sea itself. Because the dispersal happened during the last glacial period, when sea levels were lower than today, the coastlines those people walked are now under water, along with whatever camps, middens and tools they left [n14; Inferences]. The proof, if it exists, lies on the drowned shelf, and nobody has looked for it there.

The modern humans who arrived in South Asia also carried, in general accounts, a small share of DNA from archaic humans they had met on the way, Neanderthals and Denisovans, as all non-African populations do. Whether they met any surviving archaic people inside India itself, such as the makers of the Middle Palaeolithic tools at Jwalapuram or the people of the Narmada skull, is unknown.

Genetics adds a second line of evidence. The descendants of those first modern arrivals form an ancestry component that geneticists call “AASI”, for Ancient Ancestral South Indian. It underlies the genomes of all South Asians. In the largest study to date, Narasimhan and colleagues in 2019 used the genomes of 523 ancient individuals and described the primary source of ancestry in modern South Asians as “a prehistoric genetic gradient between people related to early hunter-gatherers of Iran and Southeast Asia” [n14; Reich Lab PDF; Penn State record; UCL open manuscript]. The notes flag that only abstracts and summaries of that paper were read. An earlier study, Reich and colleagues in 2009, found that the Andamanese are related to the “Ancestral South Indian” population without later northern admixture, which implies the islands were peopled before the mainland mixing that came later [n14; Reich 2009 abstract; Nature India 2009]. There is a conflict here too: Yelmen and colleagues, in 2019, argued that the AASI component is distinct from, and not closely related to, the Andamanese, and that the Paniya people of south India are a better proxy [n14; Genetics of South Asia (Wikipedia summary)].

Newer reading of the full Narasimhan paper, recorded in the notes, sharpens this. The study describes three ancestry gradients, or clines, succeeding one another in South Asia, and states that “much of the formation of both the ASI and ANI occurred in the second millennium BCE”; the paper’s text, searched in full, contains no ancient individuals from Maharashtra, the Konkan or Inamgaon [n24; Narasimhan et al. 2019, Summary and main text]. So the genetics of the first modern humans on this coast is inferred from present-day people and from ancient genomes elsewhere. No ancient DNA from the Konkan, Mumbai or the Maharashtra coast exists in anything the research found [n24; section 7 Gaps].

The later layers of that genetic history, the arrival of ancestry related to Iranian farmers and, after about 2000 BCE, of steppe ancestry, belong to the next chapters. What matters here is that one of the strongest AASI signals measured anywhere in India belongs to a people of this very district: the Warli of Palghar. Their story, too, is for the next chapter, but the thread starts here, with the first modern hunters who came along the coast.

When did modern humans first reach Maharashtra? The notes are blunt: there is no direct date. Jwalapuram’s “modern human” reading is contested [n14; Gaps]. Nearer at hand, an exploration team working on Salsette has reported larger stone tools at Khindipada, in the Sanjay Gandhi National Park, which they “believed to be over 30,000 years old”. That figure is the explorers’ own estimate, given to the press, not a laboratory date, and one member of the team said they still had to rule out a stray find [n01; Homegrown; Deccan Herald archive] [n23; Homegrown; Deccan Herald]. It is a hint, not a fact.

Down the Ghats, or along the shore?

How did the first people reach this coast? The notes pose the question directly, and conclude that no direct evidence decides it for the north Konkan [n14; Inferences].

Palaeolithic tools occur both inland, beyond the Ghats, at Bori in the Kukdi valley, and along the coast, at Kandivli and Susrondi. The later Mesolithic record, as the next chapter will show, is strongly coastal and oriented toward the sea. A fair statement, the notes suggest, is that early people probably reached this coast both by walking the then-exposed coastal plain and by coming down the valleys from the Ghats. Boats are documented on this coast only from the Bronze Age, at Lothal in Gujarat, though the settlement of Australia by about 50,000 years ago shows that sea crossings were possible much earlier [n14; Inferences].

And there was a great deal more coastal plain to walk in those days than there is now.

Toward the coldest point

The last ten thousand years of this chapter, from roughly thirty thousand to twenty thousand years ago, are the slide into the deepest cold of the last ice age. They can be told only in outline, and almost entirely from regional and global evidence, but the outline is clear enough to describe the land.

The world was cooling toward its glacial maximum. Ice sheets kilometres thick covered most of Canada and the northern United States, Scandinavia and northern Britain, and smaller ice caps sat on the mountains of Asia, including the Himalaya, whose glaciers reached far lower than today [general knowledge]. Global sea level, as the next section describes, fell by about forty metres quite suddenly near 30,000 years ago and then slowly further, to around 130 metres or more below its present level [n23; Clark and Tarasov commentary]. On the Maharashtra coast the Uran beach rock and the sequence built on it suggest a more complicated local history, with a low around 30,000 to 35,000 years ago, a rise and then a fall again, though the details of that work were not read [n24; Gawali et al. 2022].

In general terms, the monsoon over India was weaker at the glacial maximum than it is now, and the subcontinent was cooler and drier [general knowledge]. On the Konkan, that probably meant less rain on the Ghats, a longer dry season, and forests thinner and more open than the dense monsoon forest of the interglacials [inference]. Rivers carried less water but had farther to go. As the sea fell, every river on this coast had to extend its course out across the newly exposed shelf, and, in general, rivers whose outlet drops cut down into their beds, carving valleys into the land they cross [general knowledge]. The Vaitarna and the Ulhas, in that world, would have run west far beyond the present coast, in valleys cut into the dry shelf, toward a shoreline out near the shelf edge [inference, from n23; Inferences and n14; Inferences]. The terraces that B. B. Lal counted along the Ulhas, four of them above the tidal one, may be records of the river’s response to rising and falling sea levels over many such cycles; that was his hypothesis, and it was never tested by dating [n24; IAR 1957-58, p. 20].

People were in the region. At Inamgaon on the plateau, freshwater shells with a few Middle Stone Age tools date to about 19,300 years ago [n24; IAR 1971-72, p. 84]. On Salsette, the larger stone tools at Khindipada were thought by their finders to be more than 30,000 years old, an estimate without a lab date [n01; Homegrown; Deccan Herald archive]. The Middle Stone Age tools that Vishnu-Mittre and Guzder listed at Borivli, Kandivli, Erangal, Manori and Gorai may, on their hypothesis, belong to a horizon older than the Holocene raised beaches [n24; Journal of Palaeosciences record]. And one prehistorian, Ghate, collected microliths at a site the review literature calls “Karal”, described in the source as now lying far below sea level; the citation is second-hand and its meaning is unclear, so it is given here only as a pointer to the kind of evidence the drowned coast might hold [n24; UNESCO PDF, as cited].

None of these traces is at Vasai. But taken together they describe a peopled landscape. On the plateau, at the cold maximum, people made stone tools beside a living river. On the coastal hills just south of Vasai Creek, people had made tools at some time in the late Pleistocene. Between and beyond them, on a plain that then ran unbroken from the foot of Tungareshwar to a shore far to the west, the ground of Sopara and Vasai lay inland and dry, a stretch of rolling country between hills and rivers, with no creek, no island and no sea in sight [inference, from n23; Inferences].

The drowned plain

At the height of the last ice age, the sea off Vasai lay far to the west.

The numbers for the global fall in sea level are well established. Lambeck and colleagues, in 2014, described a rapid fall of about 40 metres near 30,000 years ago, followed by a slow fall to about 134 metres below present between about 29,000 and 21,000 years ago [n23; Clark and Tarasov commentary, PMC4234583]. Yokoyama and colleagues, in 2018, put the Last Glacial Maximum low of global sea level at about 125 to 130 metres below present, at about 20,500 years ago; older estimates range from 105 to 163 metres, many of them near 120 [n23; Yokoyama et al. 2018; PMC4234583].

What that meant off Mumbai depends on the shape of the sea floor. The continental shelf of western India is very wide here: about 280 kilometres from Bombay to Ratnagiri, wider still to the north, with a gentle shelf break, the edge where the shallow shelf drops into the deep ocean, at about 90 to 120 metres deep [n14; J. Micropalaeontology 24:39] [n23; J. Micropalaeontology 24:39]. Other sources give 90 to 200 metres for the shelf break along the western margin [n14; J. Coastal Research]. Submerged terraces between Bombay and Karwar lie at about 92, 85, 75 and 55 metres below present sea level, perhaps marking old shorelines where the sea paused, but a source on them says an unequivocal chronology is “not possible at present”, partly because the coast may have moved tectonically [n14; Springer, J. Geol. Soc. India] [n23; Springer, J. Geol. Soc. India]. Another note records that these terraces have been radiocarbon-dated to the Holocene and compared with global sea-level curves; the two readings of that source sit uneasily together, and the book gives both [n01; Springer]. Relic carbonates on the western margin record the rise of the sea after the glacial maximum [n14; IAS repository; ICAR record].

Put the global numbers and the local shelf together, and the notes reach an inference, one they insist must be called approximate. If sea level stood about 120 metres lower at the glacial maximum, the shoreline off Vasai would have lain near the outer edge of the shelf, likely on the order of a hundred kilometres or more west of today’s coast [n14; Inferences] [n23; LGM, Konkan shelf]. No peer-reviewed reconstruction of that ancient shoreline specifically for the Mumbai shelf was found, and no dated submerged peat or forest offshore of Mumbai [n14; Gaps]. The “hundred kilometres” is reasoning, not a measurement.

In that world, the features that define Vasai today did not exist. There was no Vasai Creek and no tidal Vaitarna estuary. The site of Vasai was inland, and rivers ran west across a wide plain toward a distant sea [n23; Inferences]. Salsette was not an island, and Kandivli and Sopara stood on the same continuous land. Somewhere out on that plain, now under the Arabian Sea, the first coastal travellers, if they did follow the shore, would have made their camps. Any such sites would now lie on the drowned shelf. That, the notes say, is a good beat for a story, but a hypothesis, not a finding [n14; Inferences].

There is one tantalising local clue. In 1919 the geologist La Touche reported a submerged, buried forest discovered during work at Prince’s Dock in Bombay. It is cited in a later study of the Kanheri area, but no date is given for it in anything the research found [n14; Journal of Palaeosciences] [n23; Inferences]. A drowned forest under Mumbai’s docks fits the story of a sea that rose over dry land. Until it is dated, it is only a fit.

Beyond the maximum: a glance ahead

The chapter’s story ends here, at the glacial maximum, a little over twenty thousand years ago, with the sea at its lowest and the ground of Vasai far inland. The next chapter, “The Ice Age Shore and the First People”, begins at exactly that moment, with a sediment core pulled from the outer shelf off Bombay, and follows the sea’s return in detail: the dated shelf corals off Bombay and Ratnagiri, the shell dates from Manori, Madh and Mahim, the making of the creeks and of the island of Sopara, and the microlith-makers of the Salsette hills [n24; section 1]. What follows here is only a short preview, kept so that the threads begun in this chapter can be seen running on.

The ice melts

Then the ice began to melt. Around 14,200 years ago, by Peltier’s 2005 chronology, or about 14,600 years ago by others, the world warmed abruptly at the start of a period called the Bølling, and a great surge of meltwater, “Meltwater Pulse 1A”, poured into the oceans. Sea level rose fast [n23; Peltier 2005 PDF]. Its local effect on the Konkan is not documented [n23; Cited Findings]. To the north, the Gulf of Kachchh was flooded by at least 15,000 years ago [n14; IAS repository] [n23; IAS repository]. The Younger Dryas, a cold snap that followed, appears in the notes only from a low-quality general-interest source, so the book leaves it aside [n23; Gaps].

On the Bombay shelf itself there are, in fact, measurements of this rise, which the next chapter sets out. A core from the outer shelf put sea level at about 101.5 metres below present around 14,500 years ago, rising with pauses at about 13,000 and 11,500 years ago and largely risen before 10,000 [n24; Nigam et al. 1992]. Corals from the shelf off Bombay and Ratnagiri gave radiocarbon ages of about 11,000 and 10,400 years [n24; IAR 1971-72, pp. 83–84]. And Deccan College researchers summarised the evidence as showing a low sea level at the end of the Pleistocene, with the sea at about its present level by around 6,000 years ago [n24; Rajaguru & Ghate, UNESCO PDF]. All of that is the next chapter’s story.

As the water climbed back across the shelf, the wide plain west of Vasai was drowned, metre by metre. River valleys turned into inlets. The coast began to march back toward the hills.

The microlith-makers of Manori

It is at about this point, at the very end of the ice age, that the nearest “first humans” site to Vasai appears.

On a hill beside Manori beach, on the western shore of Salsette just south of Vasai Creek, the Salsette Archaeological Exploration Project reported in 2018 a scatter of microliths: very small stone blades and points, often set into wooden or bone hafts to make composite tools such as arrows and harpoons. The site may have been a tool “factory”. It was provisionally dated to between 10,000 and 15,000 years ago. The press coverage was muddled, calling the finds “Middle Stone Age” in a headline but about 15,000 years old in the body of the article [n14; Hindustan Times via PressReader; IBTimes India] [n23; Hindustan Times via PressReader]. Salsette Island runs between Mahim and Vasai Creek, so the Manori hill is the closest documented early-human site to Vasai, and the notes call it a strong location for anyone telling this story on screen [n14; Inferences].

The press reports, and the provisional date, need caution. Microliths were made over a very long span of time, so a scatter of them on the surface does not by itself date a site [n23; PLOS ONE microlith review]. But if the estimate holds, the toolmakers of Manori sat on their hill at the moment the sea was rising toward them, when the ancient plain to the west was turning into shallow water, and the land on which Sopara would one day stand was still a ridge above a river valley, waiting for the tide.

The strandloopers

Commander Todd, who had found the hand-axes of Kandivli, also thought hard about the people who made the microliths of the Bombay coast. In his 1950 paper on “The microlithic industries of Bombay” he proposed that population movements along the coast had carried these toolmakers along the shore, and he compared them to the “strandloopers” of South Africa, beachcombing foragers who lived on shellfish and fish [n01; Antiquity] [n14; Antiquity, Ruikar; INFLIBNET Mesolithic Cultures].

The notes suggest pairing Todd’s idea with the genetic picture: a hypothesis of coastal foragers spreading along the shore, set against the genetic backdrop of the AASI ancestry that descends from the first modern arrivals [n01; Inferences]. Neither proves the other. Together, they describe a plausible world at the end of the ice age: small groups of hunter-gatherers moving along a coast that was itself moving, living on what the sea and the shore provided, making tiny, sharp tools of stone.

What the ice age left

Stand, for a last look, at the glacial maximum, a little over twenty thousand years ago, where this chapter properly ends. In sixty million years the black lava of the Mumbai coast had been weathered red on its hills, left in front of a retreating wall of mountains, and carried north with India until India struck Asia and raised the Himalaya. The world had cooled from a hothouse without polar ice into an age of great ice sheets. The monsoon had come to blow, by nine million years ago at the latest and perhaps much earlier, and had soaked the coast every summer since. The sea had swung back and forth across the shelf in cycle after cycle, and now stood at its lowest, more than a hundred metres down and, by inference, a hundred kilometres or more to the west. Elephants and aurochs had roamed the river valleys of the peninsula. Archaic humans had made hand-axes across India, and perhaps at Kandivli, within a day’s walk of Sopara, though the Archaeological Survey’s own re-examination found none there and no hand-axe has yet been found in Vasai itself. Modern humans had arrived, by the shore or over the hills, and their descendants were living through the cold. Soon, as the glance ahead has shown, the sea would rise, and microlith-makers would sit on the hill at Manori.

Every one of those statements depends on evidence from somewhere else, or on the general knowledge of the earth sciences: Tamil Nadu, Pune, Madhya Pradesh, Andhra Pradesh, Uran, Salsette. For this whole long age, Vasai’s own record is silent. No laterite here has been dated, no fossil found, no tool published [n14; Scope note] [n23; Gaps]. But the outlines of the place were being drawn. The hills of Tungareshwar and Kamandurg stood in the east. The rivers ran down from the Ghats. And the sea, rising now, was coming back to the land it had left.

When it arrived, it would make the creeks, the mangrove swamps and the tidal island of Sopara. And the people of the shore would be there to meet it.
