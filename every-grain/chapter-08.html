Period covered: 1534 – 1600

In this chapter. The Portuguese take Baçaim and build it into a walled city, a fleet base and a mission field. This chapter follows its walls, its captains, its friars and Jesuits, and the conversion of the villages around it.

In the last days of December 1534, off the coast of Gujarat, a Portuguese galleon called the São Mateus carried two men who had spent five years trying to destroy each other. One was Nuno da Cunha, Governor of Portuguese India. The other was Bahadur Shah, Sultan of Gujarat. The modern standard account says they signed their treaty on board on 23 December 1534. The 1882 Gazetteer dates the same agreement to 1533, and a second instrument of peace and commerce is dated 25 October 1535; the last chapter set out all three dates, and this book keeps them in view. [n04; Wikipedia: Treaty of Bassein (1534)] [n03; TG-II p. 451] [n32; BG 28–29]

The terms that changed the coast are well known, and Chapter 7 gave them in full: Bassein with its dependencies and revenues, Salsette, Thana and the islands of Bombay passed to Portugal; every Gujarati ship bound from Cambay to the Red Sea had to touch at Bassein, take a Portuguese pass and pay duty; Gujarat would build no warships and make no alliance with the Turks; captives were to be returned, and the horse trade was to be regulated. [n03; TG-II p. 451] [n04; Military history of Bassein, Wikipedia]

But one clause rarely makes the summaries, and it is the best place to begin this chapter. According to F. C. Danvers’s history of the Portuguese in India, the treaty laid down that 5,000 larins of Bassein’s revenue, which had gone to the town’s mosques, “was to continue to be so applied”. [n37; Danvers vol. 1 p. 416] The sultan was giving away a port, but he was not, on paper, giving away its faith. The Muslim prayer-houses of Basai would keep their income under Christian rule.

Within twelve years that promise was gone. In March 1546 King João III wrote from Portugal to his viceroy, Dom João de Castro, ordering that the 3,000 pardaos “formerly employed in the maintenance of the Mahomedan mosques” be given instead to the priests “who give a helping hand towards the conversion of the infidels”. [n37; da Cunha p. 215 note] [n32; DC 214–216] By 1550 the Franciscans of Bassein were drawing 2,070 pardaos a year from “a source which before the conquest was solely applicable to the lighting of the mosques in Bassein”. [n37; da Cunha p. 235] The mosques themselves had been pulled down, and in their place stood a church of St Joseph. [n10; Gazetteer 1882, Bassein entry]

The lamp oil of the mosques became the stipend of the friars. That single line of money, followed from the deck of the São Mateus to the altar of a new church, is the story of this chapter in miniature. In sixty-six years a customs port on a creek became a walled city, the capital of a province, a mission centre second only to Goa, and the birthplace of a new people. The Gujarat sultans had held Basai as a garrison and a toll-gate. The Portuguese came to hold Baçaim as a home, and to make its people over in their own image. Some of the people accepted, some resisted, some fled, and many did a little of each. Their descendants still live in the villages of the old island: at Nirmal, Agashi, Nandakhal, Gass, Papdi and Sandor.

A coast in waiting (1534–1536)

What did the new owners receive? The sources let us see the place on the eve of the change from several sides at once.

The land was an island, or nearly. Under the Portuguese the whole rich strip between the Bassein creek and the Vaitarna, a band of gardens about three miles broad, would be called the island of Bassein; before them, it had been the island of Sopara. A backwater cut it off from the mainland, running as the Manikpur creek in the south, the Sopara creek in the middle and the Bolinj creek in the north. [n11; Gaz. 1882 pp. 314–315] [n37; Gaz. XIV pp. 314–315] That backwater “was once a considerable arm of the sea”, the Gazetteer says, and it was already silting. Sopara, the old city on its garden island, had lost the trade it once commanded. “Bassein lies only six miles south of Sopara,” the Gazetteer reasoned; “it can hardly have been a place of consequence so long as Sopara (AD 1100) remained a centre of trade.” [n37; Gaz. XIV pp. 28, 322] By 1534 the balance had long tipped south. Bassein had the deep water, the landing and the custom house. Sopara had its temples, its lakes and its memory.

The people paid their taxes in kind and in cash. The Portuguese found the land guarded by stockades and fortified posts, with land revenue taken in grain and cash cesses levied on coconut oil, opium, cotton, palm spirit, vegetables, fish, sugarcane and betel, and taxes on butchers, dyers, fishermen and shepherds. [n03; TG-II p. 451] Read as a census of trades, that list describes the island’s work in 1534: palm-tappers, garden cultivators, oil-pressers, fishermen, dyers and herdsmen.

The town itself was, in Duarte Barbosa’s words of 1514, a town of “Moors and Gentiles”, Muslims and Hindus, “a good seaport belonging to the king of Gujarat”, where boats came from Malabar laden with areca, coconuts and spices. [n37; Gaz. XIV p. 28] [n32; BG 28–29] Among its Muslims were the Naitas, families descended from Arab and Persian refugees and merchants who had settled the coast between the eighth and eleventh centuries. The Portuguese physician Garcia d’Orta, who knew this coast well, would mention “Naitas of Bassein” who married local women and were rich traders when the Portuguese came. [n37; Gaz. XIII-I pp. 216–217; Gaz. XIV p. 315 note] These were the merchants the cartaz clause was designed to catch.

Around the town, in the gardens and paddy of the island, lived the communities whose names fill the rest of this book: Samvedi Brahmans growing sugarcane and betel in the north; Panchkalshis tending the wadis, the orchards of coconut and palm; Kolis on the creeks and the shore; Bhandaris at the toddy palms; Palshe Brahmans serving as the coast’s priests, physicians and astrologers. [n08; Gazetteer 1882, pp. 81–82, 129–130] [n35; Enthoven III p. 161] Inland, on the hills, were Varlis and Katkaris and Thakurs. [n35; Enthoven II, III]

The coast had also just been burnt. Portuguese fleets had raided it again and again in the years before the treaty. In 1530 and 1531, according to the Gazetteer, Agashi was destroyed and three hundred of its ships burnt; Antonio de Saldanha burnt Kelve-Mahim and Agashi on his way back from Diu. [n03; TG-II p. 451] In 1533, says a source that Wikipedia flags as unreliable, Diogo da Silveira burnt the coast from Bandora to Surat and came home with “4000 slaves and spoils”. [n04; Vasai Fort, Wikipedia] Malik Tokan’s citadel, the last Gujarati fort at Bassein, had been stormed and razed. [n03; TG-II p. 451] The Parsis of Thana, very numerous until about 1533, left at the conquest: their own tradition says they feasted the Portuguese officers, made them drunk, and slipped away to Kalyan, where they built a tower of silence in 1533. [n35; Enthoven III pp. 192–195]

The first Portuguese numbers for the island come from their own account books. A land register (Tombo) of 1538 compiled under S. Botelho records the “Rent of the caçabe (Cusbah) of Çupara… 14,122 fedeas”. [n15; Hobson-Jobson] Sopara had become a caçabe, a market town, in a Persian word the Portuguese had taken over from the sultans. [n32; DC 157–158] The customs and town revenue of Agashi stand at 281,249 fedeas (about £234) in 1536 and 313,874 fedeas in 1537, according to the Gazetteer as one note reads it; another reading of the same Gazetteer page places those yields in 1636–1643. [n11; Gaz. 1882 pp. 1–2] [n37; Gaz. XIV pp. 1–2] The page scan would settle it. If the earlier dates are right, Agashi’s customs grew by about a tenth in a single year after its destruction.

And in 1538, four years after the treaty, João de Castro, the soldier-scholar who would become viceroy, found Bassein “large, the resort of many people and nations”. [n03; TG-II p. 451] The burning had not emptied it. Ships kept coming, because the treaty made them come.

The first stone (1536)

The Portuguese did not build at once. Nuno da Cunha’s fleet was needed at Diu, where Bahadur Shah, beaten by the Mughal emperor Humayun in 1535, had allowed the Portuguese a fortress. [n03; TG-II p. 451] Only at the end of March 1536, Danvers writes, did the governor go to Bassein. He “found all the materials ready”, and “having fixed upon a site” he began the fortress. [n37; Danvers vol. 1] Hull’s mission history, copying Gerson da Cunha, gives the founding year as 1535; the Catholic Encyclopedia says Nuno da Cunha “founded the city in 1536” and built the fort in its south-west corner. [n37; Hull vol. 1 pp. 4–5] [n04; Catholic Encyclopedia, “Bassein”]

Diogo do Couto, who completed Barros’s Década for 1534, prints the treaty as a list of articles and tells the possession as Correia does, with details of his own. [n83] The Sultan gave the city “with all its lands, both mainland and islands and seas, with all its jurisdiction, mero e misto império”; the interpreters at the table were “Marcos Fernandes and Coje Peredi, a Persian man”; and one of Bahadur’s own five articles, beyond the 5,000 larins for the mosques, was that 200 pardaos a year from Baçaim’s rents would still be paid to the lascarins of two border fortresses, “Aceira and Coeja”, which lay between Baçaim’s lands and those of the Rajputs. [n83 §1] On the day of possession the envoy had the peace proclaimed “through all the tanadarias” and ordered the tanadars and pateis to give obedience; the Governor chose a site for the fortress “a little away from the water, because the beach was all loose sand”, and had stone cut “on the other side of the river, where there was a very beautiful quarry and a very good watering-place”. Couto dates the first stone to 20 January 1535, St Sebastian’s day, with the Governor and the gentlemen “dressed very richly” and the fortress named for the saint; the tanadars then came with their pateis and renters and the foros, and the Governor “gave robes and rich cabaias” to all of them. [n83 §2] This is the date Paulo da Trindade gives (Chapter 34) and it conflicts with Correia’s March 1536 and Danvers’s end of March 1536; one possible reconciliation is that 20 January 1535 was the day the Portuguese entered and began the camp, and the March 1536 ceremony the formal laying of the stone, but no source says so. [n83]

Gaspar Correia, who was in India at the time, tells how the island changed hands. [n80] The Governor, off Diu in December 1534, accepted from Bahadur’s envoy “the island of Baçaim … with the lands and its revenues”, but “would take nothing except with the Sultan’s seal”. He chose officers before the seal arrived: Gaspar Paes as factor, two scribes and a tanadar-mor, expecting a revenue “of more than a hundred thousand gold pardaos a year”. When the envoy returned, he sounded a trumpet and “all the tanadars of the lands came to Baçaim”; he showed them the seal, and “each put in the factor’s hand a little sprig of sweet-smelling herb, or flowers, as a sign of obedience”. Martim Afonso de Sousa built the factory on the landing place beside the creek, with a yard filled up into a platform, and a bazaar grew with “Canarese shopkeepers”, “because the people of the land found in ours more generosity than in the Moors”. [n80 §2] Garcia de Orta, newly arrived from Portugal, was there in December 1534 when the cession was signed, according to Ficalho’s note on the interpreters present. [n80 §6]

In 1535, when Humayun’s Mughals were in Gujarat, Garcia de Sá was sent to Baçaim with four hundred men. When the Mughal advance was reported, António Galvão argued against abandoning the place, and “the people of the land crossed the river to the other side, towards Taná, who were no more than women and children and family”. The Mughals, “knowing that in Baçaim there were only farmers”, turned back, and the villagers came home “speaking great praises of ours”. [n80 §3] The foundation in March 1536 followed Correia’s account: a Mass of São Sebastião, the Governor digging with a hoe, the first stone laid with gold coins beneath it, and the captaincy given to Garcia de Sá with six months’ pay in advance; Faria y Sousa adds, writing in Spanish a century later, that Galvão was the man honoured to lay the stone. [n80 §4–§5]

There is a small human story about the cornerstone. When Gujarati raiders threatened the new post, its commandant wanted to abandon it. A man named António Galvão persuaded him to hold. Nuno da Cunha honoured Galvão by asking him to lay the first stone of the fort. [n25; TG-XIV] [n04; Vasai Fort, Wikipedia]

The first captain was Garcia de Sá, the governor’s brother-in-law. [n18; Wikipedia: Fort Vasai] On a bastion near the citadel, an inscription carved in his time survived to be read in the 1870s. Gerson da Cunha found it with the help of J. H. Littlewood, who then leased the fort’s interior:

HO PRIMEIRO CAPITAM QUE EDIFICOU ESTA FORTALEZA FOI GARCIA DE SA POR MANDADO DO GOVERNADOR NUNO DA CUNHA ERA DE 1536

“The first Captain who built this fortress was Garcia de Sá, by command of the Governor Nuno da Cunha, in the year 1536.” [n32; DC 217; BG 39] [n15; Thana Gaz. XIV]

It is the oldest dated stone in the fort. A hundred years later, in 1634, the Portuguese chronicler Bocarro described the same spot as a small round bastion by the pillory yard, by then unmanned, and as the Moors’ former residence. [n32; DC 217; BG 39] If Bocarro was right, the first Portuguese work at Baçaim was built on or beside the last Gujarati one, a captain’s tower raised on a sultan’s foundations.

Garcia de Sá held the captaincy from 1536 to 1548, according to his Wikipedia biography, though da Cunha’s list of captains, damaged in the printing and the scan, gives the order more confusingly. He was a man who took his post seriously enough to strike his own coins: he minted copper money at Bassein, the king ordered his arrest, and he was cleared. He went on to become governor of all Portuguese India from 6 June 1548 until his death on 13 June 1549. [n25; Wikipedia: Garcia de Sá] The first captain of Baçaim died the ruler of the whole Estado da Índia. The tenure of 1536 to 1548 cannot be right as the Wikipedia biography gives it: Barros has António da Silveira replace him in January 1537 on the King’s order, and Couto has the Ouvidor Geral suspend him and send him to Goa in 1537 or 1538 before the Governor kept him in India (notes 95 and 93); da Cunha’s list has him again later. [n95; n93]

Correia’s account of 1533 (chapter 6) gives the date of the city’s capture as 20 January, St Sebastian’s day, and says that in March 1536 the Mass of São Sebastião was said “because on his day he had been given victory there”. [n102 §4; n80] Couto puts the first stone and the naming of the fortress on 20 January 1535, but the saint’s day he names is the same one. The simplest reading is that the day the fortress’s saint was chosen was the anniversary of the 1533 victory, which the Portuguese kept as the day of Baçaim, and that Couto attached it to the foundation; the sources still do not settle which year the first stone was laid. [n83; n102]

Diogo do Couto, the Crown’s chronicler at Goa, tells how the villages were fought over in 1540. [n82] After Viceroy Dom Garcia de Noronha died in April, a captain of Bahadur’s called “Bramaluco”, who had held the lands before the cession and “lived here until now very aggrieved”, came down from Damão in the monsoon with “five thousand foot and three hundred horse”, found the villages “guarded by no one”, and proclaimed safe-conducts to the farmers “so that they could cultivate their lands without receiving injury, they bringing him the ordinary foros”; many came, and “others, who did not wish to obey, withdrew to Baçaim”. The captain, Rui Lourenço de Távora, and the gentlemen wintering there, among them Dom Luís de Ataíde, the future Count of Atouguia, mustered “near six hundred Portuguese, among them fifty horse”, with “some nayques with three or four hundred piães of the land”. They found the enemy at a village called “Bulão, or Baylão”, two leagues inland, and were nearly overwhelmed while at breakfast under a grove; the musketry decided it, and next day the Moors had gone to “the river of Antora”. Rui Lourenço “ran all the lands, reducing the farmers to the service of the King of Portugal” and placed stockades at the passes, guarded by piães of the land. [n82 §1] Then Ataíde took ten ships up the Agashi river, and the Portuguese burnt the town and launched “a very beautiful” unfinished galleon that the Moors had on the slip “to send to Mecca”, with windlasses and “a great number of servants of the villages to turn the ship over”; renamed the Zambuco, it later “made many voyages to the Kingdom”. [n82 §2]

Gerson da Cunha’s list of the captains of Bassein, compiled from the Portuguese records and printed in a footnote, is the backbone of the city’s early government. For the first two decades it runs, with three dates lost to the type: Garcia de Sá in 1535, the first captain; Rui Vaz Pereira; António da Silveira; Manuel de Macedo in 1537; Rui Lourenço de Távora; Garcia de Sá again; Dom Francisco de Menezes in 1541; Dom Jerónimo de Menezes, nicknamed “o Bacalhau” (“the Codfish”), in 1545; Jorge Cabral in 1548; Francisco Barreto in 1549; and Francisco de Sá in 1554. [n32; DC 219 n.] Jorge Cabral, who came to India in 1525, followed Garcia de Sá both at Bassein and then as governor in 1549–50; the sources disagree on whether he still held Bassein at the time. [n25; Wikipedia: Jorge Cabral] [n04; Vasai Fort, Wikipedia]

After Francisco de Sá, da Cunha admits, “follows a long gap, which cannot be easily filled up”. [n32; DC 219 n.] The next captain he can name is Nuno Vaz de Castelo Branco in 1611. For the second half of the sixteenth century, the decades of the great baptisms, the men who governed the city are lost from the record that this book could reach. The captaincy was a three-year post, drawn, Dr John Fryer would later observe, from certain families, and in about 1550 it paid 600,000 réis a year. [n32; BG 30 n.8; DC 218–219] We know the salary of the office better than the names of most of the men who held it.

Walls around a town (1536–1554)

The first fortress of 1536 was a fort, not a walled city. The decisive change came in the following eighteen years, as the settlement grew round the fort and the walls grew round the settlement.

On 22 February 1554 a bastion was completed on the circuit, dedicated to São Sebastião, St Sebastian. Its inscription stone, which by 1882 lay neglected near the land gateway, named the king, João III; the viceroy, Dom Afonso de Noronha; and the captain, Francisco de Sá. [n32; BG 42–43] Modern summaries describe this as the date when “a new fortress was finished, enclosing a whole town”. [n04; Vasai Fort, Wikipedia] The fortress came to be called the Fortaleza de São Sebastião, after this bastion or its saint. [n04; Vasai Fort, Wikipedia] Wikipedia calls the bastion “Porta Pia”; neither the Gazetteer nor da Cunha uses that name. [n32]

Keep the date and the name in mind. In May 1739 the Maratha mines would bring down the tower of São Sebastião, and the city would fall through the breach. The bastion that closed the circle in 1554 was the one that opened it 185 years later. [n32; BG 42–43; DC 148]

How the walls looked in their final form belongs to the next chapter, because the circuit that survives was largely rebuilt after 1590. But the shape of the place was set in these years: a city on the sea face of the creek, with the citadel in its south-west corner, and the seaward half of the enclosure built up while the landward third stayed open. Da Cunha offered three explanations for that empty third: a clear field of fire, a rice field to feed a besieged garrison, or later depopulation by plague. [n32; DC 213; BG 38]

The walls needed stone, and the coast had a quarry ready-made in its own past. Sopara’s temples and the stone banks of its lakes, the Gazetteer says, had partly survived earlier troubles. “Enough were spared, or repaired, to excite the admiration” of the first Portuguese visitors in 1530–40. Then, in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, they were stripped, and the stones were used for Portuguese churches and forts and for “the great walls… of Bassein”. [n37; Gaz. XIV p. 323] Whether the very first courses of 1536 used Sopara stone the notes do not say. But the old city, six miles to the north, became the new city’s raw material. The blocks that had once lined a Shilahara tank or framed a temple door were carried down the creeks to be stacked into bastions named for Christian saints.

The local stone was good. Early in the next century Pyrard de Laval would call it “as fine and hard as granite”, and it was shipped south to raise the churches and palaces of Goa. [n32; BG 30–31] Federici, who passed about 1565, said that from Basain and its many towns “nothing is drawn but rice, wheat, and much timber”; the stone trade is not in his sentence. [n15; Hobson-Jobson, “Bassein”]

Before the winter of 1537, Couto gives one more first-year fact. In March 1535, with “the fortress already high enough to be defended, garrisoned with artillery”, and its storehouses nearly finished “with many provisions and munitions”, Nuno da Cunha was about to choose a captain when António da Silveira arrived from Hormuz, “having finished serving that fortress”, and the Governor “gave him the captaincy of Baçaim, and ordered eight hundred men to remain with him”. [n101 §3] In January he had ordered “new tombos” to be made for the district’s tanadars and pateis, gave them robes, and ordered “that the leases should run as they were, without innovating anything in them”: the Portuguese began by registering the Gujarat rent system and leaving it alone. [n101 §3] The same bar had been the Portuguese fleet’s last sheltered anchorage before Gujarat long before: in the winter of 1530–31 Nuno da Cunha’s two hundred sail “went to Baçaim, from where he crossed to the other coast” and came in sight of Bet Dwarka in three days, and in December 1534 he waited at the bar for his spies’ news of Bahadur’s army and of Malik Tokan at Diu. [n101 §1–§2]

The island fortified

Baçaim was the heart, but the Portuguese fortified the whole island, because its weak side was not the sea. It was the creek.

At Sopara the old city became, under the Portuguese, “a fortified post with four wooden stockades”. [n37; Gaz. XIV p. 322] At Achola, a mile and a half north of Manikpur, visitors in 1882 were sent to see “its Portuguese fort”. [n12; Gaz 1882] At Gokirva (Gokhivare), on the road to Tungar hill, the Gazetteer found “a shrine of Mahadev, a pond, and the remains of an old Portuguese stockade”, and nearby a ruined Portuguese tower built against Koli raids from Jawhar. [n10; Gazetteer 1882, p. 367] [n12; Gaz 1882] At Virar there were the remains of a Portuguese church and well south of where the station now stands, and a Portuguese tower or fortified mansion on a knoll about a mile further south. [n12; Gaz 1882, p. 382] Near Bhatela pond, east of Sopara, the remains of a Portuguese jetty and customs house are reported, and a Portuguese landing place has been found east of Gass. [n02; Shah 2020; Wikipedia: Sopara] [n31; Wikipedia: Nallasopara]

None of these posts carries a sixteenth-century date in the sources this book could read. The 1728 report that complained about the watch-towers of cocoa-palm stems and the unprotected bridge at Gokirva belongs to the last years of Portuguese rule. [n12; Gaz 1882] What the scattered ruins show is the logic of defence. The danger came from the hills to the east, where the Koli chiefs of Jawhar raided, and from the mainland across the narrow backwater, where an enemy could ford at low tide. The Portuguese ringed the garden island with stockades and towers and treated the creek as a moat.

At the island’s northern tip, guarding the mouth of the Vaitarna, stood Arnala. “About 1530”, the Gazetteer says, “the Portuguese found Arnala a strong fort in the hands of the Gujarat Musalmans with domes and Saracenic arches. These were destroyed by the Portuguese and the place made over to a Portuguese gentleman of Bassein, who built an oblong square about 700 feet with a round tower.” [n37; Gaz. XIV pp. 10–11] The Portuguese called the island the Ilha das Vacas, the Island of Cows. [n37; Gaz. XIV p. 10 note] A Muslim fort of domes and arches was knocked down and replaced by a gentleman’s square tower: on a small scale, the same exchange that was happening at Bassein.

The sea and the pass (1534–1600)

Why did Portugal want this place? Not for its rice. The cartaz clause made Bassein a gate through which the wealth of Gujarat had to pass. Every Gujarati ship sailing for the Red Sea had to stop there, obtain a cartaz, a Portuguese pass, and pay duty on its return. [n04; Military history of Bassein, Wikipedia] The province was “a revenue machine and not only a fort”, as one of this book’s research notes puts it. [n04; Inferences]

A cartaz was a licence to exist at sea. A ship without one could be seized. The treaty also forbade Gujarat to build warships, so the sultans could not protect their own merchants. The ships that had once traded freely from Cambay to Aden now had to carry a paper signed by a Portuguese official, and Bassein was one of the places where that paper was issued and paid for.

The horse trade was regulated by the same treaty. [n04; Military history of Bassein, Wikipedia] The modern summaries do not say how; the treaty’s own words, which this chapter returns to below, are more exact. Horses had moved through this coast for centuries: an eighth-century Jain romance has a merchant from Taxila bringing horses to Sopara, and Marco Polo, around 1290, described a “kingdom of Tana” whose king was in league with corsairs who brought him captured horses. [n15; LiveHistoryIndia] [n03; TG-XIV p. 356] Under the Portuguese the trade in war-horses from Arabia and Persia to the Indian kingdoms was a prize to be controlled, because whoever controlled the horses controlled the cavalry of the Deccan. The treaty fixed where the horses were to be sold, as we shall see. But no figures, no prices and no named horse-dealers survive in the sources this book could read. This is a gap worth stating plainly: one of the commercial reasons for the treaty is almost invisible in the local record.

Other goods are better attested. Cesare de’ Federici, who travelled the coast between 1563 and 1585, wrote around 1565 that after Daman “comes Basain with many towns… from which nothing is drawn but rice, wheat, and much timber”, and he thought it “a filthy place compared with Daman”. [n15; Hobson-Jobson; Thana Gaz. XIV p. 30] In 1583 the English traveller Ralph Fitch named it one of the places of most trade in corn and rice. [n15; Thana Gaz. XIV pp. 29–30] And in 1586 Abul Fazl wrote in the Ain-i-Akbari of “Bussy” as an emporium like Daman, Sanjan, Mahim and Tarapur, “all five of them being in the possession of the Europeans through the negligence of the Moghal officers”. [n15; Thana Gaz. XIV pp. 29–30] That last sentence is the view from the other side. To the chronicler of the empire that now held Gujarat, Bassein was a lost Indian port, given away by careless officials.

The timber came from the forests of the hills behind the coast, and it fed shipyards. Agashi, the Gazetteer says, was “probably an old timber trade and ship-building centre”, which around 1530 built ships “as good as Portuguese ships, able to make the voyage to Europe”. (The printed text reads “1580”; one note takes this as a slip for 1530.) [n11; Gaz. 1882 pp. 1–2] [n15; Thana Gaz. XIV p. 1] A modern study cited by the archaeologist Shah records that Agashi built ships of up to 500 tons for both Marathas and Portuguese. [n02; Shah 2020, citing Arunachalam 2002] In 1570 “Agazim” appears in Portuguese listings as a port trading with Gujarat. [n11; Gaz. 1882 pp. 1–2] [n37; Gaz. XIV pp. 1, 402]

Who were the traders? Here the record thins. The notes name communities more often than individuals. The Naitas of Bassein were rich traders when the Portuguese arrived, according to Garcia d’Orta. [n37; Gaz. XIII-I pp. 216–217] Masudi’s tenth-century remark that the Lar language of south Gujarat was spoken in the coast towns as far as Chaul suggests that Lad Vani merchants from Gujarat had long settled along this coast to trade, although the families the Gazetteer knew in 1882 mostly came later, about 1760. [n35; Gazetteer XIII-1, pp. 112–113] The Malabar boats with areca and coconuts that Barbosa saw in 1514 presumably kept coming. [n37; Gaz. XIV p. 28] Gujarati shipowners now came because they had to.

A few individuals surface. Garcia d’Orta himself, the Portuguese physician, is commonly said to have been granted a lease of Bombay, or part of it, by Viceroy Pedro Mascarenhas in 1554; the sources disagree on how much of the island the lease covered. Bombay was then a minor dependency of Bassein, administered within the Bassein province and, in church matters, under the Vigário da Vara at Bassein. [n15; LiveHistoryIndia, “Garcia da Orta”; Wikipedia: Bom Bahia] For 127 years, from 1534 until it passed to England in 1661 as part of Catherine of Braganza’s dowry, the island that would become Mumbai was an outlying piece of Baçaim. [n04; Inferences; Vasai Fort, Wikipedia]

And at Sandor, three miles north of Bassein, an inscribed stone dated Hijra 966, that is AD 1558, mentions “Nayak Laro, lord of the western ocean”. [n37; Gaz. XIV p. 402] Who Nayak Laro was, the Gazetteer does not say. But the stone is remarkable for its date alone. Twenty-four years into Portuguese rule, someone in a village beside the new Christian city was still carving an inscription dated by the Muslim calendar and honouring a man with a sea-lord’s title. The old maritime world of the Gujarat coast had not vanished overnight.

The sea gave other things than trade. It gave fish, which the Kolis caught and which the Portuguese taxed, as the sultans had. [n03; TG-II p. 451] It gave salt, though the notes find no Portuguese salt figures for Bassein. [n17; n21; Gaps] And it gave defence: the creek-moat, the island fort of Arnala at the Vaitarna’s mouth, and the gunboats that would later be counted in the fort’s arsenal. [n32; BG 30] For the people of the island, the sea had always been their road to Surat, Cambay and the Malabar coast. Now it was also the road by which the friars arrived, and by which the prisoners of the Inquisition would later be shipped south to Goa.

An anonymous report on the Estado da Índia written about 1565–68, which the Jesuit archive kept for Maffei’s history, gives an outsider’s view of Baçaim at this stage. [n79] The writer approved of the money spent “enclosing Baçaim and making it strong”, but thought it “cannot be enclosed nor attacked except by the Turks’ fleet”, because its neighbours were weak and the Sultan of Cambay had given it to Nuno da Cunha “not out of virtue or friendship” but because he “could not sustain it against the thieves of Sarzela, the Gujaratis and the Abyssinians”. He complained that the captains of Manorá, once worth 7,000 pardaos a year, now cost 3,000 of Baçaim’s revenue; that the Crown was bound to buy poor timber from the captains of Baçaim, who cut it in their own lands while the good wood went to private buyers; and that no ship of war should be built at Baçaim at all. The writer is unnamed (the editor guesses João da Fonseca, the Cochin treasury official). [n79 §1]

The first captains and the northern fleet (1535–1560)

The fortress was built by its captains, and the captains were a small, quarrelsome and very exposed company. Their sequence is hard to fix: Barros, Correia, Couto and Castanheda order the first captains differently, and the list that Gerson da Cunha printed in 1876 has lost several dates. The sections that follow set out what each source says, in the order of events, from the first winter at the bar of Baçaim to the recovery of Damão.

A poor captain and a Mughal pretender, 1537

Couto’s Da Ásia adds the winter of 1537 to the story of the first captain. [n93] The Governor, Nuno da Cunha, “was all the month of January at Baçaim, doing some business”, while his messenger Diogo de Mesquita watched Bahadur Shah’s court; when the news was bad he sailed for Diu, taking “António da Silveira, his brother-in-law, Captain of Baçaim”. Bahadur was killed there in February. A Mughal noble, Mir Muhammad Zaman, who claimed to be “of the ancient blood of the Great Tamerlane”, then proclaimed himself king of Gujarat and sent an ambassador to the Governor. The treaty that followed would have given the Portuguese the port of Mangrol and “the city of Damão with all its tanadarias and villages that it had up to the lands of Baçaim, as they were before of the state of Cambay”. In return the Portuguese were to let his coins be struck in his name and his name be read in the mosques. Couto does not say that the pretender kept any of it, and Damão would not be Portuguese for another twenty years. What the clause does show is how the district was drawn in 1537: Damão’s villages ran “up to the lands of Baçaim”, and the two were neighbours. Barros, who says he saw the document “signed and sealed”, gives it differently: the coast “from Mangalor to the island of Beth” and “the town of Damão in the gulf of Cambay up to Baçaim, with all the lands and parganas”, a rule that no Portuguese might go inland “without licence of the Governor of India, or of the Captain of Diu or Baçaim”, and fifty thousand gold pardaos paid down for the soldiers’ pay. [n95 §1] When Bahadur’s general Luchan was asked for peace at Nawanagar he demanded the same strip back, “from Mangalor to Diu, and from Damão to Baçaim, as Mir Zaman had given”, and then starved Diu of the meat and fruit that “came from Chaul and Baçaim” until the monsoon stopped even that: a hen cost ten tangas. [n95 §2] In the autumn a fusta of gentlemen who meant to visit friends at Baçaim was driven into Damão and ambushed on the river; the tanadar of Damão wrote to excuse himself to Manuel de Macedo, Captain of Baçaim, and Macedo sent a bargantim with fifty men who came back without the captives, “already sent to the Court”. [n95 §4]

João de Barros, who wrote nearer the events, gives a different order. [n95 §3] In January 1537 Nuno da Cunha found at Baçaim António da Silveira, his brother-in-law, whom “a few days earlier” he had put in Garcia de Sá’s place because the King had ordered Garcia to Portugal “on false information”. Barros defends Garcia de Sá as a gentleman “of great and honoured services, and much goodness and liberality”, whom “more truthful men” saved from disgrace, and says that “he stayed in India, and afterwards governed it”. The Governor stayed five days at Baçaim “provisioning the Armada”, received a Cambay captain with seventeen fustas who asked “very dissemblingly” what he was doing, and on 6 February sailed with Silveira for Diu, leaving Ruy Vaz Pereira in command; after Bahadur’s death Manuel de Macedo was sent as captain. Correia has Garcia de Sá keep the post under protest, “as a prisoner who served perforce” and with no wage, and by the winter of 1537–38 “had already raised the whole fortress to the height of the battlements”, so that when the Governor called on his way to Diu he “found all peaceful”. [n80 §6] Couto, writing later, says that in March 1537 Nuno da Cunha “went to take Baçaim, where he left Garcia de Sá as Captain, who had just finished being Captain of Malacca, having come from it very poor”, and sailed for Goa after “providing Baçaim and Chaul with munitions and food”. Garcia de Sá’s poverty then became a matter of state. The King had been told that at Malacca he had struck coin without licence, and ordered him sent to Goa and his goods sequestered. The Governor, thinking him “an old gentleman of great merit”, ordered a fresh inquiry. His Ouvidor Geral, Dr Pero Fernandes, came to Baçaim, suspended the captain from the fortress, and took the inventory. It listed “cauldrons, pots, basins, knives, forks, bowls, tablecloths”, his arms and bed, and “four slaves of his service”. The Governor copied it three times, sent it to the King, and kept Garcia de Sá in India. The King thanked him and wrote the old captain “honourable letters”. In 1548 Garcia de Sá would be Governor of India himself. [n93 §3]

The captains who followed arrived with the great fleet of 1538: Dom Francisco de Menezes, “one of the best-conditioned gentlemen of his time”; Luís Falcão; and Rui Lourenço de Távora, whom the Viceroy confirmed at Baçaim when his ship put in after the storm that followed the siege of Diu. During that siege, Couto says, the fortresses of Baçaim and Chaul “did not help with anything, because they dared not take anything from there, for they also feared the Turks”. The first captain’s creator, Nuno da Cunha, died on the voyage home in 1539, and Couto reports the last words a witness gave him: “Ungrateful country, you will not have my bones”, bitter at “ten years’ service as Governor of India, and at making in it three fortresses, Chaul, Baçaim and Diu”. [n93 §4]

Winterers, a duel and an arrest, 1539–44

Couto’s Década V gives Baçaim a social life in these years. [n94] The Viceroy Dom Garcia de Noronha, before his death in April 1540, “dispatched many gentlemen to winter in the fortresses, principally at Baçaim, where there went to be given a table” Fernão de Sousa de Távora, Fernão da Silva, Francisco de Sá “of the glasses”, Dom Luís de Taíde, António de Soutomaior and the cousins Dom Jorge and Dom Aleixo de Menezes. It was a gentleman’s custom to be the captain’s guest while the monsoon closed the sea; it is why Rui Lourenço de Távora could ask “the captains and gentlemen who wintered there” what to do when the Gujarat captain invaded the villages in 1540. When one of them, Fernão de Sousa de Távora, left in the monsoon for Goa in a river galliot that the captain had lent him, he reached Dabul, found “threats of winter”, and went on by land, sending the galliot back to Baçaim: “he wished rather to risk his person in going by land than his word”. Couto uses the story to say that the Portuguese of that time brought three things from India, in the words of a King of Cochin: “truth, broad swords and Portuguese gold coins”. [n94 §1]

The captaincy was a prize. In autumn 1540, when two gentlemen, Dom Francisco de Menezes and Dom Manuel de Lima, were both provided with Baçaim and the fortress “was vacant and delivered to the alcaide-mór”, the Governor settled it by making a gentleman of seventy, António de Lemos da Trofa, captain in Dom Francisco’s place while Dom Francisco went with the fleet to the Red Sea: “all the profits to be the said António’s”. [n94 §2] Rui Lourenço de Távora, the outgoing captain, had come to Goa to go to Portugal; a remark attributed to him, that “Baçaim was a small thing and was not for men like him”, was repeated to Dom Francisco, who challenged him. They fought “very well and came away friends”, Rui Lourenço with a cut above the temple and Dom Francisco with a thrust through the right arm, and neither would say what had happened. In Lisbon, Couto adds, when a lady at the Queen’s house stared at the scar, Rui Lourenço put his finger on it and said, “That is a gift from Lord Dom Francisco, which is the greatest honour I have.” [n94 §3]

The last of these stories is about the keys. In the winter of 1544 the Governor Martim Afonso de Sousa had sent a lawyer, a Veador da Fazenda, “with all powers over the treasury and justice” to winter at Baçaim, leaving the captain, Dom Manuel de Lima, “like a statue” in his own fortress. In early December, with the east winds blowing, the Governor anchored off the bar with a fleet of caravels and nine galleys, “and at once had houses taken on shore for his person, without any compliment to Dom Manuel”, instead of lodging in the King’s fortress “as all the Governors until then had done”. Lima met him on the beach “without making him the ceremony of handing over the keys, as was the custom in all the fortresses”, walked him to his lodging and left at the door, for on the way he had been told that the aunt who had brought him up had died. The Governor, “almost affronted”, had the Ouvidor Geral arrest him and hold him in a galleon. When the Governor learned of the death and sent Pêro de Faria to apologise, Lima refused to listen; shut in a cabin with the Governor he drew from his pocket a royal provision “giving him licence to go to Portugal, and ordering the Governor of India not to hinder him, even if there were a siege of the fortress or news of galleys”. “I go,” he said, “and I assure you of one thing, that in Portugal I will not make complaint of you to the King.” He went to Cochin and took ship for the Kingdom. [n94 §5] The Governor stayed four days at Baçaim, having four tables set on the shore to feed the soldiers, “because not even of his many friends did he trust”, and left in haste.

The hill forts of Karnala and Sangaçá, 1541–42

In the dry season of 1541–42 (Couto’s chapter comes just after his statement that “the year 1542 had begun”) the captain of Baçaim fought for two hill forts at the head of the Bombay creek. [n92] The Nizam Shah of Ahmadnagar, seeing Gujarat in disorder after Bahadur’s death, took Carnalá and Sangaçá, which stood “as two boundary-marks between the extremes of [the Gujarat] kingdom and the Nizam Shah’s” and had been given by Bahadur to two Muslim vassals who “ate many villages round their jurisdiction”. The vassals asked help of Dom Francisco de Menezes, who took 300 Portuguese and “some peães of the land”, with “all the people of the land under the banner of Pêro de Lemos, tanadar-mor of the lands of Baçaim”, stormed Carnalá with ladders and found Sangaçá abandoned, “without a sword-stroke”. The Muslims then made a gift of the forts to the King of Portugal in return for “some villages in the lands of Baçaim”. When the Nizam sent six thousand more against Sangaçá, a footman slipped out by night and walked to Baçaim; Dom Francisco “went to the beach and had the bell rung”, got 160 foot and 20 horse, “had the villages called out, and at once twelve hundred peães with their naiques came”, and in a battle at a ford killed five hundred of the enemy for some twenty of his own. [n92 §1–§2] Then the Governor, Dom Estêvão da Gama, who thought “five thousand gold pardaos of revenue each year, without any expense” worth more than the forts, ordered them given back to the Nizam. Dom Francisco protested that “as long as he was Captain of Baçaim he would not give them up”, and gave way only on a second order; the garrison marched out “with banners unfurled, drums and fifes, like men who went as victors”. [n92 §3] In May 1542 the new Governor Martim Afonso de Sousa’s own ship, the Santiago, ran aground “in the river of the Goats, on the island of Salsete of Baçaim”; Dom Francisco rescued the money, guns and gear and kept the crew wintering at Baçaim at his own expense. [n92 §4]

The northern relay port, 1547–1554

In the years after the second siege of Diu, Baçaim was the place from which the Governors of India ran the north. [n93] In the winter of 1546–47 (the year is inferred from the order of Couto’s books) Dom João de Castro was there when an envoy came from Aden: Diogo Correia, with “the brother-in-law of the King of Camphar”, landed at Baçaim in January and, learning that “the Governor was at Baçaim”, went to the city. Castro, “much cheered” to hear that Aden was in the King’s hands, had the guest “received very well”, sent for his son Dom Álvaro and told him to make ready for the Arabian coast; he himself left for Goa “already in April”, having “given despatch to all the matters of the northern fortresses”. In autumn 1547 he was back, to have “the war on Cambay carried on”. An ambassador of the Sultan of Gujarat landed at the watering place in three light ships, the Armada was dressed in flags, and the peace was “proclaimed through Baçaim and Diu, swearing it with great solemnity”. [n93 §5–6]

The city was also asked for ships and men. In 1550, when the Governor Jorge Cabral feared the Turks, he wrote to Chaul and Baçaim and they answered that they were “all ready to sacrifice their lives for the service of God, the King and the defence of his Estate”. In 1552 the news of the Turkish fleet at Hormuz reached India by way of Baçaim. Estêvão Gomes, the factor of Calayate, crossed the Gulf “until he saw the land of Baçaim”, and going in, “gave the message to the city”, where “some persons began to make ready to go to the relief of Hormuz”. The Viceroy Dom Afonso de Noronha came to Baçaim and stayed “nearly a month”, naming Francisco Barreto, who had “just ceased” to be its captain, to govern Cochin, and finding Dom Diogo de Almeida for Diu. The captain at Baçaim was now Francisco de Sá de Menezes, which fits the Francisco de Sá who is named on the bastion of São Sebastião in February 1554. [n93 §7]

In September 1554, after the Portuguese captured Turkish galleys off Muscat, two more ran for the Gujarat coast and were driven ashore, one at Damão and one at Dahanu, where they broke up. Francisco de Sá “fitted out ten or twelve vessels, embarked himself, and went to lie off” the bar of Surat, and the captain of Chaul did the same. A few days later, on 23 September, Dom Pedro Mascarenhas reached Goa as Viceroy. [n93 §8]

The Viceroy Dom Pedro Mascarenhas, who died in June 1555, left Baçaim a garrison figure. In Couto’s list of his ordinances he ordered “six hundred soldiers” for Hormuz, “at Baçaim another six hundred, for the security of that fortress, because of the alterations in Cambay”, and “at Chaul a hundred”. [n96 §2] The Governor who followed him, Francisco Barreto, faced the loss of the Goa fleet in a fire (Couto suspected the Idalcan’s agents) and asked the northern cities for help: he “dispatched couriers to the fortresses of the North, Chaul and Baçaim, and wrote to those Aldermen and people that they should help him with something toward the reform of the Armada, which they granted him with great pleasure”, and ordered “the Captains and Factors of those fortresses that out of their revenues they should at once arm in each of them two galleons and two caravels”. [n96 §4] When he went north himself, he “passed to Baçaim, where he landed, and was given the greatest reception that could be, because he had been Captain there and was well liked in it”. [n96 §5] One of the city’s own officers had left it for another service: Brother Fulgêncio Freire, “who had been Factor of Baçaim”, sailed from Goa in February 1555 as companion of the Jesuit Provincial, Master Gonçalo, on the mission to the Emperor of Ethiopia. [n96 §3] And when the Nizam Shah’s captain fortified the Morro opposite Chaul about 1560, two “great old galleys that came from Baçaim, loaded with timber” arrived at the bar and anchored in battery against it, while the settlers of Chaul sent their wives and children to Baçaim and Goa. [n96 §6]

Two stories from Couto’s later volumes belong with these. In 1565 Mughal horsemen entered the lands of Damão, and “the messages were given at Baçaim and Chaul”, where the captain of Chaul got “six or seven ships with two hundred men who begged to be taken”. In 1567 the Viceroy’s circular to “the cities of Chaul, Baçaim, Damão and Diu” asked each to come to the Mangalor expedition “with what it could”, and Couto’s list of the fleet shows men named by the place they lived: Dom João de Menezes de Baçaim, António de Mello de Baçaim, João Álvares Soares de Baçaim, Cristóvão de Sousa de Baçaim, the last “son of António de Sousa, who was Captain of Baçaim”, killed in the assault. [n93 §9] The suffix shows that by the 1560s the city had enough gentlemen and married soldiers for a second name to be useful.

When the Nizam Shah besieged Chaul in 1571 the Dean of Goa, Padre Braz Dias, once vicar of Chaul, wrote to the Viceroy against giving it up. If Chaul fell, he said, the Nizam “would go straight to Baçaim” and “pass to the island of Salsete”, taking from the Estate “more than five thousand cruzados of revenue and the vassals more than five hundred thousand”, and “seeing himself lord of Baçaim and its lands, which God forbid, he would gather in them more than three hundred large and small vessels, in which he would embark in that beautiful river of Bombaim, and in four days enter by the bar of Mormugão and land thirty thousand men on the beach of Old Goa”. It was an argument to keep Chaul, not a forecast, and the figures are the Dean’s own. But it is the clearest sentence in the chronicles on what Baçaim, Salsete and the river of Bombay were worth to the men who ruled from Goa. [n93 §9]

How Asserim and Manorá were bought, 1556

A Franciscan who knew the hill described it in the 1630s. [n108 §1] Fr Paulo da Trindade writes that Asserim “lies between the fortresses of Manorá and Virampur”, and that the way up is “the most dreadful and risky that can be imagined”: a ledge three feet wide with a precipice of “more than half a league” at the side, which the inhabitants ran in wooden clogs, and for the visits of two archbishops a handrail of ropes and stakes was made. Inside two trap-doors, each with a Portuguese porter, lies a plateau “so capacious that the Procession of the Passion is made in it in Lent, and the city of Baçaim or Chaul could well fit in it”, with a church and a vicar’s house, the captain’s house “despatched by His Majesty”, the baker’s, a storehouse for two years’ grain, and a ball-game court. The garrison was “almost a hundred”, “most of them Portuguese, and many married who have their wives with them”; the soldiers lived at about ten “passes”, seven or eight to a house, each with a cot, a blanket, a dried fish hung over it and a bamboo tube of oil, and many ate their quarter in advance on credit from the captain, “who sells everything”. The defence was boulders: “great galgas are always poised, each of which costs five pardaos in its place”, rolled to impress a visitor with a noise “as if a great piece of artillery were fired”. There was a Pass of the Formosa where “no man named João can live”, because an enchanted Moorish woman walks in it. A mother’s counsel, Paulo says, brought the hill to the Portuguese: the last Hindu lord’s two sons began to cut it in half, and she persuaded them to sell the whole instead. Until 1601 a Franciscan from the convent at Baçaim went up each Easter octave “not by obligation but by pure charity”, blessing a cistern that is still called Holy Water; in 1601 Archbishop Dom Aleixo posted the first parish priest, Fr Nuno Barreto. [n108 §1]

The hill forts that closed Baçaim’s eastern border were not stormed. Couto tells how Governor Francisco Barreto, finding Asserim “so impregnable by nature that it was held impossible to take by force”, used Coje Mahamede, “an old Moor from the time of Sultan Bahadur, who in Nuno da Cunha’s time had been present at the handing over of the city of Baçaim, and stayed living in it rich and honoured”. [n84 §4] He went to Asserim “as if visiting” its captain, a Gentile called Condixá, “a great friend”, and with the hill’s Hindu treasury officer, Calegi, agreed a price of 6,500 pardaos, raised by selling the King’s plate and lent on the credit of the factor of Baçaim, Duarte do Soveral. Calegi came to Baçaim and stayed as hostage until Condixá handed the hill over to António Moniz Barreto. [n84 §4] The plan was made at a council at Baçaim itself, which voted to put off taking Damão and first to “take the fortresses of Assari and Manorá … to secure the lands of Baçaim, because the rebels who held them made many incursions into the lands and villages of Baçaim”, and because the villages of the forts’ jurisdiction “were very large and very important to the King, and would feed many men”; the Governor, who had been captain there, “was given the greatest reception that could be”. [n84 §5] The hill is described in some detail: a league of steep climb, two ways up (one wide enough for a single man, and the “Pass of the Cows”, by which the garrison’s cattle were hauled up with ropes), twelve guard stations with boulders tied by ropes to roll on attackers, torches of a wood “that burns in wind and rain”, a flat summit with a church and a cistern, and at the foot a timber stockade with a naique, a hundred peães and a Portuguese field-captain. Barreto left sixty soldiers and “a naique with two hundred peães”, and “had proclamations thrown through the villages that all the farmers should cultivate and plough their villages and answer with the foros to the King of Portugal, as they did to the King of Cambay, by the same foraes and customs, without innovating anything except in their favour”. Manorá, whose Turkish captain Agader “did not dare to wait”, was then found empty, and Jorge Manhães was put there with 120 soldiers. Seven hundred men were left at Baçaim, fed at the tables of three named captains. [n84 §4]

Couto adds the district as it then stood: east to Asserim and Manorá, “eight leagues, in which there are very fertile villages and of great rents”; north to the river of Agashi; south to Bombay and Karanja; and “Taná”, a colony of “almost sixty Portuguese who in that island have their villages, which are very profitable”. [n84 §3] Barros adds a detail to the 1535 scare: when the Portuguese began to pack, “the people of the land and the foreign merchants who resided there gave themselves up for lost, and all was lamentations and cries of women and children”, and afterwards “joined with Garcia de Sá and helped him” to build the stockade. [n84 §1]

The Pasagis, the chouto and the first Christian river, 1559

In 1559 Viceroy Dom Constantino used Baçaim as the base for retaking Damão, and Couto’s account of it names the Hindu village-lords beside the city. [n91] The new captain, Dom Pedro de Almeida, rode out with 150 horse “and in his company some Gentiles called the Pasagis, who lived in some villages adjoining Baçaim, and who, when the King of Cambay gave those lands to Governor Nuno da Cunha, offered themselves to him as vassals, and he gave them a letter of this with the obligation that they would come with horse whenever necessary for the defence of the lands of the King of Portugal, which they always fulfilled very well”: twelve or fifteen horse and many foot servants with field tents. [n91 §1] These are the “Passagis” of Sabajo whom Couto names again in 1570 (below). Crossing “the river that divides the lands of Baçaim from those of Damão”, a Franciscan went ahead with a crucifix, which fell into the stream; the friar said, “Joy, Sir, that these waters are now sanctified so that in them all this heathendom may be baptised”, and Couto adds that “until then from that river onward there was no Christianity”, but that there were now “more than thirty thousand Christians in all those lands”. [n91 §2] At Manorá a brother of the Koli king, “who lived in that hinterland of Baçaim in very closed woods and hills”, came with ten or twelve horse and a hundred foot to offer himself. [n91 §2]

Couto stops to explain why the Viceroy had to pay the King of Sarzeta: “more than four hundred years ago great armies of Rajputs came down” and the farmers agreed to be left to plough in peace “paying one quarter of the crop: in their language this foro is called Chautá, and we corruptly call it Choutos”. The Sultans confirmed it to the Sarzeta kings by “a very old grant”, and when Bahadur gave Baçaim’s lands to Nuno da Cunha it was “with the condition that the King of Sarzeta should go on eating the lands given him with their foros”. The Viceroy sent the king safe-conducts to collect it. [n91 §3] He also had the district heads bring “the ancient foraes of the lands and villages” so that rents could be collected, and since few Portuguese would lease villages, leased them to Abyssinian Christians bound to keep musketeers. [n91 §4] Couto’s aside on Francisco Barreto: he “took out of the fortress of Baçaim (of which he was Captain) eighty thousand pardaos” and spent them on soldiers. [n91 §5]

Dom Pedro de Almeida’s captaincy ended badly. [n99] In September 1560, when Viceroy Dom Constantino sailed for Jaffna, “ten or twelve ships from Chaul and Baçaim arrived to accompany him”, among them Dom Pedro, “who left the fortress in the care of Manuel da Veiga, Factor and Alcaide-mór”, and his brother Dom Luís. The Viceroy, “knowing that Dom Pedro de Almeida had come there and had left his fortress (besides other grudges that were past)”, “would not have him”: he had the Ouvidor Geral take Dom Pedro’s pledge and sent him to be held in one of the palaces of the island of Goa, and gave the ships to other captains. [n99 §2] The captain Dom Pedro replaced, António Moniz Barreto, left something else: Couto says that the São Martinho, one of six ships that reached Goa from Portugal in September 1562 under Dom Jorge Manuel, “was made at Baçaim by António Moniz Barreto, when he was Captain of that fortress”. [n99 §4]

The Court of the North takes shape

Over these decades Baçaim grew into the capital of a province. In the seventeenth century it would be called “a Corte do Norte”, the Court of the North, the resort of fidalgos and rich merchants, “the second-most important centre after Velha Goa”. [n04; Vasai Fort, Wikipedia] Its province ran roughly a hundred kilometres along the coast between Daman and Chaul and thirty to fifty kilometres deep, though that extent is marked “citation needed”. [n04; Vasai Fort, Wikipedia] Its own district stretched about ten miles north to the Agashi river, about twenty-four miles east to Asserim and Manora, and about forty miles south to Karanja. [n32; BG 30 n.5]

The machinery of that province was built on what the sultans had left. Gerson da Cunha’s survey of the records lists its divisions: the Saibana de Baçaim, the Caçabe de Tana, the Ilha de Salcete, the Ilha de Caranja, the Ilha de Bellaflor de Sambayo, the Praganas of Manora and Asserim, and the Ilha de Bombaim. [n32; DC 157–158] The Portuguese did not invent new words for these units. A caçabe was the Persian kasaba, a market town; a pragana was the Indo-Persian pargana, a revenue district. The custom-house was the mandovim, and the orchards were hortas. [n32; DC 157–158] The new rulers kept the sultanate’s revenue map and wrote it out in Portuguese spelling. The chief revenue officer, the Tanadar-mor, lived not inside the walls but at “Baçaim de Cima”, Upper Baçaim, outside them. [n32; DC 221–222; BG 31]

The land was redistributed. Portuguese settlers and pensioners, and converts of rank, received grants of land “in Salsette and elsewhere on small quit-rents”. [n37; Gaz. XIII-I p. 202] The grants were nominally for three lives, and “no right in the land was conceded to the husbandmen or tenants”. [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 460] The families who actually tilled the gardens did not gain a title; the title went to a fidalgo, a church or a convert gentleman above them. Over time, by gradual grants, “about one half of Salsette became church property”; by 1695 church revenue was said to exceed the king’s. [n37; Gaz. XIII-I p. 201; Gaz. XIII-II p. 463] Those figures are for Salsette and for a later date, but the system that produced them was laid down in these years.

What that meant for a cultivator, John Fryer would describe in 1675: the gentry lived “like petty monarchs”, holding the people “in a perfect state of villainage”. [n37; Gaz. XIII-I p. 202] Fryer was an Englishman with reasons to think ill of Portuguese rule, and he wrote a century after the period of this chapter. His phrase should be read as a later outsider’s judgement on the system’s mature form, not as a description of 1550. But the Gazetteer’s own sentence is plainer and not polemical: men of rank were admitted into Portuguese society, while “those of low birth were left in a state little removed from servitude”. [n37; Gaz. XIII-I p. 202]

There were slaves in the literal sense too. The 1533 raid had come home with captives. In 1634 Bassein would count 1,800 slaves beside 400 Portuguese and 200 Indian Christian families. [n04; Vasai Fort, Wikipedia] The Gazetteer notes house slaves from Africa “made Christians and sometimes raised to be priests”, and “African blood” among the Salsette Christians. [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 460 note 3; Gaz. XIII-I p. 200 note 3] The Sidi strain, the Gazetteer adds, also appeared among Konkani Muslims, some Katkaris and Thakurs. [n35; Gazetteer XIII-1, p. 64]

The city’s institutions grew with it. The Misericórdia, the Portuguese confraternity of mercy, ran a hospital here before 1550. [n32; BG 41; DC 226–227] Its church and hospital would stand as a massive long pile on the river wall, with a cloistered courtyard and a front carved with a Maltese cross and dragons; at its height the hospital drew 140 pardaos a month, plus 79,200 réis a year for rice for the poor. [n32; BG 40; DC 225–226] The dead of the new gentry were buried in the churches under carved slabs. In the Franciscan church of Santo António lay a Councillor of His Majesty who died on 24 August 1558, with his wife, Dona Luiza da Silva. [n32; BG 41–42 and n.1; DC 237+] In the Jesuit church lay Isabel de Aguiar, who died on 24 January 1591. [n32; BG 41; DC 228–230] These are the earliest named townspeople of Baçaim whose graves survived into the nineteenth century.

Who lived inside the walls in these decades? The notes give no figure before 1634, when Bassein counted about 400 Portuguese families, 200 Indian Christian families and 1,800 slaves. [n04; Vasai Fort, Wikipedia] By 1600, the Gazetteer says, citing Portuguese chroniclers, Baçaim was “next to Goa the largest and richest of the Portuguese settlements”, with more than a hundred families “of the highest in India and proverbially rich”. [n15; Thana Gaz. XIV, pp. 30, 33] The landward fields were Indian; the walled streets were Portuguese and mestiço. The people of the villages came in to market, to the custom house, to the courts, and increasingly to church.

The friars arrive (1534–1552)

The Franciscans came first, almost with the fleet. Gerson da Cunha writes that “Agashi was the first place of this district visited by Franciscan missionaries”. [n37; da Cunha p. 159; Hull vol. 1 p. 5] Their leader was a friar named António do Porto, whom the Catholic Encyclopedia calls the “Apostle of Bassein”. [n04; Catholic Encyclopedia]

His biography, as the notes admit, is mostly missing: no birth date, no origin, no death. [n25; Gaps] What survives is a ledger of results, and it is a ledger written by his admirers. The Gazetteer’s summary of his work from 1534 to 1552 is a single sentence: António do Porto “destroyed 200 temples, made over 10,000 converts, built twelve churches, and, by founding orphanages and monasteries, secured a supply of native priests”. [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 461] The Catholic Encyclopedia gives his converts as 10,156. [n04; Catholic Encyclopedia] No modern scholar this book’s research could find has checked either figure. [n25; Gaps]

The orphanages were the foundation of everything else. The first was at Agashi: Nossa Senhora da Luz, Our Lady of Light, founded in 1535 for forty boys. [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 461 note 1; da Cunha pp. 159–160] Others followed at Thana (130 boys), Vehar (800 boys) and Mandapeshvar (100). [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 461 note 1] The boys were raised as Christians, taught Latin and the faith, and some became priests. This was how a Portuguese mission grew Indian clergy within one generation, and it is why, two centuries later, when the Portuguese friars left Bassein, native priests could keep the churches open. [n37; Inferences; Gaz. XIII-I p. 203]

The Agashi orphanage did not last. In 1540 a Gujarati force, retreating through Agashi, destroyed it. According to the Franciscan chronicle, the orphans who refused to accept Islam were burnt inside. Portuguese writers counted them as the “second batch of martyrs”, all of them native converts. [n37; Gaz. XIV p. 1; da Cunha pp. 159–160] [n11; Gaz. 1882 pp. 1–2] The Catholic Encyclopedia calls them “the first Indian martyrs”. [n04; Catholic Encyclopedia] The story comes only from the Portuguese side; no Gujarati or Muslim account of the raid is known to these notes. Agashi’s church, the Gazetteer says, was built after the orphanage’s destruction. [n37; Gaz. XIV pp. 1–2]

What the Franciscans did to the old holy places is told in the same Gazetteer footnote. The temples António do Porto destroyed were at Agashi, Bassein and Thana. At many sacred tanks “temples were thrown down”, and some pools were filled up. [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 461 note 1] At Thana, Porto is said to have raised a cathedral and the church of St Anthony “out of the stones of twelve temples”. [n25; TG-XIV, Agashi entry] On Salsette, the great Buddhist cave at Kanheri became a church of St Michael, and the Brahmanical caves at Mandapeshvar a church of Our Lady of the Conception. [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 461 note 1; da Cunha pp. 189–190]

One footnote preserves a striking case of conversion of place rather than of people. At a famous pool between Bassein and Agashi, where pilgrims had come for cures, the pool itself was converted. A chapel to Our Lady of Healing was built there, “and the pilgrimage and cure-working continued”. [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 461 note 1] The pilgrims kept coming; only the name of the power they came to changed. This book cannot prove which modern site that pool was. But Gerson da Cunha writes of a healing tank near the church of Our Lady of Remedies, on the Agashi–Bassein road, where the Inquisition would later “put a stop to all pilgrimage… under very severe penalties”, and where, in his own day, Hindus again held a jatra. [n37; da Cunha p. 163] Remedi’s church is dedicated to Nossa Senhora dos Remédios; a cross beside it, the “Milagres Cross”, is still called miraculous. [n09; Vasai Catholics blog, 2015] The healing tank appears to have passed through three hands: Hindu pilgrimage, Christian chapel, Inquisition ban, and then, in the nineteenth century, Hindu pilgrimage again. Whether Porto’s “Our Lady of Healing” and da Cunha’s tank by Our Lady of Remedies are the same place is this book’s inference, not the sources’ statement.

Couto saw the caves himself, though he does not say when. In a digression on the Buddha he describes, from his visit to Salsete, “that rare and admirable pagoda, which they call of Canará”, cut in a hill: “many halls”, one as large as the great hall of the royal palace at Lisbon, “more than three hundred chambers up the hill, almost in a spiral, each with its cistern at the door, in the same living rock, of the coldest and most excellent water”, and at the doors of the great hall figures “as large as giants, of such subtle and fine work that not even in silver could they be carved better”. A “very old man of the lands of Salsete” told him that it had been made by the father of Saint Josaphat “to take him in and bring him up in it”, which Couto, who knew the legend of the Indian prince Josaphat, thought might mean the Buddha. [n94 §6]

Some of the people converted with their holy places. On Kanheri, the two heads of the monastery, apparently Buddhist, were converted and renamed Paulo Rapozo and Francisco de Santa Maria. Paulo Rapozo was given three villages, which he later left to the college at Mandapeshvar. [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 461 note 1] It is a small story with the whole system inside it: the religious leaders of a community become Christians, receive land, and pass it to the Church.

Porto also found, at Karanja, a blue stone engraved with the Virgin, and he concluded that there had been Christians there long before him. Gerson da Cunha, himself a Catholic, was dry about it: “on the matter of finding remains of early Christianity in India the Portuguese writers are… simply untrustworthy”. [n37; da Cunha p. 202] There had in fact been Christians at Sopara two centuries earlier. In 1321–22 the Dominican friar Jordanus found “many Nestorian Christians and a church dedicated to St Thomas” at Supera, buried four martyred companions there and baptised people, as Chapter 6 told. [n37; Gaz. XIV p. 322; Gaz. XIII-I p. 200] But no source in the notes connects that community with any family the Franciscans met in 1535. If those Nestorian families survived into the sixteenth century, they did so unseen.

Each Portuguese settlement had an officer charged with all this, the Pai dos Cristãos, the “Father of the Christians”, whose work was “to further Christianity, to foster Christians and to gather others to Christ”. Salsette and Karanja were given to the Franciscans, Goa and Cochin to the Jesuits, Chaul, Diu and Macao to the Dominicans. [n37; da Cunha p. 102; Gaz. XIII-II p. 462 note 2] Gerson da Cunha called it “a dangerous appointment” whose holders’ “indiscreet zeal” led to “unseemly affrays”. [n37; da Cunha p. 102] The Jesuit visitor Valignano, writing instructions for the office later in the century, was candid about how it worked. Conversion, he wrote, “does not commonly occur as a result of sermons and doctrine”. It came by “obstructing the idolatrous practices… meting out just punishment… refusing them favours which can justly be refused, and offering them to those who are newly converted, and honouring, assisting and protecting the latter”. [n37; Valignano, quoted in Priolkar p. 58] That sentence, from inside the mission, is perhaps the clearest single description of the method this chapter follows: obstruction and punishment on one side, favour and protection on the other.

The Franciscan convent of St Antony of Padua had a founding history that the letters of 1542–52 record. [n108 §3] In December 1542 Governor Martim Afonso de Sousa complained that the friars “still threaten me that they will make two or three others at Chaul and Baçaim and Diu, and they will make them, according as they are exempt and shameless”. After the six Franciscans of 1546 reached Goa, one died at Chaul, two went home in 1548 “frightened by the things of this land”, and Fr António do Porto wrote to the King on 7 October 1548 that two friars, himself and Fr João de Goa, “a native of these lands”, had fifty boys in a boarding house in the village of the fortress of Baçaim, and “a fair devout hermitage” in Salsette “where every Sunday and feast the Christians come to hear Mass and Christian doctrine”; the converts were so many and so needy because “when they become Christian they are abandoned by their fathers, relatives and friends”. The Jesuit António Gomes wrote two months later that it was “the best land and the cheapest there is in India; there is much rice and wheat in it, and cloth” and that the Jesuits should have the college; in 1549 the Governor split the Christians’ rent between the friars and the Jesuit Belchior Gonçalves, “the people of the land insisting that I should not leave”. The church was begun in 1552, “with alms and wages that they gave us”. The convent stood “in the middle of the city” and held “forty to fifty residents”; Arts and Theology were taught there before the college of St Bonaventure at Goa; and the altar of the Conception was made privileged by Pope Gregory XIII in 1578. Paulo’s stories from it include a quince that fell from the church roof for a dying infirmarian, and a basket of bread sent to the refectorian by “the wife of Gonçalo Arrais, a leading citizen of that city” in a year of dear wheat. [n108 §2–§3]

Francis Xavier at Baçaim (1544–1552)

The most famous missionary to set foot in Bassein was there only briefly. Francis Xavier, the Jesuit whose order the Catholic Encyclopedia says reached this coast in 1542, stayed at the Franciscan house of Bassein on short visits. The Gazetteer’s Places of Interest volume says he came once at the end of 1544 and twice in 1548; its History volume gives 1544, 1548 and 1552; Wikipedia’s Vasai Fort page notes a single stop in 1548; and a Bombay Jesuit history page gives 1549. [n32; BG 41–42] [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 461] [n04; Vasai Fort, Wikipedia] [n25; bombayjesuits.org snippet] He stayed, each time, with the Franciscans at Santo António, the oldest and one of the largest churches of the city, with its arched chief chapel and a great flat arch in the style of Belém near Lisbon. [n32; BG 41–42]

His lasting act at Bassein was the founding of a Jesuit house. The Catholic Encyclopedia says Xavier founded the College of the Holy Name of God in December 1548. [n04; Catholic Encyclopedia] The Gazetteer says the foundation of the church and monastery of the Holy Name of Jesus was laid in 1548 by Father Melchior Gonçalves, “a close friend of St Xavier”, the Jesuits having arrived the year before. [n32; BG 41] [n25; TG-XIV] Other dates circulate: a secondary source puts the Jesuit church at 1549 and the large college at 1561. [n09; colonialvoyage.com snippet] The Catholic Encyclopedia puts the Jesuits’ first arrival at 1542. [n04; Catholic Encyclopedia] What all agree on is that by about 1550 the Society of Jesus had a church and a school inside the walls of Baçaim, and that it would become the most influential religious house in the city.

The two orders divided the work. The Franciscans, the Catholic Encyclopedia says, served the lower classes; the Jesuits the higher. [n04; Catholic Encyclopedia] The Franciscans had come first, with orphanages and temple-breaking and baptisms by the thousand among cultivators and fishers. The Jesuits came with a school, a library and a plan to win the Brahmans.

Xavier’s own letters about Bassein were not reached for this book. But one Jesuit letter from these years, about Goa rather than Bassein, shows what the missionaries themselves privately thought of their converts. On 10 October 1547 Father Nicolau Lancilotto wrote from Goa to Ignatius Loyola, founder of the Jesuits:

“The people of this country who become Christians do so purely for temporal advantage… Slaves of the Moors or Hindus seek baptism in order to secure their manumission… Others do so to get protection from tyrants, or for the sake of a turban, a shirt… or to escape being hanged… The man who embraces the faith from honest conviction is regarded as a fool. They are baptized… without any instruction, and many revert to their former paganism.” [n37; Lancilotto to Loyola, 1547, in Wicki, Documenta Indica I, quoted in Priolkar p. 55]

That is a Jesuit, writing to his superior in the year before Xavier’s college at Bassein was founded. Lancilotto was describing Goa, and it would be wrong to transfer his words wholesale to the villages of the island of Bassein. But he names the motives that the rest of the evidence will also show: freedom from slavery, protection, clothing, escape from punishment, and a baptism given with little teaching.

The mosques’ money (1546–1554)

Return now to the 5,000 larins.

The Gazetteer’s account of Bassein records that, after the conquest, “certain Musalman mosques were pulled down and in their place a cathedral of St. Joseph was built”. [n10; Gazetteer 1882, Bassein entry] King João III’s letter of March 1546 to Dom João de Castro ordered the church, and ordered that the 3,000 pardaos formerly spent on the mosques should go to support its clergy. [n32; DC 214–216; BG 38–39] That church was the Matriz, the mother church of the city; it would be rebuilt in 1601, and its lintel opens the next chapter. [n32; DC 214–216] The Matriz’s founding date is itself disputed. A secondary source says the Franciscans founded it in 1547 along with the convent of Santo António; another source names it São João rather than São José; and the diocese of Vasai says the first Latin-rite church in the fort was established in 1536 under the title Nossa Senhora da Vida. [n09; colonialvoyage.com; Ancient Origins; Diocese of Vasai via search snippet] Nossa Senhora da Vida was certainly “one of the oldest churches in Bassein”, with three altars when Gemelli Careri saw it in 1695. [n32; BG 40; DC 226–227] The simplest reading of these conflicting claims is that Our Lady of Life was the first church of 1536 and St Joseph’s the parish church built with the mosque money about ten years later. That is an inference.

The letter has a paper trail, printed in Silva Rego’s Documentação from the Torre do Tombo. [n72] In November 1545 the vicar-general Miguel Vaz, in Évora, told the King that Baçaim was “of the best things or the best that Your Highness has on that coast of India”, that it had “for many years yielded much money”, and that there was “no church of Your Highness in it, nor in all the land, which is fifteen leagues and more”, nor any means “to make known to those vassals the errors in which they live”. He asked for men to go “undoing the idolatry and the mosques”, for a church, and for 2,000 pardaos a year for the work, and he complained that until then 3,000 had been “taken into account by the factors” and “spent on praises of Muhammad and the maintenance of the mosques”. The King wrote in the margin: “Let the church be made, and see the rest.” [n72 §1] On 5 March 1546 he told Vaz to go to Baçaim “with the greatest speed”, to take two Jesuits “to stay in the lands and go about them”, and, “because I am having a church made in the said fortress”, to see that it was “made and finished with speed”. [n72 §2] Three days later, in the letter to Castro, came the order for a temple “with the invocation of São Joseph”, with revenue for a rector and chaplains, and the levy of 3,000 pardaos a year “on the mosques that the Moors have in our lordships”. [n72 §3] The sum is the same 3,000 that Vaz said the factors had been paying to the mosques; the treaty’s guarantee had become mission money in a single sentence. (Inference.)

By November 1547 the friars were there. The Bishop of Goa, writing from Cochin on 26 November, copied out what “some friars of Baçaim” were asking the King: a college at Baçaim, a native-Christian judge and a “father” in every fortress, village posts for Christians in place of the unconverted, land for Christians from the estates of rebels and of those who died childless, freedom from the coloarias, and “the lands and revenues of the pagodes and mosques” for the colleges of Baçaim and Chaul. Four days later Cosme Anes wrote from Baçaim that “these friars of the Piedade who came to Baçaim are very good religious and go here in this part making much fruit”. [n72 §4–§5]

The money went on flowing in the same channel. From 1550, Gerson da Cunha records, the Crown gave the Franciscans of Bassein 2,070 pardaos a year, “derived from a source which before the conquest was solely applicable to the lighting of the mosques in Bassein”. When the Jesuits arrived the sum was halved between the two orders. In 1634 the Jesuits’ share was raised to 3,000 pardaos, entered in the accounts under the heading “Conversão da Fé”, Conversion of the Faith. [n37; da Cunha p. 235]

At Agashi, in 1554, “a great mosque was converted into the commandant’s residence”. [n11; Gaz. 1882 pp. 1–2] Agashi had had a large enough Muslim congregation for a great mosque; twenty years after the treaty its building housed a Portuguese officer.

The Gazetteer is blunt about the Muslims’ place in the new order: “Their treatment of their chief rivals, the Musalmans, was specially severe.” [n37; Gaz. XIII-I p. 201 note 2] The Muslims of Basai had been the ruling faith’s people, the sultans’ garrison and the merchants of the Red Sea trade. Under the Portuguese they were the defeated enemy. The notes do not record where the Muslim families of the town went. Some, like the Naitas of Sopara, stayed: in 1882 the Gazetteer still found Naita Muslims among Sopara’s husbandmen and traders, “descendants of Arab and Persian refugees and traders”. [n11; Gaz. 1882 p. 315] At Gass, in a field called Sonarbhat, the “gold field”, the Gazetteer found the tomb of a Muslim saint, Shaikh Akbar-moi-ud-din, bordered with very large old bricks. [n11; Gaz. 1882 p. 341] At Arnala, the tomb of two Muslim saints, Shahali and Hajiali, would still draw a yearly endowment in 1882. [n11; Gaz. 1882 p. 11] Their dates are unknown; they may be older or younger than the Portuguese century. But Islam on this island did not die with the mosques of Bassein. It survived in the villages, in old families and at saints’ tombs.

The laws tighten (1546–1581)

Couto’s Década VI fills in the years around the mosque decree. [n85] In the second siege of Diu (1546) Baçaim and Chaul were where every relief fleet gathered: the Diu garrison’s messenger was told that “in Baçaim five hundred men remained with ships ready and prepared to leave as soon as the weather gave way”, and two Menezes brothers, Dom Jerónimo, captain of the fortress, and Dom Francisco, put back into Baçaim from the stormy Gulf. [n85 §1] A fleet of fifty ships driven back from the Gulf sheltered “at Agaçaim, all unrigged”, where the captain of that tanadaria, Luís Xira Lobo, “with great speed and diligence refitted the ships and provided them with everything needed”, a sign of Agashi’s yards at work in the monsoon of 1546. [n85 §1] In the autumn of 1547 Governor Castro came to Baçaim “to have the war of Cambay continued”, and Sultan Mahmud, “already weary of the war, and because of it his vassals poor and lost”, sent an ambassador in three light ships to the watering-place: the armada was dressed with flags, the fidalgos were told to dress “very sumptuously”, and the peace, renewed with half the Diu customs for Portugal, was “at once proclaimed through Baçaim and Diu, swearing them very solemnly”. [n85 §2] The captain who received the news of Garcia de Sá’s death and his own succession was Jorge Cabral, at Baçaim in 1549; his wife told him “it was better to be fifteen days Governor of India than ten years Captain of Baçaim”; he sailed on 8 August, reached Goa on 15 August, and sent Francisco Barreto to take over Baçaim. [n85 §3]

Alongside the money came the law. The legal history of the Portuguese north is a sequence of royal and church orders, each stronger than the last, each partly evaded, and each renewed.

The Gazetteer gives the sequence for the north. In 1546 the King of Portugal ordered idols broken, idol-makers and performers of Hindu rites punished, and mosques to pay tribute. The order was not enforced. It was renewed in 1555, with Hindu feasts, Brahman preachings, ritual washings and cremations forbidden, and anyone found with idols sent to the galleys with his property forfeit; “half of the property of a man found with idols went to the church”. That law was “evaded by the grant of licenses, but… enforced in 1581”. [n37; Gaz. XIII-II pp. 462–463 and note 7] For a Hindu family on the island, these laws reached into every part of life: the household shrine, the wedding, the funeral pyre and the river bath.

In 1559 King Sebastião issued two further orders, which the Goan scholar A. K. Priolkar dates variously to 22, 25 and 28 March in different places of his book. No new Hindu temple was to be built, and none repaired without special permission, on pain of destruction, with its value applied to “pious works”. And Hindu orphans, meaning children with no living father, mother or ascendant, were to be handed over to the Jesuit College of St Paul at Goa. [n37; Priolkar pp. 76–78, 128–129] The temple order “did not apply to Salsete and Bardez”, the Goa territories, at first; a viceroy’s order of 29 August 1566 extended it “to the entire territory under Portuguese rule”. [n37; Priolkar pp. 76–78] This book’s research did not find the 1559 text applied to Bassein by name. The Gazetteer’s 1546–1555–1581 sequence is the chronology tied to Bassein; the 1559 and 1566 orders show what the law of the whole Portuguese state was. [n37; Gaps]

The orphan law, wherever it reached, was feared more than any other. A petition of the Hindus of Goa in 1577 complained that the Father of Orphans removed sons from living mothers “together with the estates… to compel them by force to receive baptism”. The Inquisitors complained that orphan cases were not reported promptly, and ordered orphans to be reported to the Father of Orphans within six days, on pain of excommunication. [n37; Priolkar p. 128] Bassein was part of the same system. A century later, in 1675, the Captain General of Bassein would take the part of some orphans against the Jesuits and force the Jesuits to restore an orphan’s estate; and an English report of the same period said that Shivaji’s “chiefest cause of his hatred” for the Portuguese was “for forcing orphans of his [caste to] turn Roman Catholicks”. [n37; Orme MSS, quoted in Priolkar p. 129] Those are later witnesses, but they show the orphan law still at work in the north long after 1559.

In 1567 the first Provincial Council of Goa met. Its first resolution sounds like the opposite of everything above: “it is not lawful to bring to our faith and baptism any person by force with threats and terror”, and conversion should be by “example of our lives, preaching… gentleness”. [n37; Priolkar p. 53] The same Council, according to a Portuguese writer cited by Gerson da Cunha, barred Christians in Goa and Bassein from lending money to, entering partnership with or sheltering “Parabus”, that is Prabhus, the Hindu writer caste; from employing non-Christians in public office as clerk, interpreter, broker or shroff (money-changer); and from renting property to pagans. It ordered a roll of the infidels in every parish of Goa and Bassein, half of whom were to attend Sunday catechism on pain of fines of one, two or three tangas. [n37; da Cunha p. 142; Priolkar p. 53]

Read together, the two halves of that Council describe the whole method. Baptism by force was forbidden. But a Hindu clerk could not hold office, a Hindu tenant could not rent from a Christian, a Hindu money-dealer could not partner with one, and Hindu villagers could be made to sit through catechism on Sundays or pay. The offices barred to non-Christians, clerk, interpreter, broker and shroff, were exactly the jobs by which literate Hindu families, Brahmans and Prabhus, earned their living from a government. Here the notes give the rule, not its effect; but the effect on those families can be guessed. A Brahman or Prabhu who wanted to keep his post at the custom house or the court had a powerful reason to accept baptism.

The rewards were as clearly set out as the penalties. “Converts, especially high caste converts, were treated with honour and distinction, and, for the first fifteen years after conversion, the poorer class of Christians were freed from the payment of tithes and first fruits.” [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 462] “Men of rank were admitted into the best Portuguese society and were allowed to marry with Europeans. Like the Portuguese settlers and pensioners they received grants of land in Salsette and elsewhere on small quit-rents.” [n37; Gaz. XIII-I p. 202] For a poor cultivator, fifteen years free of tithe was a large and immediate gain. For a man of rank, conversion opened the doors of the ruling society, its marriages and its land.

The Gazetteer itself, writing in 1882, draws one cautious conclusion from the timing. “The fact that the people of Bandra remained Hindus till about 1580, seems to show that the earlier conversions were the result of persuasion and encouragement, not of force. At the same time, from before the middle of the sixteenth century, the persuasion… [was] accompanied by rules discouraging and suppressing Hinduism.” [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 462] It was in 1581 that the laws against Hindu worship were at last enforced. After about 1580, another passage says, Hinduism was no longer tolerated. [n35; Gazetteer XIII-1, pp. 201–202] The Inquisition’s officers reached Bassein in the same decades.

When King Philip of Spain succeeded to the Portuguese Crown in 1581, the Governor Fernão Teles de Meneses sent Matheus Pires, a former Secretary of State, “to Bacaim, Chaul, Damão and Dio to have King Philip sworn King of Portugal”, and the captains, among them Dom Manuel de Almada at Baçaim, “gave their homage and made oaths with the greatest solemnity possible”. [n86 §4]

The Inquisition’s commissaries (1560–1600)

The Holy Office of the Inquisition was established in Goa in 1560. It would last, with one interruption, until 1812. [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 463; da Cunha p. 235] “By 1580,” the Gazetteer says, “agents of the Inquisition, called commissaries, were at work in Chaul, Bassein and Daman, collecting offenders and sending them for trial and punishment to Goa.” [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 463] The Places of Interest volume is more specific about Bassein itself: “After 1560 there was a commissary of the Inquisition at Bassein”, and it places him at the Jesuit college. [n37; Gaz. XIV p. 41] [n32; BG 41]

Gerson da Cunha describes how it worked. Every principal settlement “sent yearly a large number of victims through their so-called Commissaries”. Prisoners at Bassein were held in the jail near the Captain’s palace until “the fleet from the north” took them to Goa for trial. [n37; da Cunha pp. 235–236] The jail whose inscribed slab da Cunha saw was built later, between 1635 and 1639. [n32; BG 39 n.7] Where the commissary held his prisoners in the sixteenth century the notes do not say.

There is an apparent contradiction here. A 1987 article in The Examiner, Bombay’s Catholic weekly, says flatly that “the Inquisition was never set up in Bassein”. [n09; The Examiner, March 1987, via Goanet] The two accounts are not as far apart as they look. There was never an Inquisition tribunal at Bassein; trials, sentences and the public autos-da-fé took place at Goa. What Bassein had was the Inquisition’s agent, the commissary, with the power to collect accused people and send them south. For a family on the island, the difference may not have mattered much: the arrest happened at home, the voyage was by sea, and the judgement came hundreds of miles away.

Who did it judge? Strictly, the Inquisition tried baptised people, the “New Christians”, for heresy and for keeping old rites in secret. The law against idolatry among people who had never been baptised was enforced by other courts. Priolkar treats the two as one system, and the travellers of the next century described the Inquisition’s rigour against both: “the Christians if they strayed from the path of orthodoxy, the Hindus if they practised their religious rites”. [n37; Inferences; Gaz. XIII-I p. 202] Under either court, the healing tank near Remedi lost its pilgrims: da Cunha says the Inquisition “had put a stop to all pilgrimage” there “under very severe penalties”. [n37; da Cunha p. 163]

How many people from Bassein were taken? No one knows. Priolkar complains that the Inquisition’s records were destroyed, so “it is not possible to obtain an idea of the numbers” burnt. [n37; Priolkar p. 60] Gerson da Cunha gives “about 1,250 persons burned or tortured in ten or twelve autos da fé” at Goa, without citing a source. [n37; da Cunha pp. 235–236] The traveller Pyrard claimed that “only the rich, the confiscation of whose property profited the Inquisition, suffered, while the poor escaped”. [n37; da Cunha p. 235] That is one traveller’s view. The single documented Bassein case the notes record belongs to the next chapter: a family burnt at an auto-da-fé on 30 December 1747, its house razed and the ground ploughed with salt. [n37; Priolkar pp. 60–61] For the sixteenth century, the Inquisition in Bassein is a presence without names.

The Jesuit harvest (1565–1588)

The great numbers came in the second half of the century, and they came through the Jesuits.

On 8 September 1565 a Brahman astrologer named Parashuram Joshi was baptised and took the name Henrique da Cunha. About 250 Hindus followed him into the font, more than fifty of them Brahmans. [n04; Machado 1999 via Wikipedia: Bombay East Indians] The figure comes from a modern history by Machado, seen only through Wikipedia, and the source does not say where the baptism took place, though it is placed among the conversions of the Province of the North. [n25; Gaps] Nothing more is known of Parashuram Joshi: not his village, not his family, not his reasons. But his name and his profession tell us something. Joshi is one of the surnames of the Palshe Brahmans, the coast’s priests, physicians and astrologers, who held the privilege of fixing the auspicious hour for marriages and thread ceremonies for all classes. [n08; Gazetteer 1882, p. 81] Whether Parashuram Joshi was a Palshe is unknown. What is clear is that the Jesuits had won a man whose word carried weight in Hindu households, and that a quarter of a thousand people followed him.

That was the method. Between 1570 and 1590, the Gazetteer says, the Jesuits “took pains to make Brahman and other high-caste converts, knowing that if the Brahmans became Christians, many of the lower classes would follow”. They made baptism “an occasion of great splendour and rejoicing”. [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 461; Gaz. XIV p. 41]

Gerson da Cunha, writing in 1876, described the ceremony with the sharpness of a Goan Catholic physician who disliked the Jesuits. The method was to convert a Brahman, then hold “a grand procession in his honour with the assistance of the Governor, the nobility, gentry and clergy”, followed by “a sumptuous banquet”. “Their bait was alluring and irresistible,” he wrote, “and the native convert was led by the nose.” [n37; da Cunha p. 234]

We can picture the scene only from those words, and must not add to them. A Brahman of the island, perhaps a village headman or a priest, walks through the streets of Baçaim in a procession with the captain and the fidalgos and the clergy; then he sits down to a feast at which the governor’s own table honours him. For the man himself, it was an elevation into the ruling class. For his relatives and his village, watching, it was a demonstration of what the new faith could offer. For his former caste-fellows, who would never again eat with him, it was a loss.

The numbers rose year by year. The Gazetteer records baptisms rising “from 1600 in 1573 to 9400 in 1588”. [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 461; Gaz. XIV p. 41] The Places of Interest volume puts the 1588 figure more vividly: “in [1588] no fewer than 9400 Hindus were baptised in Bassein church”. [n15; Thana Gaz. XIV, pp. ~39–40] The Population volume says they were baptised in “Bassein Cathedral” in a single year. [n35; Gazetteer XIII-1, pp. 201–202] The Jesuit church’s own entry in the Gazetteer repeats it: “In 1588, 9,400 Hindus were baptised.” [n32; BG 41] Machado’s modern history, via Wikipedia, gives the same two figures, 1,600 converts in 1573 and 9,400 baptisms in Vasai in 1588. [n04]

These numbers deserve a second look. Machado’s history also estimates that by the end of the sixteenth century the whole Province of the North had only 10,000 to 15,000 Catholics. [n04; Machado 1999 via Wikipedia] If 9,400 people were baptised at Bassein in 1588 alone, and António do Porto had baptised over 10,000 before 1552, the provincial total looks too small. Either many of the baptised died, fled or lapsed, as Lancilotto said happened in Goa; or the yearly figures include children and slaves who were not counted as households; or one set of numbers is wrong. The notes cannot resolve this, and the reader should see the tension rather than one clean total.

The Jesuits were also at work elsewhere in the province. At Thana, about 1560, Father Gonçalo Rodrigues bought young children and collected orphans, and baptised five to six thousand people in three years, “many orphans or children sold by their parents”. He founded a Christian village at Vehar of 8,000 people around an enclosed shrine of a “three-headed god”, which was rededicated to the Trinity. [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 461 and note 4] [n04; Machado 1999] A footnote in the Gazetteer, citing the Jesuit chronicle Oriente Conquistado, adds a sentence that should not be passed over: “The lower Hindus sold their children to Musalmans and Christians. A child at the breast cost as much as a goat in Portugal.” [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 461 note 4] Famine and poverty drove some families to sell their children, and the mission bought them. Whether that was rescue or traffic, or both, depends on whose eyes we use; the sources come only from the buyers.

At Bandra, the Jesuit Manuel Gomes, superior of the Holy Name college at Bassein and called the “Apostle of Salsette”, built St Andrew’s church in 1575 and made about 6,000 conversions between 1575 and 1590 (4,000 by 1588, 6,000 by 1591, by another count). [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 462] [n25; TG-XIV] The Bandra converts are the ones the Gazetteer used to argue for persuasion, because the people there “remained Hindus till about 1580”. [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 462]

To train priests for all these new parishes, a seminary for secular clergy was opened at Bassein about 1570, according to Hull, and a “College of the Purification” took noble children to be “brought up as missionaries”. [n37; Hull vol. 1; Gaz. XIII-I p. 202] By 1634 the city would house sixty-three friars: thirty Franciscans, fifteen Jesuits, ten Dominicans and eight Augustinians. [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 462] [n32; BG 42]

The Dominicans and Augustinians came later than the first two orders. The Dominican church of São Gonçalo is dated 1583 by the Gazetteer and 1564 by a secondary site; a Catholic Encyclopedia article says the Dominicans reached the region in 1548. [n32; BG 42] [n09; Ancient History Sites; Catholic Encyclopedia, Diocese of Damao] The Augustinian church of Nossa Senhora da Anunciada is dated 1596 by a secondary site. [n09; Ancient History Sites] By the end of the century Baçaim had all four great orders inside its walls, and the Misericórdia besides.

Gerson da Cunha records one more Jesuit detail that matters for the villages. Three Bassein parishes, he says, arose from the Jesuits: Nossa Senhora da Graça, São Tomé and São Miguel Arcanjo. Rome had ordered that the Jesuits should administer only parishes they had themselves founded from a pagan community. [n37; da Cunha p. 234] Those three dedications, Our Lady of Grace, St Thomas and St Michael the Archangel, are exactly those of three of today’s village churches: Our Lady of Grace at Papdi, St Thomas at Sandor and St Michael at Manickpur. [n32; BG 36–37] [n11; Gaz. 1882 p. 37] Da Cunha does not name the villages, so the match is this book’s inference. If it holds, the Jesuits’ “procession and banquet” converts were made, in part, in the gardens south and east of the fort.

Bassein through a Jesuit visitor’s eyes, about 1579–80

The Gazetteer’s numbers come from the 1880s. A near-contemporary description comes from the Jesuit Visitor Alessandro Valignano, whose Sumario of the Indian province for the General in Rome (the title says 1579; the surviving copy was made some years later) describes the college of Baçaim and its “residences of the North”. [n73 §1–§3] Baçaim, he says, was “a city inhabited by Portuguese of about eight hundred households”, on “a very small island”, with a vicar and two or three clerics, a Franciscan house of twelve to fourteen friars, a Dominican house of three or four, and the Jesuit college. The country round it, as far as Daman, twenty-six leagues away, was “all gentiles except in some villages near Baçain, in which there is a good number of Christians, made for the most part by ours”. [n73 §1]

The college had fifteen or sixteen Jesuits, a new church “finished last year”, and a second house being finished for twenty or twenty-two more, in which “sixty to seventy orphan boys of the land” lived in one hall and the catechumens in another until baptised. Two schools were attached, but the grammar students “do not pass fifteen to twenty”, all boys. The rector administered, beside the college’s own thousand cruzados of village rents, “a thousand cruzados and more, a revenue that was first of the mosques of the Moors”, which the King had paid out of his customs houses “to be spent by our hands in the conversion of the gentiles”: 400 for the house of boys and catechumens, “and the rest for the clothing of those who become Christians”. [n73 §2] That is the mosque tribute of 1546 (above), still being paid as mission money thirty-odd years on.

Under the rector of Baçaim, Valignano lists five residences: Santo Tomé, half a league from the city in the college’s orchard, where “more than fifteen hundred Christians were made in various villages, and among them many of the most honoured castes”; Thana, in Salsette, where there were “about a hundred” Portuguese houses and “about four thousand Christians that ours made”, with two Augustinians in a small house of their own; a village he calls “la Trinedad”, a league and a half on, “all Christians”, with more than 2,500 in the district, whose orphan boys had been set to farm a bought plot so that the produce kept them and paid for converts’ clothing, and who “may be called the mother of the village”; Bandra, a league and a half farther; and Daman. [n73 §3]

Bandra is the most revealing. It “is of the college of Goa”, which took from it nearly 3,000 of the college’s 7,000 cruzados a year. It had about 4,000 people, “partly gentiles and partly Moors”, “among whom there will be five hundred Christians”, and “some are always being converted”. The Jesuits’ house there was also a fortress by the sea, with ten or twelve Portuguese soldiers paid by the Crown for eight months of the year as “a guard of all that land against the Moorish Malabar enemies”, and a church “which is now being made”. The Jesuits held no temporal jurisdiction in the village; the King did. [n73 §3] That fits the Gazetteer’s “remained Hindus till about 1580” and sits uneasily with its date of 1575 for St Andrew’s church. (Inference.) The Jesuits’ annual letters then record the change. Ruy Vicente wrote on 20 October 1580 that at Bandorá, “the residence of Salcete of Baçaim”, nearly 2,000 souls had been “converted” and 680 were to be baptised at once, the rest waiting for instruction. The letter for 1581 says the two Fathers and two Brothers at Bandra baptised “more than 1,500 persons” in a few days, in three baptismal feasts, “so that in this village, which is very large, no trace of idolatry remained”. It adds that the college of Goa had “a good part of its revenue” from these villages, “and it seemed reason” that having enjoyed the villagers’ labour the Company should give them “of the spiritual”. [n73 §5] The sums are of this kind: 500 in about 1579, nearly 2,000 “converted” and 680 baptised in 1580, 1,500 baptised in 1581 out of a village of about 4,000, which also had Muslims. The Jesuits’ own sentence about the revenue is the plainest statement in the sources of what the village’s conversion was worth to the order. [n73 §5]

Valignano also says why the countryside was converted so unevenly. In the “lands of Baçain and Daman” the first obstacle, he writes, was “the great opposition the Portuguese make who are lords of the villages”, because all those villages “at the time of the Conquest were given in remuneration and grace to various Portuguese”, who kept the cultivators bound to labour. When the Fathers tried to baptise villagers or take orphans, “the gentiles riot and say that they want to leave that land”, and the landlords, “as this does not turn out well for the Portuguese”, defend the gentiles and “say that we want to make them Christians by force”. A second obstacle was baptismal clothing: a Portuguese-style garment was given at baptism, so that “now they will not be baptised unless these garments are given”, and “many times two and three thousand catechumens stand waiting two or three years for someone to clothe them”. A third was that after baptism the prelates tried to levy tithes, and the vicars on visitation fined converts, “leaving them sometimes in their sins and taking the money for their profit”, so that the gentiles “laugh and say that this is what they will gain by becoming Christians”. [n73 §4]

A decree of the Viceroy Dom Luís de Ataíde on 16 December 1578 shows the same strain from the government’s side. He told the captain of Baçaim that the gentiles of Baçaim, its lands “and the adjacent islands, Tana and Caranjá”, “innumerable” in number, “have fled all to the lands of the Moors”, because “some Religious with indiscreet zeal for the Christianity” had them arrested, “on the information of the Christians of the land, founded on the pretensions they have of being able to get the property of the said gentiles”. The gentiles, he wrote, were “officiaes, weavers, carpenters, sawyers, blacksmiths and merchants in provisions, from whom the State profited greatly”. He granted them a royal safe-conduct: those absent among the Moors might return within six months, their houses and lands were to be restored even if the captain or the treasury officer had given them to others, and they might not be arrested before final sentence if they gave bail. The Church’s temporary secular jurisdiction under the Council’s decrees, “granted for five years”, had expired and was not to be used. [n73 §6] The Viceroy’s reason is not charity: the gentiles were the craftsmen and traders of Bassein’s territory, and their flight “disserves God” because the Moors’ land was “more remote and indisposed to the conversion”. The decree is also a plain record that in 1578 many of the country’s people could and did leave for the Muslim-ruled lands to the east.

The college as its Jesuits remembered it

The Jesuit historian Sebastião Gonçalves, writing after 1616, gives the college its own history. [n78] Xavier, he says, went to Baçaim when Garcia de Sá was governor, negotiated “a house at Baçaim and revenue for the catechumens”, and had Miguel Vaz ask the King for the 5,000 larins “applied to the mosques” in the 1534 treaty, “seeing how badly employed these tangas were in the service of Muhammad”. Fr António do Porto of the Franciscans, “then at Baçaim and in charge of the conversion”, asked for Jesuits to help with the neophytes, and the work and alms were divided between the two orders. The Company’s house was begun on 24 October 1549, Belchior Gonçalves “in a short time made four hundred Christian souls and tore down many pagodas”, and in 1560 it became a college, the second of the Goa province. [n78 §1] Its rectors, from the Company’s catalogues, were Belchior Gonçalves, Gonçalo Rodrigues, Cuenca, Cristóvão da Costa, Francisco Cabral (1562–66, who carried stone on his back), João Cabral, Monclaro, Jerónimo Xavier, Jerónimo Rebelo, Pedro Rodrigues and others to Francisco Soares (1612–16). [n78 §3]

Gonçalves says its founder was “very doubtful”. The King gave 300 patacões a year and food. But the widow Isabel de Aguiar, whom “Mestre Gaspar” found at Hormuz and “married honourably at Baçaim”, gave one of her three Crown villages in life and left two at death, with King Sebastião’s licence, and “over 1,500 patacões” of income; she was reburied in the middle of the chancel by order of the Provincial Francisco Vieira. The ladies and gentlemen of the city added houses, orchards, altar-pieces, candlesticks and the church’s portal: Dona Filipa da Fonseca, Dona Margarida Beles, Dom João Beles, Dom Jerónimo Beles, Ambrósio Lopes (2,000 pardaos to clothe the catechumens), Duarte de Melo, Ambrósio Argueiros. [n78 §2] The church was first Our Lady’s, with her feast on 8 September, and was renamed “of Jesus” in 1578 at the request of the rector Manuel Teixeira. [n78 §3]

The college had three country parishes “in the circuit of the city”: Our Lady of Grace at Baçaim de Cima, São Miguel in “the village called Pori”, and São Tomé. Under Fr Pedro de Almeida, vicar of two of them (1575–76), “the gentiles and Moors” sent a boy to hack at the cross that stood before the church of Baçaim de Cima; the Father wept in public, and the offenders, “fearing the punishment of heaven and of earthly justice”, “took by their counsel to become Christians”. [n78 §3] On Salsette, Fr Gonçalo Rodrigues bought many children whom their gentile parents had sold to Muslims as slaves, paying “three and a half tangas” for two; in 1558, with a village called Raoobalem given him by Governor Barreto, he founded a settlement of 500 Christian farmers and turned the pagoda of the three-faced god into a church of the Trinity, with a college of two hundred boys in red gowns. [n78 §4] On 18 December 1568 twelve Malabar galleys landed at Thana during the feast of the Expectation of Our Lady, sacked the town and took the bell of the matriz; the Baçaim rector Francisco Rodrigues, who had come to help the feast, ordered the sacrament consumed; the Malabars returned five days later and were beaten off. [n78 §5] Gonçalves’s list of the Franciscans’ churches in Salsette and the district of Baçaim (Our Lady of the Candles, the Holy Spirit at Mandapeshwar, the Assumption, São Tomé, São Brás, Reis Magos, Mahim, Bombay, Mazagon, Trombay, Karanja; and in Baçaim, Madre de Deus, São João Baptista, Monte Calvário, Espírito Santo and a residence at Agashi) agrees with Paulo da Trindade’s five rectories (Chapter 34) and adds the island churches. [n78 §4]

Akbar’s captain and the Viceroy at Baçaim, 1572–73

When Akbar took Gujarat, Couto says, flatterers told him that “the lands of Damão, and even those of Baçaim with their cities” were his by right, and he sent “Cutubidican with ten thousand horse”. The Viceroy, Dom António de Noronha, put in at Baçaim, where his council voted that he “stay at the city of Baçaim and send all that power to Damão”; he went on to Damão, taking “twelve or fifteen more ships that joined him from Chaul and Baçaim, which the gentlemen there, married, had armed to accompany the Viceroy”. [n90 §4] The district’s yield was reckoned in the chronicles at “a hundred thousand pardaos” in peace, “which are of our money thirty-six” million réis. [n90 §3] In 1594, in the war of the Morro de Chaul, Dom Álvaro de Abranches came from Baçaim “where he was captain of the war people who stayed there because of the same Moors who were running all these lands”, and led 1,500 Portuguese against the Moors’ bazaar at Chaul. [n90 §5]

When the Nizam’s army besieged Chaul in 1570–71, the Captain-major went first to “provide the stockades of Baçaim and secure the island of Salsete … of the greatest importance and revenue to the State”, and Baçaim’s captain, Martim Afonso de Melo, “had great public proclamations thrown, on pain of five hundred cruzados and two years’ exile, against anyone who left that Fortress and City”, since the Nizam’s captains were going about those lands; volunteers such as Gaspar Velho left all the same, saying the fine could be taken from his estate. [n87 §3]

The aldermen, the loans and a Baçaim-born captain, 1580s

Couto shows Baçaim’s municipal body at work in the 1580s. [n88] When corsairs broke out of the Malabar rivers, “the aldermen of Baçaim armed two ships, and those of Chaul four, paying the soldiers and making all the expenses with the one per cent”, to escort the merchants’ convoy to Goa; and when the Viceroy needed an armada, “ten or twelve thousand pardaos that the cities of Baçaim and Chaul lent with much good will” began it. [n88 §1] Baçaim also sent rice and timber to the new fortress at Muscat and to Hormuz. [n88 §1] The captaincy itself could be a gift: Manuel de Sousa Coutinho had been “provided with the captaincy of Baçaim for years as a dowry for a daughter”. [n88 §3] Couto names one Baçaim-born captain, Duarte de Melo, “native of Baçaim, son of Heitor de Melo and of Dona Margarida, daughter of Manuel de Sá”, who was married there to Dona Catarina, daughter of Dom Jorge Telo, and was on his way to the captaincy of Diu when chosen captain of a crippled ship; he is the Duarte de Melo whom the Jesuit chronicler lists among the college church’s benefactors. [n88 §2]

When Mughal horse threatened Damão in the early 1580s (the year is not given; the Viceroy was the Count of Santa Cruz, 1581–84), Baçaim was the nearest refuge and the first help. [n97] The Captain of Damão told the district’s tanadarias (Sanjan, Dahanu, Tarapur and Mahim) to send “all the women and children to Baçaim”, wrote to “the Cities of Baçaim and Chaul” for relief, and “Baçaim being nearer, there arrived first at that city ten or twelve ships”, whose captains included Jorge Pereira Coutinho, “a gentleman of more than sixty years” who ran to such things “as if he were thirty”, Dom Francisco de Noronha, Dom Francisco de Sousa, Dom Dinis de Almeida, Duarte de Melo, Dom Rui Gomes da Silva and Manuel de Melo, “all with many good soldiers at their own cost”. [n97 §1] The lawyer Francisco de Frias, Veador da Fazenda, “who was at Baçaim”, paid for the Armada that Fernão de Miranda took out over the Damão bar on 24 July with twenty ships; Miranda went “in person to Baçaim and Thana” to find the sailors and, in the autumn, armed “eight very good ships at Baçaim” in a few days. [n97 §2] These are the same men and ships that marched against the Koli king in January 1583, when “all the people of Baçaim, who would be nearly three hundred men” followed “the Captain of Baçaim, who carried the banner of Christ”. [n97 §2; n86] Couto also notes that the merchants of Chaul and Baçaim “emptied themselves” at Cochin and cleared their goods through its residents at a duty of three and a half per cent, to Goa’s loss, until the Crown bought out the King of Cochin’s customs. [n97 §4]

Baçaim’s rice and timber moved the politics of the coast. [n98] In October 1571, after Goa’s siege, the Viceroy told “the Factor of Baçaim” to buy “all the rice he found for the storehouses of Goa”, and sent a fleet up the coast to collect the cáfila. In 1575 Akbar’s ambassador to Goa, whom Couto visited several times and found “very learned in their histories”, asked among his master’s articles for a Provision allowing his people to cut timber in Damão’s lands and to take “rice in the husk, from that Fortress and from Baçaim and Chaul, to the lands of Cambay, what its captains see is necessary”, and the Council, remembering how close Damão had come to falling two years before, agreed. [n98 §2] About 1585 the timber and supplies for the new fort at Ponnani, on the Malabar coast, “were to come from Baçaim”, in the cáfila that the captain-major of the North was ordered to escort. [n98 §3] And about 1598 the Viceroy wrote to Baçaim “that six very light sanguiceis should be armed” for the coast patrol and sent Dom Francisco de Noronha to enter the captaincy. [n98 §4]

Koli raiders and the Nizam’s horse, about 1570

Two wars of these years were fought on the villages’ own ground. [n87] About 1570 Couto says the King of Colle and the King of Sarzeta “had been wasting the lands of Baçaim and, almost as lords of them, eating their villages and collecting their revenues”. The Captain of Baçaim, Martim Afonso de Melo, called out “the Passagis, who are two or three Gentile brothers, vassals of the State, who hold very large villages in the lands of Baçaim called Sabajú, which Francisco Barreto left to them with the obligation to come at once whenever called, with two hundred peães and fifty horse”. With 800 Portuguese, 400 musketeers and the lands’ infantry they marched by Manorá, beat the Koli and Sarzeta force at a village called “Paléi”, burnt two Koli cities, and came home through bamboo woods to “our stockade of Saibana”. [n87 §1] In 1571 the Nizam Shah’s captains “sent four thousand over the lands of Baçaim” from the siege of Chaul, “burning our villages, with the intention of passing to the island of Salsete and Baçaim, which was always defended by our ships that kept guard at the passes of that island and the rivers”; two of them put six field guns and two thousand horse against the “fort” of Karanja, “which is so only in name”, and were routed by seventy men under Estevão Perestrelo. [n87 §2] This is the raid in which, the Franciscan editor says, the reed church at Agashi was burned (Chapter 34).

Why they came to the font

By the end of the century the result was plain to see. “The consequence was,” the Gazetteer wrote, “that the greater part of the people of Bassein and Salsette were nominally Christian.” [n34; Gaz. XIII-I p. 201] A modern historian, Sidh Losa Mendiratta, puts the same result in a single sentence: between about 1570 and 1620 the Jesuits and Franciscans converted “a majority of the population” of Salsette and of the Bassein and Agashi subdivisions. [n34; Mendiratta 2017 p. 211]

Every reader of this history, and every family descended from those converts, eventually asks the same question. Why? Why did a gardener at Sopara, a fisherman at Agashi or a headman in a Salsette village walk into a church as one person and come out with a Portuguese name?

The question is easy to ask and hard to answer, because nearly every surviving word about it was written by one side. The baptisers wrote letters, chronicles, annual reports and account books. The baptised wrote nothing that has come down to us. This book’s research found no contemporary Marathi source from the Hindu side, and no count of how many converts came from each caste in Bassein. [n37; Gaps] What can be done is to put the witnesses side by side, say who each of them was, and let the reader see where they agree.

The verdict of 1882

Begin with the Gazetteer, because it is the source most often quoted and the most often misquoted. Its editors, writing in 1882 under J. M. Campbell, drew on Gerson da Cunha and on the Portuguese chroniclers. [n37; method note] Their judgement was carefully worded: “Force may have been used in some cases, but the Portuguese accounts seem to show that persuasion and interest were the chief means of conversion.” [n37; Gaz. XIII-I p. 201 note 2] The second noun in that phrase matters as much as the first. “Interest” means material advantage. The Gazetteer was not claiming that the people of Bassein were won by sermons alone. It was claiming that they were won by a mixture of argument and reward, rather than by the sword.

In the same footnote, and to its credit, the Gazetteer recorded what the converts’ descendants said. “According to the story of the Uraps and other classes their forefathers were forced to become Christians by having pieces of flesh thrown into their wells.” It also cited the Oriental Christian Spectator of June 1859 for “the Portuguese thrusting biscuit and pork into the mouths of unwilling multitudes”. [n37; Gaz. XIII-I p. 201 note 2] [n34; Gaz. XIII p. 201 n.2] The Spectator was a mid-nineteenth-century periodical, Protestant in its leanings, writing three hundred years after the events. [n37; Inferences] The Uraps were Christian Agris and Kolis who had gone back to Hinduism in the cholera years of 1820 and 1828, a story for a later chapter. [n37; Gaz. XIII-I pp. 117, 203]

Today the same kind of story is told about the Samvedi Christians of the north. A community blog of 2009 says that missionaries threw bread into wells, and that Brahmans who drank from them were outcasted, then given new names and a new dress. A Hindu writer from Vasai, in a polemical article of 2019, repeats the bread in the wells and adds that converts were made to wear red caps and red saris as a mark. [n07; Rajan D’Mello blog 2009; Swarajya 2019] The researcher who read the Jesuit letters for this book found no Jesuit or Franciscan letter that records anything of the kind. [n34; §3 Inferences] So the well story is a tradition, attested from the 1850s, told by Hindus and Christians alike, and told of several communities. It may preserve the memory of real humiliation, or of the caste logic by which one polluting mouthful could cut a family off from its own people. It is not a documented event, and this book does not present it as one. The red cap is a different matter, and the evidence on it is taken up later in this chapter.

The physician who disliked the Jesuits

Gerson da Cunha, the Goan Catholic physician whose Notes on the History and Antiquities of Chaul and Bassein appeared in 1876, has already given us the procession and the banquet: “Their bait was alluring and irresistible, and the native convert was led by the nose.” [n37; da Cunha p. 234] His dislike of the Jesuits is plain on the page. So, a few pages later, is a second prejudice, which matters for the story of the Samvedi converts and will return below. Da Cunha’s testimony is valuable precisely because he was a Catholic: he cannot be accused of hostility to the faith, only to the way it was spread.

Priolkar and Heras

In 1961 the Goan scholar A. K. Priolkar published The Goa Inquisition, built on the Goa archives. He stated what he called the “generally accepted view”: that conversion rested on the “lure of material rewards and threat of violence and force”. He quoted Dr Antonio Noronha, who wrote that conversions took place “some by fear of physical force; others from moral cowardice… not a few to avoid the loss of their properties and interests; some with their eyes on lucrative jobs — and almost none from conviction”. [n37; Priolkar pp. 49–55]

Priolkar took particular aim at a famous scene described by the French traveller Pyrard: fifteen hundred catechumens in Goa, gathered for baptism, told in a sermon that “none should come there by constraint”. Priolkar called it “an empty gesture”. He also caught a Jesuit biographer in an admission. James Brodrick, writing in 1952 about the Goa mission, had conceded that the policy of the vicar Michael Vaz involved “a great deal of pressure, social and financial”, while insisting in the same breath that “No Hindu… was ever forced by that policy”. [n37; Priolkar pp. 54, 56]

On the other side stood the Jesuit historian Father H. Heras, whose The Conversion Policy of the Jesuits in India argued that Jesuit work came “only a posteriori… after the Hindus themselves had decided to join the Church”. [n37; Priolkar p. 51, quoting Heras p. 26] In Heras’s account, the missionaries harvested what Indians had already chosen; in Priolkar’s, the choice was made under pressure that left little room for choosing.

Two warnings belong beside both men. Priolkar’s evidence is Goan, and often about Salcete in Goa, which is a different place from the Salsette island beside Bombay; older writers sometimes confused the two. [n37; §2 laws, §3] And Heras was defending his own order. The reader should hold them as opposing advocates, not as neutral judges.

The single Goan case that Priolkar quotes from a Jesuit letter of 12 December 1567 shows how mixed a motive could be: a Brahman who became a Christian in order to marry a temple servant of low caste. [n37; Priolkar p. 56] Love, or convenience, or escape from caste: the baptism served all three, and the letter does not say which weighed most.

The Jesuits in their own words (1558 and 1562)

Better than any later advocate are the missionaries themselves, writing to their superiors from Baçaim in the very years of the work. Two such letters survive in the great Jesuit document collection Documenta Indica, and the researcher for this book read them in the original Portuguese. [n34; §3]

Gonçalo Rodrigues, 5 September 1558

Father Gonçalo Rodrigues, the Jesuit whom the Gazetteer credits with buying children and gathering orphans at Thana, wrote from Bassein on 5 September 1558. In the three and a half years since he had come up from Goa “to Taná and Baçaim”, he reported, “more than nine hundred souls” had been baptised. [n34; Documenta Indica IV doc. 16, p. 98]

Then he wrote, with startling honesty, about who those souls were and why they had come. In this book’s translation of the Portuguese: “those who come to conversion are commonly the poorest and most needy, and are converted more drawn and moved by hope of some interest and remedy of life than by preaching, which they do not understand and for which there is no interpreter”. [n34; Documenta Indica IV pp. 100–101]

He went further, and described the method as a deliberate one: “we set them the snares of human things and, once caught in them, we teach them the divine… we invite the rustics, farmers and those who live in woods and hedges”. [n34; Documenta Indica IV pp. 100–101]

The word is snares. This is not an enemy’s accusation. It is a missionary’s description of his own work, written to men who shared his faith and his purpose, and it shows that the Jesuits saw nothing shameful in it. The material gift came first; the teaching came after; and the people caught were the poorest, the farmers and the forest-dwellers, who could not even follow the sermons for want of an interpreter.

What the snares were, Rodrigues listed in detail when he described a new Christian village of the Holy Trinity on Salsette, built around a converted temple, with about five hundred souls, “all farmers and labourers”. The converts were given houses, “very cheap lands, and for many, properties we buy for them”. They were given clothes, for men, women and children. They were given rice to eat until their first harvest, and seed to sow. They were given “as many oxen as they want to plough, with their ploughs mended”. There was a common cattle shed, and a granary filled with the paddy that Christians on the land paid as rent. [n34; Documenta Indica IV pp. 100–101] The royal alms for the conversion, he noted, had risen from 1,500 to over 1,700 pardaos. [n34; Documenta Indica IV p. 102] He also described a house where women who were preparing for baptism lived, and where they were married to converts, who were given “a way of living and help”. [n34; Documenta Indica IV pp. 101–102]

Trinity was near Thana, not in the Samvedi villages of the north, and it would be wrong to assume every village of the island was founded this way. [n34; §3] But it is the clearest account this region has of how a mission village worked. To a landless labourer in a year of hunger, the offer was a house, a field, a plough and oxen, a shirt and rice until harvest, and a wife if he lacked one. Set beside that the Gazetteer’s remark, from the Jesuit chronicle, that in those years “the lower Hindus sold their children to Musalmans and Christians”, and that “a child at the breast cost as much as a goat in Portugal”. [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 461 note 4] People who are selling their children do not need many sermons to accept a granary.

Francisco Cabral, 5 December 1562

Four years later, on 5 December 1562, Father Francisco Cabral wrote from Bassein to his superior, Father Laínez. His letter shows a different side of the problem: not the poor, but the landlords. [n34; Documenta Indica V doc. 90, pp. 628–631]

The Portuguese citizens of Bassein, Cabral explained, “all have villages”, aldeas, granted to them by the Crown. Until recently they had believed that if one villager became a Christian, the village would empty, so Christianity “was little favoured” among them. [n34; Documenta Indica V p. 628] Here is a fear worth understanding. A Portuguese landlord lived on the rents and labour of his Hindu tenants. If baptism split families and drove the unconverted to flee across the creek into Muslim or Maratha country, his income fled with them. The landlord’s interest and the missionary’s interest were, at first, opposed.

After the Jesuits’ preaching, Cabral said, the landlords “no longer hinder Christianity, at least publicly”. The phrase “at least publicly” is his own. And the change in the villages was dramatic. “The inhabitants who before would riot if a vassal of their villages was made [Christian] now come to beg us to go and convert their villages.” [n34; Documenta Indica V pp. 628–629] In a single baptism he had baptised about 196 people together, and more than 300 since arriving. (The number 196 is this book’s researcher’s reading of a damaged line of the printed text, and is uncertain.) [n34; Documenta Indica V p. 628]

Cabral then described the ceremony, and his description agrees closely with da Cunha’s hostile one, written three centuries later. The catechumens were kept at the college, men and women separately. Every night a lay brother “who knows the language” explained the faith to them. Then they were baptised in procession, with the captain and the whole city attending, with garlands, psalms and flutes, and afterwards they were feasted. Those who had been “married as gentiles are married again”. About eighty of the converts’ children were kept at the college to learn to read, write and sing, “and if they are taught they may become priests”. [n34; Documenta Indica V p. 629] A variant manuscript of the letter adds that at Thana the converts were lodged in a village and given “land to plough and oxen… all bought with the money the King gives for the Christianity”. [n34; Documenta Indica V pp. 630–631, apparatus; to be checked against the printed page]

So the hostile Goan of 1876 and the Jesuit of 1562 describe the same event: a procession with the captain, music, a feast. Da Cunha called it bait. Cabral called it the glory of the faith. Neither invented it.

Villages that asked

A few years later the Jesuit letters show villages asking for priests, at least partly on their own initiative. A letter of Father Gomes Vaz, written between 1566 and 1568, reports a settlement near Bassein that offered to maintain a priest “at their own houses”. The annual letter of the same years says the Jesuit residence at Maim near Bassein was founded “because the inhabitants asked with great insistence, not only the Provincial but the Viceroy and the Archbishop”. [n34; Documenta Indica VII] Maim is probably Mahim in the Bassein district, though that reading is uncertain, and neither letter names a Samvedi village. [n34; §3] But they show that by the late 1560s some communities were pressing for the church to come to them. Whether they pressed from faith, or because a church brought the protection, the tithe exemption and the favour of the captain, the letters do not say. Probably the villagers themselves could not have separated the two.

The law and its limits

Part One of this chapter followed the laws: the royal orders of 1546 and 1555, the orphan and temple decrees of 1559, the Council of 1567, the enforcement of 1581. [n37; Gaz. XIII-II pp. 462–463; Priolkar pp. 53, 76–78, 128] Mendiratta’s study adds a layer that matters for the villages of the north.

Between 1549 and 1557 a series of royal and viceregal orders known as the Provisões a favor da Cristandade, the “Provisions in favour of Christianity”, “contributed decisively towards conversion”, he writes, “severely restricting the freedoms of Hindu and Muslim inhabitants”. In 1549 the governor Jorge Cabral, who had been captain of Bassein, ordered that “no person of whatever quality shall rebuild a [Hindu] temple or a mosque”, and that “no person shall dare to prevent another from becoming Christian”. In March 1555 the viceroy Pedro de Mascarenhas ordered measures against the gentios, the Hindus, of the Bassein territory. [n34; Mendiratta pp. 210 and n.5]

The second clause of Cabral’s 1549 order deserves a moment. “No person shall dare to prevent another from becoming Christian.” Who would prevent a conversion? A father, a caste council, a village headman, or, as Francisco Cabral’s letter of 1562 shows, a Portuguese landlord afraid of losing his tenants. The law was aimed at all of them. It protected the convert against his own family and his own master.

But Mendiratta adds two limits that the older writers did not stress. The seizure of Hindu and Muslim orphans, he says, was enforced in the Province of the North only in or near the towns. And “as far as we know, the ‘Provisions in favor of Christianity’ were never enforced in the Northern Province’s subdivisions closest to the mainland”. [n34; Mendiratta pp. 210 n.5, 211] Nirmal, Agashi and the Samvedi villages lay in the Cassabé and Agaçaim subdivisions, not inside the walls of Baçaim. [n34; §3] If Mendiratta is right, the harshest instruments of the law, the orphan seizures above all, bore down hardest on families in and around the city, and much less on the garden villages of the north, where conversion must have come more through the landlord, the headman, the priest and the reward.

That does not make the north a place of free choice. The temples were still thrown down. At Nirmal, the old pilgrimage site of the Vimaleshvar linga, the Gazetteer records that “the Portuguese had pulled down the temples and destroyed the linga”, and a church of Santa Cruz, the Holy Cross, rose in the village. [n37; Gaz. XIV pp. 292–293] The temples of Agashi and Bassein were among the two hundred that António do Porto destroyed. [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 461 note 1] The healing tank by Our Lady of Remedies was closed to pilgrims under “very severe penalties”. [n37; da Cunha p. 163] After 1581 Hindu worship was no longer tolerated at all. [n35; Gaz. XIII-I pp. 201–202] [n34; Gaz. XIII p. 201] A Hindu family in Nirmal could keep its faith only in private, without its temple, its public festivals or, in law, its household images. Mendiratta’s phrase for the result is exact: Hindu and Muslim communities that did not convert “had to revert to low-key and mostly private religious practices”, while “Christian converts who were farmers or fishermen were meted out particular benefits”. [n34; Mendiratta p. 211]

Nor did the pressure go unnoticed by the people it fell on. Writing early in the next century, the traveller Goez reported that “the persecution of the Portuguese had made many Hindus, Mussulmans and Parsees abandon their homes and live in the dominion of Shah Jehan”, so that “between Bassein and Damaun there are few natives and the greater part of the village lands being uncultivated”. The Gazetteer dates his account variously to about 1603 or 1630 and adds that, if it was correct, “the districts soon recovered their prosperity”. [n37; Gaz. XIII-II pp. 463–464; da Cunha p. 143] It is the one contemporary glimpse in the notes of the people who answered the laws by leaving. The Parsis of Thana had done the same in 1533. [n35; Enthoven III pp. 192–195] Among the Panchkalshis, as we shall see, the memory of flight survived into the twentieth century.

The tithe and the fifteen years

Against the closing of the temples stood the opening of the granary. The Gazetteer’s sentence on the reward is worth reading again in the light of the Jesuit letters: “for the first fifteen years after conversion, the poorer class of Christians were freed from the payment of tithes and first fruits.” [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 462] Fifteen years is half a working life for a cultivator of the sixteenth century. A farming family that accepted baptism kept, for that span, the tenth of its harvest that the Church would otherwise have taken, along with its first fruits. Rodrigues’s oxen and seed, Cabral’s feast, and the fifteen free years were not separate policies. They were parts of one offer.

Three ways into the Church

The researcher who read the Jesuit letters for this book proposed a way of holding all this evidence together, and it is the most useful summary the notes offer. The letters show three different modes of conversion working at the same time. [n34; §3 Inferences]

The first was the livelihood offer, aimed at the poorest cultivators and labourers: Rodrigues’s “snares of human things”, the house and field and oxen, the rice until harvest, the fifteen years without tithe.

The second was the pageant, aimed at men of standing: the Brahman or the headman baptised in procession with the captain and the city, feasted, honoured, admitted into Portuguese society, with land on small quit-rents and the right to marry Europeans. [n37; Gaz. XIII-I p. 202] The Jesuits’ calculation, in the Gazetteer’s words, was that “if the Brahmans became Christians, many of the lower classes would follow”. [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 461]

The third was the landlord’s conversion of his tenants: the aldeas whose Portuguese owners first resisted and then, after the preaching, stopped hindering, and whose villagers came to “beg” for the priests. [n34; Documenta Indica V pp. 628–629] Mendiratta describes the same mechanism from the Indian side: “whole villages were converted, normally following the conversion of the respective ‘matará’ or headman, usually from the Prabhu caste, in the case of Salsette”. [n34; Mendiratta p. 211] A village followed its head.

Force, in the strict sense of baptism at the point of a weapon, is documented in the notes most clearly for orphans, under the orphan law, and for the treatment of Muslims, which the Gazetteer called “specially severe”. [n37; §3 Inferences; Gaz. XIII-I p. 201 note 2] No source this book could read records a mass forced baptism in Bassein by name. [n37; §3 Inferences] But the line between pressure and choice ran through the middle of every village. A man whose temple had been pulled down, whose public worship was forbidden, whose landlord and headman had both been baptised, and who was offered fifteen years without tithe, made a choice. It was not a free one.

Parashuram Joshi and the men who led

Seen in this light, the baptism of Parashuram Joshi on 8 September 1565 is a perfect instance of the second mode. A Brahman astrologer, a man whose profession was to read the stars for marriages and new houses, took the name Henrique da Cunha, and some 250 people followed him into the water, more than fifty of them Brahmans. [n04; Machado 1999 via Wikipedia] [n25; §1.8] The source is a single modern history, seen through Wikipedia, and it gives neither his village nor the church. [n25; Gaps]

His new name is itself a clue to the system, though it is a clue this book can only point to. A convert took a Christian name and a surname at baptism. Da Cunha was the surname of the governor who had signed the treaty of 1534. Whether Henrique da Cunha took his surname from a godfather of that family, from a patron, or from some other source, the notes do not say. But it shows, at least, that a convert of standing could leave the font carrying a surname that ranked among the highest in the province.

The other leading converts of the century whose names survive came from the same layer of society. The two heads of the Kanheri monastery became Paulo Rapozo and Francisco de Santa Maria, and Paulo Rapozo was given three villages. [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 461 note 1] On Salsette the headmen were usually Prabhus. [n34; Mendiratta p. 211] The Gazetteer’s list of the castes that made up the Christians of the coast begins with “Brahmans, Prabhus, Pachkalshis, Charkalshis, Sonars”. [n34; Gaz. XIII p. 200] The Prabhus, whom the Portuguese called Parabus, were in Portuguese usage the high-class Hindus of Bassein, the writers and clerks; and they were the very people whom the Council of 1567 barred Christians from partnering, lending to or sheltering. [n36; da Cunha p. 144 n.] [n37; da Cunha p. 142] For a Prabhu clerk in the 1560s, the road to office ran through the font.

But the Gazetteer adds, in the same breath, that “the bulk of them are Bhandaris, Kolis and Kunbis”. [n34; Gaz. XIII p. 200] The leaders were few. The followers were many.

A people is born: the Samvedi converts of the north

Out of these decades came the Christian communities of the island, and among them the people whose story runs through the rest of this book: the Samvedi Christians of north Vasai, known today as the Kuparis.

No document of the sixteenth century names them. No parish register, baptism record or Portuguese land register naming Samvedi converts has been found online; the Tombo de Baçaim and the parish books were beyond the reach of this book’s research. [n34; §1 Gaps] No source dates the conversion of any single Kupari village, or says whether the Samvedis were baptised in one wave or over generations. [n34; §3 Gaps] What we have instead is a set of later witnesses who looked at the villages and recorded what they saw, and from them we can reason back to the century of the baptisms.

Who the Samvedis were

Before the Portuguese came, the Samvedis were Brahmans of the Sama Veda living in the north of the island. The only documentary anchor for their tradition before the Portuguese is a land grant of 1026 in Salsette, made to Brahmans of the Chhandoga branch of the Sama Veda. [n35; §1, citing note 03] By the time British officials counted them, they were not priests at all. In 1872 the Gazetteer recorded 2,563 Hindu Samvedis, “found only in Bassein and Mahim”, husbandmen and gardeners, “strong, tall, and fair”, living in one-storied houses of wood and tile and speaking what it called “incorrect Marathi”. [n34; Gaz. XIII pp. 81–83] Enthoven’s ethnography of 1920 gave them a single line: “Samavedis are found only in the Thana district, where they chiefly grow garden crops.” [n34; Enthoven I p. 249] [n35; Enthoven I]

Their own tradition says they came from Utkala, Odisha, as temple musicians, and were restored to Brahman rank by a Shankaracharya at Nirmal. No scholarly source supports it. [n35; §1] One family at Bhuigaon told a blogger in 2011 that its ancestors came from Orissa and were entertainers, not farmers, and that part of the community converted under the Portuguese and took up farming. That is one family’s oral tradition. [n07; Mumbai Paused blog 2011] The linguist George Grierson, as we shall see, heard Gujarati in their speech, which points west and north rather than east. [n35; §1 inference] The question of where the Samvedis came from belongs to the Kupari chapters of this book. For 1534, what matters is that a community of Sama Vedi Brahmans, landholders and garden cultivators, lived in the villages between Agashi and Sopara when the friars arrived.

What the observers of 1882 saw

The strongest evidence that these Samvedis became Christians comes from two sentences in the Gazetteer, written about three hundred years after the conversions.

The first is in the entry for Sopara: “The husbandmen of Sopara are Christians, many of them converted Samvedi Brahmans; Musalmans…; and Hindus, chiefly Samvedi Brahmans and Bhandaris.” [n34; Gaz. XIV p. 315] [n37; Gaz. XIV p. 315]

The second is in a chapter on crafts, describing the drying of ripe plantains at Agashi, Vatar and Koprad. There, the Gazetteer says, lived “about eighty-five families of Samvedi Brahmans, fifteen families of Pachkalshis, and about seventy-five families of Native Christians originally Samivedis and Pachkalshis”. The Christians were “gardeners who grow the fruit”. [n34; Gaz. XIII-I p. 395]

Those two sentences say three things. The Christians of these northern villages were understood, in 1882, to descend from Samvedis, and partly from Pachkalshis. They lived side by side with Hindu Samvedis who had never converted: at Agashi, Vatar and Koprad the Hindu families slightly outnumbered the Christian ones. And they did the same work. The Samvedi community, in other words, had been split by the conversions, not wholly converted. [n37; §3 Samvedi Brahmin converts]

The Gazetteer’s descriptions of the two communities’ houses match closely. Hindu Samvedis lived in one-storied wood-and-tile houses; the Christians in “substantial tile-roofed houses with walls of wooden planks, mud, or brick and stone”. [n34; Gaz. XIII pp. 82, 204] Their ornaments shared names: the mugdya and the gathe appear in both lists, though the scanned text is rough. [n34; Gaz. XIII pp. 82, 205 n.1] Both communities guarded a newborn child against the same spirit. Among the lower orders of Christians, on the third or sixth night after a birth, gram was strewn at the door against the spirit Sathi; among the Hindu Samvedis, Sati was worshipped on the fifth day. [n34; Gaz. XIII pp. 82, 210–211] Three centuries after the friars, the Christian and Hindu households of the north were still keeping watch against the same night-spirit. That is not proof of common descent. It is good evidence of a shared village life that baptism did not end.

The case against

Not everyone agreed. Gerson da Cunha, writing in 1876, described the Catholics of Bassein as “pretty numerous clans of different types of the Kolis and Bhandaris… besides the hybrid product or bastard offspring of… a European soldier and a low-class native woman”. “There is no descendant of any high-class native amongst them,” he declared. [n34; da Cunha pp. 250–251] Da Cunha was a Goan Brahman scholar living in Bombay, at a time when Goan and local Catholics were rivals and the Church itself was divided between the Portuguese Padroado and Rome’s Propaganda. Mendiratta reads his contempt as rooted in that quarrel; da Cunha’s own page complains about “parochial” pamphlets written against Goans. [n34; Mendiratta p. 234; da Cunha p. 250] His sentence is a rival opinion from a hostile, caste-conscious contemporary, not a census. [n34; §1]

There is broader evidence on the same side, though it is not about Bassein in particular. The Census of 1931 judged that at least ninety per cent of the Indian Christians of the whole Bombay Presidency descended from the “Sudra section of Hindus”. [n34; Census 1931 Vol VIII Part I] The historian Kenneth Ballhatchet, quoted by the writer Elroy Pinto, found that by 1850 “it was primarily the cultivating classes or ‘low castes’ that were catholics in Konkan and Bombay”. [n34; Pinto 2021, quoting Ballhatchet] Mendiratta describes the Christians who stayed on in Salsette after 1739 as “overwhelmingly either fisherfolk or farmers, mostly of Koli, Kunbi or Bhandari castes”. [n34; Mendiratta p. 218]

The two pictures can be reconciled, and the notes suggest how. The Gazetteer’s own general sentence includes both: the Christians of the coast were “chiefly converted Brahmans, Prabhus, Pachkalshis and Kolis”, and “the bulk of them are Bhandaris, Kolis and Kunbis”. [n34; Gaz. XIII pp. 63, 200] A Brahman origin is well supported for the Christians of the northern garden villages, at Sopara, Agashi, Vatar and Koprad. A farming and fishing origin is well supported for most of the Christians of the coast as a whole. And even a Samvedi “Brahman” origin meant, by 1882 at least, descent from a landed farming community, not from priests. [n34; §1 Inferences] The phrase “converted Samvedi Brahmins” has real nineteenth-century support in the north, but it describes part of the Christian population of the island, not all of it.

A modern sociologist’s account, written for the cultural portal Sahapedia, puts the Samvedi converts first in time: “the first converts”, it says, were Samvedi Brahmins settled near the Vaitarna between Agashi and Nalasopara, and the Samvedi Christians were “the only Bassein group to receive Jesuit education”. [n34; Rodricks, Sahapedia] The article cites no sources in the text this book’s researcher could see. It fits the evidence that the Franciscans came to Agashi first, and that the Jesuits sought out Brahmans; but it is a synthesis, not a document. [n34; §1]

A village older than the Portuguese?

One more line in the Gazetteer is tantalising. “One recent report mentions a Christian village in Bassein that claims to be older than the Portuguese. No confirmation of this statement has been received.” [n34; Gaz. XIII p. 200 n.7] The Gazetteer’s main text was blunt: “No trace seems to be left either of the Nestorian or of the Latin converts” of the centuries before 1534. [n34; Gaz. XIII p. 200] Jordanus had found Nestorian Christians at Sopara in 1321. [n37; Gaz. XIV p. 322] Whether any memory of them survived in a Bassein village into the 1880s, or whether the claim was a village’s pride speaking, no one can now say.

Pachkalshis, Kolis, Vadvals, Agris and Bhandaris

In January 1583 the Koli king’s quarrel with Baçaim became a war. Couto says the farmers of the villages that paid foros to the Crown, “to live safe from this robber, paid him secretly a pension, which was two larins for each mura of paddy”, and that the complaints had reached the Viceroys “every day” for years. [n86 §1] Manuel de Saldanha, Captain of Baçaim, mustered “two hundred Arab horse, eight hundred foot soldiers, and five hundred Gentile peães of the obligation of the lands”, with “eight hundred musketeers” in all, and marched with ships’ captains from Goa through Agashi, Manorá and Asserim to meet the King of Sarzeta, whose two sons were his hostages. After fifteen days they burned the king’s city, “built at the end of a beautiful wide and straight plain”, and “burnt all the neighbouring villages, where many things were plundered, much cattle killed, and some farmers captured”. On the way back, in a pass “where scarcely two men can fit”, the Koli archers shot “from boulder to boulder like monkeys”, and the Portuguese were saved by the King of Sarzeta, who rode up crying “Fidalgo, my brother!” The Koli king was left “so shattered that for many years his people did not come back to re-form and sow their villages” and gave up “the imposition he wanted to put on the Portuguese villages”. [n86 §2–§3] A reference of 1728 to a grão-canil paid by Baçaim’s lands “to the Koli King” (Chapter 08) is probably the same old due. (Inference.)

The Samvedis were one stream among several. The other communities of the island entered the Church too, and their descendants form the other Christian groups of Vasai today.

The Pachkalshis, or Panchkalshis, the orchard-keepers of the coconut and palm wadis, appear in the Gazetteer’s Agashi sentence beside the Samvedis. Their own community account, recorded by Enthoven, remembered the Portuguese century darkly. “Under the Portuguese… there was much persecution, and this is said to have forced many members of the caste to fly to the outlying parts of the Southern Konkan.” Their descendants there are the Ashtagarkars. “Many were then converted to Roman Catholicism by force and their descendants may still be traced at Bassein and Salsette.” [n35; Enthoven III p. 162] That is a caste’s memory, told in the twentieth century; the word “force” is theirs. The same account adds something more practical. “Under the Portuguese many of them became skilled mechanics and carpenters (house and boat builders).” [n35; Enthoven III p. 163] A Portuguese city needed carpenters for its churches and houses, and Portuguese shipyards needed boatwrights. Some Pachkalshis found work in both.

The Vadvals, whose name “literally means the managers of wadis or orchards”, were a division of the Panchkalshis found in Bassein, Mahim and Dahanu, in country “formerly known as the Feringana”, a name that echoes Firangi, the Frank. [n35; Enthoven III pp. 160–161] They are said to have separated from the main body in the fourteenth century. [n35; Enthoven III p. 162] A Christian Vadval community descends from converted farming and fishing groups, according to a secondary source cited by a blog. [n07; Deconstruct Realities blog citing Nighoskar 2020] Today the Christian Vadvals of south Vasai and the Samvedi Christians of the north are distinct communities, with distinct dialects and, by community accounts, distinct temperaments. [n07; Vasaiker; Mumbai Paused] But the Gazetteer of 1882 counted “Samvedis and Pachkalshis” together among the Christian gardeners of Agashi, which suggests the line between them was not always sharply drawn. [n34; §1]

The Kolis of the shore became the Koli Christians, a subgroup of the Son Kolis converted in the Portuguese period, found in Bassein and Agashi as well as in Uttan, Gorai and Madh. Their community history says they helped build the churches and were valued as builders of boats and warships. [n37; Wikipedia: Koli Christians] [n07; Wikipedia: Koli Christians] Marathi Wikipedia says plainly that the Portuguese of Vasai converted Kolis who had been Hindus. [n20; mr.wikipedia] In 1882 “the Uraps and other Christian Kolis in Bassein” were professional fishermen. [n08; Gazetteer 1882, fisheries] They kept their jamat, the council of a hereditary patil and karbaris, and their engagement custom, the shakarpuda, which they share with Hindu Kolis. [n07; Wikipedia: Koli Christians] The Kolis were, the Gazetteer noted, the only Christians who kept a headman or caste council at all. [n34; Gaz. XIII pp. 207–210]

The Agris, salt-makers and rice-growers, and the Bhandaris, the palm-tappers, also had Christian sections. Enthoven noted that Bhandaris, Kolis and Kunbis “contain Christian sections that only marry within such sections”. [n34; Enthoven I p. 10] The Christian Agris of Bassein are visible to us mainly because many of them later left the Church: in 1820 and 1828 they were readmitted to Hinduism by Palshe priests, and became the Urap Agris, whose surnames alone still showed their past: Gomes, Soz, Fernan, Frutad, Minez. [n37; Gaz. XIII-I p. 117] [n34; Enthoven I] Among the Bhandaris, the lowest division, the Kirpals, were said to have been Christians who went back to the old religion after the Maratha conquest; the Census of 1911 said they had been “forcibly converted to Christianity by the Portuguese”. [n08; Gazetteer 1882 pp. 149–150] [n34; Census 1911 para 136] Those reversions belong to later chapters. But they show that the Christian Agris and Bhandaris were made in this century.

There were other converts in smaller numbers. The Bhois, Kumbhars, Nhavis and Dhobis among the Christians formed separate castes, and the Christian Mhars were in 1859 still living outside the villages, forbidden to draw water from the village well or to enter the church. [n37; Gaz. XIII-I p. 201 and note 1] Some isolated families of Kumbhars, Parits, Nhavis and Buruds are still found among the East Indians. [n34; Rodricks, Sahapedia] The Portuguese did not make converts give up caste, and caste identities persisted inside the new faith. [n34; Rodricks, Sahapedia] Baptism gave a Mhar a saint’s name. It did not give him a place at the village well.

Over the top of all of them sat the converts’ masters and kin from Europe and Africa: the mestiço children of Portuguese soldiers and Indian women, whom da Cunha sneered at, and the African house slaves “made Christians and sometimes raised to be priests”. The Gazetteer noted “two strains of foreign blood, the European and the Negro”, among the Christians of the district. [n34; Gaz. XIII p. 200 n.8] [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 460 note 3] One boy of mixed birth from this world, born in the 1550s, would become the most famous person the island ever produced. His story closes this chapter.

A name no document records

The people of the northern villages are called Kuparis today. It is a striking fact, and one this book must state plainly, that the name appears in no historical text.

The researcher who prepared this book’s primary-source notes searched the full text of fifteen primary and early sources for “Kupari”, “Kumpari” and “Cupari”: the 1882 Gazetteer volumes on Thana, Enthoven’s Tribes and Castes, Grierson’s Linguistic Survey of India, the Census reports of 1901, 1911 and 1931, both of Gerson da Cunha’s books, and four volumes of the Jesuit Documenta Indica. There were no hits at all. [n34; headline finding] The first attestations found were modern: a community blog of 2007, and Wikipedia. [n34; headline finding] The search was made in machine-read text, so a spelling no one thought of could still turn up. [n34; headline finding] But for now the record is silent.

What did the documents call these people instead? In the Gazetteer they are “Native Christians” and “Portuguese Christians”. Their upper classes called themselves Firgi, from Firangi, “Frank”, a European. The lower classes “call themselves by their caste name”. Hindus called them “Firangis and more often Kiristans”. [n34; Gaz. XIII pp. 199–200] Grierson recorded that their speech was “usually called Kinstav by the Hindus and Musalmans”. [n34; LSI VII] The native priests were called Kanarin, Canarese, “considered a term of reproach”. [n34; Gaz. XIII p. 203 n.1] A later label, Norteiros, people of the North, after the Court of the North, is given by Wikipedia for the converts of Portuguese rule; they became “Portuguese Christians”, and in 1887 they would rename themselves “East Indians”. [n04; Wikipedia: Bombay East Indians] Within that umbrella, Mendiratta notes, the Catholics of Salsette and Bassein’s subdivisions came to be labelled “rural” or “samvedi” East Indians, so that “Samvedi” itself may have broadened over time into a loose label for rural Catholics. [n34; Mendiratta p. 218; §1 Inferences]

Where, then, did “Kupari” come from? Two explanations circulate, and neither is documented.

The first, and the more often repeated, derives it from the Portuguese compadre, the father of one’s godchild, a co-parent; the feminine comadre would give “Kumari”. Wikipedia’s support for this is forum posts and a dead dictionary link. [n34; §2; Wikipedia: Kupari] [n07; Wikipedia: Kupari] A community blog of 2015 repeats it, glossing “Kumpari” as a child’s godfather. [n34; Post in Mumbai blog 2015] The word compadre is not in Dalgado’s great glossary of Luso-Asian words in any form matching Kupari, and no scholarly or period source this book could open states the derivation. [n34; §2 Gaps] It remains a plausible guess, made stronger by one fact that is documented, and that is taken up in the next section: these Christians really did use Portuguese words for godparents.

The second explanation is a folk etymology from Marathi kūp, a well: “those who drank water from the well”. It was proposed by an anonymous commenter on Wikipedia’s discussion page in 2007, who linked it to a legend of a priest dropping a host into a well, and said that Hindus use the word as an insult. Another editor asked for “a strong source”; none was given. [n34; §2; Talk:Kupari] It fits the well stories of the 1850s and 1880s as a sound-alike, and that makes it plausible as folklore. It is not evidence for the word’s origin, and many in the community find it offensive. [n34; §2 Inferences]

So the honest position is this. The Christians of the northern villages existed from the late sixteenth century. The name by which they are known today has no written history before the twenty-first. It may have been a spoken name, used by insiders or outsiders or both, that simply never reached print, as Kiristav and Firgi were used in different registers by different speakers. [n34; §2 Inferences] When this book calls the converts of the 1500s “the Kuparis’ ancestors”, it is using a modern name for an old people.

Godfathers, godmothers and Portuguese surnames

Every baptism of this century made two new relationships. One was between the convert and the Church. The other was between the convert and a godparent, a sponsor who stood beside him at the font. Who usually stood as godparent at the baptisms of sixteenth-century Bassein, whether fidalgos, priests, landlords or earlier converts, the notes do not say. They do show that the relationship took root deeply enough to keep its Portuguese name for three centuries.

In 1882 the Gazetteer described a Christian baptism in the Thana villages. The child, baptised between the eighth and the fifteenth day after birth, “is taken to the church by its godfather, padrinho, and godmother, madrinha”. “The mother never goes to the christening.” [n34; Gaz. XIII pp. 210–211] In 1905 Grierson listed among the Portuguese words in the Christians’ Marathi padron, godfather, and madron, godmother. [n34; LSI VII] If “Kupari” does come from compadre, it would name the community by the very bond that baptism created: the co-parenthood of godparent and parent. That remains unproven. But it is the right kind of explanation, because the godparent tie was one of the few Portuguese institutions that reached into every Christian household.

The surnames tell the same story from another angle. “Though Christian names were given to all alike,” the Gazetteer noted, the Portuguese treated converts of good birth and of low birth very differently. [n34; Gaz. XIII p. 202] Every convert received a saint’s name and a Portuguese surname. Where did the surnames come from?

The Census of 1931, describing the Christian coast around Bassein, gave one answer. “The Portuguese parcelled out the countryside among certain of their countrymen… With the advent of the missionary a great deal of mass conversion took place, not unaided by competition among the owners of these estates, and in many villages every tenant in the village was baptized and given the surname of the owner of the village, e.g., Demonte, Pereira, Ferreira, etc.” [n34; Census 1931 Vol VIII Part I pp. 363–364] Here are Cabral’s landlords of 1562 again, now competing to have their tenants baptised, and stamping their own family names on whole villages. The census was writing of the Koli fishing coast, but the mechanism it describes was general. [n34; §3]

Godparents, landlords and parish priests between them account for the Portuguese surnames of Vasai’s Christians. No source ties a particular family name to a particular Kupari village, and no source tells us which surname came by which road. [n34; §5 Inferences] But a Pereira or a Ferreira in the villages of the old island carries, in that name, the trace of an encounter in the Portuguese century between a convert and a sponsor, a priest or a master. And the names lasted longer than the faith in some families: the Urap Agris who went back to Hinduism in 1820 and 1828 were still Gomes, Soz, Fernan, Frutad and Minez long after. [n37; Gaz. XIII-I p. 117]

Words from the parish

Language changed more slowly than religion. The converts kept speaking the tongue of their villages, and the new faith added words to it.

What was that village tongue? Grierson’s survey, published in 1905, described the Hindu Samvedis’ speech as “a mixed dialect, the chief components of which are the current Marathi of Thana and vulgar Gujarati”. Its sounds were distinctive: ch became s and then h, so that “son” was hakra and “field” hit; the genitive ended in -ho or -ha; strong masculine nouns ended in -o, “as in Gujarati and Konkani”; the dative took -do. His verdict: “a mechanical mixture of Konkan Marathi and Gujarati”. [n34; LSI VII] [n22; LSI VII] He grouped Samvedi with the small dialects of the Vadvals and the Phudgis as three that “still have preserved many of the characteristic features of Gujarati Bhili”. [n22; LSI VII] That s to h shift is still alive: in Samvedi speech today the dried fruit sukeli is hukeli. [n20; mr.wikipedia: Samvedi boli] So the base of the Kupari dialect, whatever its deeper origins, was already a Marathi-Gujarati blend before any friar arrived.

The Portuguese layer came on top. Grierson recorded separately the speech of the Thana Christians, “usually called Kinstav by the Hindus and Musalmans”. It had “adopted some Portuguese words, such as pay, father, may, mother, mass… padron, god-father, madron, god-mother”, and even the English estate. Otherwise, he judged, it was “nothing else than the usual Marathi of the district”. [n34; LSI VII] [n22; LSI VII] The Gazetteer of 1882 agreed: the Christians’ home tongue was Marathi, “very little different from the Marathi spoken by the Kunbis and Kolis of the district”. “A few know and a considerable number understand an ungrammatical Portuguese, and among some of the higher families… Portuguese is the home speech. Latin is the language of the Church.” Sermons were in Portuguese or Marathi, and prayer books in both. [n34; Gaz. XIII pp. 204, 207]

Look at which words crossed over. Father and mother. The Mass. Godfather and godmother. These are the words of the parish and the family, not of the market or the fort. The Portuguese strand entered the Christians’ speech through the church, the catechism and the baptismal font, not through settlement or trade. [n34; §4 Inferences]

Modern speakers of what is sometimes called “Vasai Marathi”, the East Indian Marathi of the coast, still use mai and pai for mother and father, where standard Marathi has aai and baba. A chair is a kadair, from Portuguese cadeira; a window a zanel, from janela. A toast is sukhala. The Palm Sunday procession is the Ramacha Pass, from ramo, the branches, and passo, the steps. [n13; Free Press Journal, 14 Mar 2024] A Kupari community blog lists the rites of a life in words that are Portuguese at the root: Baltim for baptism, Komsar for confession, Cominyao for First Communion, Chrisma for confirmation, Yentar for a funeral, Bejimat for the blessing of a new house, Pirsao for the candlelit Lenten procession, and fogey, round Portuguese bread. [n07; D’Mello blog 2009] Those terms come from a single community source and are not checked against any older record. [n34; §5 Gaps] But they show where the borrowing went deepest: baptism, confession, burial, the blessing of a house, the procession, the bread.

Did the people of Baçaim ever speak a full Portuguese creole? There is some evidence that they did, at least in and around the city. The Indo-Portuguese varieties of the old Province of the North, from Daman to Chaul and Korlai, taking in Bassein and Bombay, are known to linguists as Norteiro, and the scholar Dalgado described them in 1906. [n22; Cardoso 2006] Wikipedia says the Basseinites spoke a distinct Norteiro creole that Dalgado described in detail, but the article is poorly sourced. [n22; Wikipedia: Norteiro people] Mendiratta writes that in the 1880s Indo-Portuguese creoles were “more widespread in Salsette and Bassein… and survived until later”, while most uneducated East Indians spoke either Marathi or creole. [n34; Mendiratta p. 241] A modern paper on the Kupari dialect describes its “Indo-Portuguese” ingredient as “now extinct”. [n22; Kumar et al. 2024] So there may once have been three tongues in the villages of the island: the Marathi-Gujarati speech of the gardens, a Portuguese creole of the city and its dependants, and the Latin of the altar. By 1905, in Grierson’s ears, the villages had settled back into Marathi, carrying a handful of parish words.

Old ways under new names

What else did the converts keep, and what did they gain?

They gained a calendar of feasts and the public drama of the faith. The Gazetteer says that passion plays were “introduced by the Jesuits about the middle of the sixteenth century”: a society of penitents was formed in 1551 and brought to Bassein in 1552 by Father Melchior Nunes Barreto. [n34; Gaz. XIII pp. 207–210] The first fruits of the rice harvest came to be blessed at the church. [n34; Gaz. XIII pp. 207–210] Brotherhoods, confraternities of the parish, came to attend funerals with candles. [n34; Gaz. XIII pp. 214–215] The village’s year, which had turned on Hindu festivals, began to turn on Christmas, Lent, Easter and the patron’s feast.

They kept far more than the missionaries wished. In 1882 the Gazetteer recorded that in outlying villages of Salsette and Bassein, secret offerings to Ganpati and Gauri and vows to Shitladevi, the smallpox goddess, were “said occasionally” to continue, though the priests disapproved. [n34; Gaz. XIII pp. 207–210; da Cunha pp. 249–250] The spirit Sathi was still guarded against on the sixth night. [n34; Gaz. XIII pp. 210–211] The healing tank by Our Lady of Remedies drew Hindu pilgrims again in da Cunha’s time. [n37; da Cunha p. 163] Lancilotto had warned in 1547 that many of the baptised “revert to their former paganism”. [n37; Priolkar p. 55] In the villages of the island, reverting and keeping were often the same thing: a family could hear Mass on Sunday and strew gram at the door on the sixth night after a birth, and see no contradiction.

The red cap is a test case. A Hindu writer of 2019 claims the Portuguese imposed red caps on male converts and red saris on women as markers. [n07; Swarajya 2019] The Gazetteer of 1882 records that Hindu Samvedi men wore “red broadcloth caps like Telegu Brahmans”, and that among Christians “fishermen wear red broadcloth caps”. [n34; Gaz. XIII pp. 82, 205] The red cap was worn by Samvedi men who had never been baptised. It looks less like a convert’s badge than like a regional cap of the Brahman farmers, which the Christian Samvedis simply kept. That is an inference from a single sentence, and it should be checked against old drawings or photographs; but it is a good example of how the evidence can turn a story around. [n34; §5 Inferences]

The village parishes

The baptisms needed churches, and the churches became the centres of new villages, or rather of old villages re-centred. Inside the walls stood the city’s churches; outside, in the gardens and paddy of the island, the parish churches went up one by one through the second half of the century. Their founding dates are among the most confused in this book.

The best-known list, repeated by local history sites and by Wikipedia, gives nine Portuguese-era churches outside the fort, all still in use: Nirmal and Remedi in 1557, Sandor in 1566, Agashi in 1568, Nandakhal in 1573, Papdi in 1574, Pali in 1595, and Manickpur and Merces in 1606. [n04; Wikipedia: Vasai Fort] [n09; Vasaiker; Goanet 2020] None of these dates is confirmed by a primary source in the notes. [n34; §9] One now is. The Augustinian chronicle in Silva Rego’s Documentação says that the parish of Nossa Senhora das Mercês in the cassabé of Baçaim, “almost half a league from the city”, was instituted in 1606 by Archbishop Primate Dom Frei Aleixo de Meneses, who gave it only the parochial status, since the church and houses belonged to the Augustinians, built by the prior Fr Sebastião de Sexas, with some gardens given to the order. It was formed, the chronicle says, by taking the houses of Christians from the parishes of São Tomé and Nossa Senhora dos Remédios, “where they could not be well assisted with the sacraments, because of the great extent of those Christendoms”. Another passage says one friar served the parish as both almoner and vicar “because of the great poverty of its people”. [n70 §2] Each village deserves a separate look.

Agashi. The primary record here is clear and earlier than the list. The Franciscans built their orphanage of Nossa Senhora da Luz at Agashi in 1535; it was destroyed in 1540; and the church was “built after the destruction” of it. [n34; da Cunha pp. 159–160; Gaz. XIV pp. 1–2] The list’s date of 1568 may be a rebuilding or a formal parish foundation; the sources do not say. The dedication is also disputed. The Gazetteer’s list of 1881 calls it St James, “St. Iagos”; one modern source calls it “Mother of Light”, perhaps remembering the orphanage of Our Lady of Light. [n34; Gaz. XIII p. 206 n.3] [n09; Wikipedia: Nandakhal] Agashi was the first place in the district the Franciscans visited. Its church is the oldest Christian foundation of the island outside the city.

Nirmal. The church of the Holy Cross, Santa Cruz, stands on a site sacred to Hindus long before: the Vimaleshvar linga that the Portuguese destroyed. [n37; Gaz. XIV pp. 292–293] Its founding date is given as 1557 or 1558, with one outlier at 1580. [n09; Vasaiker; Wikipedia: Nandakhal; Daijiworld] Da Cunha mentions a Shankaracharya of Nirmal, which shows how important the place had been to Hindu tradition. [n37; da Cunha p. 127] The Holy Cross replaced the linga; after 1739 Hindu temples would rise again beside the church, and the two faiths would share the village. [n37; Gaz. XIV pp. 292–293]

Remedi. Our Lady of Remedies, beside the healing tank and the Milagres Cross, is dated 1557 by local lists and 1577 by the GCatholic directory. [n09; Vasaiker; GCatholic] Twenty years separate the two dates, and the notes cannot choose between them.

Sandor. St Thomas the Apostle at Sandor is dated 1556 or 1566, and called the first parish of south Vasai outside the fort. [n09; Wikipedia: Sandor] It was the mother parish of Papdi and, with Merces, of Giriz. [n09; Wikipedia: Sandor] In 1881 its side walls were still sixteenth-century work. [n32; BG 36–37] The village had an older identity too: the Shilahara grant of 1255 to an astrologer, and the Muslim stone of 1558 naming “Nayak Laro, lord of the western ocean”. [n37; Gaz. XIV p. 402] In the same decade that a Muslim inscription was carved at Sandor, a Christian parish was being founded there.

Nandakhal. The Holy Spirit church at Nandakhal is said to have been built by Franciscans in 1573, and to be the only Holy Spirit church in the diocese. Because it survived the Maratha attacks of 1737–39, it is called the oldest surviving church of north Vasai. Before it had its own church, Nirmal’s priest looked after its people. [n09; Wikipedia: Holy Spirit Church Nandakhal] There is a puzzle here. The Gazetteer’s list of the nine churches of Bassein in 1881 has no Nandakhal; it has a Holy Spirit church at Koprad. [n34; Gaz. XIII p. 206 n.3] Both lie in the north of the old island. The later Nandakhal parish may be the same church that the Gazetteer listed under Koprad, but this book cannot confirm it. [n34; §3] No primary source confirms the 1573 date. [n34; §9]

Papdi. Our Lady of Grace at Papdi, today the cathedral of the Diocese of Vasai, is dated 1574. [n09; GCatholic] Its area, a Wikipedia article says, was carved out of the Sandor parish between 1574 and 1606. [n09; Wikipedia: Sandor] Gerson da Cunha named Nossa Senhora da Graça as one of three Bassein parishes that arose from the Jesuits; the others were São Tomé and São Miguel Arcanjo. [n37; da Cunha p. 234] If Papdi’s Grace is da Cunha’s Graça, and Sandor’s St Thomas his São Tomé, then the two neighbouring parishes south-east of the fort were Jesuit foundations, made among “a pagan community” as Rome required, in the decades of the processions and banquets. That identification is this book’s inference, as Part One noted.

Pali, Manickpur and Merces. Pali is dated 1595; Manickpur and Our Lady of Mercy at Merces 1606. [n09; Vasaiker] A Jesuit account, seen only through a summary, says the parishes of Remedy, Papdy, Palle, Merces and Porim were carved out between 1574 and 1606. [n04; Diocese of Vasai via search summary] The Gazetteer’s 1881 list gives Pali’s dedication as Mother of God and Manickpur’s as St Michael. [n34; Gaz. XIII p. 206 n.3] St Michael would be da Cunha’s third Jesuit parish, São Miguel Arcanjo.

Put the lists together and a pattern emerges, whatever the exact years. The Franciscans began in the north, at Agashi, in 1535. The great northern parishes of Nirmal and Agashi took shape by the 1550s and 1560s, with Nandakhal or Koprad following. In the south, around the fort, Sandor, Remedi and then the Jesuit parishes of Papdi and the others were founded through the 1560s and 1570s, and more were divided out down to 1606. Each parish was a church plus village land, granted to the orders or to fidalgos, and that grid of churches is why Nirmal, Agashi, Papdi, Sandor and Pali are Catholic villages to this day. [n37; §6 Inferences]

The buildings themselves have not survived from the sixteenth century, or barely. Nirmal’s church was rebuilt by its parishioners in 1856; Papdi’s in 1865; Pali’s in 1840; Sandor’s chapel in 1838 and its front in 1858; Manickpur’s in 1851. [n34; Gaz. XIV p. 293] [n37; Gaz. XIV pp. 35–36] [n32; BG 36–37] What survived was the parish: the boundary, the dedication, the feast, and the community that gathered there.

Agashi: timber, ships and shipwrights

While the friars baptised, the island went on working, and its most famous work was building ships.

The Portuguese account books show how quickly the new rulers set the old revenue machine running. In 1535, according to Gerson da Cunha, they took the first half-year’s revenue of the Bassein district: 158,475 fedeas, at twelve réis to the fedea, drawn from the duties on coconut oil, opium, cotton, toddy, vegetables, fish, sugarcane and betel, and from the professions of butchers, dyers, Koli fishermen and shepherds. [n36; da Cunha pp. 158–159] Agashi had its own fortress with a custom house. Its captain drew 30,000 réis a year and its customs clerk 20,000. [n36; da Cunha pp. 158–159] Agashi, burnt with its boats only five years earlier, had become a Portuguese customs post with a salaried staff.

Its wealth was timber, from the forests of the hills behind the coast. The Gazetteer calls it “probably an old timber trade and ship-building centre”, which “though poor in buildings… had a rich timber trade, and built ships as good as Portuguese ships, able to make the voyage to Europe”. It gives that description for 1630, with the burning of the town and its three hundred boats in 1530 as the earlier fact; Part One noted the confusion over whether the shipbuilding sentence belongs to 1530, 1580 or 1630. [n37; Gaz. XIV pp. 1–2] [n36; Gaz XIV pp. 1–2] The Gazetteer’s history volume is clearer about the century of this chapter: “Between 1550 and 1600 great ships built at Agashi and Bassein made many voyages to Europe.” [n36; Gaz XIII-II pp. 468–469] The Gazetteer draws this from the Portuguese chroniclers, and the claim has not been independently checked. [n36; Gaps] If it is right, the creeks of the island were launching ocean-going ships for the Lisbon run in the same decades that its villages were being baptised.

What did a ship of this coast look like? The chronicler Gaspar Correa described the craft of the Konkan, and the Gazetteer preserved his account: planks either sewn together with coir thread, on keeled boats, or nailed, on flat-bottomed ones; pitched cloth and cane mats fixed against the sea; cargo chambers roofed with woven palm-leaf; one large mast with a long lateen sail, so that the boat could be steered close to the wind. [n36; Gaz XIII-II pp. 468–469] Those were the local coasters. The “great ships” for the European voyage must have been built more in the Portuguese manner, though the notes do not describe them.

Who built them? The notes give no names of master shipwrights for this century. In 1634 a “master builder” at Bassein drew £3 16s. [n36; Gaz XIV p. 31] But the communities can be named. The Panchkalshi account says that “under the Portuguese many of them became skilled mechanics and carpenters (house and boat builders)”. [n35; Enthoven III p. 163] The Koli Christians’ community history says they were valued as builders of boats and warships. [n07; Wikipedia: Koli Christians] A modern study cited by the archaeologist Shah records ships of up to 500 tons built at Agashi for both the Portuguese and, later, the Marathas. [n02; Shah 2020, citing Arunachalam 2002] So the yards of Agashi and Bassein brought together the forest timber of the hills, the carpentry of the orchard-keepers and the sea-knowledge of the Kolis, under Portuguese masters and Portuguese orders. Some of the men who knelt in the new parish churches on Sunday may well have been the men planking hulls in the creek on Monday.

The timber trade was valuable enough to corrupt. On 13 March 1587 the King of Portugal wrote complaining that the captains of Bassein and Daman supplied timber for the royal fleet “from captains themselves, who charged far higher prices than their value in the market”, because they traded on their own account. [n36; da Cunha p. 144] The Gazetteer’s verdict is flat: “The Bassein timber trade was chiefly carried on by the captains of forts and other government officers.” [n36; Gaz XIII-II p. 465] The salary of the three-year captaincy, 600,000 réis in about 1550, was not the only income a captain could draw from his post.

The rest of the island’s trade followed the same pattern. “Under the Portuguese,” the Gazetteer says, “foreign trade was a monopoly of the king. Most of the local sea trade was in the hands of free-traders or interlopers, whom the Portuguese government tried to put down.” [n36; Gaz XIII-II p. 465] After the middle of the century the Konkan’s old share of the commerce with Europe moved south to Goa and north to Diu. But Bassein, Mahim, Thana and Chaul “maintained a large coasting traffic with the Malabar, Gujarat, and Sindh ports, and a considerable foreign trade with the Persian Gulf, the Arabian and African coasts”. [n36; Gaz XIII-II p. 465] The Gazetteer lists what moved in the Portuguese period. Out went rice, pulse, vegetables, coconuts and betelnuts; basalt columns from Bassein to Goa; “great quantities of the finest teak… to Goa, Gujarat, Sindh, and occasionally to the Persian Gulf and the Red Sea”; cotton cloth and muslins, blankets and silks. In came dates and raisins, Spanish wine, silks, gold from Sofala, ivory from Abyssinia, spices from Malabar, Ceylon and the islands further east, elephants from Ceylon, slaves from Abyssinia, and horses from the Persian Gulf and the Arab coast. Pilgrims went out to Mecca. [n36; Gaz XIII-II pp. 465–468] As early as 1538 João de Castro had noticed the basalt columns across the water from Bassein. [n36; Gaz XIII-II pp. 465–468] The merchants were changing too: the Gazetteer notes “the disappearance of the Chinese, and the decrease of Arabs and Turks”, and the arrival of Portuguese and, later, other Europeans. [n36; Gaz XIII-II p. 468]

Even so, the Gazetteer says that Bassein was “never so busy a trade centre as Chaul”. It was “famous for its ship building”, and in the 1580s traded in rice, corn and “timber for ship building”. [n36; Gaz XIV pp. 30–31] Portuguese Baçaim was a capital, a fortress, a shipyard and a timber and stone depot more than it was a great market. Its wealth came from the land and forests of its villages and from the noble families who held them. [n36; §4 Inferences] That is one more reason the conversion of the villages mattered so much to its rulers. The villages were the city’s foundation.

Horses and passes

Part One left the horse trade almost invisible. The text of the treaty itself, as F. C. Danvers printed it, brings it into view.

Sultan Bahadur gave Bassein “with all its revenues” to Portugal, “provided that all the vessels from the kingdom of Guzerat, bound for the Straits, should first call at Bassein for passes, and on the return voyage call there again in order to pay the customary dues, under penalty and risk of seizure”. “No ships of war were to be built at any of the ports in the kingdom of Guzerat, but any number of trading ships might be constructed.” [n36; Danvers vol. I p. 416]

Then the horses. “All horses from the Straits of Mecca, and from other parts of Arabia, during the first three years after the completion of the fortress of Bassein, were to proceed thither, in order that Sultan Bedur and his vassals might purchase them there on payment of the customary dues to the King of Portugal, and they were not to be taken to the Deccan, Canara, or Malabar.” [n36; Danvers vol. I p. 416]

Read slowly, that clause is a small masterpiece of power. For three years after the fort was finished, every Arabian horse coming to this coast had to be landed at Bassein. The Sultan of Gujarat and his nobles could buy them there, but only by paying duty to the King of Portugal. And no horse was to go on to the Deccan, to Canara or to Malabar, which meant to the other Indian powers, the sultanates of the Deccan and the kingdoms of the south, whose cavalry depended on imported war-horses. The treaty made Bassein a horse market for one buyer, and a customs house that took a cut of every sale. It also denied horses to everyone else. The research note that reported the clause drew the obvious conclusion: the cartaz regime made Bassein a chokepoint for Gujarat’s Red Sea trade, which is why the treaty names Bassein specifically, and why the horse trade was pinned to it for three years. [n36; §4 Inferences]

What happened after the three years is not in the notes. Horses from the Persian Gulf and the Arab coast appear among the imports of the North Konkan ports in the Portuguese period, so the trade went on. [n36; Gaz XIII-II pp. 465–468] But no figures, no prices and no named horse-dealers of Bassein survive in the sources this book could read. [n36; Gaps]

The pass itself, the cartaz, was the larger instrument. A modern summary describes the system: every non-Portuguese merchant vessel had to buy a pass; under the 1534 treaty, Gujarati ships bound for the Red Sea had to take theirs at Bassein and pay duties on their return; other voyages took passes but did not have to pay duties there; coastal trade needed no cartaz. [n36; GKToday; Gateway House] That summary comes from search results the researcher could not open, and the treaty’s own wording is the firmer evidence. [n36; §4] But it explains why the town filled with clerks, interpreters and brokers, the very offices that the Council of 1567 reserved for Christians. [n37; da Cunha p. 142; Priolkar p. 53] The Prabhu writer who wanted to keep his desk at the custom house, writing passes for Gujarati shipmasters, now had to be baptised to do it. The sea and the font were connected.

A boy from Baçaim: Gonsalo Garcia (c. 1556–1597)

Into this world, in the middle of the century, a boy was born who would carry the name of Bassein to the other end of Asia.

His name was Gonsalo Garcia. He was born in 1556 or 1557, according to most modern accounts; the Catholic Encyclopedia gives about 1564, and another source agrees on the later date. [n07; Wikipedia: Gonsalo Garcia] [n04; Catholic Encyclopedia] [n07; §6 Inferences] His father was a Portuguese soldier who later stayed on as a civilian employee in the fort. His mother was a local woman. That made him a mestiço, a child of the two worlds that the treaty of 1534 had brought together. [n07; Wikipedia: Gonsalo Garcia] Documents in the Portuguese national archive in Lisbon, the Torre do Tombo, describe him as “natural de Agaçaim”, a native of Agashi, and call his mother a “Canarim”. [n07; DNA, 12 Jan 2014] [n25; §1.1] Canarim was the word the Portuguese used for the Indians of the Konkan; by the nineteenth century, applied to native priests, it was “considered a term of reproach”. [n34; Gaz. XIII p. 203 n.1] So Gonsalo was, by the reckoning of his own time, half Portuguese and half Canarim, a boy from the Franciscans’ first village on the island.

Tradition also links his birth to the fort, because it was in the fort that he went to school. Some local claims place it at Gass. The Lisbon wording points to Agashi. [n07; §6 Inferences] [n25; §1.1 Conflicts] The notes do not resolve it, and nobody disputes that he was a child of the Bassein district.

He was educated at the Jesuit school inside the walls of Baçaim and served at the Church of the Holy Name of Jesus, the Jesuit church whose foundation Melchior Gonçalves had laid in 1548. [n07; Wikipedia] [n32; BG 41] He was taught there by Father Sebastião Gonçalves, who became his mentor; whether this man was related to Melchior Gonçalves, the sources do not say. [n25; §1.7] So the boy grew up inside the institutions this chapter has described: the Jesuit college with its school for converts’ children, its catechumens lodged by night, its processions with the captain. He would have seen the great baptisms of the 1570s, when 1,600 were baptised in 1573, from inside the college that organised them. [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 461] That last is an inference from dates; no source describes what he saw.

Then he left for Japan. Most accounts say he went in 1580; one says 1572. [n07; Wikipedia] [n25; §1.1 Conflicts] The Archdiocese of Bombay says he went at about the age of fifteen, and that the Jesuits refused to admit him to their order. [n07; Archdiocese of Bombay] In Japan he served for eight years as a catechist, a lay teacher of the faith. [n07; Wikipedia] How a boy from the Konkan made himself understood in Japan, the notes do not say; but he would later serve as an interpreter.

His next turn was into trade. He became a merchant in Macao, on the China coast. [n07; Wikipedia] Then he moved on to Manila, and there joined the Franciscans as a lay brother: not a priest, but a member of the order. [n07; Wikipedia; Archdiocese of Bombay] It is a fitting turn for a native of Agashi, where the Franciscans had begun their mission on the island. In 1592 he returned to Japan with Father Pedro Bautista, the leader of the Franciscan mission there, as his interpreter. [n07; Wikipedia] [n25; §1.2] He is said to have helped build churches, convents and hospitals in Macao and in Osaka. [n07; Wikipedia; Archdiocese of Bombay]

The end came quickly. On 8 December 1596, after what the sources call the San Felipe incident, he was arrested. He walked about six hundred miles from Kyoto to Nagasaki, arriving on 4 February 1597. On 5 February 1597, on Nishizaka hill above Nagasaki, Gonsalo Garcia was crucified, aged about forty. [n07; Wikipedia] He died with twenty-five companions, among them Pedro Bautista and Paul Miki: the group remembered as the Twenty-Six Martyrs of Japan. [n09; Wikipedia; Archdiocese of Bombay] [n25; §1.2]

The Church moved quickly too, by its own standards. Pope Urban VIII beatified him in 1627; Wikipedia gives the date as 14 September 1627. His veneration was extended to the universal Church in 1629. He was canonised by Pius IX on 8 June 1862. [n07; Wikipedia] He is called India’s first saint, the first Indian-born person to be canonised. [n25; §1.1] He is the patron of the Diocese of Vasai, the second patron of the Archdiocese of Bombay, and the patron of the East Indian community. A statue of him was taken as far as Recife, in Brazil, in 1745. [n07; Wikipedia; Archdiocese of Bombay]

His feast is a small history of its own. His universal feast is 6 February, because 5 February, the day of his death, belongs to St Agatha. The Indian bishops moved it to 7 February, to keep it apart from St Paul Miki and companions. [n07; Archdiocese of Bombay] But in the fort where he went to school, his feast follows the sea. It falls on “the first Sunday nearest the neap tide after Christmas”, and the young men of the Koliwada decorate the old church for it. [n07; DNA, 12 Jan 2014] On Sunday 11 January 2026 it was held, as every year, in the roofless Jesuit Church of the Holy Name inside Vasai Fort: the feast locals call Killa Cha Sann, the fort’s festival, with hundreds of worshippers and Fr Peter Fernandez presiding, under an arrangement by which the Archaeological Survey of India allows a Sunday service in the ruin. [n07; Free Press Journal, 11 Jan 2026] [n09; FPJ 2026] Families arrive the evening before and camp in enclosures, cooking East Indian moile and stew. [n09; FPJ 2026] At Gass, a church named for him was built in 1942, as a parish divided from Nirmal. [n07; Catholics India] [n25; §1.1]

His life holds this chapter’s whole world in one person. He was the son of a Portuguese soldier and a Canarim mother, born in the Franciscans’ first village, schooled by the Jesuits in the walled city, a merchant on the China coast and a friar of Manila. He died, as the Agashi orphans of 1540 had died, for a faith that had reached his mother’s coast within living memory. The Portuguese who built Baçaim wanted to make its people over in their own image. In Gonsalo Garcia, a son of the island went out and carried that image further than any of them.

Baçaim at the turn of the century

In 1599–1600 the Viceroy, told that “the works of the fortification of the city of Baçaim were going very slowly, though sufficient money was applied to them”, made Aires da Silva de Melo, then an alderman, superintendent of the walls with powers over the officers, “with which the walls of that city grew before one’s eyes”. [n90 §6] Couto also notes in 1599 two brothers, Dom Manuel and Dom Rodrigo de Castro, “sons of Baçaim, whom in India they call the Mangaritos”. [n90 §6]

Couto, describing the Portuguese northern provinces in a late Década, gives the city’s jurisdiction as “the tanadarias and fortresses … Assari [Asserim], Manorá, Agaçaim as far as Bandorá, Taná and Curanjá, with the astonishing island of Salsete”, which “by the proud and rare pagodes it has shows that it was once the head of all these kingdoms”, and calls the city “one of the best and best-populated of all those of India, because there are in it many principal fidalgos with rents and very large villages by which they are sustained”. [n82 §3]

By 1600 the work of the sixty-six years was done, or as done as it would ever be.

The city had all four great religious orders inside its walls, the Misericórdia with its hospital, the Jesuit college with its library and its school, a seminary for secular clergy and the College of the Purification for noble children. [n37; Gaz. XIII-I pp. 201–202; Hull vol. 1] Its fort of the 1590s was described as a strong stone wall with eleven bastions mounting ninety pieces of artillery, defended by twenty-one gunboats. [n36; Gaz XIV p. 30] The Gazetteer, citing the Portuguese chroniclers, called it “next to Goa the largest and richest of the Portuguese settlements”, with more than a hundred families “of the highest in India and proverbially rich”. [n15; Thana Gaz. XIV pp. 30, 33] Its ladies would one day subscribe 200,000 xerafins to build a nunnery in Goa. [n36; Gaz XIV p. 29 n.6]

Outside the walls the villages had changed beyond recognition, though they still grew the same plantains, betel and rice. The temples of Agashi, Bassein and Nirmal were gone. Sopara’s stones were in the city walls. [n37; Gaz. XIV p. 323] In their place stood parish churches at Agashi, Nirmal, Remedi, Sandor, Papdi, Pali and the north. Much of the land belonged to fidalgos and the orders, and the families who tilled it held no right to it. [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 460] The people were Christian in name, most of them; many of them Christian in practice too; and some of them still strewing gram at the door against the night-spirit, or slipping away to a goddess in a bad year. The Hindus who had not converted kept their gods in private, or left for the lands of Shah Jahan. [n37; Gaz. XIII-II pp. 463–464]

The converts of the province now carried names like Henrique da Cunha. The speech of the villages had taken in padron and madron, pai and mãe, and the Mass. A people had been made whose descendants would one day be called Kuparis, though no one in 1600 used the word. And the island’s own first saint had just died on a cross at Nagasaki.

Ahead lay the city’s golden age, and its slow fall. In 1601 the mother church of St Joseph would be rebuilt, and a lintel carved with the name of the Archbishop of Goa and the year. [n32; DC 214–216; BG 38–39] In 1618 plague, a cyclone and famine would come in a single year. [n04; Wikipedia: Vasai Fort] The Mughals, the Arabs of Muscat and, above all, the Marathas would press on the creek-moat from every side. The Court of the North was about to reach its height, and to begin the long road to May 1739, when the mines would bring down the bastion of São Sebastião that Francisco de Sá had finished in 1554. That is the story of the next chapter.
