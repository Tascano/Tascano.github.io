Period covered: 1947 – November 2026

In this chapter. The Kuparis in the republic: the parish multiplying, the school bench, the train south, the Gulf, and the fight for the land.

In 1923 a priest at Nandakhal opened an English school. He was Mgr Francesco de Silva, and in the year before he had cleared the forest round the church of the Holy Spirit and planted it with coconuts. The school did not last. The parish’s own history, as it has come down to us through Wikipedia, gives the reason in a single sentence that it attributes to the villagers: “we get bread and butter from farming and not by studying”. The school closed for lack of pupils. [n40 §A3; Wikipedia: Holy Spirit Church Nandakhal]

The same parish history then records, in its plain way, a different century arriving. Marathi schools from 1935. St Joseph’s school in 1939, which another note attributes to Fr Raymond Mendes. On 5 January 1943, the ordination of the parish’s first local priest, Rev. Frank Lobo, “after 370 years”. Nuns of the Queen of the Apostles congregation in 1952. And finally, in a count the parish made in December 2016, a sentence that would have been unimaginable to the farmers of 1923: “this day Nandakhal parish has sent 68 priests and many nuns”. [n40 §A3; n28; Wikipedia: Holy Spirit Church Nandakhal] The figure is the parish’s own, unaudited, and the book gives it as that. But the direction of travel is not in doubt. In the eighty years after independence the Kuparis, the Samvedi Christians of the northern garden villages of Vasai, went from people who did not need a school to people who sent their sons and daughters to the seminary, the university, the office in Bombay and the airport.

This chapter tells that story from the inside of the community, as far as the record allows. The book’s author was born in one of these villages, Gass, and the temptation in a chapter like this is to fill the silences with family memory. The rule of this book forbids it. What follows rests on the research notes: on parish histories, diocesan statistics, a handful of newspaper reports, two community blogs, a Marathi dialect article, a farmer’s interview, an environmental survey, and the life records of two bishops. Where the evidence is a community’s own account of itself, the text says so. Where there is no evidence at all, the text says that too. The general history of the coast in these decades, the green-belt fight, the Thakurs, the corporation, the floods and the collapsing buildings, is told in Chapters 14, 14 and 15. Here the same years are seen from the Kupari side: from the parish, the wadi, the school bench, the railway platform and the kitchen.

The silence of 1947

What the record says, and what it does not

There is no Kupari account of 15 August 1947. The research notes found no Vasai-specific event for the independence or partition months at all, no local event for the long campaign for Samyukta Maharashtra, and nothing on the part, if any, that the Christian villages of the north played in the freedom movement. [n28 §1947–1989; n20 §Freedom struggle] The Marathi web sources searched for Quit India, the Salt Satyagraha at Agashi and Vasai freedom fighters returned nothing specific. [n20] Chapter 35 closed by listing this among its open questions. It remains open.

That silence is itself a fact worth stating. For the Kupari villages, the end of colonial rule in 1947 did not come as a single change of masters, as it had in 1739 when the Portuguese gentry and clergy sailed for Goa and the Marathas rode in. The things that mattered most in a Kupari village in 1947, the parish, the wadi, the well and the train, were not handed over on that day. The parish had already belonged since 1928 to the Archdiocese of Bombay, after more than three centuries under Goa and four decades under the Diocese of Daman; the bishops changed in 1886 and in 1928, but the priests, the churches and the feast days ran on unbroken. [n38 §3] The land was farmed as before. The trains ran to Bombay as before; the line to Virar had been electrified in 1936. [n28 §1930s]

The old frame: villages, gram panchayats and a market

What the record does show is the administrative frame within which the villages lived for the next six decades. Until 2009 almost all of the Kupari villages were governed as gram panchayats. A heritage survey of 2008, made by the Collective Research Initiatives Trust (CRIT), lists the gram panchayats of the plantation zone as it found them, among them Agashe, Vatar, Kofrad (Koprad), Bolinj, Umbrale, Nale-Rajodi, Vagholi, Madres, Navale, Ghas, Nirmal, Bhuigaon Khurd and Bhuigaon Budruk. [n40 §2; CRIT Vol I] “Ghas” is Gass. The same survey dates the Nirmal Bazaar, on the Nirmal–Kalamb road in Vagholi gram panchayat, to 1957. [n40 §A1, §A7; CRIT Vol I item 69] It is one of the very few dated civic facts for the Kupari belt in the first decade of the republic.

The districts above the villages changed several times. Bombay State was split on 1 May 1960 into Maharashtra and Gujarat; Bassein, by then usually written Vasai, stayed in the Thana (Thane) district of Maharashtra. [n28 §1960s] On 1 August 2014 it became a taluka of the new Palghar district. [n29 §3] None of these changes is recorded as having altered the life of a Kupari village in any way the notes can show.

The voters

The Kuparis voted, as every adult citizen did, in the elections that began in 1952. The research notes cannot say how. No source breaks down any election in Vasai by community, and this chapter will not guess. What the record gives is the list of winners in the Bassein, later Vasai, assembly seat: Sadanand G. Warty of the Socialists in 1952 and 1957; Hari Govindrao “Bhausaheb” Vartak of the Congress in 1962, 1967 and 1972; Pandharinath Choudhari of the Janata Party in 1978; Tarabai Vartak of the Congress in 1980; and in 1985 a Janata Party candidate named Dominic Gonsalves. [n26 §1; Wikipedia: Vasai Assembly constituency] In 1990 Hitendra Thakur, then of the Congress, beat a Janata Dal candidate named Dominik John Gonsalvis by 46,468 votes. [n29 §1] The notes have no biography of either Gonsalves, and so cannot say whether either came from the Kupari belt. The Christian name on the ballot is the only trace the record keeps of a community that made up a visible share of the taluka’s voters. [n26 §1 Gaps]

The parish multiplies

From one mother church to a family of parishes

The clearest record of the Kupari villages after 1947 is not in the census or the newspapers but in the parish lists. They show, decade by decade, a community growing large enough and confident enough to ask for churches of its own.

In 1882 the northern Kupari villages had three parishes: Holy Cross at Nirmal, with a congregation of 2,184; St James at Agashi; and the Holy Spirit church, which the Gazetteer placed at Koprad and which stands on the Nandakhal road. [n34 §3; Gazetteer XIII p. 206 n.3; XIV p. 293] Every other Kupari village walked to one of these on Sunday. In the twentieth century the walk got shorter.

Gass was first. The village petitioned in 1941, and in 1942 became the parish of St Gonsalo Garcia, cut from Nirmal, with a Fr Thomas as its first parish priest; the church was expanded or renovated in 1957 and blessed on 8 March 1962 by Cardinal Gracias, Archbishop of Bombay. [n40 §A6; East-Indians.net, Ghass; n42 §5] Its story belongs to Chapter 26. Bhuigaon came next. “Out of the thousand Catholics of the village,” the parish’s own account says, “only a handful attended services at Nirmal.” Fr Raymond Mendez began catechism classes there; a simple church went up in 1946 on land donated by the villagers, with money from the Archbishop of Bombay and the parishes of Bandra; Bhuigaon became an independent parish on 2 June 1946, under Fr Acuico Dourado. A single-storey structure took seven years from 1948, and Fr Nazareth Garbu finished the present Holy Family church in 1984. [n40 §A4; East-Indians.net, Bhuigaon]

Nandakhal’s own children followed. Mass was said at Umrale and Nanbhat from 1949; Umrale became the independent parish of St Joseph in 1970, and Nanbhat, as the parish of St Paul, on 1 June 1975, “due to the efforts of Monsignor Philip Tavares”. A centre opened at Bolinj on 10 May 1988 and at Jeladi in 1990; the Rajodi church was inaugurated on 1 May 1999; Bolinj became a separate parish, Christ the King, on 10 June 2001. [n40 §A3, §A9, §A10, §A11; Wikipedia: Holy Spirit Church Nandakhal] The Agashi side produced its own centres too: a parish community at Shirlai, Our Lady of Lourdes, is listed for 1987, and one at Jyoti, Our Lady of Light, which took the name of the Franciscan church destroyed four centuries before. [n40 §A2]

The newest of the Kupari parishes came out of Nirmal. A cluster of five hamlets in its north-west, Gomes Ali, Chowkala, Vanda, Rambai and Darseng, had their first Sunday Mass on 2 September 1979, said by Fr Edwin Colaco, then vicar of Nirmal and later Bishop of Amravati, in the primary local board school at Chowkala. Don Bosco Sisters came on 31 January 1992. In 2001 the cluster formed a committee of seventy-two “servant-leaders”; Bishop Thomas Dabre blessed the foundation stone on 2 December 2001; the church and parish house were blessed on Trinity Sunday, 25 May 2002; and on 5 June 2004 the cluster became the Parish of the Most Holy Trinity, with Fr Johnson Menezes as its first parish priest. [n40 §A1, §A5; East-Indians.net, Nirmal]

These dates come mostly from parish pages and from a community website’s “East Indian Calendar” extracts, written from inside the Church and rarely sourced. [n40, source key “C”] Some conflict with one another; Bolinj’s church is dated 2000 in a directory and 2001 in the parish history. [n40 §A11] But taken together they show a pattern that no other source captures so well. Between 1942 and 2004 the old three-parish map of the Kupari north became a map of roughly a dozen parishes and centres. Each new parish meant a village large enough to fill a church, a congregation willing to give land and money, and priests enough to serve it.

Two counts from the 1960s

The only parish-by-parish counts of the two largest village communities come from Elsie Baptista’s The East Indians (1967), whose full text has now been found online; the first version of this chapter could not read it. [n60 §4] The counts come from her fieldwork. The pages read do not date them, and an editor’s footnote says they were already “in need of revision”. Baptista calls the community this book calls the Kuparis the Samvedi Christians. The scanned text does not use the word Kupari. She counted them in five parishes of the northern half of Bassein, “together with their Hindu counterparts, in large villages in close proximity to the river Vaitarna”. [n60 §4]

The two tables measure different things from the parish counts of 1889–91 in Chapter 42: they count one community in each parish, not all its souls, so they cannot simply be subtracted from each other. [n60 §2, §4] But they show the same geography: the Samvedi Christians in the north around the Vaitarna, the Vadvals in the south, and Nandakhal larger than all the other Samvedi parishes together.

Baptista’s account of the Vadval village council is the fullest in the notes. Every head of a family with its own hearth (choola) belongs to the village assembly, the gao, and “to be out of this council is to be out of the group”. A woman who is mistress of her own choola sits too, as a gaokari, the men being gaokars. A gao may cover one vadi or several. Each elects a president (adhikari), “the wisest man of the village, known for his integrity and impartiality”, and a secretary (chitnis), the best writer among them. The gaos do not meet together, unlike the jamats of the Koli Christians. [n60 §4]

On origins, Baptista says the Samvedis “evidently migrated to Sopara between 700 B.C. and 300 B.C.”, that the Hindu and Christian Samvedis alike hold the Vaitarna, Tungari and Nirmala mahatmyas sacred, and that neither group “seem[s] to possess any written record of their early history”. Her footnotes rest this on the Gazetteer and other secondary works, so it is tradition, not evidence. For the Vadvals she follows Enthoven: Pachkalshis or Somvanshi Kshatriyas, a warrior class driven by political change to farming and carpentry, the farmers settling in Bassein as Vadvals, “managers of vadis”, the others in Salsette as Sutars. [n60 §4]

What the parish was

For a Kupari village the parish was more than a place of worship. The Bhuigaon blog of 2011 describes the Madikar Cross in the western quarter of the village as “the local equivalent of the Gaondevi”, the village goddess of a Hindu settlement: a protector and a territorial marker for the extended Marti family, with smaller crosses on the front doors of houses. [n07 §2; n40 §A4; Mumbai Paused blog, Apr 2011] The same blog says that the church had granted the land the community farmed, and that correspondence and the community magazine were in Marathi. [n07 §2] At Madres, the CRIT survey found that “local Catholic communities gather around the cross for prayers and ritual processions” by the village tank at Christmas and on the Nativity feast. [n40 §A8; CRIT Vol III asset 71] At Nandakhal, the survey called the open space in front of the church “the chief public place in the area”. [n40 §A3; CRIT Vol I item 60]

The Nandakhal count of December 2016 shows how such a parish was organised: 2,150 families and 7,339 people in twenty-nine hamlet groups, the largest of them Ghosali (767 people), Nandan (585), Persav (552), Kolbhag (485) and Jeladi (401), with smaller wards named for families and places, among them Rumao Ali (369) and Ranebhat (330). [n40 §A3] A hamlet named for the Rumao family, a lane called after the Gomes family at Gomes Ali: the Portuguese surnames that the Kuparis received at baptism four centuries earlier had become the names of the places they lived in. [n39 §1.E; n40 §A5]

The school bench

The baseline of 1882

To see how far the Kuparis travelled through education after 1947, it helps to remember where they started. In 1881 Agashi’s church had a school of seventeen pupils, taught Portuguese reading, arithmetic, singing, doctrine and music by a master who also played the violin in church. Nirmal’s Holy Cross had a private school whose teacher did the same. [n34 §8; n40 §A1–A2; Gazetteer XIV pp. 2, 293] Those were the schools of the Kupari north. The well-off Christian families of the wider Thana district sent their sons to St Mary’s or St Xavier’s in Bombay, and “a considerable number who know English are employed in Bombay chiefly as clerks”; the Gazetteer noticed that “the morning trains from Andheri and Bandra are crowded”. [n34 §5, §8; Gazetteer XIII pp. 205–206, 215–216] Andheri and Bandra are in Salsette, not Bassein. The Gazetteer’s commuting Christian clerk was a Salsette man, not a Kupari.

Forty years later, in the 1920s, the Bombay East Indian Association was still listing among its grievances “the neglect of education in the districts of Salsette and Bassein”. [n34 §7; Mendiratta 2017, p. 236] Rita Rodricks, in a popular synthesis, describes the Samvedi Christians as “the only Bassein group to receive Jesuit education”, but she gives no source, and the Gazetteer’s picture of small parish schools in Agashi and Nirmal hardly suggests a privileged schooling. [n34 §1; Sahapedia] The Nandakhal sentence of 1923, “we get bread and butter from farming and not by studying”, sounds like the voice of a community that did not yet see what school was for.

The schools that changed that

The parish histories date the turn. At Nandakhal: Marathi schools from 1935, and St Joseph’s school in 1939. [n40 §A3; n28 §1930s] At Agashi, the community website records an English primary school called “Holy Martyrs” in 1938, the St James Marathi School in 1968 and the St James High School in 1977. [n40 §A2; East-Indians.net, Agashi] The name of the Agashi primary school looks back to the orphans of Nossa Senhora da Luz, the five people da Cunha says were burned in 1540 for refusing Islam, whom he called “a batch of Christian martyrs… consisting entirely of native converts”. [n40 §A2; da Cunha 1876, pp. 159–160] At Gass there was the St Gonsalo Garcia Primary School, and above it, for the children of several villages, Holy Cross High School at Nirmal, the high school of the mother parish. [n42 §5–6; Wikipedia: Simon Almeida] Nirmal’s first Mass for the Gomes Ali hamlets in 1979 was said in a building belonging to the state, “the Primary Local Board School at Chowkala”, which shows that government schools were also present in the villages. [n40 §A5] The founding year of Holy Cross High School is not in the notes; nor is the founding of the Gass primary school, except that it was open in the 1960s, when Simon Almeida went there. [n42 §6]

Further south, in the town, the Christian Brothers opened St Augustine’s High School in 1970 or 1972 (the sources conflict). [n28 §1970s] It was there, on 9 February 1986, that Pope John Paul II spoke to Vasai. [n38 §2] In 1984 St Gonsalo Garcia College opened in Vasai, affiliated to the University of Mumbai, a degree college named for the saint of the Kupari shrine at Gass. [n38 §4; Wikipedia: St Gonsalo Garcia College] By 2009 Vatican Radio counted, across the new Diocese of Vasai, three senior and two junior colleges, 23 high schools and 56 primary schools; a later directory counted eight degree colleges, 82 high schools and four minor seminaries. [n38 §6] The counts are inconsistent and cover the whole diocese, not the Kupari belt alone. They show the scale of the Church’s school system, not what share of it the Kuparis built or used.

Two academic theses exist that might fill this gap: one on education in Vasai between 1947 and 1987 (R. J. Gonsalves, 2016) and one on local self-government (T. T. Pereira, 2018). The research behind this chapter found them but did not open them. [n34 §10] Until someone reads them, there is no measured account of how many Kupari children went to school, when girls began to go in numbers, or when the first Kupari graduates came home.

What school was for

The community’s own accounts are clear about what came of it. The two community blogs that are the only written attempts to describe the Kuparis as a whole, Sachin Mendes’s “Vasai Kupari” blog of 10 September 2007 and the “Post in Mumbai” blog of 25 March 2015, say that farming dominated the community until the late 1960s and early 1970s, and that people then moved into engineering, medicine, banking and information technology. [n34 §8] Rajan D’Mello’s blog of 2009 makes the same point: until the late 1960s and early 1970s the Kuparis depended mostly on farming. [n07 §2] One of the blogs claims “100% literacy”. The research notes treat that as a boast, and so does this book. [n34 §8] No census measures it, because no census counts the Kuparis.

The dates the blogs give fit the parish record. A generation that began school in the Marathi schools of the late 1930s and the 1940s would have been looking for work in the late 1950s and 1960s; their children, schooled in the 1960s and 1970s in the new English and Marathi high schools, were the ones who left the fields. The blogs do not explain the change. The likeliest explanation, and it is this book’s inference rather than any source’s, is the one the Nandakhal villagers had turned upside down: once school could put bread on the table more reliably than the garden, the garden lost its children.

The train south

The kawad and the commuter

For the whole of the twentieth century the Kupari villages’ link to Bombay was the railway. The line from Bombay through Bassein Road had opened in the 1860s, and its embankment, the Gazetteer said, had dammed the old Sopara creek and sent the trade of the Gass and Bolinj landings onto the trains. [n42 §1; Gazetteer XIV p. 315] Large quantities of plantains and betel leaf went south to Bombay by rail by 1882. [n42 §4]

The best picture of the next stage comes from a 2015 food article. Farmers carried their produce on a kawad, a shoulder pole with two loaded baskets, on the four o’clock train to Victoria Terminus, and sold it at Bora Bazaar and near Dadar’s Kabutarkhana; customers called them “Vasaiwale mama” and “Vasaiwale maushi”. [n17; India Food Network, 6 Oct 2015] Chapter 14 opens with that figure. The article does not say which communities the kawadis came from, and it would be wrong to make them all Kuparis; Vadval, Kupari, Hindu Samvedi and Pachkalshi growers all farmed the garden belt. But the Kupari villages sent their bananas, vegetables and flowers south on the same trains. The article gives three reasons why the kawadis faded: families moved to office jobs, contractors tied farmers to supermarket chains, and produce from Nashik and Pune took the market. [n17] The first of those reasons is the Kupari story of the 1960s and 1970s seen from the platform.

The trains changed too. Twelve-coach services began between Dadar and Virar on 28 October 1986. [n28 §1980s] Between 1981 and 1991 the population of the Vasai-Virar area rose by about 150 per cent, and by about 290 per cent in Vasai, Virar, Sopara and Sandor, driven by people who worked in Bombay and moved north to live. [n17; TIFR] That was the inflow. The outflow from the Kupari villages, the sons and daughters who took the same trains every morning to offices in Bombay or moved into the city itself, is nowhere measured. The research notes found no migration data for the Kuparis at all, and no census migration table for Vasai. [n06 §1 Gaps]

A way of life on the platform

What can be said is that the railway made it possible for a Kupari family to change its work without changing its village. A man or woman could study at a Vasai college, take the train to a job in Bombay, and come home at night to a house in Nandakhal or Gass, to the parish and the wadi. The heritage survey of 2008 still found the Kupari villages as gram panchayats with their tanks and churches intact. [n40 §2] The commuting Kupari remained a villager.

Further away: the Gulf, Europe, America, Australia

The Kuparis’ migration abroad is the least documented part of their modern history. The only sources are the community blogs. Rajan D’Mello’s blog of 2009 mentions emigration to the Gulf, Europe, North America and Australia; the “Vasai Kupari” and “Post in Mumbai” blogs list the United States, Europe, the Gulf and Australia. [n07 §4; n34 §8] Neither gives a number, a date, a destination city or a named family. The research found “no source on cultural revival groups or migration figures”. [n07 §4 Gaps]

The brief for this chapter asked about the United Kingdom specifically. None of the sources names Britain; they say “Europe”. It may be that Kupari families are in Britain, and a reader from the community may know them. This book cannot cite them.

What the sources do allow is a sketch of the shape. The blogs’ list of professions, engineering, medicine, banking and information technology, is a list of qualifications that can be carried across borders, and the blogs place the emigration after the turn from farming in the late 1960s and 1970s. Beyond that the record is empty. The Kupari parish of Nandakhal counted 7,339 people in 2016 inside the parish. [n40 §A3] It did not count the people of Nandakhal who were living abroad. No source the research found has.

Sons and daughters for the Church

From “paucity” to sixty-eight

The Kupari villages’ other road out of the fields led to the altar. It was an old road. In 1882 the Gazetteer wrote that “wealthy families who are village headmen and owners of rich garden lands often bring up one or more of their sons for the Church”. [n34 §5; Gazetteer XIII p. 205] But the parish priests of Bassein in that period were Goans or men from Salsette, educated at Goa, and in the 1920s the Bombay East Indian Association complained of “the paucity of vocations to the priesthood among the community”. [n34 §7–8; Gazetteer XIII pp. 207–208; Mendiratta 2017, p. 236] At Nandakhal, the parish history marks the moment when a local man first stood at its altar as priest: Rev. Frank Lobo, ordained on 5 January 1943, “after 370 years”. [n40 §A3]

Seventy-three years later the same parish claimed sixty-eight priests “and many nuns”. [n40 §A3] Even allowing that the figure is the parish’s own and covers its whole history, it describes one of the most remarkable changes in the community. The notes found no count of Kupari priests and nuns for the community as a whole, and no list of where they serve. [n34 §8 Gaps; n38 §6 Gaps] The diocese’s own figures, for all its Catholics, are larger in scale and less specific: at the end of 2022 the Diocese of Vasai had 185 priests, 102 of them diocesan and 83 from religious orders, and 458 religious, 103 brothers and 355 sisters. [n34 §8; n38 §6; GCatholic] Those are the priests and religious working in the diocese, not those it sent out.

Women are almost invisible in these records, though “many nuns” suggests they were not few. The parish histories mention religious sisters arriving rather than leaving: the Queen of the Apostles sisters at Nandakhal in 1952 (the same history also gives 1935), the Don Bosco Sisters at Gomes Ali in 1992. [n40 §A3, §A5] No Kupari nun is named in any source the research found. That is a gap in the record, not in the history.

Two bishops from two villages

Two lives stand out, because their records are full enough to follow.

Thomas Dabre was born on 23 October 1945 at Bhuigaon, the village of wells two kilometres south-west of Nirmal whose Holy Family parish was founded the year after his birth. He was ordained priest on 31 October 1971, became auxiliary bishop of Bombay in 1990 (one diocesan page says 1988), and was installed on 15 August 1998 at Papdi as the first Bishop of Vasai. He moved to the Diocese of Poona in 2009. [n34 §8; n39 §1.E; n40 §A4; n26 §4; Catholic-Hierarchy; GCatholic] Bhuigaon is listed as a Kupari village by the community’s own writers. No source in the notes states that Bishop Dabre himself is Kupari; the book infers it from his birthplace and says so. [n34 §8] His surname is one of the few in the community that is not Portuguese. The research found no etymology for it. [n39 §1.E]

Simon Almeida was born at Gass on 4 November 1960. He went to the St Gonsalo Garcia Primary School in Gass and to Holy Cross High School at Nirmal, entered Prakash Bhavan Minor Seminary at Pune in 1978, and was ordained priest for the Diocese of Poona on 1 April 1990 by Bishop Valerian D’Souza. On 1 November 2025 Pope Leo XIV named him Bishop of Poona, and on 17 January 2026 he was ordained bishop and installed by Cardinal Filipe Neri Ferrao, taking the motto “To make all things new in Christ”. His elder brother, Fr Peter Almeida, is a priest of Vasai, and was parish priest of Gass in 2023. [n42 §6; n38 §6; Wikipedia: Simon Almeida; Diocese of Poona] The Fides news agency reported the appointment; one local source and the Poona diocese give Gass as his birthplace (the diocese writes “Gass, Bhatori, Vasai”), and Wikipedia spells it “Ghas”. [n38 §6; n42 §6]

So the Diocese of Poona has twice in a generation taken its bishop from a Kupari village of the Vasai garden belt: first Thomas Dabre of Bhuigaon, then Simon Almeida of Gass. The coincidence is in the record; the reasons are not.

Other bishops came from the wider Vasai Catholic community. Felix Machado, born at Remedy in 1948, served for about a decade in the Vatican’s Pontifical Council for Interreligious Dialogue, became Bishop of Nashik in 2008 and Bishop of Vasai from 2009 to 2024. Thomas Francis D’Souza, born at Chulne in 1970, became Bishop of Vasai in December 2024. Malcolm Sequeira, born at Giriz in 1961, became Bishop of Amravati. [n38 §6; n40 §B; GCatholic] Remedy and Chulne lie in the southern parishes, the Vadval and mixed belt round the old fort, and Giriz’s parish was carved from Sandor in that same south; none of the three is among the Kupari villages. [n40 §1] Edwin Colaco, the vicar of Nirmal who said the first Mass at Chowkala in 1979, later became Bishop of Amravati too; his birthplace is not in the notes. [n40 §A1]

The priest who wrote in Marathi

The most famous priest of the Kupari belt in these decades was neither a bishop nor a diocesan priest. Fr Francis D’Britto was born on 4 December 1943 (some reports say 1942) and came, by the Nandakhal parish’s account, from Jeladi, one of its hamlets. [n38 §6; n40 §A3] He was a Franciscan. He edited the diocesan Marathi monthly Suvarta from 1983 to 2007. Over many years (the reports say thirteen or fifteen, finishing about 2010) he made the Subodh Bible, a Marathi translation with introductions and about two hundred illustrations, which won the state translation award in 2013 and the Sahitya Akademi Award in 2014, reported as the first Bible translation to win a national award. He received the Dnyanoba-Tukaram Puraskar in 2015, and on 22 September 2019 he was elected president of the 93rd Akhil Bharatiya Marathi Sahitya Sammelan, held at Osmanabad in January 2020, reported as the first Christian to hold the post. [n07 §7; n38 §6; n40 §A3; Wikipedia: Francis D’Britto; FPJ] He died at Vasai on 25 July 2024, aged 81, and about three thousand people came to his funeral at Nandakhal. [n29 §4; FPJ, 25 Jul 2024]

His life joins three threads of this chapter. He was a son of a Kupari parish who became a priest. He wrote in Marathi, the language in which the Kupari villages had long kept their correspondence and community magazines, and carried the Christian Marathi of Vasai to the highest honours of Marathi letters. [n07 §2] And he led the fight for the land.

A pope and a diocese

On 9 February 1986 Pope John Paul II made Vasai the first stop of his Bombay visit. At St Augustine’s High School he called it the place “where the Christian faith was first proclaimed and Christian communities were first established”, said that “the Franciscans and the Jesuits, the Dominicans and the Augustinians, went forth” from it to the villages and towns, and named “Saint Gonsalo Garcia, the young native Franciscan of Vasai, who was martyred together with his confreres in Japan”. [n38 §2, §4; Vatican: Address at Vasai, 9 Feb 1986] Two months later, on 6 April 1986, Mother Teresa visited Nandakhal. [n40 §A3] In 2011 the Nandakhal church was given a statue of the pope. [n40 §A3]

Twelve years after the papal visit, on 22 May 1998, John Paul II erected the Diocese of Vasai by the decree Cum ad aeternam, taking its parishes out of the Archdiocese of Bombay. The announcement was made on 28 June 1998 at the Shrine of Our Lady of Remedy, and on 15 August 1998, the fifty-first anniversary of India’s independence, Thomas Dabre of Bhuigaon was installed as its first bishop at Our Lady of Grace, Papdi, which became the cathedral. [n38 §6; n40 §B; n07 §7] The new diocese took as its patron St Gonsalo Garcia, whose shrine church stood at Gass. [n38 §4] The book calls it the Diocese of Vasai, not an archdiocese: it is a suffragan of Bombay. [n07 §7]

For the Kupari villages the diocese meant something the 1887 East Indian Association had never given them: a church whose centre was their own taluka. In 1926 the “urbanised” East Indians of Bombay had drifted to the Archdiocese of Bombay, while those of Salsette and Bassein “remained by and large under the jurisdiction of the Padroado”, the Goan line. [n34 §7; Mendiratta 2017 p. 236] Since 1928 Vasai had been at the northern edge of a Bombay archdiocese. From 1998 its parishes, Kupari and Vadval, northern and southern, were a diocese in their own right, led by a son of Bhuigaon and under the patronage of the saint whose major shrine stood at Gass.

The fight for the land

August 1988

The decision that threatened the Kupari villages was taken in Bombay. The Regional Plan for the Vasai-Virar sub-region was sanctioned on 31 August 1988, and in that year, according to a TIFR account, the state government “de-reserved this region from agricultural use, contrary to the recommendation of the MMRDA”. Private builders began at once. [n17; TIFR; VVCMC DCR 2013] Sabrang India, in a profile of the movement’s leader, put it more simply: in August 1988 the state converted the Vasai-Virar green belt to residential use without infrastructure. [n29 §1; Sabrang, 30 Nov 2000] In May 1990 the Regional Plan was amended to convert about 8,500 hectares from green zone to urbanisable zone, for a planned city of 12 lakh people by 2011. [n17; Revised Land Use Plan] And on 14 May 1990 the City and Industrial Development Corporation (CIDCO) became the planning authority for the sub-region (some sources say 1991). [n29 §1, §5]

For the Kupari villages the meaning was plain. Their wealth was land: the garden belt between the Vasai creek and the Vaitarna, the agar of Sopara and Agashi that the Gazetteer had described in 1882 as “full of trees and sugarcane gardens”. [n42 §1; Gazetteer XIV] A green zone protected that land from being built on, and so protected the villages’ way of life; it also limited what a family could get for its land if it chose to sell. The 1988 decision changed both at once. The sources do not record a single Kupari view of it, and it would be false to suggest there was one. Some families must have seen a fortune in the change. What the record does show is that the organised opposition came overwhelmingly from the Church and from the Christian villages.

Harit Vasai

On 1 April 1989 Fr Francis D’Britto, then editor of Suvarta, convened a meeting of public figures. Out of it came the Harit Vasai Saurakshan Samiti (HVSS), “Green Vasai Protection Committee”, formed in 1989 with D’Britto as convenor. On 1 October 1989 it held a rally of about 35,000 people, at which Vijay Tendulkar and Kisan Mahta were among the speakers. [n29 §1; Sabrang] Wikipedia describes D’Britto as founder of the movement against “ecological plunder” by builders in the 1980s and 1990s. [n07 §7] The movement’s aim, as later reports describe it, was to protect villages, farms, forests and fishing grounds from urbanisation. [n38 §6; FPJ]

In September 1990 CIDCO released an interim draft development plan, and HVSS opposed it, saying it put holding ponds and dumping grounds in the green zone. [n29 §1; Sabrang] Then came the water. Builders needed water that the new city did not have, and tankers began drawing it from the wells of the green belt. On 20 May 1991, according to a Times of India report quoted by Sabrang, there was violence against the area’s Christian residents, linked to the agitation over the tanker water. After talks at ministerial level in which HVSS took part, tankers were banned from drawing water from the green belt; the date of the ban is not given. Builders’ extraction of groundwater in the 1990s raised salinity and damaged farm produce. [n29 §1; Sabrang] That last sentence matters for the Kupari villages. Their gardens stood on raised land that the Gazetteer of 1882 measured at “four or five feet” above the salt waste; the wells beneath them were what made the gardens possible. [n42 §1]

On 26 January 1992, Republic Day, about 100,000 people gathered at the Vasai grounds. About 100,000 signatures were sent to the Governor, P. C. Alexander, and, Sabrang says, to the Prime Minister V. P. Singh, an apparent error in the source, since V. P. Singh had left office in November 1990. CIDCO later revised its plans to preserve the green belt. [n29 §1; Sabrang] It was the largest gathering in the modern history of Vasai that the notes record.

In February 1995 UCA News reported that HVSS’s main activity was opposing “Thakur’s land acquisitions, unauthorized, ill-planned construction and illegal land transactions”, and described a priest promoting an election code before that month’s elections. This is HVSS’s claim as reported, not a finding against anyone. [n29 §1; UCA News, 1 Feb 1995] Chapter 15 tells the political side of that conflict: the rise of Hitendra Thakur from 1990, the Bahujan Vikas Aaghadi, and the allegations and acquittals that surrounded the family. Here it is enough to say that the land question divided Vasai along lines that were partly political, partly economic and partly, though the sources do not measure this, communal. In a much later and polemical article, a local Hindu writer described the coast’s villages as still “highly segregated socially and communally”, while saying he knew of no communal incident in his lifetime. [n07 §1–2; Swarajya, 5 Mar 2019]

What the movement won

When D’Britto died in July 2024, an advocate in the movement, Noel Dabre, said: “The greenery that you see now is because of the movement.” The reports of his death said that the development plan had been changed, that villages had been excluded from the urbanisation plans, and that the state had limited development to 1.5 kilometres on either side of the railway line. [n29 §4; n38 §6; FPJ, 25 Jul 2024] Bishop Thomas Dabre also spoke on the building boom. [n38 §6; AsiaNews]

The northern Kupari villages lie mostly beyond that strip, west of the railway towards the sea. The towns grew around the stations, Vasai Road, Nallasopara and Virar, and the garden villages between them and the coast were, for a generation, held back. That is the movement’s legacy as its supporters describe it, and the map bears it out: the villages that the 2008 heritage survey found with their tanks and churches intact are the villages of the green zone. [n40 §2]

2009: the corporation and the villages

The next fight came in 2009. On 3 July 2009 the state constituted the Vasai-Virar City Municipal Corporation (VVCMC) from four municipal councils and fifty-three villages (some sources date the corporation to 2010). [n29 §2, §5; n40 §2] Forty-nine of the villages opposed being included. Vivek Pandit, the campaigner against bonded labour who won the Vasai seat that year as an independent, led the Gao Bachao Andolan Samiti (“save the villages”) against the merger. [n26 §2; n29 §2] On 31 May 2011 the state moved to exclude twenty-nine villages after protests led by the Vasai Janandolan Samiti; the exclusion was stayed and contested in the Bombay High Court, and a 2025 report said the twenty-nine villages were again in litigation. [n40 §2; vasairoad blog; FPJ] The research did not find the list of the twenty-nine.

What the research did find is that the great Kupari villages, Nirmal, Agashi, Nandakhal, Gass, Bhuigaon and the rest, are now inside the corporation, and so have no separate village figure in the 2011 census. [n40 §2; n39 §2] A Kupari village of 2026 is a ward of a city of more than a million people.

The salt pans and the draft plan

The green belt was challenged again by the Metropolitan Region Development Authority’s draft regional plan for 2016–2036, which proposed to allow buildings of fifteen metres in most green zones and twenty-four metres in the old village sites (gaothans), and to open about 1,560 acres of former salt pans, previously a no-development zone, as a “growth centre”. The Quint reported at least 40,000 objections from Vasai-Virar and over 62,000 across the region; an activist, Samir Vartak, put the local figure at about 63,000. Residents credited the green zone with keeping Vasai less polluted than Mumbai, and said the salt-pan land flooded every monsoon. [n17; ICSF/Quint] Harit Vasai opposed the plan. [n26 §4] The final status of the 1,560-acre growth centre in the sanctioned plan was not confirmed by the research. [n17 Gaps]

Wadis, bananas, betel and ponds

The garden the Kuparis inherited

The Kuparis entered independent India as gardeners. The 1882 Gazetteer had found about seventy-five families of “Native Christians originally Samvedis and Pachkalshis” drying ripe plantains at Agashi, Vatar and Koprad, beside about eighty-five Hindu Samvedi families and fifteen Pachkalshi families, “the dried plantains being the best in the district”. [n34 §1, §5; Gazetteer XIII p. 395; XIV p. 2] The Sopara garden tract, with Gass, Bolinj, Koprad, Umbrala, Nirmal, Vagholi and Mardesh in it, grew sugarcane, plantains, betel leaf and rice under coconut and brab palms, mango, tamarind and karanj, and shipped molasses, rice and betel by boat to Gujarat and plantains and betel by rail to Bombay. [n42 §4; Gazetteer XIV pp. 314–316] Sugar was made by “Pachkalshis, Malis, Native Christians and Samvedi Brahmans”. [n34 §5; Gazetteer XIII p. 395]

That garden economy lasted, with changes, into the second half of the twentieth century. A farmer quoted in 2023 remembered a green belt that grew betel leaf, exported to Pakistan, and the small velchi bananas that sold at a premium; sugarcane had come before, and paddy was widely grown. Villages of the sub-region supplied vegetables to Mumbai’s suburbs until the early 1980s. [n17; 101Reporters, Jan 2023; Vikalp Sangam 2019] Vasai’s sweet yellow bananas were chosen for temple offerings and for shikaran; the dried banana, suki keli, was the region’s signature snack. Seasonal Vasai vegetables included garlic chives, coriander, teasel gourd and banana flower in the monsoon and field beans and toor in winter. [n17; India Food Network, 2015] The research notes found no figures for banana area or output at any date, nothing on the betel acreage beyond the line about Pakistan, and no survey of who owned the wadis that were sold. [n17 Gaps; n41 Gaps]

Sukeli, or hukeli

One product carries the Kupari story better than any statistic. Sukeli is made by drying ripe bananas in the sun, mainly of the Rajeli variety, and Agashi, the old centre of the 1882 plantain-drying families, is its main centre. Marathi Wikipedia says “Vasai’s sukeli was once very famous”; a Marathi summary drawing on the state encyclopaedia adds that over time the orchards in the area have decreased. [n20 §Food; mr.wikipedia: वसई; Vishwakosh, केळ (not opened)] In the Samvedi speech of the Kupari and Hindu Samvedi villages the word is pronounced hukeli, an example of the dialect’s change of initial s to h that Grierson had recorded among the Samvedis in 1905. [n20; n22 §2; mr.wikipedia: सामवेदी बोली]

On 19 June 2026 a man named Pius Alex Rodrigues of Nandakhal-Parsav died. Sakal wrote that he had “spread the name of sukeli, Vasai’s famous dry banana, across India and abroad”, and that his family donated his eyes and skin. [n20 §Food; eSakal, Jun 2026] Persav is one of the hamlets of Nandakhal parish, with 552 people in the 2016 count. [n40 §A3] The research notes, having read the report only as a search snippet, did not establish his community; his village is in the heart of the Kupari belt. And the snack he sold is sold too at the Nirmal yatra, the fifteen-day Hindu fair that begins on Kartiki Ekadashi at the samadhi of a Shankaracharya: stalls from Nirmal Naka to Bhuigaon Phata sell toys, sweets and dry fruit, “Vasai sukeli, dates, pedhe, singhade and groundnuts”. [n20 §Jatras; Lokmat] The fair that the Gazetteer saw in 1882 attended by “Hindus, Musalmans, Christians, and a few Parsis”, with dried plantains among its goods, was still in the twenty-first century a market for the dried bananas of the Christian villages around it. [n40 §A1; Gazetteer XIV p. 292] In November 2025 an agricultural paper reported Konkan root vegetables, purple yam, kanak and sweet potato, in high demand at the Nirmal yatra “amid the rising tide of junk food”. [n20; Agrowon, 19 Nov 2025]

The ponds

The gardens lived on water, and the water came from the bavkhal (also written bawkhal or bhowkal): irrigation ponds dug into an unconfined aquifer, never used for drinking, each owned by a family group of between five and sixty people. [n17; Vikalp Sangam] Marcus Dabre, editor of the Vasai Times, explained in 2023 that the bawkhals hold rainwater and feed the fields through underground connections. [n17; 101Reporters]

The numbers that survive come from the late years of the system. A Minor Irrigation survey in 2007 counted 479 ponds in twenty-seven gram panchayats. In 2013 a volunteer group, Yuva Vikas Sanstha, counted again and found more than the records showed in three Kupari villages: Bhuigaon had 39 (the records said 16), Nandakhal 55 (the records said 32), Wagholi 54 (the records said 16). A study called Water Crisis in Vasai counted 108 lakes within a sixty-kilometre radius, of which only twenty-eight survived. [n17; Vikalp Sangam] A walker in Bhuigaon in 2011 found about forty large wells behind the village’s steel cross, kept by the women of the community, watering vegetables, hybrid bananas and flowers and holding monsoon water for the paddy. He predicted that sprawl would eat them within ten years; that forecast was speculation. [n07 §2; n40 §A4; Mumbai Paused blog]

The ponds decayed after the mid-1990s building boom, “as the paddy fields and the family orchards gave way to high-rise buildings”, and many became rubbish dumps that contaminated the groundwater. [n17; Vikalp Sangam] Coastal water bodies, a member of the Paryavaran Samvardhan Samiti, Macanzy Dabre, explained, are brackish and linked underground; Yuva Vikas Sanstha’s restoration of ponds cut the dissolved salts in nearby wells from 750 to 300 parts per million. [n17] The names in this paragraph are worth noticing. Marcus Dabre, Macanzy Dabre, Noel Dabre: the surname of the first Bishop of Vasai recurs among the people who explained and defended the garden belt’s water. The notes give none of them a village or community, and the book draws no conclusion from the name.

In November 2026 the Marathi press reported that Vasai-Virar’s historic lakes were choked with rubbish and water hyacinth, as reservoir levels fell and a water shortage was feared. [n06 §4; eSakal, 15 Nov 2026]

Who kept farming

The three stages of decline, as the research notes reconstruct them, are these: the kawadi trade and vegetables to Mumbai, which lasted until the early 1980s; the 1988 de-reservation and the mid-1990s building boom, which ate the paddy and the wadis; and the climate and urban-heat stress on the flowers that remained, in the 2020s. [n17 Inferences] For the Kuparis the first stage had a cause of its own. The community’s writers place the turn from farming in the late 1960s and early 1970s, a decade or more before the land became valuable to builders. [n34 §8] The Kupari garden, in other words, was losing its young people to the school and the train before it was threatened by the bulldozer. When the green belt was opened in 1988, many families no longer depended on it for their living. This is the book’s inference, not a source’s, but it may be part of why the fight to save it was led by a priest and fought in the name of a way of life, ecology and the village, rather than of a farmers’ income.

Mogra and sonchafa

When the bananas and betel declined, flowers took their place. About five hundred families of flower growers in about twenty-five villages of Vasai-Virar, on plots of four gunthas to three acres, were working in 2023 along an eighteen-kilometre stretch from the Arnala coast to Vasai. [n17; 101Reporters, Jan 2023] Their earlier favourite was mogra, the Arabian jasmine, which fetched premium prices at the Dadar flower market. Sonchafa, the golden champak, came to Arnala in the late 1980s, from Uday Velankar’s nursery at Kudal, through a farmer named Moreshwar Bhoir; his grandson Kiran Patil chairs the Arnala Farmers’ Cooperative, which has 1,100 members. [n17] A box of a hundred flowers sold for about Rs 150, rising to about Rs 700 at Ganesh Chaturthi; each shrub gives 150 to 200 flowers a day. A grower at Satpale, Subhash Bhatte, produced about 11,000 flowers a day from one acre and earned about Rs 75,000 a month, until his output fell to a few thousand. [n17] A 2011 study found that Arnala and Agashi had an extensive flower system peaking at Ganesh Chaturthi, Diwali and Navratri, and that 90 per cent of flower growers named price swings as their main constraint. [n17; Vazhacharickal & Buerkert 2011]

The flower economy is a Vasai story, not specifically a Kupari one; the sources do not name the growers’ communities, and Arnala, its centre, is better known as one of the coast’s main fishing harbours. [n20] But Agashi, the old Kupari town, is named as part of it, and the Bhuigaon wells were watering flowers in 2011. [n17; n40 §A4] There is a small irony here that the sources do not remark on. The Kupari villages, which are Christian, sell their flowers into the Hindu festival calendar: the price of a box of champak rises more than fourfold at Ganesh Chaturthi.

Then the climate turned against the flowers too. Unseasonal rain, the humidity and fungus after Cyclone Mandous in December 2022, and fewer hours of light roughly halved the yield. The growers get no crop insurance and no Kisan Credit Card loans. Waterlogging worsened as open fields vanished. [n17] On 13 September 2026, overnight rain flooded the flower market and main market near the station in Nallasopara East on the eve of Ganpati. [n29 §4; FPJ]

The red cap and the lugda

What the dress was

The Kupari dress, as the community remembers it, was red. Rajan D’Mello’s blog of 2009 describes Kupari men in a dhoti, a white shirt, a black sleeveless jacket and a red cap, with an earring in the left ear, and Kupari women in a red blouse and a red checked kashta sari. [n07 §3; D’Mello blog] The East Indian lugra, of which the Kupari women’s lugda is a local form, is described by a 2017 East Indian heritage site as a hand-draped ten-yard cotton sari “with a distinctive checked weave and lined border”, once worn every day by all classes along the north Konkan coast, Koli women tucking it tighter and paddy farmers wearing it shorter, with silk lugras for feasts. [n07 §3; East Indian Memory Co., 1 Dec 2017]

Where the red came from is disputed, and the dispute matters. A polemical Hindu article of 2019 claimed that the Portuguese made converts wear red caps and red saris as markers. [n07 §1, §3; Swarajya] The 1882 Gazetteer does not support that. It records Hindu Samvedi men wearing “red broadcloth caps like Telegu Brahmans” and Christian fishermen wearing “red broadcloth caps”. [n34 §5, §9; Gazetteer XIII pp. 82, 205] The red cap, on this evidence, was a regional cap shared by Hindu and Christian farmers and fishers, not a badge imposed on converts. The Gazetteer also describes the Christian women of the district dressing “in local Hindu fashion”, in robes “generally of cotton and in colour dark purple, green, or black”, with married women drawing the end over the right shoulder. [n34 §5; Gazetteer XIII p. 205] Red is not in that list. The book cannot say when the Kupari women’s red checked lugda became the community’s mark; it may be later than 1882, or the Gazetteer’s general description may simply have missed a local form.

What happened to it

By the twenty-first century the dress had all but gone from daily life. An article of 29 June 2026 on the red cap and the lal lugda of the Kupari community found them “largely absent from daily life but remain part of community identity”, and blamed urbanisation, education, migration and media; it listed ideas for preserving them but named no group, event or person doing so. [n34 §8; All About Vasai Virar, 29 Jun 2026] The East Indian lugra had, by 2017, become “a novelty item”, worn at the Moya before a wedding and the Umbra-cha Paani, the fetching of the bride’s or groom’s bathing water. [n07 §3] A Loksatta column by Disha Khatu, “Vasaitil Khristayan” (“Christianity in Vasai”), reportedly described the traditional dress of the Samvedi (Kupari), Somvanshi (Vadval), East Indian and Koli Christians; only its teaser could be read. [n34 §8] The research found no news coverage of a Kupari dress revival group. [n07 §3 Gaps; n20 Gaps]

The decline of the dress has the same causes as the decline of the garden. A woman who takes the train to an office in Bombay does not wear a ten-yard cotton sari tucked for paddy work. What the community kept was the dress as a sign: worn at weddings and festivals, photographed, written about, and, in the 2026 article’s phrase, part of identity rather than of daily life.

Kadodi: a language spoken, then written down

The speech of sixteen villages

The Kuparis’ speech is the Samvedi dialect, also called Kadodi. A Marathi article in Saamana of 22 March 2026 describes it as spoken by Samvedi Brahmins, Pachkalshis and Samvedi Christians, about 50,000 people in all, in sixteen villages of north Vasai. It mixes Marathi, Konkani and Portuguese, with some Gujarati; it is spoken with a sing-song intonation, by what the article calls a “singing community”, and has a rich store of folk songs, proverbs and stories. [n20 §Kupari; Saamana, 22 Mar 2026] The article’s claim that it has been spoken “since about the sixth century” has no support. [n20] The sixteen villages, as Saamana and Marathi Wikipedia list them, are Bhuigaon, Wagholi, Kofrad, Gass, Umrale, Bolinj, Gavdhani, Vatar, Valunje, Vanda (Mardes), Padhai, Karmale, Navale, Somadi, Bankar and Mandpe. [n20; mr.wikipedia: सामवेदी बोली] English Wikipedia, relaying Ethnologue, gives about 60,000 speakers. [n07 §4; n22 §2]

It is shared across the line of faith. The Hindu Samvedis and the Christian Kuparis speak it; the Vadval villages to the south speak a different dialect, and the 2011 Bhuigaon blog was careful to note that Kupari Kadodi “differs from Vadval speech”. [n07 §4; n40 §A4] Grierson in 1905 had called Samvedi “a mechanical mixture of Konkan Marathi and Gujarati” and counted about 2,700 speakers, but he counted the Christians of Thana separately, as speakers of “Kinstav”, a Marathi with Portuguese loanwords for father, mother, Mass and godparents. [n22 §2–3; n34 §4] The two counts do not show growth from 2,700 to 50,000; they show two ways of counting. The Kupari dialect as a named category did not exist in 1905. [n34 §4]

Its sounds are distinctive. Initial s often becomes h: sukeli becomes hukeli, sheni becomes heni. Standard Marathi alo, “came”, becomes alô, and jevlo, “ate”, becomes jivlô; asked “have you eaten?”, a speaker answers jivyasa hay. [n20; mr.wikipedia] A 2024 paper describes its sound inventory as lacking several Marathi vowels and consonants and preferring plain to aspirated dentals, and gives the sentence tou majuri karon pout bhartae, “he makes a living by working as a labourer”, and tou nejlay, “he is sleeping”. [n22 §2; Kumar et al., WAT 2024]

The people who wrote it down

The dialect was oral for most of its life. It began to be written down, as a subject of study, by people from inside the community.

Paul Rumav, a teacher from Nandakhal, edited Samvedi Lokgeete (“Samvedi folk songs”), Madhachya Ghagari (“Pots of honey”), Samvedi Khristi Samaj (“The Samvedi Christian community”) and Vasai Parisaratil Mhani (“Proverbs of the Vasai area”). [n20 §Kupari; mr.wikipedia: सामवेदी बोली] The notes do not give the dates of these books. They are the most likely printed source for the Kupari wedding songs and work songs that community sites describe. [n20 Inferences] Dr Naresh Naik of Virar wrote Samvedi Boli – Sanrachana ani Swarup (“Samvedi dialect: structure and form”), which won the Krishna Mukund Ujalambkar Smriti award and, in 2018, a P. Na. Go. Kalelkar award. [n20] A magazine and website called Kadodi (kadodi.in) is cited by Marathi Wikipedia and by the 2024 paper. [n20; n22 §2] The 2024 paper itself cites earlier studies of the community by Francis Correia (1992) and Pereira (2007), which the research did not read. [n22 §2]

In 2024 a team of researchers (the notes cite the paper both as Dabre, Dabre and Pereira and as Kumar et al.) published “Suman”, a Kadodi–Marathi–English dictionary of 949 entries and 942 sentence triples, made by native Kadodi speakers, at a workshop on machine translation. They called it “the first work on machine translation involving Kadodi”. [n34 §4; n22 §2; ACL Anthology 2024.wat-1.3] It is a striking fact: a dialect to which Grierson in 1905 gave a single specimen, in a survey that said of the coast’s small caste dialects that “it would be waste of time and paper to give specimens of all of them”, was being prepared for computers by its own speakers a hundred and twenty years later. [n22 §3]

Songs that are rarely heard

What the dialect carried is fading faster than the dialect itself. A community music site says that the folk songs of Nandakhal, “also called Kupari songs”, “are rarely heard as lifestyles have changed”. Their themes were the bride leaving her father, women fetching fodder, the groom’s first shave in the days when boys married young, and a daughter coming home after the rice planting. The same site describes the Kupari community as “more conservative” than the “more fun-loving” Vadvals of the south, whose music, it says, supplies most of south Vasai’s wedding bands. [n07 §4; Vasaiker, “Music of Vasai”] Every one of the Kupari song themes is a theme of the farming year and the village marriage. When the rice planting stopped and child marriage ended, the songs lost their occasions.

The Kupari words for the rites of life, as the D’Mello blog of 2009 records them, show the same layering as the dialect: Baltim for baptism, Komsar for confession, Cominyao for first communion, Chrisma for confirmation, Hamdao, Lagin and Varad for the stages of a wedding, Saudio for the celebration after it, Yentar for a funeral, Saatvoh for the seventh-day Mass and its meal, Paya bharni for the laying of a house’s foundation, Bejimat for the blessing of a new house, Pirsao or miravnuk for the candlelit Lenten procession through the villages, Upas for the forty-day Lenten fast. [n07 §4] No primary source confirms these terms. [n34 §5] They are the community’s own record of itself, and they are given here as that.

The language of the Church

The Church has been both a cause of the dialect’s decline and one of its keepers. Since at least the nineteenth century, sermons in Bassein were in Portuguese or Marathi, and prayers were printed in both. [n34 §4; Gazetteer XIII p. 207] Thomas Stephens’s Kristapurana was printed in Devanagari “in the East Indian dialect” at Vasai in 1922 and reprinted in 1927. [n38 §6] The parish correspondence and the community magazine at Bhuigaon were in Marathi in 2011. [n07 §2] The diocesan monthly Suvarta, which D’Britto edited, was in Marathi. [n38 §6] The Church thus gave the Kuparis a written standard, Marathi, and the standard displaced the spoken dialect in print.

The broader East Indian community has begun to push back. On 15 June 2024 the Mobai Gaothan Panchayat, an East Indian organisation in Bombay, asked Cardinal Oswald Gracias to use the East Indian dialect in Mass and hymns, to promote devotion to St Gonsalo Garcia, to create an “East Indian Bhavan” at the Goregaon seminary, to simplify certification for the backward-class list, to put the community’s name on baptism certificates, and to preserve the crosses and gaothans, the old village sites. [n34 §8; FPJ, 16 Jun 2024] A priest from Uttan, Fr Victor Dalmet, composes songs in the “East Indian dialect of Marathi” and was named “East Indian Priest of the Year” by the same organisation, which is preparing a second edition of its dictionary. [n34 §8; FPJ, 30 Nov 2023] Neither report mentions Kadodi or the Kuparis. The East Indian revival in Bombay and the Kupari dialect work in Vasai are parallel movements, as far as the record shows, not one.

Who the Kuparis say they are

Inside the East Indian umbrella

Since 1887 the Catholics of the north Konkan, once called “Portuguese Christians”, have called themselves East Indians: a name chosen at a meeting of about thirty community leaders in Bombay on 26 May 1887, when the Bombay East Indian Association was founded, to mark them as the Crown’s oldest Catholic subjects and “sons of the soil” and to separate them from Goan and Mangalorean migrants. [n34 §7; n07 §5; Mendiratta 2017, pp. 234–236] Chapter 35 told that story. The Kuparis lived inside that name without being defined by it. Rural Bassein and Salsette Catholics came to be labelled “rural” or “samvedi” East Indians in Bombay usage, a label that, as the historian Sidh Mendiratta notes, broadened “Samvedi” from a caste name into a loose word for the farming Catholics of the north. [n34 §1, §7; Mendiratta 2017, p. 218]

The book that fixed the classification was published after independence. In 1967 Elsie Wilhelmina Baptista’s The East Indians: Catholic Community of Bombay, Salsette and Bassein described five cultural groups within the community: the Kulbis, the Samavedi Christians “commonly called Kuparis”, the Koli Christians, the Wadvals and the Salsette Christians. [n28 §1960s; n34 §7; Catholic Connect] The research could not read the book; it is not online, and the phrase is quoted from secondary sources. [n34 §1, §7 Gaps] It may be the first scholarly use of the word “Kupari” in print. The research searched the full text of fifteen primary and early sources, from the 1882 Gazetteer to the 1931 census, and found the word in none of them; its first attestations online are a community blog of 2007 and Wikipedia. [n34 headline] The name the community uses for itself became visible to the written record only in the decades this chapter covers.

The word’s origin is uncertain. The derivation usually given is from Portuguese compadre, the godfather or co-parent, with the feminine comadre giving “Kumari”; Wikipedia’s sources for it are forum posts and a dead dictionary link. [n34 §2; n07 §1] The Christians’ own vocabulary did use Portuguese godparent terms, padrinho and madrinha in the Gazetteer of 1882, padron and madron in Grierson’s word list of 1905, so the idea fits; it is unproven. [n34 §2] A second explanation, that “Kupari” means those who drank from the well (Marathi kup) into which, legend says, Portuguese missionaries threw bread, was offered by an anonymous commenter on Wikipedia in 2007, with the warning that Hindus used the word as an insult. [n34 §2; Talk:Kupari] It is folklore, and an insult in some mouths. The well story itself was recorded in 1859 and 1882, but as a story of the Urap Agris and other classes, not of the Samvedis, and the Gazetteer’s editor judged that “persuasion and interest were the chief means of conversion”. [n34 §2, §9; Gazetteer XIII p. 201 n.2] A name that some outsiders once threw as an insult is the name the community has put on its blogs and in its histories. That is not unusual in the history of names.

Brahmin, cultivator, backward class

The Kuparis’ self-image is of Samvedi Brahmin descent. Wikipedia calls them a Roman Catholic “Brahmin” sub-group of the East Indians; the community blogs say most Kuparis trace their descent to Samvedi Brahmins. [n07 §1] The 1882 Gazetteer gives the claim real support for the northern villages: it says the husbandmen of Sopara were Christians, “many of them converted Samvedi Brahmans”, and that the Christian plantain-driers of Agashi, Vatar and Koprad were “originally Samvedis and Pachkalshis”. [n34 §1] But it also says the Samvedis of 1882 were “husbandmen and gardeners”, not priests. [n34 §1 Inferences] Chapter 33 weighed that evidence. The point for this chapter is how the claim sits beside modern Indian law.

In Maharashtra, “East Indians” are listed at serial number 341 of the state list of Other Backward Classes (OBC), following a government resolution reported as issued in 2006, after a campaign led by Oneil Kinny, Prem Moraes and the Maharashtra Christi Boomputra Bandhav Sangathana, with the Bombay East Indian Association as a party. [n34 §8; east-indians.com] The central OBC list does not include them. In October 2026 the Free Press Journal reported a proposal for thirty-three Maharashtra groups, “including East Indian Catholics”, “awaiting final approval from the Union Cabinet”, citing Vicky Misquitta of the Mobai Gaothan Panchayat; the national commission’s list of October 2024, as printed, does not name East Indians. [n34 §8; FPJ, updated 4 Oct 2026] The Koli Christians are separately classed as OBC. [n07 §5]

Neither source names the Kuparis or the Samvedi Christians. The research could not read the text of the 2006 resolution. [n34 §8] As the research notes read it, a Kupari family that wants an OBC certificate obtains it as East Indian, under the umbrella name, not under its own. That is an inference from the wording of the list. [n34 §8 Inferences] The book notes, without resolving it, the tension that this creates for a community whose own account of itself is of Brahmin descent, and whose umbrella community now seeks recognition as backward. The sources record no Kupari debate on the question. If there has been one, it has not reached print the research could find.

Segregation and mixing

How separate are the Kuparis from the other Catholics of Vasai, and from their Hindu neighbours? The sources disagree. The 2019 Swarajya article said that the coast’s villages are “socially and communally segregated” while urban areas are not, that each Catholic caste group has its own community organisations, and that they generally do not intermarry. [n07 §2, §5; Swarajya] An East Indian blog said there was “hardly any stratification according to caste”. [n07 §5] Sahapedia’s survey says the Portuguese did not make converts give up caste, so caste identities persisted, and names five modern groups within the East Indians. [n34 §7] The Bhuigaon blog of 2011 found Kupari speech different from Vadval speech. [n40 §A4] The music site called the Kuparis “more conservative” than the Vadvals. [n07 §4]

None of these is a survey. Together they suggest a community that keeps its own village, parish, dialect and marriages, but shares a diocese, a saint and a feast calendar with the other Catholic groups, and a dialect, a fair and a garden belt with the Hindu Samvedis. The research found no Kupari-specific organisation with a verifiable name and founding date, no sabha or samaj of the kind the Vadvals have in the Somvanshi Kshatriya Samaj Sangh. [n34 §8 Gaps; n41] The parish, it seems, has done that work.

Voting and office

There is no published study of how the Kuparis vote. The general record is in Chapters 15 and 15: the Socialist and Congress decades, the Janata interlude, the three decades of Hitendra Thakur and the Bahujan Vikas Aaghadi from 1990, Vivek Pandit’s win in 2009, and the BJP’s capture of the Vasai and Nallasopara assembly seats in November 2024. [n26 §1–2; n29] The Christian share of the Vasai taluka’s population in 2011 was 8.25 per cent, about 110,800 people of all Christian groups. [n34 §8; Wikipedia: Vasai taluka] That is enough to matter in a close contest; the Vasai seat in 2024 was decided by 3,153 votes. [n26 §1] But no source says how any part of it voted.

In January 2026 the Vasai-Virar corporation, after six years without an elected body, held its election. The BVA kept control with seventy-one of 115 seats; the BJP won forty-three. On 3 February 2026 Ajiv Patil of the BVA became mayor and Marshal Dominic Lopes, also of the BVA, deputy mayor. [n06 §3; n26 §3] An earlier deputy mayor of the corporation was named Prakash Rodrigues. [n26 §3] The research notes found no biography of either man, and so cannot say whether they come from the Kupari belt. [n26 §3 Gaps] Their names are, like the Gonsalves names on the ballots of 1985 and 1990, a trace of the Christian vote in the public record, and nothing more.

The city reaches the villages

Nallasopara and the garden edge

The growth of the towns is told in Chapters 15 and 15. From the Kupari side, what matters is that the towns grew towards the villages from the stations. Nallasopara, the town in whose western part Gass now lies, had 184,664 people at the 2001 census. [n29 §2; n42] The corporation’s area grew from 219,868 people in 1981 to 1,343,402 in 2011, and the corporation projects nearly two million now. [n06 §1; n40 §2] In 2011 Marathi was the mother tongue of only 46.49 per cent of the city’s people, with Hindi 26.58 per cent and Gujarati 9.26 per cent. [n22 §4] The Kuparis, never more than a part of the taluka’s people even in 1881, when Agashi counted 1,500 Christians among 6,823, became within one generation a small minority in a city of strangers. [n40 §A2]

The pressure on the garden belt never ended. Illegal buildings were the defining problem of the new city. Forty-one buildings at Agarwal Nagari in Nallasopara East, built from 2006 on land reserved for a sewage plant and a dumping ground, were declared “wholly illegal” by the Bombay High Court and demolished between November 2024 and February 2025; the Enforcement Directorate arrested a former municipal commissioner in August 2025 in a case linked to them, and the High Court later called the arrest illegal. [n06 §4; n29 §4] Those events were in the eastern town, not the Kupari villages. But they set off a chain that reached one of them.

Gass, 2025: the dumping ground

When the Agarwal Nagari land was cleared, the dumping ground and sewage-plant reservation that had been on it had to go somewhere. In March 2025, villagers of Gass submitted more than 2,500 objections to a corporation plan to move the reservation from Agarwal Nagar to a plot in Gass. The Hindustan Times headline was “No dumping ground in our midst, say Vasai residents”, and it described Gass as “a predominantly Christian village often described as ‘Mini Goa’ for the ancestral mansions that dot the surrounding farmland”. The plot was reported as 1,500 acres, a figure the research notes regard as implausibly large. In October 2025 the corporation withdrew its application, though residents still wanted a formal commitment. [n42 §6; n06 §4; HT via Magzter; newsminimalist summary] The population of Gass in that coverage was given as about 5,500 in one version and about 9,000 in another. [n42 §4]

Chapter 26 tells this fight in full. Its place in this chapter is as the latest form of the oldest Kupari struggle since 1988. In 1990 Harit Vasai had objected that CIDCO’s interim plan put “holding ponds and dumping grounds in the green zone”. [n29 §1] Thirty-five years later a Kupari village was objecting to the same thing, in almost the same words, and winning, for the moment, by the same method: thousands of signatures, filed in good time.

11 August 2023: the church at Gass

Two years earlier, on 11 August 2023, the church of St Gonsalo Garcia at Gass was ransacked. Vandals defaced the altar, threw down the statue of Our Lady and scattered the hymn books. A complaint was filed at Manickpur police station. Archbishop Felix Machado, then Bishop of Vasai, condemned the attack. The lack of CCTV cameras hampered the police, and the church decided to install them. The parish priest was Fr Peter Almeida. [n42 §5; UCA News; AsiaNews; Vatican News] Vatican News reported the attack in German with the line that Gonsalo Garcia was “India’s first saint”. [n38 §4] The research found the details only through search summaries, because the original pages were blocked, and found no report of arrests or of a motive. [n42 §5 Gaps] It remained, in November 2026, the most recent attack on a Vasai church the research found. [n38 §6]

The book records the event and declines to interpret it. No source attributes it to any group, and no source connects it to the 2019 article on segregation, which said its author knew of no communal incident in his lifetime. [n07 §1]

The deaths of 2024

On 25 July 2024 Fr Francis D’Britto died. [n29 §4] His funeral at Nandakhal, attended by about three thousand people, was the end of a generation: the priest who had led the green-belt movement, made the Marathi Bible and presided over Marathi letters was buried in the Kupari parish that had produced him. In June 2024 Felix Machado’s term as Bishop of Vasai ended; on 9 November 2024 Pope Francis named Thomas Francis D’Souza of Chulne, a former rector of the Shrine of Our Lady of Remedies, the third bishop, and he was consecrated on 15 December 2024. [n38 §5–6; Fides]

The Jubilee years

The diocese opened the Catholic Jubilee of 2025 on 29 December 2024 at Our Lady of Grace Cathedral, Papdi. On 4 March 2025 Bishop D’Souza blessed “Mini Jubilee Crosses” for each parish, as a substitute for distant pilgrimages. [n38 §5; Catholic Connect] In 2025 the cathedral, in the southern Vadval belt, won a UNESCO Asia-Pacific Award of Merit for its community-led restoration. [n40 §B; Outlook Traveller] In July 2025 the diocese ordained its first deacon in five years, a small fact in a press snippet that may say more about vocations in the 2020s than any count: the flow of priests that the Kupari parishes once sent out was not what it had been. [n38 §6; Catholic Connect (snippet)] The book offers that reading cautiously; the snippet gives no reason for the gap.

On 11 January 2026 the feast of St Gonsalo Garcia was kept, as every year on the first Sunday nearest the neap tide after Christmas, in the ruined Church of the Holy Name inside Vasai Fort, the “Killa cha Sann”, with Fr Peter Fernandez presiding before hundreds of worshippers; families came the evening before, cooked East Indian dishes and, in older days, came by cart or boat. [n38 §5; FPJ, 11 Jan 2026] Six days later, on 17 January 2026, Simon Almeida of Gass was ordained Bishop of Poona. [n42 §6]

The Umralla pilgrim centre, 2026

The latest Kupari event the research found is a designation. Pope Leo XIV proclaimed a Year of St Francis, from 10 January 2026 to 10 January 2027, for the eight-hundredth anniversary of the saint’s death. On 7 June 2026 the Diocese of Vasai designated St Joseph’s Church, Umralla, as a Pilgrim Centre for the Year, with a plenary indulgence for pilgrims. [n38 §5; Catholic Connect] Umralla is Umrale, the Kupari village in the north of the old Sopara garden tract that the 1882 Gazetteer spelled Umbrala and listed among the plantain-drying villages; Mass was first said there in 1949 and it became the independent parish of St Joseph in 1970. [n40 §A9; Gazetteer XIV p. 314; XIII p. 292]

The choice of a Franciscan year fits the Kupari villages’ history. It was the Franciscans who came first to this coast after 1534, built the orphanage at Agashi in 1535 and, by the heritage survey’s account, the churches at Nirmal and Nandakhal; Bassein’s rural churches began to pass to native secular priests from 1570. [n38 §2; n40 §A1–A3] The notes do not record why Umralla was chosen. That a Kupari village church should be the place where the diocese marked a Franciscan anniversary in 2026 is, at least, a pleasing symmetry.

Floods and water

The monsoon of 2026 was the worst the notes record. Between 4 and 8 July heavy rain flooded the region; Palghar’s collector reported an average of 203 millimetres in twenty-four hours on 7 July; ten people died in rain-related incidents in Palghar district from 1 July, and by 13 July nine rain-related deaths had been reported in Vasai-Virar; 389 families were moved to relief centres in the Palghar, Vasai and Wada talukas; trains between Vasai Road and Virar stopped when the tracks flooded near Nallasopara. [n29 §4; FPJ; The Quint] The research found no report of flooding in a named Kupari village. But the garden villages stand on the same low land that the activists of 2018 had called a natural floodplain of wetlands and salt pans, and that Harit Vasai said was about half low-lying or marshy. [n17; n06 §4]

How many Kuparis?

Every count of the Kuparis is an estimate, and every estimate comes from the community itself. It is worth setting them out with their caveats, because numbers repeated often enough start to look like facts.

  • No census counts them. The Indian census has not tabulated caste for most communities since 1931, and no census, before or after that, ever used the word “Kupari”. The Gazetteer of 1882 counted 2,563 Hindu Samvedis in 1872 and about 13,000 Christians in the Bassein sub-division, but did not divide the Christians by origin. [n34 §3, §8–9; Gazetteer XIII pp. 71, 81–82]
  • Community blogs: “about 40,000 to 45,000” (Sachin Mendes, 2007); “around 60,000+” (Post in Mumbai, 2015); about 97 or 95 per cent Roman Catholic. Neither gives a method. [n34 §8]
  • Speakers of the dialect: about 60,000 for Kadodi (Ethnologue via Wikipedia, a 2016 figure) and about 50,000 for Samvedi (Saamana, 2026). These include Hindu Samvedis and Pachkalshis, and they are the same kind of self-reported figure as the blogs. [n34 §8; n20; n22 §2]
  • All Christians in the taluka: 8.25 per cent of Vasai taluka’s 1,343,402 people at the 2011 census, about 110,800, a figure that includes Kuparis, Vadvals, Koli Christians, Goan and other migrants and Protestants. [n34 §8; Wikipedia: Vasai taluka]
  • All Catholics in the diocese: 117,026 at the end of 2022 (GCatholic), in a diocese of 4.44 million people that reaches well beyond Vasai; other sources give 135,677 for 2010 and about 136,800 or 137,600 in undated or 2022 rows. The difference more likely reflects different sources than a real fall. [n34 §8; n38 §6]
  • Parish counts: Nandakhal, the largest Kupari parish, counted 7,339 people in 2,150 families in December 2016; Wikipedia gives Gass about 5,000 Roman Catholics in 2009; Bhuigaon’s parish history speaks of “the thousand Catholics of the village” in the 1940s. [n40 §A3–A4; n42 §4]

The research notes conclude that a defensible statement is “tens of thousands”, between 40,000 and 60,000 on community self-report, flagged as unsourced. [n34 §8 Inferences] This book adopts that. It adds one caution of its own. The blog figures appear to count the people of the Kupari villages. Whether they count the Kuparis who have moved to Bombay or abroad, and their children, is not stated, and the answer would change the number a great deal.

Where the Kuparis stand, November 2026

On the evidence of this chapter, the Kuparis in November 2026 are a community that has kept almost everything except the life that made it.

They have kept their villages. Nirmal, Agashi, Nandakhal, Gass, Bhuigaon, Umrale, Nanbhat, Bolinj, Wagholi, Mardes, Rajodi and Gomes Ali are still where the Gazetteer found their predecessors in 1882, on the raised garden land between the old Sopara creek and the sea, and the green-belt movement that the Kupari parishes led kept most of the towers away from them for a generation. [n40; n29 §4] They are inside a municipal corporation now, wards of a city, without separate census figures; Gass in 2025 had to fight off a dumping ground. [n40 §2; n42 §6]

They have kept, and multiplied, their parishes. Three Kupari parishes in 1882 had become a dozen or so parishes and centres by 2004, and in June 2026 one of them, St Joseph’s at Umralla, was a pilgrim centre of the universal Church’s Year of St Francis. [n40; n38 §5] Their villages have given the Church at least two bishops, Thomas Dabre of Bhuigaon and Simon Almeida of Gass, both Bishops of Poona, the second ordained in January 2026; one parish alone claims sixty-eight priests. [n40 §A3–A4; n42 §6] Their saint, Gonsalo Garcia, is the patron of their diocese, and his major shrine is the church at Gass.

They have kept their dialect alive on paper, and in a computer dataset, more securely than in their children’s mouths. Kadodi has its grammarian, its folk-song collector, its magazine and its 949-entry dictionary. [n20; n22 §2] Its songs, the songs of the rice planting and the bride’s leaving, are rarely heard. [n07 §4]

They have kept their dress as a memory: “largely absent from daily life”, in the 2026 phrase, “but… part of community identity”. [n34 §8]

They have lost, or given up, the garden. The bananas that made Agashi’s dried plantains “the best in the district” in 1882, the betel that went to Pakistan, the paddy, the kawadi on the four o’clock train, have mostly gone; the bavkhal that watered the wadis are silting or full of rubbish; the flowers that replaced the bananas are being halved by a changing climate. [n17; n06 §4] The man who carried Vasai’s sukeli to the world, Pius Alex Rodrigues of Nandakhal-Parsav, died in June 2026. [n20]

And they have gone out into the world: into the schools and colleges their parishes built, onto the trains to Bombay, into engineering and medicine and banking and computing, into the Gulf and Europe, North America and Australia, and into the priesthood and religious life. [n34 §8; n07 §4] None of those journeys is counted. The research found no Kupari organisation, no history of the community written by a historian, no anthropological study, no list of notable Kuparis and no count of Kuparis abroad. [n34 §1, §8 Gaps]

That is the honest state of the record in November 2026. The Kuparis have been written about for less than twenty years under their own name. Most of what is known about them since 1947 is known from the parish, the blog and the dialect article, not from the archive. The open questions at the end of this chapter are therefore not a list of footnotes. They are an invitation to the community, in Gass and Nandakhal and Bhuigaon and wherever its families now live, to write down what this book could not find. The next chapter, K6, turns to the family names, villages and voices through which that record might begin.

What is still unknown

  • Any Kupari account of 1947, of the Samyukta Maharashtra movement, or of the freedom struggle in the Christian villages.
  • The founding dates of Holy Cross High School, Nirmal, and the St Gonsalo Garcia Primary School, Gass; school enrolment and literacy in the Kupari villages by decade; when girls began to attend school in numbers. The theses of R. J. Gonsalves (2016, education in Vasai 1947–87) and T. T. Pereira (2018, local self-government) should be read.
  • Any figure for Kupari migration to Bombay or abroad, by decade or destination; whether Kupari families settled in the United Kingdom specifically.
  • A count of Kupari priests and religious sisters, and where they serve; the name of any Kupari nun.
  • Whether Bishop Thomas Dabre, Fr Francis D’Britto, Deputy Mayors Marshal Dominic Lopes and Prakash Rodrigues, the 1985 MLA Dominic Gonsalves and the sukeli trader Pius Alex Rodrigues identify as Kupari. The sources give birthplaces or parishes, not community.
  • The first written use of the word “Kupari”; what Elsie Baptista wrote in 1967.
  • The text of the 2006 Maharashtra resolution on East Indian OBC status, and whether any Kupari family or organisation has argued for or against it.
  • Banana, betel and flower acreage in the Kupari villages at any date after 1882; who owned the wadis that were sold after 1988.
  • The dates of Paul Rumav’s books; the contents of the Kadodi magazine.
  • Any Kupari-specific association, sabha or samaj, with its founding date.
  • Arrests, motive or outcome in the 11 August 2023 attack on the Gass church.
  • Why St Joseph’s, Umralla, was chosen as the Year of St Francis pilgrim centre.
