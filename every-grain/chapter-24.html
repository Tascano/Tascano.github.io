Period covered: the island of Sopara and its creek from the first written notices, through the silting of the creek, the garden and landing economy recorded in 1882 and the railway that took both the rock and the water, to the chawls, the floods, the demolitions and the heritage visitors of 2026

In this chapter. The island, its creek and its water: the silting of the creek, the garden and landing economy, the salt waste and the lakes.

About a mile north of the old Portuguese fort at Achola, near the top of the steep west face of the hill the villagers called Rakshi Dongar, the compilers of the 1882 Gazetteer found a wall of black rock. “About a mile to the north of Achola, near the top of the west face of the steep Rakshi Dongar, or Tulinj hill, perhaps about 300 feet above the plain, is a fine basalt dyke. The rock is dark, glistens with mica, and when struck rings like an anvil.” The basalt stood out from the hill in six-sided pillars, six to twenty-two feet long. And the visitor noted what had happened to them: “Many of the pillars were used when the railway was made, and the people still carry them away.” [n31 §2; n53 B1; n12 §3; Gaz XIV pp. 314, 339, 342]

The railway took something else from the same country. A few miles west, the backwater that had made Sopara an island, and once a port, had silted up. “Silting has been rapid,” the same volume says, “especially since the long railway embankment has dammed the flow of water from the south.” [n53 B1; n36 §6; Gaz XIV pp. 314–315] The line that the Bombay, Baroda and Central India Railway drove north across the plain in the 1860s was built partly of Tulinj’s ringing pillars, and its embankment choked the creek. The railway took the rock from the east of Sopara and the water from the west.

A hundred and sixty years later, in July 2026, the trains between Vasai Road and Virar stopped for two evenings running because the water would not leave the tracks at Nallasopara. Western Railway’s officials explained that the track levels near Nallasopara were “almost the same as the surrounding city”; the General Manager, Ramashray Pandey, ordered a feasibility survey to raise them. [n53 B2; FPJ, 8 July 2026] The embankment that had once dammed the creek was now too low for the water that had nowhere else to go.

This chapter tells that ground and the livelihoods made on it: the island of Sopara and its creek; the death of the creek and of the fishing and salt-making it carried; the basalt of Tulinj; the lakes and ponds, Nirmal, Bhatela, Gass and the rest; the floods and the drinking water; and the work, from the gardens and landings that exported molasses, plantains and betel to Gujarat in 1882, through the chawls and buildings that replaced them after 1988, to the heritage visitors who come, few and unprovided for, to the stupa. Its twins are Chapter 19 for Vasai and Chapter 30 for Virar; the history of Sopara through the ages is in Chapter 22, the modern town in Chapter 23, and the village of Gass in Chapter 26. Where a story is told in full there, it is told here only from the side of land, water and work.

The evidence is lopsided, and the chapter says so as it goes. For 1882 the Gazetteer of the Bombay Presidency, Volume XIV, Places of Interest, gives the island of Sopara at a level of detail that no later document approaches: the creek and its boats, the ponds and their sizes, the products and where they went, the people and their trades. [n53 B1; n31 §2] For the twenty-first century there are flood reports, court cases, a student research project, a census figure and some property listings. For the hundred years between, there is almost nothing. Where Nallasopara’s economy is unrecorded, such as its markets, this chapter states plainly that nothing was found.

The island of Sopara

In 1882 the Gazetteer described Sopara as “still a rich country town with a crowded weekly market and a population of about 1700”, lying “about three and a half miles north-west of the Bassein Road station”. [n53 B1; n11 Q2; Gaz XIV p. 314] It was the centre of a garden: “The richly wooded well-watered and highly tilled tract, including Bolinj, Koprad and Umbrala in the north, Nirmal and Vagholi in the west, Gas in the south, and Sopara and Mardesh in the centre, is locally known as the garden or agar of Sopara.” [n11 Q1; n53 B1; Gaz XIV p. 314] That garden was the middle of a strip of garden land about three miles broad that ran from the Bassein creek to the Vaitarna, with the agar of Agashi to the north and the agar of Bassein to the south. “Under the Portuguese the whole of this rich strip, with its patches of salt marsh, was known as the island of Bassein, and, before Portuguese times, as the island of Sopara.” [n11 Q1; Gaz XIV pp. 314–315]

So the island bore Sopara’s name before it bore Bassein’s. The town that had been a port of the western sea in the time of Ashoka, of the Periplus and of the Shilahara kings, whose story is told in Chapters 04, 05 and N1, gave its name to the land around it; when the Portuguese made Bassein their capital, the island took the name of the new town. [n11 Q1; n36 §6]

The Gazetteer’s description of the inside of the island is the best picture of the land of Nallasopara before the city. “Within this rich garden island, four or five feet below the level of the fields, shady lanes… wind among mango and plantain orchards, fields of rice and sugarcane.” Sugarcane grew under coconut and brab palms; mango, tamarind, banyan, pipal and karanj trees shaded it; high hedgerows lined the lanes. Old footways of basalt blocks crossed the muddy hollows. “Till the month of March the island is full of water. Runnels from garden wells cross or flow alongside of the lanes,” and there were “round ponds and long winding lakes, whose banks are fringed by high pile-supported Persian wheels”. The houses were tiled, with wooden walls, on high masonry plinths, often built of older carved stones. [n11 Q1; n48 §1; n53 B3; Gaz XIV pp. 314–315]

Three things in that description matter for everything that follows. The lanes ran four or five feet below the fields: the land was low, and the paths were its lowest part. The island was “full of water” until March: water stood on and in it for most of the year. And it was an island, surrounded by water that drained it. Remove the drain and fill the low ground, and the water stays.

The creek: three names, one water

The island was an island because of a backwater. “It is called an island, because it is cut from the mainland by a backwater which leaves the Bassein creek a little above the railway bridge and winds north to the Vaitarna. This backwater used to be called the Sopara creek. It is now locally known by three names, the Manikpur creek in the south, the Sopara creek in the centre, and the Bolinj creek in the north.” [n53 B1; n48 §1; Gaz XIV pp. 314–315] “Even from the south side,” the Gazetteer added elsewhere, “the whole backwater is still, from the ancient trade centre, known as the Sopara creek.” [n48 §2; Gaz XIII-I, general description] It had once been “connected by a deep channel” with the Bassein creek, and had its mouth in the Vaitarna. [n48 §1]

The creek was Sopara’s harbour and its wall. “Its broad backwater guarded it from pirate raids or the forays of the wild hill and forest tribes,” the Gazetteer wrote. [n36 §7; Gaz XIV p. 316] In the twelfth century the Arab geographer al-Idrisi placed Subara a mile and a half from the sea, the first measure of how far the shore had already drawn back, and wrote of pearls fished in its creeks. [n36 §6, §7; Gaz XIII-II p. 430] A Kutchi sea manual of the seventeenth century, cited in a modern study by Shah and colleagues, described a regular cargo route through the Bolinj–Sopara creek. [n36 §6; Shah et al. 2020] In 1535 the Portuguese recorded that Sopara had four wooden stockades. [n36 §4; Da Cunha p. 159] The palm stems that still stood in the dry creek in 1882 were called “Burna Raja’s stockade”, and the English strengthened the line with a palm stockade of their own in 1803. [n53 B1; Gaz XIV p. 338]

The creek was silting long before the railway. A Portuguese report of 1728 complained that the channel between Sopara and Bolinj was fordable at low water for about four thousand feet, and warned that it should be dug “as deep as the height of a man, otherwise it would soon be filled to a level with the fields, because of the great amount of silt that was brought by the river… from its two mouths”. [n36 §6; n12 §1; Gaz XIV p. 322] The French scholar Anquetil du Perron, in 1760, noted that Vagholi had “a creek of its own”. [n11 Q2; Gaz XIV p. 322] Modern scholarship puts the long process in a frame. The sea stood about half a metre higher around the second and third centuries BCE; silt from the Vaitarna and the Ulhas, increased by the clearing of the hinterland’s forests, filled the lagoon over two thousand years; and the railway on its high embankment finished it in the second half of the nineteenth century. [n36 §6; Shah et al. 2020, citing Arunachalam 2002] The art historian Pia Brancaccio, in 2023, confirmed that the lagoon “has been filled in by river deposits” and that the site is now mudflats encroached by settlement. [n36 §6; Brancaccio 2023 p. 11] The 1728 report and the 1882 report together, the researchers observe, make a century-and-a-half time series of a silting channel. [n36 §6 Inferences] No core sample or radiocarbon date measures the rate. [n36 §6 Gaps]

What the creek carried: the island’s older livelihoods

Before the gardens became the island’s living, the creek was. The ancient port is the subject of Chapters 04 and N1, and only its economy, what people on this ground lived by, is gathered here.

The Gazetteer explained why Sopara became a trade centre in terms of land and water: its distance from the coast and its broad backwater guarded it from pirates; its water linked the Vaitarna to the north and the Ulhas to the south; and “the easy land route along the Tansa valley made it an early centre of trade”. [n36 §2; Gaz XIV p. 316] Inland, at Bhatane on the Tansa, a landing station north of Tungar, the Gazetteer recorded “a reservoir of dressed stones and quantities of old bricks”; Pelhar and Mandvi, along the same valley, “were once places of importance”. [n12 §7; Gaz XIV, Sopara fn.] The island and the valley behind it were one system: the creek for ships, the valley for carts, the island for the market.

The record of who earned a living from that system is in stone, far from Sopara. In the Buddhist caves of Kanheri, Nashik, Karle and Nanaghat, donors from Sopara paid for cisterns and pillars in the first centuries CE: Samika, “a merchant of Sopáraka”, who gave a cistern in Kanheri’s Cave VII; Nagapalita, a jeweller of Surparaka; Govindadasa of Sopara, who gave a cistern at Nanaghat; Satimita, nephew of the monk Dhamutariya of Sopara, who gave a pillar with relics at Karle. [n36 §2; Indraji 1882 p. 277; Puratattva digest] At Nashik, the Kshatrapa governor Ushavadata recorded rest-houses at “Sorparaga” and a gift of 32,000 coconut stems for ascetics at its Ramatirtha. [n36 §2; Indraji 1882 p. 277] Coconuts were already the island’s wealth: thirty-two thousand palms, given as an endowment, two thousand years before the Bhandaris tapped their descendants.

The Periplus of the Erythraean Sea, of the first century CE, names “Suppara” as the first market-town south of Barygaza, without listing its goods. [n36 §2; Schoff 1912 §52] The Buddhist story of Purna, a legend with realistic period detail, imagines Sopara as a city of merchants who traded cloth and sandalwood, where “a strong merchant-guild ruled the trade” and no member might buy from a visiting ship on his own, on pain of a fine of sixty silver coins. [n36 §2; Gaz XIV pp. 320–321; Brancaccio 2023 pp. 12–13] Whatever the legend’s date, it imagines the island’s livelihood as the creek’s: customs, freight, warehouses and the profit of the middleman. The “Sopara stone” was famous about AD 500, and al-Idrisi in the twelfth century wrote of pearls from the creeks. [n36 §2, §7; Gaz XIII-II pp. 416, 430]

In the Portuguese centuries, the island gave up its stone. “During the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, the lake banks were stripped and the temples pulled down, and the stones used in building Portuguese churches, forts, and houses.” [n36 §6; Gaz XIV p. 323] The tanks of Sopara, with their stone-lined banks, were quarries for the new capital at Bassein, as the Tulinj pillars would be quarries for the railway two centuries later. By 1826, in Clune’s gazetteer, Sopara was a kasba, a market town, and a post-runner’s station; the port was a memory, and Da Cunha in 1876 called Supara “once a famous bandar, now almost abandoned”. [n31 §2; n32 §9; Da Cunha pp. 156–157] What the island lived on by then was its gardens, and the creek served them only as a road to the boats.

The embankment and the death of the creek

The railway reached the plain in the 1860s; trains ran from Grant Road to Bassein Road from 1 November 1865 and reached Virar in April 1867. [n31 §1] The line ran north across the island’s eastern edge, on a long embankment, between the island and the mainland, close beside the backwater, and it crossed the creek’s southern mouth on a bridge “a little above” where the backwater left the Bassein creek. [n53 B1; Gaz XIV pp. 314–315]

The Gazetteer named the result in plain words. “With the mud-laden Vaitarna on the north and the Bassein creek or Ulhas river on the south, silting has been rapid, especially since the long railway embankment has dammed the flow of water from the south. Small boats of ten or twelve tons (30–40 khandis) still come from the south as far as Gas… and from the north as far as Bolinj, about two miles from Dedar or the old Sopara landing. At Sopara the creek is dry.” [n36 §6; n53 B1; n11 Q1; Gaz XIV pp. 314–315] By 1882 the creek was crossed “only by the railway and the bridges at Bolinj and Gokhivare”. [n53 A1; Gaz XIII-I p. 11]

The Gazetteer also recorded how fast the end had come. Twenty years earlier, in the early 1860s, before the embankment, boats of twelve tons had come up from Manikpur to Sopara, “and there was much fishing and salt making”. In 1882 the creek at Sopara was dry except in the rains. [n53 B1, B5; Gaz XIV p. 338] Within two decades of the railway’s arrival, a waterway that had carried cargo since antiquity had become a ditch.

The end of creek fishing and salt-making

That single sentence, “there was much fishing and salt making”, is the only record of two livelihoods of the old creek, and of their end. Who fished it and who made the salt, the Gazetteer does not say at Sopara. Elsewhere on the Bassein coast, the fishermen were Son Kolis and Christian Kolis, the Uraps, and the salt-makers Agris and Kharvas. [n21 §3, §4; Gaz XIII-I pp. 55, 365–366] The Urap Agris, the community of Christians who had returned to Hinduism, lived in a list of villages that included Achole and Manikpur, on the creek’s edge. [n48 §1; Gaz XIII-I] It is likely, though no source says so, that the creek’s fishers and salt-makers came from these communities. [inference]

By the 1880s their work had moved. The salt works of the Bassein division were “widely scattered on the north bank of the Bassein creek”, on the open estuary south of the island, with the Dublas brought from Surat working “only on the salt-works near Bassein Road station”; the sea fishermen worked the coast and the great creek. [n21 §3; Gaz XIII-I pp. 364–366] Chapter 19 tells those trades. Sopara’s own creek, by then, carried neither fish nor salt.

Boats were still built nearby. In 1837, by the Gazetteer’s account, “very good fishing boats and country vessels were built” at Sopara. [n36 §5; Gaz XIV p. 323] That was a generation before the embankment. No later record of boatbuilding at Sopara exists in the notes.

Salt came back to the edge of the island in another form. In the city plan of 2010 the low lands along the coast and the two creeks were “marshy, khazan lands and some of them mainly to the south of Nallasopara Railway Station are still used for salt cultivation”. [n48 §3; CDP pp. 24–25, 28] These pans lay on the tidal flats toward the Vasai creek, not in the old Sopara channel; how much of them survives in 2026 is not recorded. [n53 A6 Gaps]

Tulinj: the anvil hill

East of the old creek, across the salt waste, stands Tulinj hill, the Rakshi or Rakehi Dongar, Nallasopara East’s one piece of high ground. Its basalt dyke, described above, is the most vivid geological sight the Gazetteer recorded anywhere on this coast: dark rock glinting with mica, ringing when struck, breaking into six-sided pillars six to twenty-two feet long. [n53 B1; n31 §2; Gaz XIV pp. 314, 339, 342] Across a ravine stood a circle of upright basalt pillars. [n31 §2] The dyke is the same Deccan basalt that underlies the whole coast, told in Chapters 01 and 02; on Tulinj it was pushed up as a dyke and cooled into columns.

The pillars were a ready-made building stone, and the railway used them. “Many of the pillars were used when the railway was made, and the people still carry them away.” [n53 B1; Gaz XIV p. 342] The researchers call this an early example of the quarry economy that now works the foothills of Tungar. [n53 B1 Inferences] It also made Tulinj, in the 1880s, a sight: the dyke was an excursion for visitors from the Manikpur bungalow. [n53 B7; Gaz XIV p. 342]

The basalt of Tulinj and of Brahma hill, the bush- and palm-covered knoll to the south-west, was worked into the island’s paths. A raised basalt footway, the dagdi bandh or “stone dam”, ran from Sopara toward Achola, about half a mile south of the house of a Samvedi Brahman named Bab Naik; broken basalt blocks formed old footways across the muddy hollows near Gass and Bolinj; and the houses of Gass had basalt blocks before their verandas, many carried from Brahma hill. [n12 §3; n53 B1; n42; Gaz XIV pp. 341–342] Some of those blocks carried inscriptions two thousand years old, as Chapter 26 tells. The 1882 visitor’s tour ran from Manikpur to Brahma hill, Gass, Sopara and the stone dam, and back by Chulna village to Manikpur. [n11 Q2; Gaz XIV p. 341]

No modern geological map of the Nallasopara East plain and no account of what became of the Tulinj dyke was found; the hill is now inside the city, with a police station at its foot that stood under two feet of water in one of the floods told below. [n53 B1 Gaps; n31 §3; Lokmat Times]

The eastern edge: Achole, Vajirgad and the Nil hill

South of Tulinj lay Achola, Achole, “a mile and a half to the north of Manikpur”, with “its Portuguese fort”, which the 1882 visitor was sent to see in the afternoon; the road to Tungar passed “between the villages of Achola and Rajavli to Gokirva”. [n12 §3; n31 §2; Gaz XIV] West of the island, Vajirgad hill carried the ruins of another Portuguese fort. [n53 B1] A Portuguese report of 1728 complained that “the Nil hill near Sopara” had been fortified without an engineer, with bastions too small for a four-pounder gun. [n31 §2; Gaz XIV, citing O Chronista de Tissuary] The forts tell what the land was for the Portuguese: an island of gardens with a soft eastern flank, guarded on the high points against the hill country beyond.

The salt waste

Between Tulinj and the island lay the land on which most of modern Nallasopara is built, and the Gazetteer had a name for it: salt waste. The track from Manikpur to Sopara crossed “a salt waste that flooded and became impassable in the rains”. [n12 §3; n48 §1; Gaz XIV p. 316] Salt wastes bordered the creek on both sides. [n48 §1] Sopara, though “not more than three-quarters of a mile from the line of railway”, was “somewhat badly off for communications”, because to reach it from the station one crossed this flooded ground. [n31 §1; Gaz XIV p. 316]

The researchers’ synthesis is blunt. The West of modern Nallasopara is the old garden-and-Sopara side; the East, Achole, Tulinj, Santosh Bhuvan and the neighbourhoods round them, was salt waste and rice flats below Tulinj hill, cheap land that later filled with dense, often unauthorised housing. [n12 §3 Inferences] The same low ground the Gazetteer called “salt waste… flooded and impassable” is where flooding recurs; “the geography has not changed, only the buildings on it”. [n31 §3 Inferences] The city plan of 2010 gives the average elevation of the area as 1.5 to 2 metres above the sea. [n48 §3; CDP pp. 24–25]

Lakes and ponds

If the creek was the island’s drain, its ponds and lakes were its reservoirs, and Nallasopara holds most of the old tanks of the island. They are its oldest public works, its holiest places and, in the modern city, its least studied.

Nirmal lake

In 1882 the Bassein sub-division had, by the Gazetteer’s count, only two reservoirs worth the name, and the larger was at Nirmal, on the western edge of the island. The Nirmal lake measured 4,488 feet by 1,551, held water for about eleven months of the year, and was ten feet deep at most. [n53 A3, B3; Gaz XIII-I pp. 12–13] The other was the Dhavpani-tirth at Malonde in Vasai town, a fifth of its length, told in Chapter 19. [n53 A3]

Nirmal’s name and its lake carry the land’s history in two explanations. Local legend, recorded in the Nirmal Mahatmya, told how the demon Vimal harassed the Brahman seers who lived near the sacred Vaitarna; Parshuram cut off his hands and feet, which kept growing back, until with Shiva’s help Vimal was subdued and pardoned; Parshuram set up the linga Vimaleshvar where he fell, and the place became nirmal, stainless. The pit of the lake, in another telling, was hollowed by a giant’s blood. [n11 Q2; n53 B3; Gaz XIV p. 292 fn.] The Gazetteer offered a second, earthier etymology: Dravidian nir, water, and mal, hill, “the sea hill”, for “a wooded mound rising from bare flats once under water”. [n11 Q2; n12 §8; Gaz XIV p. 292 fn.] The Skanda Purana calls Nirmal “the best of holy pools”. [n11 Q2] Either way, Nirmal was a rise of ground with water beside it, on a coast that had been sea.

The lake’s present form is Maratha. “The Portuguese had pulled down the temples and destroyed the ling. On the fall of Bassein (1739) Nirmal was purified, prints of the feet of Shri Dattatraya took the place of the ling, and a reservoir was built.” [n11 Q2; Gaz XIV p. 293] About 1750 Shankarji Keshav, the Sarsubhedar of Bassein, built eight temples around the lake: the Shankaracharya Svami temple, which drew a yearly government grant of Rs 421; Mahavishnu, the Vimaleshvara Mahadev and Bhuteshvar west of the lake; Ganpati, Durga and Hanuman east of it; and Siddheshvar to the north, supported by his descendants; with grants for a Brahman feeding-house and a Bairagi alms-house. [n11 Q2; Gaz XIV pp. 292–293] The lake was public works and endowment at once: water held for the gardens and the pilgrims, by a state that paid for temples.

The Nirmal fair

The lake made Nirmal a market once a year. Tradition holds Nirmal to be the burial place of a Shankaracharya, and on Kartik Vadya 11, in November, a week-long fair drew six to seven thousand pilgrims in 1882, “Hindus, Musalmans, Christians, and a few Parsis”, from Thana, Gujarat, Bombay, the Deccan and the south Konkan. Brass and copper vessels, dried plantains, sweets, cloth and cattle were sold there, about Rs 10,000 worth in all. [n11 Q2; n53 B3; Gaz XIV p. 292] The fair was the island’s great trading event: the place where its dried plantains met the brass of the Deccan and the cattle of the hills, and where every community of the coast came together. It is still held; Chapter 44 describes it as heritage one can attend. Its modern volume of trade is not recorded.

The lake’s present condition is not recorded either. No recent report of Nirmal lake’s area, pollution or rejuvenation was found. [n53 B3 Gaps] A Marathi report of 15 November 2026 said the historic lakes of Vasai–Virar were choked with garbage and water hyacinth, without naming Nirmal. [n06 §4; eSakal]

Bhatela pond and Ram’s Pool

At the heart of old Sopara lies the Bhatela pond, 240 yards by 146, with Muslim graves on its north and west banks and, at an old landing place, the site of a Portuguese tower. Boats of ten to twelve tons, the Gazetteer said, still came at high tide to a point about a quarter of a mile south-east of it. [n53 B3; n31 §2; Gaz XIV p. 339] Wikipedia speaks of remains of a Portuguese jetty and customs house there. [n31 §3; Wikipedia: Nallasopara] The Bhatela pond, in other words, was the last landing of the Sopara port, the place where the creek’s water reached the town after it had stopped reaching the old landing at Dedar. Beside it, in Chapter 22’s telling, the Ashokan edict stone was found.

Near it lies Ram’s Pool, Ramkund, about forty-five feet square, where the Hindus of Sopara bathe in the month of Shravan. [n53 B3; Gaz XIV p. 339] South of the town lay the Khare or Shirmoli pond, where a statue of Brahma was found that by 1882 stood near the Chakreshvar temple. [n31 §2; n42] Sopara village had at least four large lakes in 1882, by the researchers’ reading of the Gazetteer, one of them called Sarpala, “snake pond”. [n48 §3] Chakreshwar lake, with its temples and the sculptures gathered around it, lies in Nallasopara West and is told in Chapter 22. [n31 §3] At Bolinj, carved stones were said to lie in the Devhara pond. [n11 Q2; Gaz XIV p. 342]

Gass lake

South of Sopara, Gass had the island’s most elaborate lake. In 1882 the Gazetteer described it as “a winding hollow, 120 yards broad and 800 long with two cross dams”, fringed with gardens and Persian wheels, with dressed stone steps at its south end; on its shore, at a spot called Mala, were the foundations of a Brahmanical temple of the eleventh or twelfth century and a carved stone lotus pendant from a temple ceiling. [n11 Q2; n42; Gaz XIV p. 341] The two cross-dams show that the lake was worked: divided to hold water at different levels for the gardens around it. Whether it was a natural remnant of the old creek, a dug tank or both, no source says. [n42 §1 gaps] Wikipedia in the 2020s described Gass simply as “a natural village with a big lake”. [n42; Wikipedia: Gass] No modern survey of the lake was found. [n53 B3 Gaps] Chapter 26 tells the lake and the village around it.

The bavkhal of the Sopara garden

Beside these public lakes were the private ponds of the gardens, the bavkhal, family irrigation ponds fed by the shallow aquifer and never used for drinking, whose system and decline are told in Chapter 19. [n17; Vikalp Sangam] The Sopara garden was part of that system. When the volunteers of the Yuva Vikas Sanstha surveyed three villages in 2013, one was Wagholi, the Vagholi of the Sopara agar, where the records showed sixteen ponds and the volunteers found fifty-four. [n17; n53 A3] The citizen study Water Crisis in Vasai counted 108 lakes over a span that ran from Arnala to Malonde and from Achole to Umelle, of which twenty-eight survived; Achole, in Nallasopara East, marked one end of its survey. [n53 A3; Vikalp Sangam] At Bhuigaon, on the island’s western edge, a villager named Sachin Marti began reviving the ponds from about 2010, when he saw his neighbours hauling water although ponds lay near them. [n53 B3; The Better India (summary)]

The Gazetteer’s own phrase sums up the old water system: the island was “full of water” until March, with “round ponds and long winding lakes” fringed by Persian wheels on their pile-supported frames. [n53 B3; Gaz XIV p. 314] The modern sources sum up its end: ponds turned to garbage dumps after the mid-1990s “as the paddy fields and the family orchards gave way to high-rise buildings”. [n17; Vikalp Sangam]

Rain on the island

Nallasopara’s climate is the Vasai monsoon described in Chapter 19: almost all the year’s rain between June and September, July the wettest, an annual total given variously as about 1,586 millimetres, about 1,825 (the Gazetteer’s 71.87 inches for Bassein in the decade to 1881) and 2,200 or more (the city plan), with no reconciled series. [n17; n21 §10; n48 §3] The bad years of the nineteenth century, the failed rains of 1824–25, 1837 and 1850 and the failed salt-rice crops of 1848 and 1854, struck the Sopara gardens with the rest of the sub-division. [n21 §10; Gaz XIII-I p. 303] What changed in the twentieth century was not the rain but the ground it fell on.

The floods

The floods of modern Nallasopara are the floods of the old salt waste, and of the railway that runs across it.

July 2018. The first well-reported flood of the modern town came in July 2018. On 10 July the National Disaster Response Force and the Navy evacuated passengers from trains stranded at Nallasopara; about 1,500 were rescued across the region. [n53 A2; Business Standard/ANI] The corporation deployed pumps at Achole Road, Chheda Nagar and Samta Nagar; floodwater mixed with the water in underground household tanks, poisoning the supply people had stored. The pattern runs back to 1882. The Gazetteer’s “salt waste” that “flooded and became impassable in the rains”, its “dry creek”, its lanes “four or five feet below the level of the fields” and its island “full of water until March” map exactly onto the flooded neighbourhoods of the 2020s, the researchers observe. [n53 Overall inferences] The railway embankment that dammed the creek in the 1860s is the same embankment whose tracks the water now stands on. The flood problem of Nallasopara is a hundred-and-forty-year-old fact of the land.

Drinking water

For a town that floods every monsoon, Nallasopara has been short of drinking water for most of its modern life. The paradox is the land’s: rain that falls for four months runs off or stands on the low ground, the shallow groundwater beneath is brackish where the ponds have gone, and the town buys its water back from the hills.

The island’s old water, the wells and ponds and Persian wheels, served gardens of a few thousand people. When the green belt opened in 1988, the town grew with almost no supply: in the early 1990s there was no piped water in the region, and in 1996 some residents got as little as ten litres a day. [n17; n53 A3; TIFR] Tankers drew from the bore wells of the green belt until Harit Vasai won a ban; by 2012 about thirteen lakh people in the region depended on tankers and bore wells. [n29 §1; n17; Vikalp Sangam] The catchments of the Pelhar dam and the Juchandra tank, inside the Tungareshwar sanctuary, “supply water to Vasai and Nallasopara”. [n53 A3, B3; DNA, 25 Apr 2017] Since late 2023 the corporation has also drawn on the Surya regional scheme, with an allotment of about 170 million litres a day; Chapter 19 tells its history. [n53 A3, B3]

The flood of 2018 showed one cost of the shortage: Nallasopara’s flats stored water in underground household tanks, and the floodwater mixed into them. [n53 B2; NewsBytes] The sewage of the city goes the other way: in 2019 Vasai–Virar treated thirty of its 105 million litres of sewage a day, at one plant at Bolinj, and the Sopara nallah, one of the three drains of the Pelhar river, measured a biochemical oxygen demand of 76 milligrams a litre. [n48 §2; MPCB 2019] The Pelhar river runs nine or ten kilometres from its dam to the Sopara creek: the old channel of the island’s port now carries the city’s drainage. [n48 §2]

No figure for the water supplied to Nallasopara alone, and no account of which of its neighbourhoods still depend on tankers, was found. [n53 B3 Gaps; n06 §4 Gaps]

Living things

Nallasopara has had no dedicated survey of its plants, birds or animals. Its biodiversity, in the researchers’ summary, lies on its two edges: the Tulinj–Achole eastern side toward the Tungareshwar foothills, and the fringe mangroves along the old Sopara–Manikpur creek. [n53 B4] The National Green Tribunal’s joint committee noted that land beside the Vasai creek is in the Coastal Regulation Zone and needs mapping at 1:4,000 to settle its status. [n53 B4; NGT OA 201/2023 (summary)] Western Railway’s replanting for mangroves felled for its fifth and sixth lines was moved from Vasai to Rajodi. [n53 B4; n17] The eastern edge shares the pressures on the Tungareshwar sanctuary told in Chapter 19: illegal building, quarrying and the narrowing of the leopard corridor. [n53 B4] No bird survey and no map of the Sopara-creek mangrove strip was found. [n53 B4 Gaps] The island whose lanes were shaded by mango, tamarind, banyan and pipal in 1882 has, in the record of 2026, no count of its trees.

The garden of Sopara at work, 1882

The work of the island in 1882 is set out in one dense passage of the Gazetteer, and it repays reading slowly.

“The chief products of Sopara and its neighbourhood are molasses, rice, plantains, and betel-leaves. Molasses, rice and betel-leaves go by sea from the Gas, Bolinj and Vagholi landing-places to Surat, Broach, Jambusar, Dholera, Bhavnagar and Gogha. By rail, chiefly from the Virar station, molasses, rice and betel-leaves pass north to Ankleshvar, Nadiad, Viramgam and Patan, and large quantities of plantains and betel-leaves are sent south to Bombay.” The imports were cloth and hardware from Bombay, and provisions and oilcake from Gujarat; the oilcake was the manure for the sugarcane. [n53 B5; n48 §1; Gaz XIV pp. 315–316]

Four products, then: molasses from the cane, rice from the fields, plantains and betel leaf from the gardens. Two routes: by sea from three landings to the ports of the Gulf of Cambay and Kathiawar, and by rail, north to the towns of Gujarat and south to Bombay. And one exchange that tied the island to Gujarat in both directions: the oilcake of the Gujarat presses came south to manure the cane, and the molasses of the cane went north. The island’s trade map in 1882 still pointed north-west across the sea to Gujarat, as it had for two thousand years, and at the same time south to Bombay by the new railway. It is the moment of the turn caught in a sentence.

The landings: Gass, Bolinj, Vagholi

The sea trade went from landings on the dying creek. In the south, Gass, “about a mile” from Sopara, could still be reached by boats of ten or twelve tons; in the north, Bolinj, “about two miles from Dedar or the old Sopara landing”; and in the west, Vagholi, with “a creek of its own”, from which small boats came within about two miles of Sopara. [n11 Q2; n53 B1; Gaz XIV pp. 315–317, 322] Dedar, the old landing of Sopara itself, was dry. [n11 Q2] The Bhatela pond’s landing, a quarter of a mile from where boats could still come at high tide, was the town’s last direct link with the water. [n53 B3]

So the export economy of 1882 depended on the creek at precisely the points where it still held water: its two ends and one side-channel. Every year of silting moved the landings farther from the gardens. The Gazetteer did not record what the boats were or who sailed them; across the Bassein division, the local vessels were built “by Marathas and Native Christians” and crewed from Bassein and its villages, and the boat owners of the coast included Vanis, Brahmans, Agris, Bhandaris, Kolis and Khojas. [n36 §5; Gaz XIII-I pp. 342–343, 358–359] The landing trade, like the creek, did not long survive the railway; no later record of it was found. [n36 §5 Gaps]

Molasses and sugarcane

The cane was the island’s richest crop. Across the Bassein sub-division, the Gazetteer recorded, raw sugar and molasses were made by Pachkalshis, Malis, Native Christians and Samvedi Brahmans, in a season from February to June, with wooden roller mills costing Rs 7 or 8 and copper pans at Rs 3 or 4; women and children carried the cane. [n53 A5; n21 §2; Gaz XIII-I pp. 390–392] Three of those four communities, the Christians, the Samvedi Brahmans and the Pachkalshis, were the husbandmen of the Sopara garden. The molasses of the coast was prized in Gujarat for gudaku, the tobacco paste of the hookah. [n36 §5; Gaz XIII-I p. 338] The sugar factories that failed at Bassein are told in Chapter 19; the village mills of the Sopara garden were the ones that worked.

Rice

Rice was grown in the monsoon on the fields between the gardens, and on the embanked salt rice land at the island’s edges; in 1880–81 rice covered 29,687 acres of the sub-division, nearly nine-tenths of its grain land. [n21 §2; Gaz XIII-II p. 680] Sopara’s rice left by sea and rail with its molasses and betel. [n53 B5] At Bhuigaon, on the island’s western edge, the women’s wells in 2011 still held back the monsoon water for paddy. [n11 Q2]

Betel leaf

The betel vine, pan or nagvel, grew in the shaded gardens, and its leaves were among the island’s chief exports, by sea to Gujarat and in “large quantities” by rail to Bombay. [n53 B5; Gaz XIV p. 316] Betel leaves are perishable; that they went to Bombay by rail in quantity in 1882 is an early sign of what the railway did for the gardens. It let them sell fresh, perishable produce to the city, which the sea could not. The plantains went the same way.

Plantains, fresh and dried

The plantain was the island’s emblem. The Bassein gardens grew nine varieties, basrai, mutheli, tambdi, rajeli, lokhandi, sonkeli, bankeli, karanjeli and narsingi, manured with oilcake and fish. [n21 §2; Gaz XIII-I pp. 291–292] The fresh fruit went to Bombay by rail.

The famous product was the dried plantain, made from the rajeli variety. “The well-known Bassein dried-plantains are the fruit of the rajele variety,” the Gazetteer said, prepared only in Agashi, Vagholi, Vatar, Bolinj, Koprad, Nala, Umrale, Rajodi and Murdes. [n21 §2; n48 §1; Gaz XIII-I p. 292] Of those nine villages, five, Vagholi, Bolinj, Koprad, Umrale and Murdes (Mardes), belong to the Sopara garden, and one is Nala. About eighty-five families of Samvedi Brahmans, fifteen of Pachkalshis and about seventy-five of Native Christians dried ripe plantains in a season from October to January, and the dried fruit sold “to Thana, Bombay, Surat, Poona, and Sholapur” at about Rs 9 a Bengal maund of eighty pounds. [n21 §2; n48 §7; Gaz XIII-I p. 395] The plantain-drying families the Gazetteer named lived at Agashi, Vatar and Koprad, and Agashi’s yield alone was about 160 tons a year, worth about Rs 27,000; Chapter 30 tells the Agashi trade. [n21 §2] At the Nirmal fair in November, dried plantains were among the goods sold. [n11 Q2; Gaz XIV p. 292]

Nala in the record

The list of plantain-drying villages contains a name that matters for this book. The Gazetteer of 1882 has no entry of its own for a village called Nala, and Chapter 22 and the research for it found no Nala place-name near Sopara in the Places volume. [n31 §1] But the name does appear, twice, in the district volume: among the villages where the rajeli was dried, and among the villages where the old bills of exchange were still cashed. “Bills of exchange” of the hundi kind were honoured in 1882 at Mahim and Bassein for sums up to Rs 20,000, and “in the Bassein villages of Nala, Agashi, Sopara and Navghar” up to Rs 10,000. [n48 §1; n21 §2; Gaz XIII-I] So in the 1880s there was a village called Nala in the Bassein sub-division, important enough to dry plantains for export and to cash a bill of ten thousand rupees, alongside Sopara itself. The research does not establish that this Nala is the Nala of Nallasopara, though its listing beside Sopara makes it likely. [inference] The popular account that the town’s name joined two villages, Nala east of the line and Sopara west of it, gives no dates and no source. [n31 §1; Lokshahi] If the two are the same, then Nala enters the written record not as a name on a station board but as a place of plantains and credit.

Sopara as a credit centre

The bills of exchange say something more. A town where a bill for Rs 10,000 could be cashed in 1882 had merchants with capital and correspondents in Bombay and Gujarat. Sopara, with Nala, Agashi and Navghar, was a small credit centre of the island, a step below Bassein and Mahim. [n48 §1 Inferences] The merchants were named in the Gazetteer’s description of the town’s people.

The people of the garden

“The husbandmen are Christians, many of them converted Samvedi Brahmans; Musalmans, many of them Naitas; and Hindus, chiefly Samvedi Brahmans and Bhandaris. The traders are Musalman Naitas and Gujarat Vanis of the Lad caste, who came from Cambay about 1760.” [n53 B5; n11 Q2; n31 §2; Gaz XIV p. 315] The Naitas, the Gazetteer explained, were “descendants of Arab and Persian refugees and traders”; the Lad Vanis had fled Cambay to escape the exactions of its ruler, Momin Khan II, and with them several Gujarat Brahmans settled in Sopara, Bassein and other coast towns between about 1760 and 1766. [n11 Q2; n36 §5; Gaz XIII-I p. 63; Gaz XIV p. 315] The Gazetteer’s informant on Sopara was “a native of Sopara”, Ramdas Kasidas Modi. [n11 Q2; Gaz XIV p. 314 fn.]

Each of these communities had a place in the island’s economy.

The Kupari gardens: Gass, Mardes, Wagholi, Nirmal, Bolinj, Umrale

The Christian husbandmen of Sopara, “many of them converted Samvedi Brahmans”, were the community now called the Kuparis, the Samvedi Christians, whose history is told in Part Seven of this book. The island of Sopara is their heartland. The community’s own writers list as Kupari villages Nirmal, Agashi, Nandakhal, Gass, Bhuigaon, Umrale, Naanbhat, Shrilaiwadi, Bolinj, Mardes, Wagholi, Gomes Ali and Rajodi; of those, Gass, Mardes, Wagholi, Bolinj, Umrale and Nirmal are the villages of the Sopara agar as the Gazetteer drew it in 1882. [n42 §3; n39 §2; n11 Q2; Gaz XIV p. 314] When the Gazetteer listed the island’s products, it was describing, in large part, Kupari work.

Their gardens appear in the record village by village. Gass was the southern landing of the island, where boats of ten or twelve tons came to load molasses, rice and betel for Surat; its lake, with its two cross-dams, steps and Persian wheels, watered the gardens around it. [n11 Q2; n42; Gaz XIV pp. 315–316, 341] Mardes stood “in the centre” of the agar, and Wagholi and Bolinj were landings in their own right; Wagholi, Bolinj, Umrale and Murdes (Mardes) were among the nine villages that dried the rajeli plantain for export. [n11 Q2; n21 §2] About seventy-five families of Native Christians dried plantains in the Gazetteer’s count; the record does not divide them by village. [n21 §2] At Bolinj an inscribed Devanagari stone served as the doorstep of the house of the Christian “Juju Manu Lop, that is Joseph Manuel Lopez”. [n11 Q2; Gaz XIV p. 342]

The gardens paid for the churches. The church of the Holy Cross at Nirmal, originally built in 1557 by the community’s tradition, was rebuilt by its parishioners in 1856 for about Rs 24,000; in 1881 its congregation was 2,184, and its private school’s teacher played the violin in church. [n11 Q2; Gaz XIV p. 293] Rs 24,000 raised from a parish in 1856 is a measure of what the gardens of Nirmal earned; it is more than three times the whole yearly income of the Bassein municipality in 1881, a derived comparison across a quarter-century. [n11 Q2; n21 §9] Gass built its own church in 1942, enlarged in 1957. [n42 §5]

The Kupari economy turned in the same decades as everyone else’s, and earlier than some. The community’s writers say the Kuparis depended mainly on farming until the late 1960s and early 1970s, and then moved into the professions and emigrated, to the Gulf, Europe, North America and Australia. [n11 Q3; Rajan D’Mello blog, 2009] The garden villages were left with old houses and family land: in 2025 the Hindustan Times described Gass as a predominantly Christian village “often described as ‘Mini Goa’ for the ancestral mansions that dot the surrounding farmland”. [n42 §6; HT via Magzter] In the same year that land was threatened by the city’s waste. After the forty-one buildings at Achole, told below, were demolished, the corporation proposed to shift the dumping-ground and sewage-plant reservation to a plot at Gass; in March 2025 the people of Gass filed more than 2,500 objections, and in October 2025 the corporation withdrew its application. [n42 §6] Chapter 26 tells the fight from inside the village. The garden that had exported molasses to Surat was defended, in 2025, as farmland and a lake against a landfill.

What the Kupari record lacks is numbers: no village count of Kupari farmers at any date, no named Kupari plantain-drier, vendor or flower grower in the Sopara villages, no figures for the move to the professions. [n34; n40; n48 Gaps] Those are gaps the community itself could fill.

The Samvedi Brahmans

The Hindu Samvedi Brahmans, from whom by tradition many of the Christian husbandmen had converted, were the island’s other great gardening community. They were husbandmen and gardeners whose daily food the Gazetteer recorded as “rice pulse and vegetables”. [n50 §1; Gaz XIII-I p. 81] Eighty-five Samvedi families dried plantains, the largest group of the Gazetteer’s driers, and Samvedi Brahmans made molasses. [n21 §2] They appear in the island’s record as householders: the Samvedi Brahman Bab Naik, at whose house an inscribed basalt block stood, and the headman of Koprad, Bhiku Patil, “a Samvedi Brahman”, near whose house lay a fifteenth-century inscribed stone. [n11 Q2; n12 §3; Gaz XIV pp. 341–342] That the headman of Koprad was a Samvedi Brahman says something about who held land and office in the northern garden.

The Bhandaris

The Bhandaris, the palm-juice drawers, were among the Hindu husbandmen of Sopara in 1882. [n53 B5] Their trade, tapping the coconut and brab palms of the gardens, and the tree-tax disputes that left them “unruly and discontented”, are told in Chapter 19. [n21 §5] No modern record of Bhandari livelihoods in Nallasopara was found.

The Naitas

The Muslim Naitas were both husbandmen and traders in 1882: farmers in the gardens and merchants in the market. [n53 B5; Gaz XIV p. 315] The old Sopara village kept their name in its quarters: the mohallas of Nawayat, Kharkhandi and Bazar Peth appear in a secondary listing of Nallasopara’s localities, and the Bhatela pond had Muslim graves on two banks. [n31 §1, §2] At Gass, in the field called Sonarbhat, lay the tomb of a Muslim saint, Shaikh Akbar-moi-ud-din, bordered with very large old bricks. [n11 Q2; Gaz XIV p. 341] The Naitas were the descendants of the Arab and Persian traders who had come to the ports of this coast in the Shilahara centuries, and they were still trading at Sopara eight hundred years later. [n11 Q2; n36 §3]

The Lad Vanis and the Gujarat Brahmans

The Lad Vanis from Cambay, who came about 1760, were the island’s shopkeepers and dealers, the link between the gardens and the merchants of Gujarat to whom the molasses and betel went. [n53 B5; n36 §5] Vegetarian, living on rice, millet and wheat, pulse, vegetables, butter and sugar, they were by the Gazetteer’s description “great eaters” who used much butter. [n50 §1; Gaz XIII-I p. 112] They and the Naitas held Sopara’s credit: the bills of exchange of 1882 were theirs to cash. [n48 §1 inference] Their descendants are part of Nallasopara’s large Gujarati-speaking population: in 2001, 17.82 per cent of the town spoke Gujarati. [n31 §3; Wikipedia: Nallasopara]

The Kolis, the Agris and the creek

The creek’s fishers and salt-makers, who disappeared with the creek in the 1860s, are unnamed; the Kolis and Agris of the Bassein coast are the likeliest. [n53 B5; inference] The Urap Agris, who lived at Achole and Manikpur among other villages, are the only creek-edge community the Gazetteer places in Nallasopara East. [n48 §1] By the twenty-first century the researchers found fishing in Nallasopara “minimal”; the beaches on the Nallasopara–Rajodi side are unsourced. [n53 B5 Gaps]

The market of Sopara

In 1882 Sopara had “a crowded weekly market”, one of four weekly markets in the Bassein sub-division, with Agashi, Virar and a place the scan reads as “Dhovh”, each attended by five or six hundred people; there were fifty-three market towns in the district. [n53 B6; n48 §7; Gaz XIII-I; Gaz XIV p. 314] The town had “a broad market place, bordered by large one and two storied houses with rich wood carving”. [n53 B6; n11 Q2; Gaz XIV pp. 314–315] The Virar–Agashi road, metalled, carried Sopara’s traffic as far as Bolinj and helped its trade. [n53 B6; n12 §2; Gaz XIV p. 316] A heritage survey of 2008 listed the Burhanpur Chowk market precinct at Sopara. [n40 A15]

A carved-wood market square, crowded once a week, in a town of 1,700 people with merchants who cashed ten-thousand-rupee bills: this was the commercial centre of the island of Sopara in its last decades as a garden town.

A station that was wanted

What Sopara lacked was a way to the railway. The Gazetteer’s complaint is the first economic argument for the town that became Nallasopara. Sopara was “not more than three-quarters of a mile from the line of railway” but “somewhat badly off for communications”; the track from Manikpur crossed the salt waste and was impassable in the rains; and so “a station at Sopara or a made road to the town from the Agashi high road would be a boon to its people”. [n31 §1; n12 §3; Gaz XIV p. 316] In 1882 the town’s rail-borne goods went north from Virar station, three and a half miles away, and its visitors came from Virar or from the Manikpur bungalow. [n53 B5; n12 §2]

The station came, but when is not settled. Wikipedia’s infobox gives 1865, from a Western Railway heritage book, which the Gazetteer’s complaint of 1882 contradicts; an early Back Bay–Virar service is said, in an article drawing on a 1923 railway magazine, to have halted at “Neela or (Nalasopara)”; a tourism page cites 1869 for regular trains from Bombay to Bassein, Nallasopara and Virar, unverified. [n31 §1; n11 Q2; FPJ, 12 Apr 2022] Chapter 23 weighs the evidence. For this chapter the point is economic: the station, when it came, was put on the eastern side of the old island, at the edge of the salt waste, and the town that grew from it grew on the waste.

The twentieth-century garden

For the hundred years after the Gazetteer, the record of the Sopara gardens is thin, and the island’s story must be told from the wider green belt. The gardens shifted from sugarcane and dried plantains to fresh produce for the city by train: betel leaf, exported in the twentieth century as far as Pakistan until relations soured; velchi, the miniature banana that “sold at a premium” until blight replaced it with tissue-culture Grand Naine; vegetables carried by the Vasaiwale kawadi vendors to Dadar and Bora Bazaar on the four o’clock train until the early 1980s; and, from the late 1980s, flowers, mogra and then sonchafa. [n17; n11 Q3; n53 B5; 101Reporters; India Food Network] Those trades are told in Chapters 19 and V4. Traditional farming with crop rotation and intercropping persisted, a 2011 study found, in Arnala, Virar, Vasai and Nallasopara. [n17; Vazhacharickal & Buerkert 2011]

Nallasopara’s own share of these trades is not separated in any source. The one survival the record names is a market: the flower market and the main market near Nallasopara station, where the flowers brought in for Ganpati stood in floodwater on 13 September 2026. [n29 §4; FPJ, 13 Sept 2026] The flower farms that feed it lie along the eighteen-kilometre stretch from Arnala to Vasai. [n17; 101Reporters] No Nallasopara-specific crop acreage after 1881 was found. [n53 B5 Gaps]

After 1988: the chawl economy

On 31 August 1988 the regional plan for the Vasai–Virar sub-region was sanctioned, and the state “de-reserved this region from agricultural use, contrary to the recommendation of the MMRDA”; in May 1990 about 8,500 hectares were moved from the green zone to urbanisable land. [n17; TIFR; Scribd] Chapters 14, 14 and N2 tell that decision and the town it made. This chapter tells what it did to how people lived on the land of Nallasopara.

The first thing it did was turn land into rent. The student researchers of the Tata Institute of Social Sciences, in their project on Nallasopara, describe two stages. In the 1970s, small-scale industrial zones, marked purple on the plan, were set beside the green zone, but the plan provided little housing or infrastructure for the workers; entrepreneurs built chawls for them. Then, after 2000, the landowners of Nallasopara turned their own farmland into chawls for migrants, many of them displaced from Mumbai. [n53 B5, B6; n31 §3; TISS Make-Break (summaries; the pages returned errors when fetched)] The chawl, a row of single rooms with shared facilities, was the crop the farmland grew next. A landowner who had grown paddy or plantains on an acre could put up rooms on it and draw rent every month, from tenants who needed a roof within reach of the train.

The tenants came from the city’s evictions. In Makarandnagar, the TISS project found, many residents had been displaced from the Sanjay Gandhi National Park; between 1997 and 2004 more than 200,000 people had been displaced in evictions tied to slum rehabilitation in Mumbai, and only about 33,000 of an estimated 80,000 houses qualified for resettlement. [n53 B6; n31 §3; TISS (summary)] Those who did not qualify went where rooms were cheap, and Nallasopara East, on the old salt waste, was among the cheapest. The low-lying, flood-prone land, the TISS researchers noted, overlaps the urbanisable zones. [n31 §3; TISS (summary)]

The second stage replaced the chawls with buildings. A student project at TU Delft described the low-rise baithi chawls of Nallasopara East being replaced by blocks of ground and four storeys. [n31 §3; TU Delft repository (summary)] By 2020 the Deccan Herald, during the COVID-19 pandemic, described East Nallasopara as holding more than five lakh people and called it “Mini Dharavi”: Dhanivbaug and Bilalpada were slums of narrow lanes and small industries. [n53 B6; Deccan Herald 2020 (summary)] A 2025-era report described Rahmat Nagar as dotted with substandard illegal buildings; a tilted building in Nallasopara forced the evacuation of three structures. [n31 §3; FPJ]

The population figures follow the land. Sopara had about 1,700 people in 1882. [n53 B1] The census of 2001 counted 184,664 in Nala Sopara; of its minority-language speakers, 22.92 per cent spoke Hindi, 17.82 per cent Gujarati and 12 per cent Urdu. [n31 §3; Wikipedia: Nallasopara] For 2011 the estimates differ with the boundary used, about 338,820, about 460,000 and about 564,664, and should not be quoted without the census town tables. [n53 B6; n31 §3] In a hundred and thirty years the island’s garden town grew by a factor of a hundred or more, almost all of it in the last forty.

The price of the land, in 2026, is known only from advertisements: a listing site put the average asking price in Nallasopara East at about Rs 10,103 a square foot in March 2026, above Virar’s; another listing site suggested one-bedroom flats from about Rs 25 to 60 lakh, undated and unreliable. [n48 §8; n53 B6; Squareyards; NoBroker] No sale-price or rent series was found. [n53 B6 Gaps]

The forty-one buildings at Achole

The cost of the land economy is clearest at one place on the eastern side. At Agarwal Nagari, also called Lakshmi Nagar, near Achole in Nallasopara East, forty-one buildings went up on land that the municipal plan reserved for a dumping ground and a sewage treatment plant. [n12 §3; n31 §3; n53 A7, B6] The sources disagree on when: Land Conflict Watch says from 2006 on; ThePrint says 2009–12. [n31 §3; n48 §8] About thirty of the sixty acres were reserved and the rest was private land, by ThePrint’s account. The people who bought the flats were, in its words, “daily wage earners, labourers, and lower-middle-class families”. [n48 §8; ThePrint]

The courts ordered the buildings down. The Bombay High Court ordered demolition on 8 July 2024 and refused the residents relief; eviction notices went out in October 2024; demolition began on 28 November 2024 with seven structures; after the Supreme Court dismissed the residents’ special leave petition, the rest came down in early 2025, thirty-four of them in a single day in January, and all forty-one were gone by February 2025. [n12 §3; n48 §8; Lokmat Times; LatestLY/ANI; PropNewsTime; ThePrint] The number of people displaced is given as about 2,000 in some reports, about 2,100 families in another, and 8,000 residents in a third. [n12 §3; n48 §8; Land Conflict Watch; Lokmat Times] A former corporator, Sitaram Gupta, disputed that the land was reserved at all. [n12 §3; Lokmat Times] No rehabilitation policy had been framed for the families, ThePrint reported. [n48 §8]

The ED case

The demolitions led to a money-laundering investigation. The Enforcement Directorate alleged a fraud of about Rs 9,000 crore in the illegal-building trade of Vasai–Virar, and in raids in May 2025 seized about Rs 32 crore from a corporation town-planning official, Y. S. Reddy, and others; its press release of 3 July 2025 described searches in what it called the Jayesh Mehta case. [n48 §8; ThePrint; ED press release, 3 July 2025] In July 2025 it raided twelve locations linked to a former municipal commissioner, Anil Kumar Pawar, reportedly finding about Rs 1.32 to 1.33 crore in cash, and in August 2025 arrested him, with a town-planning official and a former corporator. [n53 A7; FPJ; Deccan Herald (summaries)] These are the Directorate’s allegations and the reported steps of an investigation. The notes record no court verdict, and none of the accused is described here as guilty. Chapter 16 and Chapter 23 tell the case.

For the history of land and livelihood, the episode has a plain shape. Land the plan had reserved for the city’s waste lay on the cheap eastern side; it was built on and sold to the poor; the buildings were torn down by court order; and the investigation alleged that the building permissions and town-planning fees that should have prevented it had been bought. The fees in question are the same town-planning fees that the corporation’s budget of 2026–27 said had fallen short. [n53 A7; FPJ, 11 Mar 2026] The corporation’s budget for that year included a 400-bed hospital at Achole. [n48 §8]

Small industry

Nallasopara’s formal jobs are mostly elsewhere: in the industrial estates of Waliv, Sativali and Gokhivare in Vasai East, told in Chapter 19, and in Mumbai. [n53 B6] But the East has its own small-unit economy, recorded mostly through its fires. The Deccan Herald’s “Mini Dharavi” of 2020 was a place of small industries as well as slums. [n53 B6] Fires have gutted a plastic company at Champion Compound and scrap warehouses at Dhanivbaug, where plastic footwear stocks burned. [n53 B6; FPJ (summary)] The TISS researchers trace the industry to the purple zones of the 1970s. [n53 B6] The district says only that Vasai, Virar and Nallasopara have small and large industrial areas. [n47; Palghar district]

No count of Nallasopara’s units, workers or trades was found: no list of what the small workshops of Dhanivbaug and Bilalpada make, who owns them, or who works in them. [n53 B6 Gaps] The scrap warehouse and the plastic unit are the two trades the record names, and it names them because they burned.

Markets: what is recorded, and what is not

This chapter must say plainly what the record holds about Nallasopara’s markets, because it holds very little.

For 1882 it holds a great deal: Sopara’s crowded weekly market, one of four in the sub-division, with its broad market place lined with carved one- and two-storeyed houses; the Nirmal fair each November, with its six or seven thousand pilgrims and Rs 10,000 of brass, copper, dried plantains, sweets, cloth and cattle; and the merchants, Naita and Lad Vani, who cashed bills of exchange for Rs 10,000. [n53 B6; n11 Q2; n48 §1; Gaz XIV pp. 292, 314–315] For the modern town of perhaps half a million people it holds one item: the flower market and the main market near Nallasopara station, which flooded on 13 September 2026, the night before Ganpati. [n29 §4; FPJ, 13 Sept 2026]

Nothing else was found. There is no description of any other market, bazaar or shopping street of Nallasopara East or West; no account of the station-area trade; no figures for footfall, traders or turnover; no record of when the weekly market of Sopara stopped, or whether it still meets. [n53 B6 Gaps; n31 §3 Gaps] The heritage survey of 2008 names the Burhanpur Chowk precinct at Sopara, but not what is sold there. [n40 A15] This is not because Nallasopara has no markets; anyone who has stepped off a train there knows otherwise. It is because no one has written them down in a source this research could reach. The Marathi and Hindi press, and the traders themselves, are where the record would begin.

The commuter town

The economy of modern Nallasopara is the economy of its station. The Vasai–Virar city plan of 2010 says that about sixty per cent of the sub-region’s workers commute to Mumbai and calls the area “a growing dormitory town to the Mumbai city”; in 2001 about one per cent of its workers were cultivators or agricultural labourers. [n48 §8; CDP p. 23] Those figures are for the whole sub-region, and Chapter 30 sets out the census tables; there are no separate figures for Nallasopara. [n53 B6 Gaps] No station footfall for Nallasopara was found. [n53 B6 Gaps]

The station that Sopara wanted in 1882 became the town’s reason for being. Its trains are the town’s livelihood, and their stopping is its crisis. When the trains stopped in July 2026, the town’s commuters walked the tracks or paid Rs 100 to 150 to ride a tractor through the water, and auto-rickshaws across the region charged ten to fifteen times the fare. [n53 A2, B2; FPJ, 8 July 2026; Khaleej Times] The survey to raise the track, if it leads to building, will be the second time the railway has reshaped the land of Sopara: the first embankment dammed the creek; the second is meant to lift the line above the water that the first one trapped.

The stupa and the pilgrims

The last livelihood of Nallasopara is the oldest reason anyone came to it: pilgrimage. The town’s heritage, in the researchers’ summary, is the Ashokan edict site and the stupa mound, Buddhist; the Chakreshwar temples and their sculptures; the Nirmal temples and the Shankaracharya’s samadhi; and the sacred waters of the Bhatela pond and Ram’s Pool. [n53 B7; Gaz XIV pp. 292–293, 316–340] Chapters 22 and T6 tell them as history and as places to visit.

In 1882 the pilgrimage economy was already alive. The Hindus of Sopara bathed in Ram’s Pool in Shravan; the Nirmal fair drew its thousands every November; and the Tulinj dyke was an excursion for visitors from the Manikpur bungalow. [n53 B3, B7; Gaz XIV pp. 292, 339, 342] That same year the stupa at Sopara was excavated by Bhagwanlal Indraji; Chapter 22 tells the excavation and what it found. [n50 §2; n36 §9]

The heritage has not become an economy. Nallasopara, the researchers found, has “few visitor facilities”, and no tourism statistics exist for it: no count of visitors to the stupa, the edict site, Chakreshwar or Nirmal, apart from the Gazetteer’s 6,000 to 7,000 pilgrims at the Nirmal fair in 1882. [n53 B7] A Rs 222 crore flood-retention pond planned at Nilemore and Gogte is reported to include a tourism component, but its status is unverified. [n48 §3] The contrast with Vasai, where the Archaeological Survey announced conservation of three churches and better visitor facilities at the fort in June 2026, is marked. [n53 A7] Sopara, which drew merchants and monks from across the Indian Ocean two thousand years ago, draws in 2026 an uncounted trickle of visitors to a mound in a city that has grown up around it.

Every community on the island

The island of Sopara has held, at one time or another, nearly every community of this coast, and each has had a place in its economy.

In 1882 the Kupari Christians, “many of them converted Samvedi Brahmans”, were the husbandmen of the gardens of Gass, Mardes, Wagholi, Nirmal, Bolinj and Umrale; they made molasses, dried plantains and loaded the boats at the landings; they paid for the rebuilding of Nirmal church in 1856; from the late 1960s many left the gardens for the professions and the world; and in 2025 the people of Gass defended their farmland and lake against a dumping ground. [n53 B5; n11 Q2; n42 §6] The Samvedi Brahmans were gardeners, molasses-makers and the largest group of plantain-driers, and held the headmanship of Koprad. [n21 §2; n11 Q2] The Pachkalshis dried plantains and made molasses beside them. [n21 §2] The Bhandaris tapped the palms and farmed. [n53 B5] The Naita Muslims farmed and traded, kept their quarters in the old village, and buried their dead by the Bhatela pond. [n53 B5; n31 §1–2] The Lad Vanis from Cambay and the Gujarat Brahmans who came with them about 1760 were the merchants and creditors of the market. [n53 B5; n36 §5] The Kolis and Agris, the likeliest fishers and salt-makers of the old creek, lost that work with the creek in the 1860s; the Urap Agris lived at Achole on the eastern edge. [n53 B5; n48 §1] The Jains, whose tirtha of Soparaka Chapter 22 tells, are part of the Gujarati-speaking town of today. [n31 §3] The Warlis and other Adivasi communities appear in no Nallasopara source found; their labour in the quarries and kilns of the foothills is told in Chapter 19. And the migrants, from Mumbai’s evictions, from the national park, from north India and Gujarat, Hindi-, Gujarati- and Urdu-speaking, are the chawl tenants, the flat-buyers, the workshop hands and the commuters of the modern town, and the families who lost their homes at Achole. [n31 §3; n53 B6]

From the creek to the culvert

Put the two ends of the record side by side and the change on the island of Sopara is the starkest on this coast.

In 1882 Sopara was a garden town of 1,700 people at the centre of a well-watered island, its lanes sunk below its fields, its ponds and winding lakes fringed with Persian wheels, its lake at Nirmal a quarter of a mile wide. It grew molasses, rice, plantains and betel leaf, and sent them by boat from Gass, Bolinj and Vagholi to the ports of the Gulf of Cambay, and by rail to Gujarat and Bombay. Its Christian, Samvedi, Pachkalshi and Bhandari husbandmen worked the gardens; its Naita and Lad Vani traders kept a crowded weekly market in a square of carved houses, and cashed bills of ten thousand rupees. Its creek, which had carried cargo since the Periplus and pearls in al-Idrisi’s day, had died within twenty years, and with it the fishing and the salt-making; its eastern hill had been quarried for the railway that killed the creek. The Gazetteer’s one wish for it was a station.

In 2026 the island is a town of perhaps half a million people, most of them living on the old salt waste east of the station that Sopara wanted. Its creek is a drain, and its old channel carries the Sopara nallah to the Pelhar river. Its gardens survive in the Kupari villages of the west, defended as farmland against a landfill. Its farmland in the east became chawls, then buildings, some of them on land reserved for the city’s waste, torn down by the courts, the subject of a money-laundering case. Its workshops are known by their fires and its markets hardly at all. Its stupa, which once drew the Buddhist world, draws no counted visitor. And every monsoon the water that the creek once carried away stands on the old salt waste, fills the ground floors, mixes into the household tanks, and stops the trains on an embankment that the railway now proposes to raise.

The ground did not change. The basalt of Tulinj still rings like an anvil where it has not been carried away; the plain is the same silt, a metre and a half above the sea; the monsoon is the same monsoon. What changed was the drain, the work, and the number of people standing on the ground when the water came. The rest of this book’s story of Nallasopara, its peoples, its faiths and its stones, is told in Chapters 22, N2 and K7, and in Part Seven.

What the record does not hold

  • The creek. No core sample or radiocarbon date for its silting; the date when large ships stopped reaching Sopara; who fished it and made salt in it before the 1860s; any record of the landing trade at Gass, Bolinj and Vagholi after 1882. [n36 §6 Gaps; n53 B1, B5]
  • Geology. No modern geological map of the Nallasopara East plain; no laterite source; what became of the Tulinj dyke. [n53 B1 Gaps]
  • Lakes and ponds. No current area, depth, pollution or rejuvenation record for Nirmal lake, Chakreshwar lake, the Bhatela pond, Ram’s Pool or the Gass lake; how many of the Sopara garden’s bavkhals survive. [n53 B3 Gaps]
  • Floods. No Nallasopara-specific record for 2005 or 2024; no death toll for any flood in Nallasopara alone; the status of the Nilemore–Gogte retention pond and of the track-raising survey. [n53 B2 Gaps; n48 §3]
  • Water. No ward-wise supply figures; which neighbourhoods depend on tankers. [n53 B3 Gaps]
  • Biodiversity. No bird, tree or mangrove survey of Nallasopara; no map of the Sopara-creek mangrove strip. [n53 B4 Gaps]
  • Crops. No Nallasopara crop acreage after 1881; no village split of the plantain-drying families; no named Kupari or Samvedi farmer, drier, vendor or flower grower in the Sopara villages. [n53 B5 Gaps; n21 §2]
  • Nala. Whether the plantain-drying and bill-cashing “Nala” of the 1882 Gazetteer is the Nala of Nallasopara. [n31 §1; n48 §1]
  • Markets. Every market of the modern town except the flower and main markets near the station; when Sopara’s weekly market ended. [n53 B6 Gaps]
  • Industry and work. Unit counts, trades and workers for Dhanivbaug, Bilalpada and the rest of the East; Census 2011 worker tables for Nallasopara; station footfall; rent and price series. [n53 B6 Gaps]
  • Population. A reliable 2011 figure; the 1881–1951 counts for Sopara and Nala. [n53 B6; n31 §2 Gaps]
  • The Achole buildings. The true number displaced; whether the land was reserved (disputed by a former corporator); the outcome of the ED case, of which the notes record no verdict. [n12 §3; n48 §8]
  • Tourism. Any visitor count for the stupa, the edict site, Chakreshwar or Nirmal after 1882. [n53 B7]

The 1982 Thane District Gazetteer, the census village and town tables, the corporation’s development plan and ward records, the court files of the Achole case, the Marathi and Hindi press, and the memories of the garden families of Gass, Nirmal, Mardes and Wagholi, and of the traders by the station, are where these gaps would begin to close.
