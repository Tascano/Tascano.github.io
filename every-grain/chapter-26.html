Period covered: from the lavas that made the ground, about 62 million years ago, to Gass in November 2026

In this chapter. One village, studied in full from the lava under its fields to November 2026, as a model of what a village’s whole history can look like.

A washing stone in front of a house

Some time in 1881 or 1882, a party of antiquarians walked south out of Sopara along the garden lanes. They were working for the Gazetteer of the Bombay Presidency, the great survey that the British government of Bombay was compiling district by district, and the Sopara pages of its Thana volume were being drawn, as a footnote says, from Pandit Bhagvanlal Indraji’s paper on the antiquarian remains of Sopara, with local help from Mr W. W. Loch, Mr Govind Moreshvar Pandit and Mr Ramdas Kasidas Modi, “a native of Sopara”. [n42; Gazetteer XIV p. 314 fn.] [n11; Gaz. XIV p. 314 fn.] About a mile south of Sopara they came into a village of raised garden land, thick with trees and sugarcane, which the people of the place told them was “the site of old Sopara”. [n42; Gazetteer XIV p. 341]

In front of one house, the house of a man the Gazetteer names as Degu Pobria Mhatara, lay a smooth six-sided block of basalt, about two feet broad, two and a half feet long and two feet high. The family used it as a stone for washing clothes and bathing, and, it seemed, for grinding curry powder. On its smooth top, cut in large letters, was a single word. The surveyors read it as “Badhuya”, “of Badhu”, apparently someone’s name, and judged the letters to be “perhaps about B.C. 200”. [n42; Gazetteer XIV p. 341]

About two hundred yards south-west, at the house of a Samvedi Brahman, “Bab Naik by name”, they found a second block, about a foot and a half across and high, carved on two sides: “Kodasa Kalavadasa”, “of Kalavad the Kod”, and “Ugudeveya”, “of Ugradeva”. [n42; Gazetteer XIV p. 341] All through the village, blocks of basalt stood in front of verandas and were built into the plinths of houses. Only one more had letters. [n42; Gazetteer XIV p. 341]

The village was Gass. The Gazetteer spells it “Gas”; its OCR text sometimes garbles it as “Gds” or “Gés”; a 2020 archaeological paper and a 2025 newspaper write “Gaas”; Wikipedia’s article on its most famous modern son writes “Ghas”; the municipal gazette writes गास; the church directories, the Diocese of Poona and the village itself write “Gass”. [n42; spelling note] This book writes Gass throughout, and where a quotation spells it otherwise, the name is given here as [Gass].

That scene, a twenty-two-centuries-old name used as a household washing stone, is a fair picture of Gass’s whole history as the record keeps it. The village is old ground with very old things in it, but almost nothing was ever written about Gass for its own sake. The documented record, as the research behind this chapter found it, is thin: one passage in the 1882 Gazetteer, one archaeological summary published in 2020, a handful of church directory entries, a Wikipedia article flagged for missing citations, and news reports since 2023. [n42; key warning] There is no history book of Gass, no parish chronicle that could be found, no Portuguese register entry that could be verified, and no census table that counts it separately. Everything else has to be read from the villages around it, from Sopara, Nirmal, Bolinj and Vagholi, and from the long story of the coast that the rest of this book tells.

This chapter tries to do that honestly. It tells Gass’s story in time order, from the rock to November 2026. Where there is evidence, it gives the evidence and its source. Where there is a tradition, it calls it a tradition. Where there is silence, it says so, and then says what the neighbours were doing and how we know. The author of this book was born in Gass. That is a reason to be more careful with it, not less.

The ground: basalt, a hill of loose blocks, and an island that was not quite an island

Rock from the time after the dinosaurs

Gass stands on the Deccan basalts, the vast lava field that covers much of western India. The main Deccan eruptions came around 66 to 65 million years ago, across the extinction of the dinosaurs. The rocks of the Mumbai coast, on which Vasai lies, are a little younger: about 62.5 million years old, from the time when the Seychelles block and the Laxmi Ridge tore away from India’s western edge and the land bent seaward along a fold that geologists call the Panvel flexure. [n14; Pande et al. 2017; Sheth & Pande 2014] No published date exists for basalt inside Vasai taluka itself; the nearest dated rocks are the Dongri and Uttan rhyolites just across the Vasai creek, which are about the same age as that break-up or slightly younger. So the rock under Gass is most likely the Bombay-type sequence of about 62.5 to 61 million years ago, but that is an inference from neighbouring dates, not a measurement made at Gass. [n14; IAVCEI 2025 abstract; inference stated in note]

No geological or geomorphological study of Gass itself has been found. [n42 §1 gaps] What the record does give is the stone at the surface, and how people used it.

West of Gass, between it and the sea, rises Brahma hill, a bush- and palm-covered knoll. [n11; Gaz. XIV p. 315] In 1882 the Gazetteer described large blocks of basalt piled on it “in places as if into an artificial wall”, and drew a conclusion from what the surveyors saw on the way there: “The number of these blocks, laid as a footway along some of the lowest parts of the road between [Gass] and Nirmal and built into the plinths of [Gass] and Sopara houses, shows that Brahma hill has long been used as a quarry.” [n42; Gazetteer XIV, Brahma hill passage] Between Gass and Brahma hill, under a banyan tree, stood a slab carved with a cobra god, a nagoba. [n42; Gazetteer XIV, Brahma hill footnote]

East, beyond the railway line, the Tulinj hill (the Gazetteer’s “Rakehi Dongar”) was a basalt dyke of six-sided pillars, six to twenty-two feet long. “Many of the pillars were used when the railway was made,” the Gazetteer says, and people were still carrying them away in 1882. [n31; Gazetteer, Tulinj passage] The six-sided washing stone in front of Degu’s house was the same kind of natural column. Basalt cracks into such prisms as thick lava cools. The people of this coast have been picking them up, carving them, building with them and walking on them for as long as there are records.

South of Gass the Gazetteer found a line of large basalt blocks that local people called the dagdi bandh, the “stone dam”. It lay about half a mile south of Bab Naik’s house, “on the way to Achola village”, and looked like “the remains of an old footway raised across the low muddy land to the south of [Gass]”. There were similar footways near Bolinj and in the low land between Gass and Brahma hill. [n42; Gazetteer XIV pp. 341–342] Nobody knows who laid them or when. They are the oldest roads of Gass that can still be described, causeways of loose basalt thrown across mud that was under water for part of every year.

The island of Sopara

To understand why there is a village at Gass at all, one has to see the land as it was before the railway, and before the silt.

The Gazetteer of 1882 describes a strip of “richly wooded well-watered and highly tilled” garden land about three miles broad, running north from the Bassein creek to the Vaitarna river. Its middle part was “locally known as the garden or agar of Sopara”, and the Gazetteer names the villages that made it up: “Bolinj, Koprad and Umbrala in the north, Nirmal and Vagholi in the west, [Gass] in the south, and Sopara and Mardesh in the centre”. The agar of Agashi lay to the north and the agar of Bassein to the south. [n42; Gazetteer XIV p. 314] [n11; Gaz. 1882 p. 314]

This strip was cut off from the mainland by water. “Under the Portuguese the whole of this rich strip, with its patches of salt marsh, was known as the island of Bassein, and, before Portuguese times, as the island of Sopara.” [n11; Gaz. 1882 pp. 314–315] The water that made it an island was a backwater that left the Bassein creek “a little above the railway bridge” and wound north to the Vaitarna. By 1882 it had three local names: the Manikpur creek in the south, the Sopara creek in the centre and the Bolinj creek in the north. It “was once a considerable arm of the sea overflowing a large breadth of land on both banks of its present channel”. [n42; Gazetteer XIV pp. 314–315] [n39 §3]

So Gass was born on the edge of an island. It sat on the raised garden land at the southern end of Sopara’s agar, with the creek, an arm of the sea, coming up to it from the south. The Gazetteer’s surveyors, walking from Manikpur, crossed “a bare flat, formerly flooded during the rains and now being gradually reclaimed for rice”, and saw to the north “the raised lands of [Gass] and Sopara, full of trees and sugarcane gardens”, with Nirmal’s long wooded hill and Brahma hill to the west. The garden land stood “four or five feet” above the salt waste. [n42; Gazetteer XIV, Sopara “Objects of Interest”] Four or five feet is not much. It is the difference between a field that floods with salt water and a field that grows sugarcane, and it is the reason Gass exists where it does.

The sea itself has moved. A modern study summarised by Shah and his co-authors puts sea level on this coast about half a metre higher around the second and third centuries BCE, when Sopara was a great port, and describes silting over about two thousand years. [n02; Shah 2020, citing Arunachalam 2002] A mid-Holocene deposit of shells near Nala Sopara has been carbon-dated to about 3250 ± 110 years before the present, and a geographer, Ghate, wrote in 1988 on the late Holocene changes of the estuary around Sopara. [n42; UNESCO-hosted paper, seen as a snippet; Ghate 1988 not read] Neither study is about Gass in particular. Together they say that the low land around Gass was a shifting margin of sea, mud and salt marsh while people were already living on the raised ground.

There is a popular idea that “Gass and Nirmal were once part of the creek”. The research behind this chapter met it only in a search summary of a page that could not be found, so it is not used here as a source. [n42 §1 inferences] It fits the Gazetteer’s words about the creek once overflowing “a large breadth of land on both banks”, but the raised gardens of Gass, four or five feet above the marsh, were more likely the shore than the water.

Lake, ponds and wells

Gass has always been a village of water held in hollows. In 1882 the Gazetteer described the “[Gass] lake” as “a winding hollow, 120 yards broad and 800 long with two cross dams”, fringed with gardens and Persian wheels, the bullock-turned water wheels that lifted water into the channels of the gardens. [n42; Gazetteer XIV p. 341] At its south end were dressed stone steps. [n42; Gazetteer XIV p. 341] Wikipedia today describes Gass simply as “a natural village with a big lake”. [n42; Wikipedia: Gass, Maharashtra]

On the Sopara side, the Gazetteer describes a cluster of ponds at the boundary. At the southern limit of Sopara was the Musalman pond. “About fifty yards south-east, within [Gass] limits, is the Khere or Shirmoli pond”, and about fifty yards south of that was found the statue of Brahma that in 1882 stood near the Chakreshvar temple. The Bhatela pond, 240 by 146 yards, lay about fifty yards east of the Khare pond. [n42; Gazetteer XIV, Sopara section, near p. 340] [n31; Gazetteer pp. 313–340]

No source says whether the Gass lake and these ponds are natural remnants of the old creek or tanks dug by people, or both. [n42 §1 gaps] A 2020 paper speaks of a “Ram Kund (Gaas tank)”, but it cannot be confirmed that this is the same water as the Gazetteer’s Gass lake. [n42 §1 gaps; n42 §2]

The landing

The most important single fact about the Gass of the past is that boats came to it.

In 1882 the Gazetteer wrote that “with the mud-laden Vaitarna on the north and the Bassein creek or Ulhas river on the south, silting has been rapid, especially since the long railway embankment has dammed the flow of water from the south.” Even so, small boats of ten to twelve tons, thirty to forty khandis, still came up from the south “as far as [Gass], about a mile from Sopara”, and from the north as far as Bolinj, about two miles away. “At Sopara the creek is dry.” [n42; Gazetteer XIV p. 315]

A second sentence in the same passage gives a slightly smaller size and names the place: “Small boats of from eight to ten tons … come at high tide from the south as near as [Gass] landing-place about a mile from Sopara, from the west as near as Vagholi about two miles, and from the north as near as Bolinj also about two miles.” [n42; Gazetteer XIV pp. 315–316] The two figures, ten to twelve tons and eight to ten tons, are both in the Gazetteer; the difference is probably between the largest boats and the usual ones, but the text does not say.

So in 1882 Gass was the southern, still-navigable head of a dying waterway. Old Sopara’s own landing, which the Gazetteer calls “Dedar”, was dry. [n31; Gazetteer pp. 314–316] Gass, a mile further south, was where the tide still brought boats. From the landings at Gass, Vagholi and Bolinj, molasses, rice and betel leaf went by sea to Surat, Broach, Jambusar, Dholera, Bhavnagar and Gogha, the ports of the Gujarat coast. [n42; Gazetteer XIV pp. 315–316] [n11; Gaz. 1882 pp. 315–316]

That is the land under Gass: a raised garden on an island of basalt and silt, with a lake in a winding hollow, a hill of loose blocks to the west, causeways across the mud to the south, and a landing where the sea came in at high tide. Everything in this chapter happens on that ground.

Gass in the world of ancient Sopara

A mile from a famous port

For about six hundred years, from the third century BCE to the third century CE, the village ground of Gass lay a mile from one of the best-known ports of the Indian Ocean.

The hard evidence for that port begins with the emperor Ashoka. In 1882 Bhagvanlal Indraji found “a broken slab of basalt bearing parts of some lines of Rock Edict VIII” near the Bhatela pond, east of Sopara. [n02; Sircar, Epigraphia Indica 32] That is the pond the Gazetteer places about fifty yards east of the Khare pond, which itself lies “within [Gass] limits”. [n42; Gazetteer XIV near p. 340] Whether the Bhatela pond is inside Gass or just across the line in Sopara the sources do not say plainly, but the first Ashokan inscription ever found on this coast came out of the ground a few dozen yards from the Gass boundary. A second fragment, of Rock Edict IX, was found on 1 January 1956 at Bhuigaon, another Kupari village to the west, by N. A. Gore, librarian of the Bombay Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society. Both are in the museum in Mumbai now called CSMVS. [n02; Sircar, EI 32] The epigraphist D. C. Sircar concluded that a full set of the fourteen Rock Edicts “must have originally existed near Sopara”. [n02; Sircar, EI 32] One modern paper calls the edict “perhaps not only Sopara’s but also Mumbai’s earliest historical evidence”. [n02; Shah 2020]

The stupa, the brick mound that local people called the “Burud Rajacha Kot”, the Burud king’s fort, stands in Merdes village. [n02; Wikipedia: Sopara] By the Gazetteer’s own walking route of 1882, Sopara village lay about half a mile west of the mound, and Gass about a mile south of Sopara, so the stupa was roughly a mile and a half from Gass by that path. [n42 §1 inferences] In April 1882 Indraji opened the mound and found a stone coffer holding nested caskets of copper, silver, stone, crystal and gold, gold flowers, fragments believed to be of the Buddha’s begging bowl, a Satavahana silver coin, and eight bronzes of the seventh to ninth centuries CE: six past Buddhas, Shakyamuni and Maitreya. The relics are now with the Asiatic Society of Mumbai. [n02; Asiatic Society of Mumbai; LiveHistoryIndia] The stupa was first built in the Mauryan period, enlarged between the first century BCE and the third century CE, and expanded again under the Rashtrakutas. [n02; LiveHistoryIndia] The coin is attributed by some to Gautamiputra Satakarni and by others to Yajnashri Satakarni; the question cannot be settled without Indraji’s original paper. [n02 §1 gaps]

Around the stupa was a port the Greek and Roman world knew by name. The Periplus of the Erythraean Sea, a first-century merchant’s guide, lists “Suppara” as the first market town south of Barygaza (Bharuch), before Calliena (Kalyan). The geographer Ptolemy called it “Soupara”. [n02; Periplus, Schoff tr.; Wikipedia citing Thapar] An excavation in 1993, by the Archaeological Survey of India with the British Academy, found Satavahana and Kshatrapa coins, semi-precious stone beads, sherds of Northern Black Polished Ware and pieces of Roman amphorae. [n02; Shah 2020, citing Tripati & Gaur 1997] Donors from Sopara appear in the cave inscriptions of the Deccan: at Nashik, Ushavadata, son-in-law of the Kshatrapa Nahapana, built a guesthouse with four doors at “Sorparaga”; at Karle a Satimita of Sopara gave relics; at Naneghat a Govindadasa “of Soparaya” cut a cistern. [n02; Shah 2020] Trade roads ran inland from Sopara by Kalyan to Nashik, Paithan and Ter. [n02; Shah 2020]

Buddhist tradition made Sopara the home of Purna, a merchant who, the Divyavadana says, built a sandalwood hall near Surparaka and invited the Buddha, who came flying through the sky with five hundred monks. That is legend; no historical evidence shows the Buddha ever came here. [n02; LiveHistoryIndia; 84000] It is, though, a legend that ancient people told about this very creek-side country. When the boats came up to Gass in 1882, they were moving on water that had carried merchants in the stories of the Buddha.

How did the ancient port actually work with its creek? The study cited by Shah describes Sopara in the early historical period facing Agashi island to the north and Bassein to the south, with backwater anchorages, and creek navigation continuing into the nineteenth century with vessels of about twenty tonnes. [n02; Wikipedia citing Tripati & Gaur 1997] Islands and creeks protected the harbour, and local boats piloted ships in. [n02; LiveHistoryIndia] The village ground of Gass, on the southern shore of the island where the creek still reached in 1882, was part of that sheltered water world, whether or not anyone lived there yet.

The two stones

Now return to the two blocks in Gass.

The Gazetteer’s description is precise. Degu’s block: smooth, six-sided basalt, about two feet broad, two and a half long and two high, used for washing, bathing and grinding curry powder, carved “in large Pali letters, perhaps about B.C. 200, the letters ‘Badhuya’, that is ‘Of Badhu’, apparently some one’s name.” Then comes a sentence that changes how we must read it: “about three years ago the stone was struck against in ploughing Kaklai’s field close to Brahma hill. It is probably a grave-stone.” [n42; Gazetteer XIV p. 341]

Bab Naik’s block: about a foot and a half across and high, carved on two sides, “Kodasa Kalavadasa”, “of Kalavad the Kod”, and “Ugudeveya”, “of Ugradeva”. And again: “like Degu’s stone, this was brought from near Brahma hill.” [n42; Gazetteer XIV p. 341]

Three things follow, and each should be said carefully.

First, the readings and the date. The Gazetteer calls the letters “Pali”. Today they would be described as early Brahmi script; “Pali” was the nineteenth-century label, used loosely for the language of such short personal names. “Badhuya”, “of Badhu”, is a genitive, the form a name takes when it means “belonging to” or “in memory of”. [n42 §2 inferences] The date “perhaps about B.C. 200” is the surveyor’s own estimate, and he wrote “perhaps”. No modern palaeographic study of these stones has been found. [n42 §2 gaps] It is fair to say that the letters were judged, in 1882, to be of roughly the age of the Ashokan edicts or a little after, the age of the great port. It is not fair to give the date as fact.

Second, who read them. The Gazetteer does not say that Indraji personally read the Gass stones. It says that the antiquarian parts of the Sopara section were taken from his 1882 paper, and the Gass passage sits inside that section, so Indraji is the likely reader. That is an inference; his paper in the Journal of the Bombay Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society for 1882 was not read for this chapter. [n42 §2 inferences and gaps]

Third, and most important, what they prove. Both stones had been carried to Gass from fields near Brahma hill, Degu’s only about three years before the surveyors saw it, after a plough struck it. The Gazetteer’s guess is that Degu’s was a grave-stone, a memorial. So the stones show that people who could write, or could pay someone to carve letters, were naming their dead or their gifts near Brahma hill, between Gass and the sea, in roughly the time of the port. They do not show that a settlement stood on the spot where Gass now stands. The honest description is “Sopara-era stones reused in Gass houses”. [n42 §2 inferences]

Where are they now? No source after 1882 mentions either stone. They may still lie in front of a house in Gass, built into a plinth, buried under a new floor, broken up, or moved away. The research could not find out. [n42 §2 gaps] It is one of the questions this chapter will hand back to the families of Gass at its end.

“The site of old Sopara”

The people of Gass in 1882 told the surveyors that their village was “the site of old Sopara”. [n42; Gazetteer XIV p. 341] That is a tradition, recorded in 1882, and it deserves respect as a tradition: it shows that Gass families, more than a century ago, thought of their village as the ancient city’s heart. But the Gazetteer itself places the main Buddhist mound and the remains of the city around Sopara village and the stupa, not at Gass. [n42 §2 inferences]

There is something in between. A popular history website says that the city of the Shilahara period, from the eighth to the thirteenth centuries, covered “present-day Sopara, Bolinj, Nirmal and Gaas”. [n42; LiveHistoryIndia] [n02 §4] That claim, from a popular site, may be right, and the medieval finds in Gass (described below) support a busy medieval landscape there. But it is not an inscription, and it is about the medieval town, not the port of Ashoka and the Periplus. The villagers’ tradition, the Gazetteer’s map and the popular claim can all be held together: Gass was part of the wider Sopara country, close enough to the old city that its fields gave up the city’s stones, without being proved to be the city’s centre.

An anchor near Taki Pada

In 2018 a stone anchor was found near Gass, at Taki Pada. The finders’ report appeared in the Marathi newspaper Loksatta, which the research for this chapter could not open; the find is known through a 2020 paper by Monish Shah, Siddharth Kale and Jainacharya Bhagyayashsuri. They wrote that the anchor’s find-spot “coincides with the place where an earlier port would have been; which has now been filled up due to siltation”, and that a “Bandar Masjid”, a port mosque (bandar means port), stands nearby. [n42; Shah 2020 §§2–3] [n02 §6]

A stone anchor is a heavy shaped stone with a hole or holes for a rope, used by Indian Ocean boats for many centuries. The paper does not give a date for this one, and the research could not find one. [n42 §1 gaps] So the anchor cannot be placed in Ashoka’s time, the Shilaharas’ or the Portuguese. What it does show, together with the Gazetteer’s landing, is that boats once moored on the Gass side of the old creek, at a spot now silted and built over. The name “Bandar Masjid” carries the same memory in a word.

The same paper reports the remains of a Jain temple in the Taki Pada part of Gass: lintels, an image of Vishnu with the ten avatars, hero stones and debris from the water tank, and a temple plinth where a slum now stands. Some of these remains are now with the University of Mumbai and some with local temples. [n42; Shah 2020 §§3–4] [n02 §6] These belong to the medieval story, which comes next.

What the earliest people of Gass did

No source says when people first lived at the spot called Gass, what they called it, or what language they spoke there in the time of the port. No source gives the origin of the name “Gass”. [n42; gaps throughout] Across the island, the people of the ancient port were Buddhist, Brahmanical and Jain, as the stupa, the Puranic praise of “holy Surparaka” and the later Jain tradition show. [n02 §2] Their trade went in ivory, shells, silk, elephants and horses, according to one modern summary. [n02; LiveHistoryIndia] Farming, fishing and salt are generic truths of a creek-side garden island, and the Gazetteer’s 1882 picture of sugarcane, rice and betel on the raised land, and rice reclaimed from the flats, is the earliest detailed picture of Gass farming. Anything earlier would be invention. The ancient people of Gass, if there were any, are known only by three names cut on two stones that came from a field nearby: Badhu, Kalavad the Kod, and Ugradeva.

The medieval centuries: stones without words

Say it plainly: Gass is not named

From the end of the ancient port to the arrival of the Portuguese, roughly thirteen hundred years, there is no written record that names Gass. No Shilahara copper plate or stone has been found that mentions it. No Arab geographer, no friar, no sultan’s chronicle, no Marathi bakhar names it. [n42 §3] The research behind this chapter looked for a Shilahara inscription naming Gass and found none; it noted that the popular claim placing Gass inside the Shilahara city is unverified. [n42 §3 gaps]

That is the silence. But it is a strange silence, because the ground of Gass is full of medieval things. The stones speak, even though no text speaks of the village. This section first says what the neighbours were doing in these centuries and how we know it, and then turns to what was found in Gass itself.

The quiet centuries around Sopara

For Sopara as a whole, archaeologists see two peaks of activity: from before Ashoka to the third century CE, and again from the ninth to the thirteenth century. Between them, from the fourth to the ninth century, the survey by Sila Tripati and A. S. Gaur found “no cultural remains”. [n02; Wikipedia citing Tripati & Gaur 1997] Literary sources do not fill the gap. The old Bombay Gazetteer’s history volume says flatly: “No further notice of the North Konkan has been traced till the rise of the Silaharas.” [n03; TG-II p. 421] There are a few faint lights. Kosmas Indikopleustes in the sixth century describes a Christian bishopric at Kalyan, and the Gazetteer thought Christian settlements at Kalyan and Sopara were probably strengthened by Nestorian refugees from Persia. [n03; TG-II p. 420 n. 4] At Nirmal, the village just west of Gass, the Gazetteer recorded a seventh-century Sanskrit inscription on a dressed stone standing in front of a house about five hundred yards north of the chief temple. [n11; Gaz. 1882 p. 293] The Buddhist bronzes of the seventh to ninth centuries in the stupa show that the great mound was still being cared for, perhaps repaired under the Rashtrakutas. [n02; Shah 2020; LiveHistoryIndia]

Then come the Shilaharas of North Konkan, a Shaiva dynasty that ruled about 1,400 villages for about 450 years, from about 800 to 1260, first under the Rashtrakutas and later on their own. [n03; TG-II pp. 421–424] Sopara, which they called Shurparaka, was one of their places of note, a customs port and the head of a district. A copper plate of King Mummuniraja dated 20 February 1049 grants villages to fourteen Brahmins, some of them in the “Shurparaka-shatshashti vishaya”, the territory of sixty-six villages round Sopara. The villages it names include Virar, Pelhar and Adane. [n03; CII-VI pp. 115–118] Gass is not among those named, but if the sixty-six villages round Sopara were a real unit, Gass, a mile from Sopara, was almost certainly inside it. That is an inference from geography, not a record.

The Shilahara court reached far. In the mid-twelfth century King Aparaditya sent the scholar Tejakantha “from Shurparaka” to a literary congress in Kashmir. [n03; TG-II p. 426 n.; TG-XIV p. 321] A stone of King Haripaladeva dated 1149 was found built into a house plinth at Sopara, just as the ancient blocks were built into Gass plinths. [n03; TG-II p. 426 n. 3] The Gazetteer says the Shilahara capital “seems to have been between Bassein and Agashi”, and suggests that their capital Puri “may therefore have been Sopara”, though another volume of the same Gazetteer prefers Elephanta and the modern epigraphist Mirashi leaves the question open. [n03; TG-XIV p. 355 n. 5; TG-II p. 423 n.; CII-VI p. 2]

Arab writers knew the port. Al-Idrisi in 1153 described “Soubara” as a mile and a half from the sea, well peopled, with a great trade, “one of the emporiums of India”, with pearls fished there and a nearby island called Bara where coconut palms grew. [n03; TG-XIV pp. 321, 355–356] A mile and a half from the sea is a description of a town on a creek, not on the shore: the island-and-creek world in which Gass sat. The Arab sources, as the Gazetteer synthesised them, mention rice, salt, coconuts and betel, laced shoes made at Sopara and Sanjan, and pearls from the creeks near Sopara. [n03; TG-II pp. 429–430] Between the eleventh and the thirteenth century the main trade moved from Sopara to Thana, “but Sopara was still a place of consequence”. [n03; TG-XIV p. 321]

What was found in Gass

Against that background, read what the Gazetteer found in Gass itself in 1882. [n42; Gazetteer XIV p. 341] [n11; Gaz. 1882 p. 341]

In a field of Gass called Sonarbhat, which might be translated “gold field”, was a mound with a broken cross-legged female figure, perhaps the Jain goddess Ambika, and the foundations of a temple “said to have been dedicated to Parasnath”, the twenty-third Jain tirthankara. From a well nearby had been dug the bull, the Nandi, that in 1882 stood at the Chakreshvar temple in Sopara. [n42; Gazetteer XIV p. 341] Elsewhere the Gazetteer describes that headless Nandi as “probably of about the same age (AD 1060) as the Ambarnath temple”. [n03; TG-XIV pp. 336–337] A Shiva bull of the eleventh century, then, came out of a well in a Gass field.

There was a damaged sculpted stone, perhaps the Sun god holding a lotus in each hand, “probably of the eleventh or twelfth century”. On the east shore of the Gass lake was a richly carved stone lotus pendant, the kind that hangs from the centre of a temple ceiling. At a spot called Mala, on the lake shore, were the foundations of “probably a Brahmanic temple of the eleventh or twelfth century”. At the south end of the lake were dressed stone steps. [n42; Gazetteer XIV p. 341]

And about fifty yards south of the Khare pond, inside Gass limits, had been found the statue of Brahma that by 1882 stood near the Chakreshvar temple. [n42; Gazetteer XIV near p. 340] A modern survey lists, among the sculptures now kept at a Shri Ram temple by the Chakreshwar lake, “a bearded three-headed Brahma”. [n02; Shah 2020] Whether that is the Gass Brahma is not stated anywhere; it is a possibility worth checking.

Put together, these are the remains of at least two medieval temples in Gass, one Jain by tradition and one probably Brahmanical, with sculpture of the eleventh and twelfth centuries, a lake with stone steps and a carved ceiling-piece on its shore. That is the Shilahara age in Gass. It is not a village record, but it is a strong sign that the ground of Gass, in the time when Sopara was a district headquarters and Tejakantha went to Kashmir, carried shrines worth carving.

The Jains of Gass

The Jain thread in Gass runs from the Shilahara age right up to the eve of the Portuguese.

Jain tradition gave Sopara a high place. In the fourteenth century the Jain author Jinaprabhasuri listed Sopara among the eighty-four great Jain tirthas, with an image of the first tirthankara, Adinath, believed to be a “living” image. A Sopara gachha, a Jain monastic lineage, was still remembered in the twentieth century. Jain tradition also says Pethad Shah (1223–1283) built a Parshvanath temple there. [n02; Shah 2020] Research in 2019 by Siddharth Kale, Monish Shah and two Jain monks found inscriptions of the thirteenth to sixteenth centuries showing a Jain Modha-nyati clan at Sopara. [n03; Dalal, LiveHistoryIndia 2019]

The Gazetteer’s “temple said to have been dedicated to Parasnath” in the Sonarbhat field of Gass fits that picture. Shah’s paper adds that Indraji, in 1882, already described “the area near Ram Kund (Gaas tank) as a place with remains of old Jain temple”. [n42; Shah 2020 §3, citing Indraji 1882:4]

Then there are dated objects. Shah and his co-authors report a Surya image from Gass inscribed with a date equal to 1412 CE, now held by the Sopara Acchalgachha Jain Sangh, and seven inscribed Jain bronzes dated between 1458 and 1555 CE, held by the Chalpeth (Agashi) Jain Sangh. [n42; Shah 2020 §§3–4] [n02 §2] The research did not see the objects. [n42 §2] If the dates are as reported, a Jain community was making and dedicating images in the Sopara–Gass neighbourhood for a century and a half before the Portuguese arrived, and the last of these bronzes is dated about two decades after the Portuguese took Bassein in 1534. That last date matters, and the chapter will come back to it.

Muslims at Gass

The Muslim layer of Gass is older than many people think.

The Gazetteer recorded, in a Gass garden, the tomb of a Musalman saint, Shaikh Akbar-moi-ud-din, bordered with old bricks sixteen by ten by three inches, the same large bricks that were found at a road corner and in a new well. [n42; Gazetteer XIV p. 341] At the Bhatela pond, at the Gass–Sopara boundary, were Musalman graves on the north and west banks. [n31; Gazetteer pp. 313–340] Near Taki Pada stands the “Bandar Masjid”, the port mosque. [n42; Shah 2020] None of these is dated in the sources.

The wider history explains them. Delhi forces reached this coast in the early fourteenth century; Thana and Salsette were occupied in 1318. [n03 §5] The Franciscan friars Jordanus and Odoric, here in the 1320s, found that “Saracens” had lately seized the country and “destroyed an infinite number of idol temples and likewise many churches”. [n03; TG-II pp. 439–440] Jordanus went from Thana to Broach “via Supera”, buried at Sopara the bodies of four Franciscan friars executed at Thana, found Nestorian Christians and a church of St Thomas there, and baptised and gave communion to about ninety Christians in a stay of fifteen days. [n03; TG-XIV pp. 321–322] That is the first time Christians are recorded on this island, two centuries before the Portuguese, and it happened a mile from Gass.

Real Delhi control lapsed after about 1350, and Hindu chiefs kept granting land. The Gazetteer found a land-grant stone of 1351 near Pada, a hamlet of Sondavli, and wrote that “after their first conquest in 1290, the Muhammadans left to the local chief the right of making land grants”. [n42; Gazetteer XIV, Sopara pages; the stone is not at Gass] From about 1390 the sultans of Gujarat held the coast through garrisons and tributary rais. [n03 §5] A stone at Koprad, a hamlet of Bolinj, dated 1464 and written in Devanagari, mentions Muslim names alongside Mahim, Thana, Koprad and “Bimbstan”; in 1882 it lay near the house of the headman Bhiku Patil, a Samvedi Brahman. [n03; TG-XIV Koprad entry] [n39 §1]

By 1882 the Muslims of Sopara included many Naitas, whom the Gazetteer called “descendants of Arab and Persian refugees and traders”. [n11; Gaz. 1882 p. 315] Whether the saint of the Gass garden and the port mosque belong to those traders is not stated by any source. It is a fair reading that a landing and a port mosque on the Gass side of the creek mean Muslim sailors and traders used it, but that is inference.

Where were the Samvedis?

The Kuparis of Gass descend, in part at least, from the Samvedi Brahmans, a farming and gardening community found, as the Gazetteer said in 1882, “only in Bassein and Mahim”. [n34; Gazetteer XIII pp. 81–83] When did Samvedis first live at Gass? No source says. The Samvedis’ arrival on this coast is tied by community tradition to the legendary king Bimba of the Mahikavatichi Bakhar, a Marathi chronicle whose oldest layer dates from about 1448, but the research found no scholarly source for the Samvedis in the Bimba story. [n03; §3 gaps] The earliest document that places a Samvedi Brahman at a named spot on the island is the 1464 Koprad stone lying by a Samvedi headman’s house in 1882, and the stone’s date tells us about the stone, not about the house. [n03; TG-XIV Koprad entry]

So the honest summary of medieval Gass is this. The village is never named. Its ground carried a Jain temple and a Brahmanical temple with eleventh- and twelfth-century sculpture, a lake with stone steps, a Muslim saint’s tomb with old bricks, a port mosque and, if Shah’s reading is right, a silted landing where a stone anchor lay. A Jain community dedicated images nearby from 1412 to 1555. Christians were on the island in the 1320s. Who lived in the houses of Gass, and what they called it, is not recorded.

The Portuguese: conversion, and a missing entry

1534 and after

On 23 December 1534, by the Treaty of Bassein, Sultan Bahadur Shah of Gujarat, under pressure from the Mughal Humayun, ceded Bassein and its dependencies to Portugal. [n03 §5] [n02 §5] Within a few years Baçaim, as the Portuguese called it, was the seat of the Província do Norte, the Province of the North, and Sopara’s long role as a regional centre was over. [n02; LiveHistoryIndia] The whole garden strip from the Bassein creek to the Vaitarna became, in Portuguese usage, “the island of Bassein”. [n11; Gaz. 1882 pp. 314–315]

Archaeological exploration has found a Portuguese landing place to the east of Gass, credited by Shah’s paper to the survey of Tripati and Gaur (1997). [n42; Shah 2020] [n02 §6] Near the Bhatela pond, at the Gass–Sopara edge, the Gazetteer recorded “the site of a Portuguese tower, an old landing-place”, and Wikipedia reports remains of a Portuguese jetty and customs house there. [n31; Gazetteer; Wikipedia: Nallasopara] [n02 §6] At Achola, the village south of the Gass “stone dam”, the Portuguese had a fort, a mile and a half north of Manikpur. [n31; Gazetteer, Achola/Tulinj passage] In 1728 a Portuguese report complained that the Nil hill near Sopara had been fortified without an engineer, with bastions too small for a four-pounder gun. [n31; Gazetteer footnote citing O Chronista de Tissuary] So the Portuguese used the same creek that the people of Gass used, and fortified and taxed it. The landing east of Gass and the tower at the Bhatela pond put Portuguese officers, soldiers and customs men within sight of Gass’s gardens for two centuries.

How the people of the island became Christian

The conversion of this coast is told in full in Chapter 34. Here it is enough to recall what the evidence says about the villages around Gass, and why.

The first Franciscan, Fr Antonio do Porto, built an orphanage for forty boys at Agashi “as early as 1535”; it was destroyed in April 1540 when a Gujarat army invaded. [n34; Da Cunha 1876 pp. 159–160] The Gazetteer credits do Porto with the bulk of the early conversions in Salsette and Bassein: “He threw down Hindu temples, rebuilt them as churches … by providing orphanages and in times of war and famine filling them with deserted children, prepared a class of native priests.” [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 201] The Jesuits followed; Francis Xavier founded their college at Bassein in 1548. [n34; New Advent]

The Jesuit letters are frank about method. Fr Gonçalo Rodrigues, writing from Bassein on 5 September 1558, said that “those who come to conversion are commonly the poorest and most needy, and are converted more drawn and moved by hope of some interest and remedy of life than by preaching, which they do not understand”, and that “we set them the snares of human things and, once caught in them, we teach them the divine”. [n34; Documenta Indica IV, doc. 16, pp. 98–102] In 1562 Fr Francisco Cabral wrote that the Portuguese landholders of Bassein, who “all have villages”, had at first resisted conversion because they feared it would empty their villages, but now came “to beg us to go and convert their villages”. [n34; Documenta Indica V, doc. 90, pp. 628–631] Whole villages followed their headman, the matará. [n34; Mendiratta p. 211] The Census of 1931 explained the Portuguese surnames of the Bassein coast this way: “in many villages every tenant in the village was baptized and given the surname of the owner of the village”. [n34; Census 1931 Vol VIII Part I pp. 363–364]

Was there force? The Gazetteer recorded a tradition among the Uraps that their forefathers were “forced to become Christians by having pieces of flesh thrown into their wells”, but added: “Force may have been used in some cases, but the Portuguese accounts seem to show that persuasion and interest were the chief means of conversion.” [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 201 n. 2] Laws from Goa between 1549 and 1557 barred the rebuilding of temples and mosques and pressed hard on Hindus and Muslims, but the historian Mendiratta notes that, as far as is known, these “Provisions in favour of Christianity” were never enforced in the subdivisions closest to the mainland. [n34; Mendiratta pp. 210–211] At Nirmal, the village beside Gass, the Gazetteer records that “the Portuguese had pulled down the temples and destroyed the ling”. [n11; Gaz. 1882 p. 293] Between about 1570 and 1620 a majority of the people of Salsette and of the Bassein and Agashi subdivisions became Christian. [n34; Mendiratta p. 211]

The parish that matters most for Gass is Nirmal’s. Holy Cross, Nirmal, was founded by Portuguese missionaries in the 1550s; sources give 1556, 1557 or 1558. [n42 §3] Hull’s mission history says the present church of 1856 stands on the ruins of a church of 1580 “destroyed by the Mahrattas in 1737”. [n38; Hull I p. 5] Its priest served Nandakhal too until Nandakhal got its own church in 1573. [n42 §3; Wikipedia: Holy Spirit Church Nandakhal] Agashi’s church dates by community tradition from 1568. [n11; D’Mello blog]

Return for a moment to the Jain bronzes, the last of them dated 1555. [n42; Shah 2020] If that date is right, a Jain image was dedicated in this neighbourhood two decades after the Portuguese took Bassein and in the very years when Holy Cross was being founded at Nirmal next door. The research found nothing more about it. But it is a reminder that conversion here was not a single day. For some decades Jains, Hindus, Muslims and new Christians lived on the same island while the temples came down and the churches went up.

Gass and the Tombo de Baçaim

The best document for who held which village in the last years of Portuguese rule is the Tombo de Baçaim of 1727–1730, a register of the foreiros, the holders of Crown villages and gardens on lease or hereditary tenure, with the folio number of each entry. It was published in 2007 by André Teixeira and Silvana Pires in the Anais de História de Além-Mar, and its table was read for this book by text extraction. [n39 §1.B]

No entry for Gass was found in it. [n42 §3] The research searched the register and the other sources for Gass under any Portuguese spelling, “Gaz”, “Gás”, “Guas”, and found nothing; the Gazetteer and the other sources searched do not give a Portuguese name for Gass either. [n42 §3] That is a negative result from one published table and some web searches, not proof that Gass was absent. Gass may be hidden inside a compound entry under another village’s name, as many entries combine two or three places; it may appear under a Portuguese form nobody has thought to search; or its lands may have been held as gardens, hortas, recorded under the holder’s name rather than the village’s. The Portuguese name of Gass is, for now, unknown. [n42 §3 gaps]

What the register does show is the neighbourhood. [n39 §1.B] In the Caçabé (the kasba, the home district) of Baçaim, the pacaria “Bolinja” (Bolinj), with the land called Belvar, was held by D. Maria Henriques Coelho da Silva (fl. 54v), and “Gris”, which may be Giriz, by D. Margarida Teles de Meneses (fl. 23). The Jesuits held “Maroy” and other pacarias. In the Caçabé of Agaçaim (Agashi), “Mardes Sirgoly” was held by Lucas de Almeida de Andrade (fl. 47v); “Vagoly” (Vagholi) by Manuel de Abreu de Lima (fl. 41v); “Vastal Vagoly” by Fernão da Silveira e Meneses (fl. 45); “Vatar Rajoly” by Manuel Varjão da Fonseca, at a rent of 700 pardaus, the highest in that list (fl. 45v); “Cholna Roba” by the Jesuit Procurador Geral (fl. 49); Agashi and Arnala island, the Portuguese “Ilha das Vacas”, by D. Domingas Pereira de Azevedo (fl. 41, 42); and “Nala Magir” by Miguel Francisco Mira de Vasconcelos (fl. 43). [n39 §1.B]

So in the 1720s the Gass neighbourhood, Bolinj, Mardes, Vagholi, Vatar, Chulne and Agashi, was held by Portuguese fidalgos, by widows of rank and by the Jesuits, most of them probably absentee landlords drawing rent. The holders are Portuguese, not converts. [n39 §1.B and inferences]

Two names in that list catch the eye of anyone from Gass, and both must be handled with care. “Mardes Sirgoly” joins Mardes, a Kupari village at the centre of the Sopara agar, to a second name, “Sirgoly”. A locality in Nallasopara West today is called Mothi Sargodi Road. [n42; onefivenine.com] Whether “Sirgoly” and “Sargodi” are the same place-name, and whether any part of that holding lay in what is now Gass, is this chapter’s own question, prompted by the resemblance of the words. No source makes the link. And “Nala Magir” contains “Nala”, the word that later joined “Sopara” to make “Nallasopara”, whose origin is otherwise undocumented. [n31 §1] Again, no source connects them. Both are leads for a researcher with the Tombo itself, not findings.

The surname of the holder of Mardes Sirgoly, Almeida, is also the surname of the most famous modern family of Gass. Almeida was a common name among the Portuguese of Baçaim; it appears on twenty lines of the extracted register. [n39 §1.B] Nothing documents any link between Lucas de Almeida de Andrade and any family of Gass.

A note on one surname: Tuscano

Since the author of this book is a Tuscano, born in Gass, the record of that surname belongs here, told as honestly as the record allows.

Toscano is a Portuguese and Italian word meaning “Tuscan”, someone from Tuscany. In Portuguese it is an ordinary adjective. Italian surname dictionaries record Toscano and the variant “Tuscano” in southern Italy. A commercial heraldry site claims an Italian origin for a Portuguese Toscano family, but no independent confirmation was found. [n44 §1] “Tuscano” with a u is the same name. In Vasai it is more likely a later respelling, in English-era or Marathi-shaped records, than a separate Italian form, since o and u sit close together in Marathi-influenced speech; but no document shows when or why the spelling changed. [n44 §§1, 4 inferences]

In the whole Tombo de Baçaim of 1727–1730, the surname Toscano appears three times, all for one man. [n44 §2] He is the licenciado André Toscano. A licenciado was a university-trained man, in law or in theology or arts; the register does not say which, nor whether he was a layman or a cleric. [n44 §2] On 7 May 1727 the clerk João Pinheiro entered marginal notes recording that “the licenciado André Toscano became the holder of these hortas in enfiteuse, by purchase” (fl. 136), with the condition that he have the rent newly valued within a year on pain of losing the holding to the Royal Treasury. The register’s table lists him also, on folio 136v, in the section for the pragana of Herá, as holder of the “aldeia Sircar” in enfiteuse, a hereditary lease, at a rent that reads as 100 pardaus a year. [n44 §2] [n39 §1.B] The neighbouring entry on folio 136 is D. Maria Coutinho’s “aldeia Sircanacer”, held “in lives”. [n44 §2]

Where “Sircar” lay is unknown. It sits in the Herá block of the register, a pragana of the Bassein province, and the research could not locate it on any map. It should not be assumed to be a present-day Vasai village, still less Gass. [n44 §2 inferences and gaps] Nothing else is known about André Toscano: not his birthplace, his family, or whether he was Goa-born or connected with the Jesuits. [n44 §2 gaps] Because he bought his holdings only in May 1727, he was a newcomer as a landholder, twelve years before Bassein fell to the Marathas. [n44 §2 inferences]

How did convert families come to carry Portuguese surnames at all? The documented mechanism, for Goa and for Bassein, is baptism. Converts took the surname of the priest, governor, soldier or layman who stood as godfather; a council at Goa in 1567 barred the use of former Hindu names; and brothers baptised at different times could end up with different surnames. [n44 §3; n39 §1.A, quoting Prabhu, de Sousa and do Carmo Costa via Wikipedia] For Bombay, Salsette and Bassein, converts “were encouraged, or in some cases compelled, to take Portuguese names”, and Marathi speakers then “modified the Portuguese names to suit their language”. [n39 §1.A; Sahapedia] And the Census of 1931 gives the landlord’s version: tenants baptised and “given the surname of the owner of the village”. [n34; Census 1931 pp. 363–364]

So a Vasai Tuscano family could have taken the name from a godparent or priest called Toscano, from a landholder or employer of that name, or by some later adoption. The record does not say which, for any family. [n44 §3 inferences] André Toscano bought land in 1727, late in the story; the main waves of conversion on this coast were between about 1535 and the early 1600s. If he had any part in giving his name, it would more plausibly be as godfather at later baptisms than as the source of an original convert surname, and there is no evidence that he did either. [n44 §3 inferences] No baptismal register, Jesuit letter or Tombo passage links any Toscano to any convert. No public directory, parish history or community source found locates Tuscano families by village or names them as Kupari, Vadval, Koli or Salsette Christian. Tuscano does not appear on the Goan or Mangalorean Catholic surname lists that were checked. [n44 §§3–4, 6]

The honest sentence is the one the research itself proposed: the surname Toscano means “Tuscan”; a licenciado André Toscano appears in the Tombo de Baçaim in 1727 as the new holder, by purchase, of the aldeia Sircar and some gardens in the Bassein province; that shows the name was present among Baçaim’s Portuguese landholders twelve years before the fall; but no document links him to any convert family, and how Vasai’s Tuscano families acquired the name remains unknown. [n44, suggested writing line] The real evidence, if it survives, is in parish baptismal books, where a godfather’s name stands beside a convert’s. Those books are not online. [n39 §1 gaps]

The name in sixteenth-century Goa: the Chancellor, the Dean and the Augustinian

Two chroniclers add Toscanos in the sixteenth century: Garcia de Orta’s Colóquios (1563) names Simão Toscano as his tenant (rendeiro) of the island of Bombay, the first Toscano found near the Salsette coast, and Diogo do Couto lists Jorge Toscano as Captain of Cananor in 1581. [n80 §6; n86 §4] Both are Portuguese officers or lessees, not converts, and neither is linked to any Vasai family. A later search of the Portuguese source collections found the surname Toscano in more places than the Tombo of 1727, all in Goa and Portugal and none in the lands of Baçaim. [n70 §1] The earliest is Dr Francisco Toscano, Chancellor of India and chief officer for the estates of the dead (provedor-mor dos defuntos), who came to Goa in the 1540s with the first Regimento of the law court, which was ordered, the document says, because “the number of Christians grows and more officers are needed for justice”. The editor of the Documentação notes that he “took an active part in the foundation of the Confraria da Santa Fé and of the seminary of the same name”, the Goa bodies through which converts were baptised and taught. A letter of his to the Viceroy Dom João de Castro, written at Goa on 8 January 1547, is in Lisbon. [n70 §1] An António Toscano is named on the register copy of the royal alvará of 16 March 1583 that gave the property of dead gentiles to their wives and daughters, or to their nearest kin, who became Christians within six months. [n70 §1] Fr Sebastião Toscano, an Augustinian preacher to the King, was provincial of the order in Portugal in 1572 and again in 1578, and in March 1579 sent the second mission of four friars to India; his nephew Fr Simão Toscano died at sea on the way. [n70 §1] The Augustinians founded their house at Baçaim about 1595 and held the parish of Mercês from 1606. [n70 §2] The noble Abreu Toscano families also sent sons east. [n70 §1]

What this does and does not show is the same as before. The name was one of rank in the machinery of Goa’s conversions from the 1540s, not a name first seen with a landholder in 1727. That adds weight to the idea that a godfather, priest or official named Toscano may have given a convert family the name. But no document in these sources links any Toscano to a convert in the lands of Baçaim, and the Vasai spelling with a u, Tuscano, occurs in none of them. [n70, Inferences]

The Kuparis’ ancestors at Gass

When did the families of Gass become Christian? No source says. No mission record mentions Gass, no conversion date is known for it, and no narrative survives. [n42 §3 gaps] A community blog says the Kupari community took shape under Portuguese rule “in the 15th century”, but the Portuguese did not take Bassein until 1534–35, so the date is almost certainly an error for the sixteenth century, and in any case the blog gives no evidence. [n42 §3]

What can be said is this. Gass lay inside the Portuguese “island of Bassein” garden belt, between Nirmal, where a parish existed from the 1550s, and Sopara, where in 1882 “the husbandmen … are Christians, many of them converted Samvedi Brahmans”. [n42 §3 inferences] [n34; Gazetteer XIV p. 315] The Christians of Gass were in all likelihood converted in the same decades and in the same ways as their neighbours, through the parish at Nirmal and the landholders of the island, and with the mixture of livelihood, landlord pressure, pageantry, belief and episodic force that the sources describe. But that is an inference from the neighbourhood, and should be read as one.

Under the Marathas, 1739–1818

The fall and the five churches

The Marathas laid siege to Baçaim on 17 February 1739, and the garrison surrendered on 16 May. [n04; n32; as in Chapter 35] The terms let the Portuguese and the religious orders leave and promised the Christians who stayed liberty of worship, with five churches protected: three in or near Bassein city, one in the “Cassabé” subdivision and one in Salsette. [n34; Mendiratta pp. 216–218] All the Portuguese, the reinóis and descendentes, “fled or were expelled … leaving only the Indian converted population behind”. The Marathas would accept only non-white priests, and Goan clergy rebuilt parish life from Kurla from 1740. [n34; Mendiratta pp. 216–218] Nirmal’s church of 1580 had been, Hull says, “destroyed by the Mahrattas in 1737”, in the campaign before the siege. [n38; Hull I p. 5]

At Nirmal, a few hundred yards from Gass, the conquest was marked in stone. “On the fall of Bassein (1739) Nirmal was purified, prints of the feet of Shri Dattatraya took the place of the ling, and a reservoir was built.” About 1750 the Maratha governor of Bassein, the Sarsubhedar Shankarji Keshav, built eight temples at Nirmal: the Shankaracharya Svami temple, Mahavishnu, Vimaleshvara Mahadev and Bhuteshvar west of the lake, Ganpati, Durga and Hanuman east of it, and Siddheshvar to the north, with grants for a Brahman feeding-house and a Bairagi alms-house. Stones from Portuguese churches were probably reused in them. [n11; Gaz. 1882 pp. 292–293] The stones of this coast changed hands again.

Some Christians returned to Hinduism. Da Cunha says the Peshwa Madhavrao, who ruled from 1761 to 1772, set up a tax to pay Brahmans to purify native Christians and receive them back into their castes; the Census of 1911 says the Kirpal Bhandaris “were given the choice of reverting to Hinduism, which they accepted” soon after 1739. [n34; Da Cunha 1876 p. 149; Census 1911 para 136] Most did not. The Gazetteer says the Marathas “allowed them the freest exercise of their religion, their processions and festivals were respected”. [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 203] In 1760 Anquetil du Perron found the lanes of Agashi “full of people going to church as freely as in a Christian country”. [n34; Gazetteer XIV pp. 1–2] The same traveller mentions “Vagholi with a creek of its own”. [n11; Gaz. 1882 p. 322]

There is one more Maratha-era arrival in the Sopara country. About 1760, Lad Vani traders from Gujarat came to Sopara from Cambay, fleeing the exactions of the Nawab Momin Khan II; with the Naita Muslims they were Sopara’s traders in 1882. [n11; Gaz. 1882 p. 315] The molasses, rice and betel that left the Gass landing for Surat and Bhavnagar went through the hands of such merchants, though no source names any trader at the Gass landing itself.

Gass under the Marathas: no record

For Gass itself, no Maratha-period record has been found: no land document, no temple grant, no tax roll, no mention. [n42 §4 gaps] Its people were presumably among the Christians whom the Goan priests kept in the faith, walking to Nirmal, where the church had to be rebuilt, past Nirmal hill where the Peshwas’ governor was raising eight temples, and selling their garden produce in Sopara’s market. That is a picture drawn from the neighbours, and it should be read that way.

Under the British, 1818–1942

Company rule, cholera and the census

The British took the Bassein subdivision early in the nineteenth century, finding, by one count, eight working churches and about 5,000 Catholics. [n34; Mendiratta p. 219] They were wary of the Goa-linked clergy, and in the cholera of 1820–21 about 3,000 Christians rejoined the Hindu fold, which a British report blamed on the lack of “respectable ministers”. [n34; Mendiratta p. 220] In 1826 Clune’s gazetteer called Sopara a kasba and a post-runner’s station. [n31; Gazetteer, citing Clune 1826] By 1872 the Bassein subdivision had 13,126 Christians. [n34; Gazetteer XIII pp. 71–72, 199] In 1881 the Gazetteer counted nine churches for Bassein, under nine priests, including Holy Cross at Nirmal (“St. Cruz”), the Holy Spirit church at Koprad and St James at Agashi. [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 206 n. 3] There was no church at Gass.

The railway that dried the creek

The event that most changed Gass in the nineteenth century was not a war but an embankment. The Bombay, Baroda and Central India Railway opened its line north from Bombay in the 1860s, with Bassein Road station at Manikpur; local service from Grant Road to Bassein Road began on 1 November 1865, and Virar’s first service ran on 12 April 1867. [n31; Wikipedia: Vasai Road; n42 §4] To carry the line across the low ground the company built a long, high embankment, and quarried Tulinj hill’s basalt pillars for it. [n31; Gazetteer, Tulinj passage]

The Gazetteer of 1882 says plainly what the embankment did to the creek: silting had been rapid, “especially since the long railway embankment has dammed the flow of water from the south.” [n42; Gazetteer XIV p. 315] A later geographer, Arunachalam, wrote that the rail line on its high embankment “dismembered the channel”. [n02; Shah 2020, citing Arunachalam 2002] The railway also took the trade. Large quantities of plantains and betel leaf went south to Bombay by rail by 1882, and oilcake came back as manure for the cane. [n42; Gazetteer XIV pp. 315–316]

There was, though, no station for Sopara. The Gazetteer said Sopara was “not more than three-quarters of a mile from the line of railway” yet “badly off for communications”, and that “a station at Sopara or a made road to the town from the Agashi high road would be a boon to its people”. [n31; Gazetteer p. 316] Wikipedia gives the Nallasopara station an opening year of 1865, from a Western Railway heritage book, but that does not fit the Gazetteer’s complaint, and the date is best left unresolved. [n31 §1] An early Back Bay–Virar service is said to have halted at “Neela or (Nalasopara)”. [n31; Free Press Journal 2022] The 1882 Gazetteer knows no village called Nala at all. [n31 §1] How “Sopara” became “Nallasopara” is told in Chapter 12; for Gass the important point is that by the late nineteenth century its people lived beside a railway that had made their landing obsolete and their creek a ditch, and that the town whose name would swallow theirs was taking shape around a halt to the east.

Gass in 1882

The 1882 Gazetteer is the one primary description of Gass, and it is worth gathering all it says in one place. [n42; Gazetteer XIV pp. 314–316, 340–342]

Gass was the southern village of the garden or agar of Sopara, raised land “full of trees and sugarcane gardens”. The crops of the agar were sugarcane, plantains, betel leaf and rice, with coconut and brab palms, mango, tamarind, banyan, pipal and karanj trees. Molasses, rice and betel leaf were shipped by boat from the landings, and plantains and betel went to Bombay by rail. [n42; Gazetteer XIV pp. 315–316] Small boats of eight to twelve tons still came at high tide to the “[Gass] landing-place”. [n42; Gazetteer XIV pp. 315–316] The village had its lake, the winding hollow 800 yards long with two cross dams, gardens around it and Persian wheels turning. It had a field called Sonarbhat with a mound and temple foundations, a Musalman saint’s tomb in a garden, carved stones by the lake and a spot called Mala with the foundations of an old temple. Its houses stood on basalt plinths and had basalt blocks before their verandas, many of them carried from Brahma hill. A causeway of basalt ran south across the mud towards Achola, and another stretched west towards Nirmal. A cobra stone stood under a banyan on the way to Brahma hill. [n42; Gazetteer XIV pp. 341–342]

Two people of Gass are named: Degu Pobria Mhatara, at whose house lay the “Badhuya” stone, and Bab Naik, “a Samvedi Brahman”, at whose house lay the other. [n42; Gazetteer XIV p. 341] They are the earliest people of Gass whose names survive.

What can be read from those two names? Bab Naik was a Hindu Samvedi Brahman, so in 1882 Hindu Samvedis lived in Gass beside Christians, just as they did in Sopara, Agashi and Koprad. [n42 §3 inferences] “Naik” is also a Kupari surname. [n42 §3 inferences] Degu Pobria Mhatara’s religion is not stated. “Mhatara” is close to Mhatre, a surname of the Samvedis and Vadvals of this coast, but the Gazetteer gives no caste for him, and the resemblance is only a resemblance. [n39 §1.A] The Gazetteer also names a Christian neighbour two miles north, at Bolinj, whose doorstep was an inscribed stone: “Juju Manu Lop, that is Joseph Manuel Lopez”. [n39 §1.A; Gazetteer p. 342] That is how a Portuguese Christian name sounded in the mouths of the island in 1882, and it is the closest thing in the record to the voice of a Kupari of that generation.

The Gazetteer gives no separate population for Gass. Sopara, the market town a mile north, had “a population of about 1700”, and its husbandmen were “Christians, many of them converted Samvedi Brahmans; Musalmans, many of them Naitas; and Hindus, chiefly Samvedi Brahmans and Bhandaris”. [n42; Gazetteer XIV pp. 314–315] No caste table for Gass exists. [n42 §4 gaps] The census village tables for 1872, 1881 and 1901, which might have counted Gass, were not found. [n42 §4 gaps]

How a Christian family of the island lived

The Gazetteer has no chapter on the Kuparis by that name. The word “Kupari” does not appear in the Gazetteer, in Enthoven’s Tribes and Castes, in Grierson’s Linguistic Survey, or in the censuses of 1901, 1911 and 1931; the Christians of Bassein appear there as “Native Christians”, “Portuguese Christians”, “Firgi” and “Kiristav”. [n34; headline finding] But the Gazetteer’s chapter on the Christians of Thana, with its Bassein details, gives a picture of the life a Gass Christian family would have shared with its neighbours. [n34 §5]

They lived in “substantial tile-roofed houses with walls of wooden planks, mud, or brick and stone”; the Hindu Samvedis lived in one-storied houses of wood and tile. [n34; Gazetteer XIII pp. 81–83, 204] They ate two meals a day of rice, nagli, vari, pulses, vegetables, meat and fish, the whole family eating together. [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 204] In Bassein, unlike Salsette, the proposal of marriage came from the boy’s side. [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 211 n. 1] A child was baptised between the eighth and fifteenth day, carried by its godfather, padrinho, and godmother, madrinha; the mother did not go to the christening. On the third or sixth night after a birth the lower orders strewed gram at the door against the spirit Sathi, and the Hindu Samvedis worshipped Sati on the fifth day: the same spirit, kept by both communities. [n34; Gazetteer XIII pp. 82, 210–211] Hindu Samvedi men wore “red broadcloth caps”; Christian husbandmen wore a long white cotton hat or a woollen cap. [n34; Gazetteer XIII pp. 82, 204–205] Women of almost every class dressed “in local Hindu fashion”, in dark purple, green or black, and covered themselves for church with a white sheet-like robe. [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 205] The first fruits of the rice harvest were blessed at church. [n34; Gazetteer XIII pp. 207–210] Wealthy families who were headmen and owners of rich garden land “often bring up one or more of their sons for the Church”. [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 205] And sugar was made in Bassein by “Pachkalshis, Malis, Native Christians and Samvedi Brahmans”, which fits the sugarcane gardens and the molasses shipped from the Gass landing. [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 395]

Every one of these details is from the Gazetteer’s general account, not from Gass. They describe the world into which Gass’s Christian children were born in the 1880s, and the closeness, in house, food, spirits and dress, between those Christian families and their Hindu Samvedi neighbours like Bab Naik.

Parish life before 1942: walking to Nirmal

Which church did the Christians of Gass go to before they had their own? No document says. [n42 §3 gaps] A community list of Kupari parishes gives Gass as “St. Gonsalo Garcia Church (bifurcated from Nirmal)”, meaning split off from Nirmal’s parish. [n42; Rajan D’Mello blog 2009] That is a community source, not a parish record, but it agrees with geography: Holy Cross at Nirmal was the nearest of the 1881 churches, and it is where Gass children still went to high school in the 1970s. [n42 §6; Wikipedia: Simon Almeida] So this book follows the tradition: before 1942, Gass was almost certainly part of Nirmal parish, and its families walked west to Holy Cross for Mass, baptisms, weddings and funerals. It is tradition supported by circumstance, not a documented fact.

Nirmal’s church in those years was a big one. In 1882 it had “a congregation of 2184 Christians”. It had been rebuilt by its parishioners in 1856 at a cost of about Rs 24,000, ninety-six feet long by twenty-eight broad and twenty-six high. It had a vicar with a house and a monthly British grant, and a private school whose teacher played the violin in church. [n34; Gazetteer XIV p. 293] Each church paid one or two music masters to play the violin. [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 207] Somewhere in that congregation of 2,184, in all likelihood, were the grandparents and great-grandparents of today’s Gass families.

The twentieth century broke Nirmal’s big parish into pieces. St Francis Xavier’s at Giriz had its foundation stone laid on 14 February 1917; Holy Family at Bhuigaon was founded in 1946 because Bhuigaon families had to travel to Nirmal; and Umrale got its own parish in 1970. [n42 §5; n39 §2; n11] Gass’s turn came in 1942.

The church of St Gonsalo Garcia, 1942

What is known

“St. Gonsalo Garcia Church in Gass village of the Vasai Tehsil was built in 1942 and later expanded in 1957.” That sentence, from a Catholic directory of pilgrim centres, is the core of the documented history of the Gass church, and it rests on a single directory source repeated by others. [n42; CatholicsIndia pilgrim centre] Another listing describes the 1957 work as a renovation “to maintain its stability and serve the growing parish better”, and says the parish keeps a week-long feast in February. [n42; Prayers & Petitions] One source, an article by Leon Bent in Indian Catholic Matters as reported in this book’s research, says the church was built in 1942 by Msgr Louis Caetan D’Souza. [n38 §4; Indian Catholic Matters] Another research note found no founder named at all, no first parish priest, no architect, no account of the money, and no priest for 1957. [n42 §5 gaps] So Msgr D’Souza’s name should be read as one source’s statement, waiting for the parish’s own records to confirm it.

Why 1942? No source says. [n42 §5] It was the middle of the Second World War, the year of the Quit India movement. The pattern of Giriz (1917), Bhuigaon (1946) and Umrale (1970) suggests the likely reasons: a congregation large enough and far enough from Nirmal to need its own church. But the reason for Gass’s foundation is not documented. [n42 §5 inferences] Nor is it known whether the 1942 church replaced an earlier chapel or cross in Gass. [n42 §5 gaps]

Why Gonsalo Garcia

The patron chosen for Gass was the saint of Bassein. Gonsalo Garcia was born in Bassein about 1556 or 1557, to a Portuguese father, a soldier, and an Indian mother. He studied at the Jesuit college in Bassein fort, went to Japan as a young man, worked there as a catechist for about eight years, became a merchant at Macao and then a Franciscan lay brother at Manila, and returned to Japan in 1592 as interpreter with a Spanish embassy. He was arrested in December 1596, had the tips of his ears cut off on 3 January 1597, and was crucified at Nagasaki on 5 February 1597 with twenty-five companions. Pius IX canonised them on 8 June 1862. [n38 §4; Catholic Encyclopedia; Archdiocese of Bombay; da Cunha pp. 211–212] He is called India’s first saint, is patron of the Diocese of Vasai and second patron of the Archdiocese of Bombay, and his feast was fixed in India on 7 February. [n38 §4] Devotion to him on this coast lapsed “from about 1739” and was revived after his canonisation. [n38; Catholic Encyclopedia]

Naming the new Gass church after him tied the village to Bassein’s own saint, whose cult had revived after 1862. [n42 §5 inferences] Today the Diocese of Vasai and Wikipedia call the Gass church a “major shrine” of the saint. No source was found for the phrase “national shrine”, and it should not be used. [n42 §5; n38 §4] No relics of the saint are recorded at Gass. [n38 §4] His great public feast, the “Killa cha Sann”, is not held at Gass but at the ruined Jesuit church of the Holy Name inside Vasai fort, on a Sunday near the neap tide after Christmas; in 2026 it fell on 11 January. [n38 §4; Free Press Journal 11 Jan 2026] The Gass feast is its own, in February, around the saint’s day; sources say “around 5th–6th February”, but the parish’s own programme could not be read. [n42 §5 gaps]

Was Gonsalo Garcia born in Gass?

Some people in Gass and in the wider East Indian community say he was. In February 2021 a community column by the Mobai Gaothan Panchayat, an East Indian organisation, called Gass “the place St. Gonsalo Garcia was born”. It said a team from Vasai would visit Gass with residents, that senior members would compile research on the devotions and beliefs of Gass villagers, and it asked the Diocese of Vasai to start projects in the saint’s “native village”. [n42; Gaothan Voice, Feb 2021, mobaikar.in]

That claim is a tradition, and it has no evidence behind it in any source found. It conflicts with the standard account. The Catholic Encyclopedia says he was “born in Bassein”, and a Lisbon archive record, as reported by two modern writers, calls him “natural de Agaçaim”, a native of Agashi. [n38 §4; Indian Catholic Matters; DNA India 2014] “Bassein” in sixteenth-century usage could mean the city or the whole district, so “born in Bassein” does not by itself rule out any village of the island, and “natural de Agaçaim” may mean the Agashi subdivision rather than the village. But no document names Gass. The fairest thing to say is that Gass is the saint’s shrine and that a recent community tradition makes it his birthplace; the documents point to Bassein generally and, in one archive record, to Agashi.

Gass after independence, 1947–2009

A 1960s count gives one figure for the village. Elsie Baptista, in The East Indians (1967), counted 1,825 Samvedi Christians in the parish of “Ghas”, Gass, the third largest of the five Samvedi parishes after Nandakhal (6,000) and Nirmal (1,998). Her editor noted that such figures were “in need of revision”. The count is not dated in the pages read. Chapter 37 gives the full table. [n60 §4]

A garden village

The record for Gass between 1947 and the end of the century is very thin. What can be pieced together comes from the life of one Gass family and from the general history of the Vasai garden belt.

The Kupari community’s own writers say that farming dominated their villages until the late 1960s and early 1970s, after which people moved into engineering, medicine, banking and IT, and abroad to the United States, Europe, the Gulf and Australia. [n34 §8; Vasai Kupari blog 2007; Post in Mumbai 2015] Those are community blogs without sources, but they match what is known of the garden belt. About 1988, by a farmer’s account published in 2023, the green belt grew betel leaf, exported to Pakistan until relations soured, and tiny velchi bananas, later hit by blight; sugarcane had come before, and kharif paddy until the land went to housing; flowers, mogra and then sonchafa, replaced them. [n11 Q3; 101Reporters 2023] Wikipedia says Gass is known for salt farming and green-vegetable farming. [n42; Wikipedia: Gass] No survey of Gass’s own crops was found. [n42 §6 gaps] Wikipedia also gives the only population figure for the late twentieth century’s end: about 5,000 Roman Catholics, as of 2009. [n42; Wikipedia: Gass]

A boy from the parish school

The life of Simon Almeida shows the path a Gass child could take in those decades. [n42 §6; Wikipedia: Simon Almeida; Diocese of Poona]

He was born on 4 November 1960 at Gass, which Wikipedia spells “Ghas” and the Diocese of Poona gives as “Gass, Bhatori, Vasai”. He went to the St Gonsalo Garcia Primary School, the parish school in Gass, and then to Holy Cross High School at Nirmal, the old mother parish next door. In 1978 he entered Prakash Bhavan Minor Seminary in Pune. He was ordained priest on 1 April 1990 for the Diocese of Poona by Bishop Valerian D’Souza. [n42 §6] His elder brother, Fr Peter Almeida, became a priest of Vasai; in August 2023 he was the parish priest of Gass. [n42 §§5–6]

The parish school, the high school at Nirmal, the seminary in Pune: it is the path the Gazetteer described in 1882, when wealthy garden families “often bring up one or more of their sons for the Church”, followed a century later. [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 205]

A new diocese and a pope’s words

On 9 February 1986 Pope John Paul II spoke at Vasai and named “Saint Gonsalo Garcia, the young native Franciscan of Vasai, who was martyred together with his confreres in Japan”. [n38 §4; Vatican] On 22 May 1998 he created the Diocese of Vasai, taking its parishes out of the Archdiocese of Bombay; Gass passed into the new diocese. [n42 §5] Its first bishop, Thomas Dabre, was born in 1945 at Bhuigaon, the Kupari village west of Nirmal. [n39 §1.E; Catholic-Hierarchy] The diocese took St Gonsalo Garcia as its patron. [n38 §4] The village with his “major shrine” now belonged to a diocese under his protection.

The city arrives, 2009–2024

Nallasopara grows around Gass

While Gass stayed a garden village, the town that took its name from the railway halt grew into one of the densest suburbs of the Mumbai region. Nallasopara had 184,664 people in 2001. [n31; Wikipedia: Nallasopara] A student project at TU Delft, seen only through a search summary, puts it at about 564,664 in 2011 on 43 square kilometres. [n31 §3] A Tata Institute of Social Sciences project describes how, after 2000, landowners turned farmland into chawls for migrants, many from Mumbai, and how the low, flood-prone land overlapped the zones marked for building. [n31 §3; TISS, via search summary] The 2001 figures counted large Hindi-, Gujarati- and Urdu-speaking minorities. [n31; Wikipedia: Nallasopara]

In 2009 the Nallasopara municipal council, with Vasai, Virar and Navghar-Manikpur and fifty-three villages, was merged into the Vasai-Virar City Municipal Corporation, the VVCMC. [n31 §1; n12] Gass came inside the city. The 2011 Census, as a result, has no separate figure for it: the green-belt villages, Gass among them, were counted inside the urban corporation and do not appear in the rural village table. [n11; n39 §2] A 2022 VVCMC ward-boundary notification lists “Gaskopari” (गासकोपरी) next to the Kopari lake, though the ward number could not be confirmed. [n42 §6; VVCMC gazette, via snippet] “Gass Road” and “Mothi Sargodi Road” appear as locality names of Nallasopara West. [n42 §6; onefivenine.com]

The low land around old Sopara floods. In July 2018 residents of Achole Road and Chheda Nagar in Nallasopara were stranded for five days, and Tulinj, Vishal Nagar and Achole went under water. [n31 §3] The Gazetteer’s “salt waste … flooded” ground of 1882 had not changed, only the buildings on it. [n31 §3 inferences] No report found mentions flooding in Gass specifically. [n42 §6 gaps]

Through all this Gass kept its look. In 2025 the Hindustan Times described it as “a predominantly Christian village often described as ‘Mini Goa’ for the ancestral mansions that dot the surrounding farmland”. [n42 §6; HT via Magzter reprint] Its lake, its farmland and its church were its anchors. [n42 §6 inferences]

The attack on the church, August 2023

On 11 August 2023 vandals broke into the church of St Gonsalo Garcia at Gass. The altar was defaced, the statue of Our Lady was thrown down and hymn books were scattered. A complaint was filed at Manickpur police station. Archbishop Felix Machado condemned the attack. The church had no CCTV cameras, which hampered the police, and the parish decided to install them. The parish priest was Fr Peter Almeida. [n42 §5; UCA News; AsianNews; Vatican News] Vatican News carried the story with the line that Gonsalo Garcia is “India’s first saint”. [n38 §4] These details come from search summaries of the reports, because the original pages could not be opened. [n42 §5 gaps] No arrest or motive was found in the sources. It remained, as of the research for this book, the most recent attack on a church in the diocese that could be found. [n38]

The fight for the land, 2024–2025

A dumping ground moves on paper

The trouble that came to Gass in 2025 began elsewhere. In Agarwal Nagari, in Nallasopara, forty-one unauthorised buildings had gone up from 2006 on, on land the municipal plan reserved for a dumping ground and a sewage treatment plant. Notices were issued in October 2024, and in November 2024 the buildings were demolished on the orders of the Bombay High Court. Reports give between 2,000 and 8,000 residents displaced. [n31 §3; Land Conflict Watch; n02 §1 gaps; Lokmat Times]

Then the reservation itself was to move. The VVCMC proposed shifting the dumping-ground and STP reservation from “Agarwal Nagar (Vasai East)”, as one report puts it, to a plot in Gass. [n42 §6] Whether the “Agarwal Nagar” of the 2025 reports and the “Agarwal Nagari” of the 2024 demolitions are the same reservation is not stated in so many words; the descriptions, a dumping ground and STP reservation in both, suggest they are, but that is this chapter’s reading. [n31 §3; n42 §6]

Two and a half thousand objections

In March 2025 the people of Gass submitted more than 2,500 objections to the proposal. [n42 §6; newsminimalist summary; HT via Magzter] The plot was reported as “1,500 acres”, a figure that looks implausibly large for a single plot in Gass and should be checked. [n42 §6] The Hindustan Times report, read only through reprints and a summary, gave the village’s population as about 5,500 in one version and about 9,000 in another; the two figures conflict, and neither can be checked here. [n42 §4]

The argument of the objectors, as the headline of the reprint put it, was simple: “No dumping ground in our midst.” [n42; Magzter reprint] Behind it lay everything this chapter has described: a garden village of old houses, farmland and a lake, a parish with a major shrine, on low land that floods, beside a city that had already swallowed its neighbours.

In October 2025 the VVCMC withdrew its application. Residents still wanted a formal commitment that the plan would not come back. [n42 §6; newsminimalist summary] The reports found do not say whether they got one.

A bishop from Gass, 2025–2026

On 1 November 2025, within weeks of the dumping-ground proposal being withdrawn, Pope Leo XIV appointed Fr Simon Almeida, born in Gass, as Bishop of Poona. [n42 §6; Wikipedia: Simon Almeida; Fides] He was ordained bishop and installed on 17 January 2026 by Cardinal Filipe Neri Ferrão. His motto is “To make all things new in Christ”. [n42 §6] The see of Poona had been held before by another son of a Kupari village: Thomas Dabre, born at Bhuigaon, first Bishop of Vasai, was appointed Bishop of Poona in 2009. [n34 §8; Catholic-Hierarchy]

One earlier research note recorded the Gass birthplace as resting on a single local source and unconfirmed; a later, fuller check found it given by both Wikipedia and the Diocese of Poona. [n38; n42 §6] He should not be confused with Cardinal Simon Pimenta, who was born at Marol in Mumbai and is not from Gass. [n42 §6]

So a boy from the St Gonsalo Garcia Primary School of Gass became, at sixty-five, a bishop of the Church. His brother Fr Peter Almeida is the other person of Gass whom the sources name in these years. No other notable people of Gass were found in the research, though there must be many: priests and nuns, teachers, engineers, farmers, people abroad. Their names are in families’ memories, not in the sources this book could use. [n42 §6 gaps]

Gass in November 2026

What is Gass like as this book is finished, in November 2026? The honest answer is that the sources do not say. A general search for news of Gass in 2026 found nothing specific after the bishop’s consecration in January. [n42 §6 gaps] What can be described is the setting.

Gass is a locality of the Vasai-Virar City Municipal Corporation, in Nallasopara West, Vasai taluka, Palghar district. [n42 title; n42 §6] Its people are largely Catholic and Kupari, members of the community of Samvedi Christians that is counted as part of the wider East Indian community. [n42 §6; n34 §7] It is one of the thirteen or so villages that the community’s own writers list as Kupari: Nirmal, Agashi, Nandakhal, Gass, Bhuigaon, Umrale, Naanbhat, Shrilaiwadi, Bolinj, Mardes, Wagholi, Gomes Ali and Rajodi. [n42 §3; n39 §2] A Marathi report counts sixteen Samvedi villages, Gass among them, with about 50,000 speakers of the dialect. [n42 §6; notes 20 and 22] Its church, built in 1942 and enlarged in 1957, is called a major shrine of the diocese’s patron, with a week-long feast in February; in the directory listing its weekday Masses were at 7.15 in the morning in Marathi and 7 in the evening in English. [n42 §5; Prayers & Petitions] Its parish school bears the saint’s name. [n42 §5] Its lake and its farmland are still there, and in 2025 its people fought to keep a dumping ground off them, and won, for now. [n42 §6]

Around it, the neighbours’ news in 2026 gives the season’s colour. In January the Gonsalo Garcia feast was kept at the fort ruin, with families arriving the evening before to cook East Indian dishes as they once did after coming by cart or boat. [n38; FPJ 11 Jan 2026] In June 2026 the diocese opened a Year of St Francis pilgrim centre at Umrale, Gass’s northern neighbour in the old agar. [n38] In the same month a local website wrote that the Kupari red cap and red lugda had “largely disappeared from everyday life”, kept now as identity rather than dress. [n34 §5; All About Vasai Virar, 29 Jun 2026] In October 2026 the Free Press Journal reported that East Indian Catholics were among thirty-three Maharashtra groups awaiting inclusion in the central list of Other Backward Classes; the reports name East Indians, not Kuparis, and a Kupari family obtains any such certificate as East Indian. [n34 §8; FPJ, Oct 2026] In Nallasopara, a demolition drive in February 2026 was halted by a mob and civic staff assaulted. [n02 §1 gaps; Swarajya] The ancient stupa, a mile and a half from Gass, is protected by the Archaeological Survey and still, as one report puts it, “largely neglected”. [n02 §1]

Nothing in that list is about Gass’s own inner life in 2026: who was born, who married, who left for the Gulf or Canada, who still farms, how many go to the 7.15 Mass in Marathi, which old houses have come down. That is the Gass that the families themselves know, and the sources do not.

What is still unknown, and how Gass can help record it

The research behind this chapter was careful and wide, and it found less than the village deserves. Here is what is still unknown, gathered from the gaps the research recorded. [n42 gaps throughout; n44 gaps; n39 gaps]

The stones. Where are the two inscribed blocks the Gazetteer saw in 1882, the “Badhuya” stone at Degu Pobria Mhatara’s house and the “Kodasa Kalavadasa / Ugudeveya” stone at Bab Naik’s? No report after 1882 mentions them. Many other basalt blocks stood before verandas and in plinths. If any family has an old six-sided basalt block, a washing stone or a step, with marks on its face, it should be photographed in raking light, measured, and shown to an epigraphist, without moving or cleaning it. The same applies to carved stones from the Sonarbhat field and the lake shore at Mala, and to the cobra stone under the banyan towards Brahma hill.

The anchor and the landing. The 2018 stone anchor near Taki Pada has no published date or present location in the sources. Who found it, and where is it? Where exactly was the “[Gass] landing-place” of 1882, and when did the last boat come in? The oldest people of Gass may remember parents or grandparents who spoke of boats, or of the creek before it dried.

The name. What does “Gass” mean, and what was it called in Portuguese? No source says. The Tombo de Baçaim should be searched by someone with the full text for every form (“Gaz”, “Gás”, “Guas”, “Ghas”) and for compound entries; the possible link of “Mardes Sirgoly” with today’s Sargodi, and of “Nala Magir” with Nala, should be tested.

The conversion. When did Gass become Christian, under which priests, and on whose land? No mission letter or register found names Gass. The Portuguese parish books of Nirmal, if any survive in Goa, Lisbon or the diocese, are the place to look.

The parish before 1942. The tradition is that Gass belonged to Nirmal. The baptism, marriage and burial registers of Holy Cross, Nirmal, would show Gass families from the nineteenth century onwards, and with them godparents’ names and the old surnames.

The church. Who built it in 1942? One source names Msgr Louis Caetan D’Souza; the parish should confirm it. Who were the first parish priest and the priests since? Who paid for the 1957 expansion, and why was it needed? Was there an older chapel or cross? Why was Gonsalo Garcia chosen? When did the February feast take its present form? Any family that has photographs of the 1942 church, the 1957 works, early feasts, processions or first communions holds part of the parish’s history.

The birthplace tradition. Where does the belief that Gonsalo Garcia was born in Gass come from? When was it first said, and by whom? Even if it cannot be proved, its history is part of Gass’s history, and the oldest people’s memories of it should be written down with their names and dates.

The surnames. Which surnames are Gass surnames, and since when? No source links any Kupari surname, Tuscano included, to a family or village. Families’ baptism certificates, old land documents and the parish registers, read across generations, are the only way to trace them. For the Tuscano name in particular, the question is whether any Nirmal or Gass register shows a godparent or landholder named Toscano, and when the spelling first appears as “Tuscano”.

The numbers. No census figure for Gass has been found, from 1872 to 2011. The old village tables of the Bombay censuses and the VVCMC’s ward data may hold them.

The twentieth century. Almost everything: when the betel and plantain gardens gave way, when the salt pans closed, when the first families went to the Gulf, when the first car, the first bus, the first piped water came; floods; weddings; the war years when the church was built; the dialect as Gass spoke it.

How can families help? By recording what they have before it goes. Photograph old documents and pictures and write on the back, or in a notebook, who, where and when. Record the oldest members of the family speaking, in the Samvedi dialect if they speak it, about their parents and grandparents, the landing, the gardens, the church’s beginning and the feasts, and keep the recordings with their names and the date. Note where old carved or lettered stones lie, and leave them where they are. Ask the parish whether its registers, and Nirmal’s, can be read for family history. Gather it in one place, with the parish or a village group, so that the next person to write the history of Gass will not have to say, as this chapter has had to say so often, that the record is silent.

The people of Gass told the Gazetteer’s surveyors in 1882 that their village was the site of old Sopara. Whether or not that was true, they were claiming a place in a long story. The stones under their verandas, the boats at their landing, the temples by their lake, the church they built in a year of war and the land they defended in 2025 are their part of that story. The rest is still in their houses and their memories.
