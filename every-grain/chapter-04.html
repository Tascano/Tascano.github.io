Part Two — The First Coast

Two hearths on the Ghod

On the bank of the Ghod river, in the dry country east of the Western Ghats in what is now Pune district, there was a village. Its people grew barley and wheat, kept cattle, built houses and fired pots, and cooked on hearths whose charcoal they left behind in the floors. About thirty-four centuries later, archaeologists from Deccan College dug those floors at the site called Inamgaon, and two pieces of the charcoal went to the radiocarbon laboratory of the Tata Institute of Fundamental Research in Bombay. They came back as 3,390 ± 110 and 3,355 ± 105 radiocarbon years before present, laboratory numbers TF-1085 and TF-1087. [n24; IAR 1971-72, p. 84]

Those numbers were printed in Indian Archaeology 1971-72 – A Review, in the same list of Maharashtra radiocarbon dates that, two lines away, records the shells of Manori and the corals off Bombay. [n24; IAR 1971-72, pp. 82–84] Set side by side, the entries tell the story of this chapter in miniature. Inland, beyond the mountains, there are dated villages: hearths, houses, grain. On the coast, there are dated shells. The shells say that the sea was there. They say nothing about people.

This chapter covers the twenty-seven centuries from about 3000 BCE to about 300 BCE. Across India, they were centuries of enormous change. On the Indus and in Gujarat, people built cities of baked brick, sailed to the Persian Gulf, and then, after about 1900 BCE, abandoned the cities. In the Deccan, farming villages spread along the rivers and then, after about 1000 BCE, mostly emptied. New peoples and new ancestries moved into the subcontinent. Iron came into use. In the Ganges valley a second age of cities began, with kingdoms, coins, and new religions whose founders, the Buddha and the Jain teacher Mahavira, taught there. Roads ran south from that world toward the Deccan. And at the end of the period, an emperor whose capital lay on the Ganges had his words cut in stone at Sopara.

For almost all of that time, the coast of Vasai has no archaeological record of its own. Not a hearth, not a sherd, not a grave. This chapter does what the book promises for every silent period: it says plainly where there is no data, tells what was happening nearby and how we know, and asks how it may have touched this coast. The labels from the last chapter still apply. Regional evidence comes from Gujarat, the Deccan, the Ganges valley, Mumbai or the wider Konkan. Vasai-specific evidence comes from inside Vasai taluka. In this chapter, the Vasai-specific evidence before the third century BCE consists of one radiocarbon date on a shell, and a handful of texts that cannot be dated.

A coast without farmers

Start with the absence, because it is the frame for everything else.

Across the Deccan plateau, archaeologists have found hundreds of farming villages of the third, second and early first millennia BCE. On the Konkan coast, below the Ghats, they have found none. A 2023 paper by Garge, Apte and Risbud, which the research could read only in quotations from search results because its host was unreachable, states it directly: “Discoveries in this region, so far, have also shown the absence of Mesolithic/Neolithic/Chalcolithic sites in Konkan.” The hunter-gatherers of the Konkan, the authors suggest, “entered in Historical period without attending any technological transition experienced in the Neolithic and Chalcolithic stages”, and archaeological evidence for the transition from the late Acheulian to the Early Historical “was either missing or not reported earlier”. [n24; Garge, Apte & Risbud 2023]

That is a remarkable claim. It means that, as far as the evidence goes, the people of the Konkan went from making stone tools to living in the world of Ashoka’s edicts without passing through the stages of farming village and copper-using town that the rest of Maharashtra went through. It may be true. Or it may be an artefact of where archaeologists have looked. The archaeologist Kurush Dalal is quoted as saying that the Konkan has seen minimal archaeological activity, and that what is known of its history derives from Shilahara-period inscriptions, a thousand years and more after the end of this chapter. [n24; Live History India, Chaul]

The specific searches the research made bear this out. No Neolithic or Chalcolithic site is reported from Thane, Palghar or Raigad. No Harappan site is known on the Maharashtra coast. No archaeobotanical evidence, no charred grain or seed, has been reported from anywhere on the Konkan before the Early Historic period. No megalith or Iron Age burial is reported from the north Konkan. [n24; Section 4, Gaps] [n23; Section 6, Takeaway; Gaps] For Vasai taluka, Palghar and Dahanu, no site of any of these periods appears in any readable volume of the Archaeological Survey’s annual review. [n24; Section 2, Takeaway; Gaps]

The notes put the conclusion carefully. “The long gap at Vasai between the Mesolithic (about 10 ka, regional) and the Mauryan layer (3rd c. BCE) is a gap in data, not proof that nobody lived here.” [n23; Section 6, Inferences] The best description of the Vasai–Sopara plain between about 5000 and 400 BCE, they suggest, is foragers and fisher-gatherers with, at most, indirect contact with the farmers inland. That is an inference, not an observed record. [n24; Section 4, Inferences]

With that warning in mind, the story moves to where the evidence is.

The farmers beyond the Ghats

The Sahyadri, the Western Ghats, rise steeply behind Vasai to heights of a thousand metres and more. They catch the monsoon. On their western side lies the Konkan, which a modern summary calls “the wet, forested biodiversity hotspot of the Konkan coast”. On their eastern side lies the Deccan plateau, drier, open, cut by the long rivers that run east to the Bay of Bengal: the Tapi in the north, then the Godavari and its tributary the Pravara, the Bhima and its tributary the Ghod. [n24; Schug, INFLIBNET ch. 28] It was along those rivers that the farmers of Maharashtra lived.

The archaeologist G. Robbins Schug, in a teaching chapter on “Chalcolithic Cultures of Western India”, lays out their sequence. The word Chalcolithic means “copper and stone”: these were people who used copper tools alongside stone ones. The first culture, called Savalda after a site in the Tapi valley, dates from about 2200 to 1800 BCE; it is found in the Tapi and Pravara valleys, in the districts of Dhule, Ahmednagar and Nashik. Then comes a Late Harappan phase, about 1800 to 1600 BCE, known above all from Daimabad on the Pravara. Then the Malwa culture, named after the region of central India from which its pottery style came, about 1600 to 1400 BCE. Then the Early Jorwe, about 1400 to 1000 BCE, and the Late Jorwe, about 1000 to 700 BCE, named after a site on the Pravara near Sangamner. [n24; Schug, INFLIBNET ch. 28] [n23; Savalda culture (iaspoint)] Other sources date Jorwe slightly differently, 1500 to 900 BCE, and the notes flag the disagreement. [n24; Jorwe culture, Wikipedia; Live History India]

Inamgaon, the village of the two hearths, was founded, by Schug’s account, in the Malwa phase, between 1600 and 1400 BCE, and lived on into the Jorwe period. Its radiocarbon dates of about 3,390 and 3,355 years before present fall in that range. [n24; Schug; IAR 1971-72, p. 84] Daimabad was the largest site, home to about five thousand people by Schug’s estimate, and about twenty hectares in extent by another source; Inamgaon and Prakash were among the other large settlements. [n24; Schug] [n23; Jorwe culture (Wikipedia)]

What did they grow? Barley and wheat from the start, Schug writes. Rice appears once in her list of early crops, without discussion. Finger millet, sorghum and kodo millet were added in the Early Jorwe period, and later more lentils. [n24; Schug] That list matters for this coast, because the Konkan of every later chapter is a rice country, and the question of when rice first came here is one of the questions this chapter cannot answer.

By the Jorwe period, more than two hundred sites were spread across Maharashtra. Two secondary sources agree on the striking exception: Jorwe sites are found everywhere in Maharashtra “except for the Konkan coast”. [n24; Jorwe culture, Wikipedia; Live History India] Another states it the other way round: “no such evidence is yet found from the coastal Konkan area.” [n23; Jorwe culture (Wikipedia)] And then, after about 1000 BCE, “the majority of these communities were abandoned”, Schug writes. [n24; Schug] The farming world of the Deccan contracted, for reasons the next sections will consider.

Shell, fish and haematite

Did the farmers of the Deccan know the coast? There is one thread of evidence that suggests they did, and it runs through what they wore.

The Jorwe people used shell. Since there is no sea on the Deccan plateau, that shell had to come from a coast. One source infers that coastal shell was probably obtained from the coast of Kutch or the Konkan; another states that the Jorwe people traded with Gujarat and the Konkan for fish, conch shell and haematite, a red iron ore used as a pigment. [n24; Jorwe culture, Wikipedia; Live History India] [n23; Beyond Harappa, Live History India] Both are secondary sources, and both are giving an inference rather than a finding: nobody has traced a particular Jorwe shell to a particular beach.

If the inference is right, it is the first glimpse of a pattern that will run through the whole history of this coast: the Konkan as the supplier, the Deccan as the market, and the passes of the Ghats as the road between them. The notes suggest what the exchange might have been: shell, fish and salt from the coast for grain from the plateau. They mark it explicitly as an inference, not an observed record. [n24; Section 4, Inferences] It is not even known whether any of that shell came from as far north as Vasai, rather than from the Gujarat coast, which the Harappans already knew well.

And that is where the story turns next: to the north-west, where the first seafarers of this coast’s wider world built their harbours.

The Harappans at sea

About four hundred kilometres north of Vasai, at the head of the Gulf of Khambhat, on what was once a course of the Sabarmati river, lies the Harappan site of Lothal. Its most famous feature is a great rectangular basin, lined with baked brick, that its excavators read as a dock, dated to about 2350 or 2300 BCE. [n23; IndianCulture.gov.in] [n24; Vora, Gaur, Sundaresh & Tripati 2006] The National Institute of Oceanography’s scientists call it “the earliest archaeological evidence for maritime activity in India”. [n24; Section 5, Cited Findings] A study by IIT Gandhinagar in 2024 traced an old course of the Sabarmati running past the site, which supports the reading of the basin as connected to a navigable river. [n14; Organiser report] But the reading has always had critics. Harappa.com, the leading popular resource on the Indus civilisation, cautions that absolute proof of the dock interpretation is elusive. [n14; Harappa.com]

What is not in doubt is that Lothal’s people traded by sea. A circular seal of the kind used in the Persian Gulf, the “Dilmun” type associated with Bahrain, was found there. And Indus beads, seals and weights have been found in Mesopotamia, in the Gulf and in Elam, in what is now south-western Iran. [n14; Harappa.com] Somebody was carrying goods between the Gujarat coast and the Gulf in the late third millennium BCE.

Lothal was not alone. At Padri, on the Saurashtra coast, a mature Harappan settlement yielded a copper fishhook fourteen centimetres long, which its excavators Shinde and Thomas in 1993 took as evidence of deep-sea fishing; the site has also been linked to salt production. At Kuntasi, a Harappan port and bead-making centre stood near the Gulf of Kutch. At Bet Dwarka, an island off the tip of Saurashtra, the oldest habitation has been dated to 3,470 ± 80 years before present, in the late Harappan period, followed by a long gap until the Early Historic period, about 2,590 ± 100 years ago. [n24; Vora, Gaur, Sundaresh & Tripati 2006, pp. 2–3] Excavations at Dwarka and Bet Dwarka found no settlement earlier than the late Harappan phase. [n24; Section 5, Cited Findings]

There are bolder claims, and the book reports them with their disputes. In 2001 government ocean scientists (the source calls them the NIO) announced a Neolithic settlement of about 7500 BC lying under thirty to forty metres of water in the Gulf of Khambhat. Nigam and Hashimi, in 2002, plotted its claimed age against the 1995 NIO sea-level curve and found that it matched the depth, quoting the geomorphologist Rajaguru that “Bhavnagar and Hazira were probably connected at 7000 BC”. But the claim was publicly challenged at once, in an article titled “Questionable claims” in Frontline in March 2002, and the notes treat it as unproven. [n24; Nigam & Hashimi 2002] At the other end of the scale of caution, a 2012 overview is cited as arguing that India’s early maritime history is poorly documented and that some scholars see no firm Indus-period maritime evidence at all. [n24; Section 5, Cited Findings]

The balance of evidence is that people of the Indus civilisation did sail the Gujarat coast and the waters to the Gulf in the late third millennium BCE, at Lothal and elsewhere, and that this is the first documented seafaring anywhere on India’s west coast. No Palaeolithic or Mesolithic boat has been found anywhere on this coast. [n24; Section 5, Takeaway; Gaps]

Daimabad: the southern edge

The Harappans also reached inland Maharashtra, at least once.

At Daimabad, on the Pravara in Ahmednagar district, about two hundred kilometres east of Vasai across the Ghats, a layer of Late Harappan material lies between the Savalda culture below and the Malwa and Jorwe cultures above. One source calls Daimabad the southernmost known Harappan site. It is the only site in Maharashtra with both Savalda and Late Harappan pottery, and it “seem[s] to bear traces of culture contact until 1600 BCE”, in Schug’s phrase. [n23; Daimabad (Wikipedia)] [n24; Schug] In 1974 a hoard of bronze figures was found at Daimabad: a chariot with a driver, an elephant, a rhinoceros and a buffalo. Whether the hoard is Late Harappan or merely Harappan-influenced is disputed. [n23; Daimabad (Wikipedia); Abhijna e-museum]

Yet even at Daimabad the Harappan influence was limited. “The Indus people seem to have had less influence than nearer neighbors in the Malwa and Kayatha regions”, Schug concludes. [n24; Schug] The Harappan world touched Maharashtra at one point on the Pravara, and that point lies on the far side of the mountains from Vasai.

Why not Sopara?

So the Harappans sailed the Gujarat coast, and reached the Pravara valley overland. Why is there no Harappan site at Sopara, or anywhere on the Konkan?

The honest answer is that nobody knows whether there was one. No Bronze Age, Harappan or Mesopotamian find on the Sopara–Vasai coast surfaced in any source the research read. [n36; Section 1, Gaps] The coast between the Gulf of Khambhat and Bombay has had little archaeological attention of the kind that found Lothal and Padri, and the sea-level history makes the search harder: if the sea in the middle Holocene stood a few metres higher than now, as Merh proposed for the Gujarat and Maharashtra coasts, a Harappan landing place on the north Konkan would have stood on a shore that has since moved, and may lie under silt or under the sea. [n24; Vora et al. 2006, citing Merh 1992] That is a possibility, not an argument.

The question is not new. When J. M. Campbell and his collaborators compiled the history chapter of the Thana Gazetteer in 1882, they opened it with a bold sentence: “From B.C. 2500 to B.C. 500 there are signs of trade with Egypt, Phoenicia, and Babylon; from B.C. 250 to A.D. 250 there are dealings with, perhaps settlements of, Greeks and Parthians”. [n36; Gaz XIII-II p. 400] The “signs” they listed for the earliest period were all literary: the historian Rawlinson on the Arabian port of Gerrha, a Babylonian tradition of teachers coming from the southern sea, the prophet Ezekiel on ivory from Dedan. None of them was a find on this coast. [n36; Gaz XIII-II p. 400] The same compilers argued at length that Sopara might be Ophir, the land from which King Solomon’s ships brought gold, precious stones, sandalwood, ivory, peacocks and apes about 1000 BCE; but they concluded that “the site of Ophir is unsettled”, and that the latest Egyptian scholarship “make[s] the balance lean in favour of Arabia”. Their own history chapter calls the Ophir identification “though not unlikely, … doubtful”, and states that the carving of Ashoka’s edicts “remains the earliest known fact in the history of the Thana coast”. [n15; Thana Gaz. XIV, pp. 317–318] [n36; Gaz XIII-II pp. 400–401] Bhagwanlal Indraji thought the finds at Sopara “strengthen” the Ophir identification, but offered no object of the tenth century BCE. [n36; Indraji 1882, p. 277]

Modern scholarship leaves Ophir unlocated, with Arabia and the Red Sea and Somali coasts as the main candidates; an inscribed potsherd from Tell Qasile of the eighth century BCE, reading “gold of Ophir to Beth-Horon … 30 shekels”, shows that Ophir was a real gold-exporting place, but not where. Among Indian candidates, current reference works favour Poovar in Kerala, not Sopara. [n15; Wikipedia, “Ophir”] The Sopara–Ophir link rests mostly on the likeness of names, Sophir and Souphir in the Greek Bible and in Josephus, and on the Indian character of the goods. It is a nineteenth-century hypothesis, and the book tells it as one. [n15; Inferences]

There is one modern geomorphological study that touches the question of a Bronze Age harbour on this coast. A preliminary survey reported that Kintasi, a site on the western coast, was a creek port in the middle Holocene. The notes record this only in passing, from the same Rajaguru and Ghate summary that gives the Nala Sopara shell date, and they do not locate Kintasi precisely. [n23; Section 5, Cited Findings] It is not evidence for Sopara.

The drying

Something happened to the climate of South Asia a little over four thousand years ago, and the farmers of the Indus and the Deccan both felt it.

The event is often called the 4.2 ka event, for 4,200 years before present. In the cave of Mawmluh in Meghalaya, in north-east India, stalagmites record an abrupt weakening of the monsoon at about 4,100 years ago, a result published by Berkelhammer and colleagues in 2012; the event has given its name, through that cave, to the most recent age of geological time, the Meghalayan, though the research found that link only in a search summary. [n23; Section 5, Cited Findings; Gaps] Foraminifera from the Arabian Sea show a weaker summer and winter monsoon by 4,100 years ago, with the decline of the Indus cities after it. But the picture is not uniform. Other cave records in north and north-east India show little change; Sahiya cave shows drying from 4,200 to 3,500 years ago; and Scroxton and colleagues argue that the event lacks regional coherence. Whether it caused the decline of the Harappan cities is debated. [n23; Climate of the Past 14:1869 (2018); Goldschmidt 2020; Climate of the Past 15:73 (2019)]

On the Konkan, no specific record of the 4.2 ka event has been found. [n23; Section 5, Gaps] But there are regional records of a drying later in the Holocene. The 2021 review of west-coast monsoon studies reports, from Sandeep and colleagues, a decreasing trend in the south-west monsoon from 8,600 to 4,500 years ago and fluctuating, decreasing rain after 4,500, which the review matches to the 4.2 ka aridity. Kumaran and colleagues read a decline in mangrove pollen near 4,000 years ago, notably of the tree Cullenia exarillata, as a sign of intense aridity at the start of the Late Holocene. And Limaye and Kumaran, from mangrove records in the northern Konkan, inferred reduced rainfall and drier conditions in the Late Holocene, though the review gives no date for that northern record. [n24; Frontiers in Earth Science 2021 review]

Schug adds one more line: “Cores from Maharashtra’s coastal estuaries support increasing aridity by the middle of the second millennium BCE.” [n24; Schug, INFLIBNET ch. 28] It is a tantalising sentence, because “Maharashtra’s coastal estuaries” may well include the creeks of the north Konkan, but she does not name the cores, and the research did not trace them.

So there is a drying signal on this coast in the second millennium BCE, and a drying signal inland, where the Jorwe villages were abandoned after about 1000 BCE. The notes observe that the mid-second-millennium aridity in the estuary cores lines up with the Limaye and Kumaran drying and with the end of Jorwe settlement. Then they add the necessary caution: “a causal link for Vasai is speculation.” [n24; Section 4, Inferences]

Shells on the Salsette shore

While the farmers rose and fell beyond the Ghats, the sea went on working at the coast of Vasai and Salsette, and a scatter of radiocarbon dates on shells shows it at work.

At Mahim, at the southern end of Salsette, shells from the intertidal zone gave a date of 3,650 years before present, with a very wide error (plus 2,035, minus 1,670 years), laboratory number TF-1149; the list placed Mahim, oddly, under Thana. At St Xavier’s College, in the heart of Bombay, shells 1.95 metres below the surface gave 2,800 ± 110 BP (TF-1197). At Madh Point, on the west coast of Salsette, shells only 0.45 metres below the surface and 5.5 metres above low-water level gave 2,115 ± 90 BP (TF-1186). Farther south, on the Kolaba and Ratnagiri coasts, shells from Chaul gave 2,180 ± 95, from Korlai-Borlai 2,410 ± 95, and from Murud 2,600 ± 105 years before present. [n24; IAR 1971-72, pp. 82–84] As always with this list, the figures are uncalibrated radiocarbon years, and the samples are natural, not cultural. [n24; IAR 1971-72, note on BP values]

Taken together with the older Manori shells of about 4,200 to 4,500 years ago, they show the coast of Salsette as an active, shell-bearing shore from the middle Holocene through to about 2,100 years ago: from before the Harappan cities to the age of the Mauryas, when Sopara emerges. [n24; Section 1, Inferences] Farther down the Konkan, beach rock from Devgad to Guhagar formed between about 3,130 and 1,100 years ago (Kumar and colleagues, 2000), and the sand dunes of Dive Agar are two to four thousand years old (Deswandikar and Karlekar, 1996). [n24; Gawali et al. 2022, p. 146]

And then there is the one date from Vasai itself.

The shell of Karal

Rajaguru and Ghate’s summary of Quaternary sea levels on the western coast reports that shells from the beach-dune rock at a place called “Karal”, near Nala Sopara, gave a radiocarbon date of 3,250 ± 110 years before present. [n24; Rajaguru & Ghate; VASAI-SPECIFIC, weakly documented] [n23; UNESCO Silk Road paper]

It is the only radiocarbon date from anywhere near Sopara that the research found. It is a fragile thing. The research could not retrieve the page itself, only a search summary. The original sample location is not given, and neither is its context: whether the shell was lying where it lived and died, or had been washed in and reworked from an older deposit. The source calls the date “mid-Holocene”; by the standard divisions of geological time, 3,250 years ago is Late Holocene. [n24; Rajaguru & Ghate; Gaps] The same paper says that changes in the estuarine zone around Sopara in the Late Holocene had been studied, but the research could not find the study. [n24; Rajaguru & Ghate]

What the date can show, if it is sound, is limited and precise: that about 3,250 years ago, give or take a century, there were tidal or beach conditions near Nala Sopara, where a dune of shelly sand was being cemented into rock. That is the age of the Late Harappan settlement at Bet Dwarka, of the Malwa and early Jorwe villages beyond the Ghats, of the time when, by the genetic evidence, new ancestries were spreading across India. The notes are careful to say that no evidence ties the Karal shell to people. It is marine, not cultural. [n24; Section 5, Inferences] [n23; Section 5, Inferences]

It is worth imagining, without pretending to know, what that shore looked like. A dune of pale shelly sand, at the edge of the sea or of a broad tidal creek. Mangroves in the channels. The low rises of the island, perhaps wooded. The hills of Tungareshwar and Kamandurg to the east. Whether there was a fire on that dune, a boat drawn up beside it, a fish trap in the creek, the evidence does not say. The shell is the only witness, and it speaks only of the sea.

Rice, millet and what the coast may have eaten

Every later chapter of this book is full of rice: the rice fields of the island, the rice-paste paintings of the Warli, the rice pot of the epilogue. So it is a strange discovery that the evidence for rice on this coast before the historic period is nil.

Inland, rice is named once in Schug’s list of the early crops of the Deccan Chalcolithic, without discussion; the staple crops there were barley and wheat, with millets added later. [n24; Schug] The first clear charred rice in the region comes from Ter, in Osmanabad district, on the Deccan plateau, which the Archaeological Survey excavated in 1957-58. Ter was occupied from about the fourth century BCE to the fourth century CE, with Northern Black Polished Ware, the fine glossy pottery of the Gangetic world, in its lower levels; the excavators reported “charred grains of rice, wheat and pulses”. [n24; IAR 1957-58, pp. 21–23] Ter lies at the inland end of the Early Historic road that climbed from Kalyan through the Ghats, and its rice comes from the very end of this chapter’s period. [n24; IAR 1957-58]

For the Konkan itself, no crop evidence of any date before the Early Historic period has been found. A major international project on early rice, run from University College London, might hold relevant data, but its pages could not be retrieved. [n24; Section 4, Cited Findings; Gaps] The book therefore cannot say when rice was first grown on the island of Sopara, or by whom, or whether the first farmers here grew rice at all.

What can be said is that the setting suited it. The Konkan is a land of heavy monsoon rain, and the coastal plain floods every year. And the setting suited other foods: fish and shellfish from the creeks and the sea, salt from the tidal flats. Every later record of this coast, from the Gazetteer’s “brisk trade to Gujarat and Bombay in grain, garden produce and salt” in 1882 onwards, shows these as its staples. [n01; Gazetteer of the Bombay Presidency, Thana] [n17; NVLI Thana Gazetteer] The notes’ suggestion that the people of the Vasai plain in this age were “foragers and fisher-gatherers” is a reasonable inference from the setting and from the absence of farming sites. [n24; Section 4, Inferences] It is not a finding.

Iron and stone circles

Around the end of the second millennium BCE, iron came into use across much of India, and with it, in the Deccan and the south, a new way of honouring the dead: the megaliths, burials marked by circles of stone, slabs or cairns.

The dating of the Indian megaliths is poor. Of nearly three thousand known sites, few are dated. In Vidarbha, in eastern Maharashtra, dates cluster near 600 BCE, at sites such as Naikund and Takalghat; the archaeologist S. B. Deo proposed 700 to 800 BCE. A 2022 study in Radiocarbon dated two Vidarbha burials to between AD 250 and 874, far later than expected. [n23; Radiocarbon, Vidarbha chronology; Heritage Univ. Kerala journal] On the south-west coast, the megaliths of Malabar and south Canara, many built of laterite, are mostly Iron Age, between about 1000 BCE and 500 CE. [n24; Section 4, Cited Findings] An “Explorations in Maharashtra” volume lists a chapter on “Megalithic Culture in South Konkan”, but the research saw only its title. [n24; academia.edu contents listing]

From the north Konkan, nothing. No stone circle, dolmen or Iron Age burial has been reported from Thane, Palghar or Raigad. [n24; Section 4, Cited Findings] The Iron Age of this coast is, in the notes’ phrase, “unexcavated”. [n24; Section 5, Inferences] When the coast reappears in the record with Ashoka’s edicts, it is already part of the iron-using world of the Mauryas; how and when iron first reached Sopara is unknown.

Genes on the move

While the archaeology of the coast is silent, the genetics of the subcontinent in these centuries has become, in the last decade, one of the most discussed subjects in Indian history. It, too, is silent about this coast in particular. But it describes what was happening around it.

The largest study is by Narasimhan, Patterson and many colleagues, published in Science in 2019 as “The formation of human populations in South and Central Asia”. It analysed 523 ancient individuals. Its central finding is a sequence of three “clines”, gradients of mixed ancestry, each succeeding the one before. Before 2000 BCE there was what the authors call the Indus Periphery Cline, a mixture of ancestry related to early Iranian farmers and hunter-gatherers with the ancient South Asian (AASI) ancestry, with no detectable ancestry from the Eurasian steppe. Then came the Steppe Cline, as people with steppe-related ancestry moved south in the first half of the second millennium BCE, contributing up to about thirty per cent in some groups. Then came the Modern Indian Cline. [n24; Narasimhan et al. 2019] [n23; Reich Lab PDF]

Out of these mixtures formed the two great ancestral populations of modern India that geneticists call the ANI (Ancestral North Indians) and the ASI (Ancestral South Indians). The ASI, the paper argues, arose as Indus Periphery Cline people mixed with groups to the north and east, with a minimum of about 55 per cent Indus Periphery ancestry in the ASI and about 70 per cent in the ANI. “Much of the formation of both the ASI and ANI occurred in the second millennium BCE”, the authors write, and the ASI “were not fully formed at the time of the IVC”, the Indus Valley Civilisation. For one present-day group, the Palliyar of south India, the authors estimate an average of 107 ± 11 generations since mixture, which at 28 years a generation corresponds to between 1700 and 400 BCE. Steppe ancestry is higher in Brahmin groups, and mostly came through men. [n24; Narasimhan et al. 2019] The geneticist Kumaraswamy Thangaraj, interviewed in 2026, dates the mixing of ANI and ASI to about 1,900 to 4,200 years ago, followed by long endogamy, marriage within closed groups. [n23; The Week, Aug 2026] [n14; The Week]

The second key study is from Rakhigarhi, a great Harappan city in Haryana. Shinde and colleagues, publishing in Cell in 2019 with co-authors from Deccan College in Pune, tested 61 skeletal samples from the site’s cemetery. Only one, catalogued as I6113, yielded authentic DNA. Direct radiocarbon dating of the bones failed for lack of collagen; the habitation has seven charcoal dates between 2800 and 2300 BCE, and the cemetery is dated by its context. The genome of that one woman, the authors found, “fits as a mixture of people related to ancient Iranians (the largest component)” and South-East Asian hunter-gatherer-related ancestry, with “no evidence of Steppe pastoralist-related ancestry”. The paper also notes that the share of ancestry related to “tribal southern Indians” in her genome is smaller than in present-day groups. [n24; Shinde et al. 2019, PMC6800651] It is a single genome, and press claims about it have gone well beyond the data. [n23; Nature India; Sci.News]

Now the crucial point for this book. A text search of the full Narasimhan paper found no Maharashtra, Konkan or Inamgaon samples. Rakhigarhi is in Haryana. No ancient DNA has been recovered from the Konkan, from Mumbai, or from anywhere on the Maharashtra coast, and the skeletons of Inamgaon have not been reported in these studies. [n24; Section 7, Cited Findings; Gaps] The genetic history of India in these centuries is written without a single person from this coast.

So what may be inferred, and how far? The notes offer one inference and label it clearly: if the sequence of AASI, Iranian-farmer-related and steppe ancestry applies to the western Deccan, then the Konkan’s earliest settlers carried forager (AASI-like) ancestry, the Iranian-related component would have arrived with Indus-linked farming people, and steppe ancestry after 2000 BCE. “No Konkan data test this.” [n24; Section 7, Inferences] The living people of the Palghar hills, the Warli and Kokna, carry, as the last chapter showed, one of the highest AASI shares measured anywhere, about 0.54 by one measure; that is a present-day result, not an ancient one. [n24; Section 7, Cited Findings] [n01; Debortoli et al. 2020]

Speech before writing

What language did the people of this coast speak in these centuries? The plain answer is that nobody knows, and the research found no reliable source that even tries to say.

No pre-Indo-Aryan substrate language specific to this coast, whether Munda, Dravidian or something now lost, is described in any source the research found. Grierson, in 1905, mentioned that the Kolis “have often been connected” with the Munda-speaking Kols of eastern India, but recorded it only as a past opinion, and the notes call it weak. [n22; Gaps; LSI vol. VII] No source was found for a Dravidian layer in the place names of the Konkan or in Marathi. [n01; Gaps] The 1882 Gazetteer’s derivation of Nirmal from Dravidian nir and mal is a compiler’s guess. [n10; Gazetteer 1882, p. 292 n.]

What is known is the end-point. Every language spoken by the old communities of this coast today is Indo-Aryan, related to Marathi, Gujarati and Konkani: Warli and Katkari, classed by Grierson as Bhil dialects turned Marathi; Koli, Agri, Samvedi and Vadvali, each a local variety. [n22; LSI vol. VII; Living dialects] The Warli, who carry such deep forager ancestry, speak an Indo-Aryan tongue; the notes infer that their ancestors adopted it later, from incoming speakers. [n01; Inferences] When, and how, Indo-Aryan speech reached this coast is not known. The first written language here, at the very end of this chapter, is Prakrit, an Indo-Aryan vernacular, cut in the Brahmi script on Ashoka’s stones. [n22; Ashokan Prakrit at Sopara]

The second age of cities

In the middle of the first millennium BCE, far to the north-east, a new urban world rose in the Ganges valley. Historians call it the second urbanisation, the first having been the Harappan. It had cities and kings, coins and trade guilds, and, in its texts, a map of India divided into sixteen great realms, the mahajanapadas, which the Buddhist canon lists in the Anguttara Nikaya.

None of the sixteen is on the Konkan. The only one in the Deccan was Assaka, or Ashmaka, which lay inland on the Godavari. Its capital is named Potana or Potali in the texts, and has been tentatively identified with Bodhan. The grammarian Panini mentions Ashmaka. Modern summaries date the mahajanapada period to about 700 to 425 or 345 BCE, without good citations. The lists do not include Aparanta, Sopara or the Konkan. [n24; Wikipedia: Assaka]

One early Buddhist poem gives a glimpse of how that northern world imagined the south. In the Sutta Nipata, in verses 976 and 977, a Brahmin teacher named Bavari lives by the Godavari, in the territory of Assaka, on the Dakshinapatha, the “southern road”. [n24; Wikipedia: Assaka] The phrase is the key to the next part of this chapter’s story. For people in the Ganges valley, the south was a direction reached by a road.

The road south

The Dakshinapatha was not a single road but a direction of travel and a set of routes. A summary of the evidence traces the word through several layers of Indian texts: the Aitareya Brahmana, a Vedic ritual text, refers to southern peoples; Panini links Ashmaka with the Dakshinatya, the southerners, around 500 BCE by one modern estimate; and, much later, the Junagadh inscription of about 150 CE calls a Satavahana king “lord of Dakshinapatha”. By the Buddha’s time, according to the historian Sanyal as summarised, a road ran from Varanasi through Vidisha to Pratishthana, the later Paithan, on the Godavari. In the Mauryan period a branch ran from Ujjain to the ports of Gujarat. [n24; Wikipedia: Dakshinapatha]

How did the road reach the sea? The notes are careful here. An earlier pass at the research found no source for the route from Paithan to the coast, Panini’s reference to Aparanta, or the Ghat passes, and flagged any Dakshinapatha statement as unsourced. [n23; Section 6, Gaps] The later pass filled some of that gap, with evidence from the Early Historic period, at the very end of this chapter and after it. At Ter, on the plateau, the Survey’s excavation found occupation from about the fourth century BCE, with Northern Black Polished Ware; Ter is the Deccan end of the road that came up from Kalyan through the Bhor Ghat. [n24; IAR 1957-58, pp. 21–23] At Kalyan, on the Ulhas, Early Historic red polished ware has been found; Kalyan is the “Kalliena” of the Periplus of the Erythraean Sea, a Greek sailing guide of the first century CE. [n24; Dounga Raghavan 2019] The pass of Naneghat, which appears as the Konkan–Deccan crossing in popular summaries, carries Satavahana inscriptions of the second and first centuries BCE. [n24; Wikipedia: Dakshinapatha] [n22; Wikipedia: Naneghat]

So by the end of this chapter, perhaps, and certainly by the next, the southern road of the Ganges world had a western spur that climbed down through the Ghats to the creeks of the Ulhas, Kalyan and Sopara. The 1882 Gazetteer reasoned that Sopara’s position, high above the marsh, with fresh water, linked by the creeks to the Vaitarna and the Ulhas and by land up the Tansa valley, “must early have made Sopara a chosen settlement” and “an early centre of trade”. [n24; Gazetteer Vol. XIV, p. 316] That is the compilers’ reasoning, not evidence of an early date. But it names the geography that made the island valuable when the road arrived: a sheltered harbour at the foot of the passes, facing a sea that led to Gujarat, to Arabia and beyond.

When did the road first arrive? There is no evidence. But the earliest texts that seem to name this coast belong to the world at the end of the road, and they are the next part of the story.

Sunaparanta: the country Punna chose

The scene is set in the city of Savatthi, in the Ganges plain, in the Buddha’s lifetime. A monk named Punna comes to the Buddha for a brief teaching, and when it is given, the Buddha asks him where he will live.

In the translation of the Buddhist translator Sujato, published on SuttaCentral, Punna answers: “there’s a country named Sunāparanta. I shall live there.” The Buddha replies with a warning: “The people of Sunāparanta are wild and rough, Puṇṇa.” He asks what Punna will do if they abuse and insult him; what if they strike him with their fists, with clods of earth, with sticks, with knives; what if they kill him. To each question Punna answers that he will think them kind for not doing worse, and at the last that some monks seek a knife to end their lives, while he will have found one without seeking. Satisfied, the Buddha lets him go. Punna “set out for Sunāparanta”. [n24; MN 145, Sujato translation, 5.2–5.3, 7.1–7.2]

The text is the Punnovada Sutta, number 145 in the Majjhima Nikaya, the “Middle-length Discourses” of the Pali canon. [n24; MN 145] Buddhist tradition places its composition before Ashoka, but its date is not known. [n24; Earliest texts, Cited Findings]

Two things about it matter for this book. First, it does not name Sopara. The identification of Sunaparanta with Aparanta, and of both with the country around Sopara, comes from the commentaries and from later scholars. [n24; MN 145] Second, it gives the oldest picture in any text of how the Ganges world saw the western coast: as a frontier, a country at the edge of the known world, whose people were “wild and rough”. A modern summary of another text glosses Aparanta in a similar spirit, as “the region which is not habituated by civilised people”. [n15; LiveHistoryIndia] That is the view from the centre. The people of the coast, if they had a view of the Ganges, did not record it.

The notes draw a firm conclusion from the sutta. It shows that a western frontier land called Sunaparanta was known in the Buddhist tradition. It does not date a settlement at Sopara before the edicts. [n24; Earliest texts, Inferences]

The sandalwood hall

Around the sutta, the Buddhist tradition grew a richer story, and here Sopara does appear, though in texts much later than the events they describe.

In the Pali commentarial tradition, the Buddha’s disciple Punna is a merchant of Sunaparanta. Merchants, after he saved them from a storm, gave him part of their cargo of sandalwood, and with it a red-sandalwood hall, the Candanasala, was built in Sunaparanta. The Buddha came with five hundred arahants, saints, spent one night and left before dawn. [n02; DPPN: Candanamala/Candanasala; palikanon.com: Mankulakarama]

In the Sanskrit Divyavadana, a collection of Buddhist legends compiled, by one estimate, around the beginning of the Common Era, the same story is set explicitly at Surparaka. Purna is the son of a rich merchant by a slave woman. Denied a share of the family property, he takes to the sea, leads maritime expeditions, meets the Buddha at Shravasti and invites him home. He builds a sandalwood temple near Surparaka, and the Buddha arrives with his monks “flying through the sky”. [n02; LiveHistoryIndia; 84000 Knowledgebase] The Purnavadana is one of about half a dozen stories found in every surviving version of the Divyavadana. [n02; 84000 Knowledgebase]

The 1882 Gazetteer, summarising the French scholar Burnouf’s translation from Nepalese and Tibetan sources, which it dated to about the fourth or fifth century CE, gives the legend’s picture of the town: “the seat of a king, a city with several hundred thousand inhabitants, with eighteen gates, and a temple of Buddha adorned with friezes of carved sandalwood … Caravans of merchants came from Shravasti … and great ships with 500 … merchants … traded to distant lands.” The trade goods were fine cloth and goshirsha sandalwood, and a strong merchant guild ruled the trade. One merchant in the story asks: “Who … that has made six safe voyages, has ever been known to tempt Providence by trying a seventh?” And “Purna, the son of a rich Sopara merchant by a slave girl … turned the people of the Konkan from their old faith to Buddhism.” [n15; Thana Gaz. XIV, pp. 320–321] A modern summary gives the town seventeen gates rather than eighteen; the Sanskrit has not been checked to settle it. [n15; LiveHistoryIndia] The Gazetteer linked the “Musalaka” hill of the legend to Padan hill on Salsette, where Todd found his microliths. [n24; Gazetteer Vol. XIV, pp. 320–321]

Bhagwanlal Indraji recorded a local version in 1882: the Buddha converted the Brahmin sage Vakkali and five hundred widows, and gave them relics of his hair and nails, over which they built the “Widows’ Stupa”. [n02; Shah 2020] In the fifth century CE the Purna story was painted on the walls of Cave 2 at Ajanta. [n02; LiveHistoryIndia]

What is this worth as history? The archaeologist Suraj Pandit calls the Buddha’s visit “not historically true”, though symbolic of Sopara’s rise as a centre of pilgrimage. [n02; LiveHistoryIndia] Every version is later than the Buddha by centuries; the description of the city, with its king and its gates and its great ships, describes the Sopara of the storytellers’ own age, which the next chapter will meet, not the coast of the fifth century BCE. The notes call Purna the natural protagonist for the story of a merchant who brings a new faith home by sea, “provided it is framed as legend recorded about 2,000 years ago, not history”. [n02; Inferences]

The blind mariner and the exiled prince

Two more Buddhist stories have been tied to Sopara, and both need care.

The first is a Jataka, one of the tales of the Buddha’s former lives: the Supparaka Jataka, number 463. In it the Bodhisatta, the future Buddha, is a blind master mariner of Bharukaccha, the port of Broach in Gujarat. He pilots a ship through a succession of marvellous seas, named in the text Khuramali, Aggimali, Dadhimali, Nilavanna-kusamali, Nalamali and Valabhamukha. [n24; Ja 463, Ancient Buddhist Texts] The 1882 Gazetteer took the name to mean that “in one of his former births Gautama Buddha was Bodhisat Suppurak, that is, a Bodhisattva of Sopara”. [n15; Thana Gaz. XIV, p. 316] A modern reference, citing the historian H. P. Ray, describes the Jataka as portraying a prosperous port trading with south-west Asia, Gujarat, Malabar and Sri Lanka, and dates it to about the sixth century BCE. [n02; Wikipedia: Sopara] But the story is set at Bharukaccha, not Sopara, and the link rests on the mariner’s name. The research could not confirm from the text either that Sopara appears as a port in it or the sixth-century date, and the Pali Jataka commentary is a much later compilation. Both points, the notes conclude, are unsupported. [n24; Ja 463] [n15; Flag]

The second is from the Mahavamsa, the great chronicle of Sri Lanka, composed about the fifth century CE. It tells how Vijaya, the legendary first king of the Sinhalese, was driven out by his father with seven hundred followers. In Turnour’s 1837 translation: “The King compelling Wijayo and his retinue, 700 in number, to have the half of their heads shaved, and having embarked them in a vessel, sent them adrift on the ocean … Wijayo himself landed at the port of Supparaka”. [n15; H-J, “Supara”] The historian Romila Thapar, as cited, places this at Mahavamsa VI.46–47. [n24; Earliest texts, Cited Findings] The 1882 Gazetteer dated Vijaya’s voyage to 640 BCE; that is an 1882-era date built on the chronicle and should not be reused. [n24; Gazetteer Vol. XIV, pp. 316–317] Vijaya’s Supparaka is a stop on a legendary voyage, told nine centuries or more after its supposed date.

Shurparaka in the epics

The Sanskrit epics know Shurparaka too, and as something different again: a holy place.

The pilgrimage section of the Mahabharata’s Vana Parva brings the Pandava brothers to “very holy Shurparaka”, where they see the altar of Parashurama, on their way from Gokarna in north Kanara to Prabhasa near Veraval in Kathiawar; the 1882 Gazetteer cited the passage as chapter 118 and suspected it might be “a late Brahmanical interpolation”. [n15; Thana Gaz. XIV, p. 316; Kanheri n.] Arjuna, too, comes to “very holy Surparaka” on his western tour, as Indraji recorded. [n01; Shah et al. 2020] In the Sabha Parva, describing the conquests of the Pandava Sahadeva in the south, a translation of the Ganguli type reads that he “reduced to subjection, Surparaka and Talakata, and the Dandakas also”. [n24; Krishnakosh, Sabha Parva ch. 30] The Harivamsa gives the city’s dimensions, “500 bows” broad, built on land Parashurama won from the sea. [n15; Thana Gaz. XIV, p. 316] Later texts multiply the praise: the Padma Purana lists 108 tirthas at Shurparaka, and the Skanda Purana says vows made there bring multiplied merit. [n01; Shah et al. 2020] The Sanskrit lexicon called the Yadava Kosha defines the region: “Aparanta is the western country, Shurparaka and others.” [n15; Thana Gaz. XIV, p. 319] A popular account adds that the Ramayana names Shurparaka among the ports of its time, but the research found no Ramayana verse to support it. [n15; LiveHistoryIndia; Gaps]

The difficulty with all of these is the same, and it cannot be argued away. The Mahabharata is a compilation built up over many centuries, with no secure date for any particular passage. [n24; Krishnakosh; Earliest texts, Gaps] The 1882 Gazetteer put the epic at “B.C. 1400”, and from that built a history in which Sopara’s importance as a religious and trading centre lasted “for over two thousand five hundred years (B.C. 1400–A.D. 1300)”. [n36; Gaz XIV pp. 316–317, 323] That figure is a calendar built on a nineteenth-century guess at the epic’s date, not on archaeology, and the notes say it should not be reused. [n36; Section 1, Cited Findings] [n24; Gazetteer Vol. XIV, pp. 316–317]

What the epics do show is how this coast was imagined by the Sanskrit tradition once it was known: as a holy place at the western edge of the land, made by Parashurama out of the sea. The notes set the texts in order: undated epic and Pali traditions, in which Shurparaka is a holy, reclaimed coastal place and Sunaparanta a rough frontier, come before the first datable anchor. “None of them dates a settlement at Sopara before the edicts.” [n24; Earliest texts, Inferences]

The Jain memory of a first age

The third of India’s great religious traditions makes the boldest claim of all for Sopara’s antiquity, and it too must be told as tradition.

In Jain texts, as the 2020 study by Monish Shah, Siddharth Kale and Jainacharya Bhagyayashsuri summarises them, Sopara is the earliest taleti, the foothill shrine, of the sacred hill of Shatrunjaya, set up by Bharata Chakravarti, the eldest son of the first Tirthankara, Rishabhanatha or Adinath. In Jain cosmology that places the shrine’s founding at the beginning of the present age of the world. The taleti, the tradition says, later moved to Valabhi and then to Palitana, in Saurashtra, where Shatrunjaya’s temples stand today. [n01; Shah et al. 2020] The Paumacariyam, the Jain telling of the Ramayana, has Lava and Kusha, the sons of Rama, conquer Sopara. In the legend of Shripal, the king of Sopara is Mahasena, whose daughter Tilakasundari the mythical king Shripal married. [n01; Shah et al. 2020] [n15; Thana Gaz. XIV, p. 319] A Gujarati story says that King Vikram, traditionally of 56 BCE, got one of his five magic wands from Sopara. [n15; Thana Gaz. XIV, p. 319]

None of these stories can be dated, and none is evidence that Sopara existed in the age it describes. What they show is the place Sopara came to hold in Jain memory, and that memory is old and long. The Jain teacher Jinaprabhasuri, in his fourteenth-century Vividhatirthakalpa, lists Sopara among the eighty-four most important Jain tirthas and notes an image of Rishabhadeva there in his own lifetime; one of the eighty-four Jain gachchhas, or lineages, was named after Sopara, and a “Sopariya” gachha was still active in 1953, according to the scholar P. K. Gode as cited. [n15; Thana Gaz. XIV, p. 319] [n02; Shah 2020] Jain tradition records an assembly to recite the scriptures, held at Sopara by Acharya Yakshadevsuri after a twelve-year famine, in Vira Samvat 585, about 58 CE. [n02; Shah 2020] Those belong to later chapters. The tradition of Bharata’s taleti is the Jain equivalent of Parashurama’s reclaimed land: a claim that this coast was holy from the first age of the world.

Laid side by side, the three traditions make a striking pattern. The Brahmanical texts make Shurparaka a land won from the sea and a place of pilgrimage. The Buddhist texts make it a merchant’s home, a frontier, a port of departure. The Jain texts make it a foothill shrine of the first age. Sopara is one of very few Indian towns named in Hindu, Buddhist and Jain sacred literature alike. [n15; Takeaway] That does not date it. It does suggest that by the time those traditions took their written forms, in the centuries around the turn of the era, Sopara was a place that every tradition wanted a share of. The notes put the order plainly: all of these texts are much later than the events they describe, and the hard evidence starts with Ashoka’s edicts. [n01; Origin legends, Takeaway]

How we know so little

Before the anchor, a word on why the record before it is so thin, because the reasons are themselves part of the history of this coast.

The first reason is the sources. The Archaeological Survey’s annual review is the official register of Indian excavation and exploration, but of its 49 yearly volumes from 1953-54 onwards, about forty survive online only with an unreadable text layer. The research recovered a few entries by running its own character recognition over page images, and stopped well short of reading every volume; any Thana or Palghar entry in the years from 1961-62 onwards remains unchecked. [n24; Method note; Section 3, Gaps] Several of the papers that matter most, Garge and colleagues on the Konkan, Limaye and Kumaran on the northern Konkan mangroves, Tripati and Gaur on Sopara, were known only from abstracts or quotations because their hosts were unreachable. [n24; Method note] The book’s statements of absence are therefore statements about what could be read, not about everything that has ever been found.

The second reason is the work itself. The Konkan has seen little systematic archaeology, and the surveys that have been done on this coast have concentrated on ports, coins and monuments of the historic period: Elephanta, Kanheri, the Bassein fort, the coin hoards of Thana. [n24; Section 2, Cited Findings] The prehistoric record of the north Konkan is, in the notes’ phrase, “Mumbai-Salsette-centred and mostly undated”. [n24; Section 2, Inferences]

The third reason is the ground. Sopara has been lived on continuously, by Kale’s reckoning, for twenty-three centuries, and it is now a dense part of a city. Brancaccio concedes that excavation there is “especially difficult” because the site is built over, and describes the 1993 trial trenches as having uncovered disturbed sequences, with no secure stratigraphy. [n36; Section 1, Gaps; Cited Findings] The creeks have silted, the railway has cut the channel, and building has filled the rest. [n01; Shah et al. 2020] Whatever lay under the Mauryan town, if anything did, lies under two thousand years of later Sopara and a modern suburb on top of it.

And the fourth reason is the climate. The monsoon soaks the Konkan every year; the soils are acid laterites and tidal muds. Even the botanists who studied the Bombay mangroves found the mud too poor in organic matter and pollen to build a dated sequence. [n24; Vishnu-Mittre & Guzder] Bone, wood and seed do not survive easily here.

So the silence of these twenty-seven centuries has causes, and they are not the same as emptiness. That is why the book, following the notes, calls it “a gap in data, not proof that nobody lived here”. [n23; Section 6, Inferences]

With those causes in mind, the chapter turns to the anchor itself.

Aparanta: the western end

In the third century BCE the Mauryan empire, ruled from Pataliputra on the Ganges, became the largest state India had yet known. Its third emperor, Ashoka, reigned from about 268 to 232 BCE. After a bloody war in Kalinga, in the east, he turned to the teaching he called dhamma, and had it proclaimed in edicts carved on rocks and pillars across his realm, from the north-west frontier to the far south. [n24; Section 6, Takeaway]

In those edicts the western coast seems, for the first time, to have a name. Rock Edict V tells how Ashoka appointed officers of dhamma, and says, in the translation of the Buddhist scholar Dhammika, that they “work among the Greeks, the Kambojas, the Gandharas, the Rastrikas, the Pitinikas and other peoples on the western borders”. [n24; Dhammika, Wheel 386/387] Other scholars read the version at Girnar, in Gujarat, as naming the “Aparantas”, the people of Aparanta, “the western end”. [n24; Dhammika] [n23; Aparanta (Wikipedia); Major Rock Edicts (Wikipedia)] The readings vary from one copy to another. Rock Edict XIII, which lists the peoples on the empire’s edges, names the Greeks, Kambojas, Nabhakas, Nabhapamkits, Bhojas, Pitinikas, Andhras and Palidas, and does not name Aparanta. [n24; Dhammika]

The difference matters. If Edict V names Aparanta, then Ashoka named the Konkan coast as a region among his borderlands. If it says only “other peoples on the western borders”, as Dhammika renders it, the edict does not name the region at all. The notes conclude that the second reading would weaken the argument that Ashoka named the Konkan in Edict V, and that the fragments of the edicts actually found at Sopara are the firmer evidence. [n24; Earliest texts, Inferences] The research did not check Hultzsch’s text of the Girnar version. [n24; Earliest texts, Gaps]

Later sources fill in a Mauryan administration and a Buddhist mission. The Junagadh rock inscription of the Saka ruler Rudradaman, of about 150 CE, names a Yona, a Greek, called Tushaspha as governor under Ashoka, and the historian Romila Thapar (2001) is cited as making him governor of Aparanta. [n24; Wikipedia: Aparanta, citing Thapar 2001] The Mahavamsa tells that after the Third Buddhist Council, Ashoka’s teachers sent missionaries to the frontiers, and that the Yona elder Dhammarakkhita went to Aparantaka. [n24; Mahavamsa xii.5] The numbers it gives for his success differ between the notes’ sources: one summary reads the chronicle as saying that 37,000 were converted (Mahavamsa xii.34–36); another, from the 2020 Sopara study, that 70,000 heard him preach and over a thousand men and a thousand women were ordained. [n24; Wikipedia: Aparanta; Dharmaraksita] [n02; Shah 2020] The chronicles are Sri Lankan, written centuries later, and Ashoka’s own edicts do not mention the Third Council. [n24; Earliest texts, Cited Findings]

The 1882 Gazetteer drew the two lines of evidence together. The edict fragment found that year at Sopara, it wrote, “seems to show that it was the capital of Aparanta in B.C. 250, and makes it probable that the Yavana Dharmarakshita … came and preached the law in Sopara”. [n15; Thana Gaz. XIV, p. 319] [n24; Gazetteer Vol. XIV, pp. 319–320] Indraji believed Dhammarakkhita made Sopara his base. [n02; Shah 2020] It is an inference from a stone and a chronicle. But the stone is real.

Ashoka’s world reaches Sopara

The next chapter opens with the stone itself, a broken slab of basalt by the Bhatela pond, so this one need only tell how it came to light and what it means.

In April 1882 the scholar Bhagwanlal Indraji, working on the antiquities of Sopara for the Gazetteer, found a fragment of Ashoka’s Rock Edict VIII. [n24; Section 5, Cited Findings] Seventy-four years later a second piece turned up at Bhuigaon, near Sopara, and the Archaeological Survey’s review for 1955-56 reported it in words worth quoting in full, because they come from the primary record: “A broken part of an Asokan inscription was discovered by a school-boy and was brought to the notice of Shri N.A. Gore, Assistant Secretary and Librarian, Bombay Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society. On examination, it turned out to be a damaged version of the Ninth Edict, a part of the Eight Edict from the same place being already known. It is now almost certain that Sopara contained a complete set of the Rock-edicts of Asoka.” The review’s summary called it “the earliest” of that year’s epigraphic discoveries. [n24; IAR 1955-56] Some modern summaries call the Bhuigaon fragment a piece of Edict XI; the primary text says Ninth, and the book follows it. [n24; IAR 1955-56] [n22; Ashokan Prakrit at Sopara]

Edict VIII, the one found first, is the edict in which Ashoka describes how earlier kings went on pleasure tours, and how he had replaced them with tours of dhamma. [n24; Dhammika] It is, in a sense, an edict about travel. Its presence at Sopara means that someone carried Ashoka’s words to this island and had them cut in local basalt; whether the emperor’s officers, a governor of Aparanta, or Buddhist monks, the stone does not say.

Around the edicts, the archaeology of Sopara begins. Small fragments of Northern Black Polished Ware, the fine pottery of the Gangetic world, were reported from the 1993 trial trenches dug by the Archaeological Survey with the British Academy, though whether they came from excavated layers or from the surface is not clear. [n24; Section 5, Cited Findings] [n23; Section 6, Gaps] The Buruda Rajacha Kot, the great relic mound of Sopara, has been described as a stupa of the third century BCE in origin, though the relic casket found in it in 1882 belongs to the first or second century CE. [n01; Shah et al. 2020] [n36; Brancaccio 2023] On the strength of the edicts, the archaeologist Pia Brancaccio, citing Harry Falk, concludes that Sopara was “probably the oldest of the Ulhas ports”. [n36; Brancaccio 2023, p. 11] The researcher Siddharth Kale, who surveyed Vasai taluka, is quoted as saying that Sopara is apparently the only site in the Konkan with unbroken human habitation over the last 2,300 years; that is a remark reported in the press, not the result of a study. [n01; Shah et al. 2020] [n24; Section 5, Gaps]

The Jain tradition adds its own claim to this moment. Ashoka’s grandson Samprati is said to have protected Jain monks in the region; the 2020 study dates this to the second century BCE, where conventional chronology puts Samprati late in the third. It is tradition only. [n01; Shah et al. 2020] [n02; Gaps]

The rest of the Konkan was entering the record at about the same time. Inland, at Bhaja near the Bhor Ghat, a wooden rib of the Buddhist chaitya cave carries an inscription in characters of the second century BCE, which fixes the cave’s date. [n24; IAR 1955-56] At Ter on the plateau, the occupation with Northern Black Polished Ware begins about the fourth century BCE. [n24; IAR 1957-58] The southern road and the sea were about to meet at the foot of the Ghats.

Was Sopara a settlement before Ashoka? The notes weigh it carefully. Tripati and Gaur’s survey, as relayed by the 2020 study, is said to have found Sopara active from a “pre-Ashokan” phase, but the research could not read that paper, and the claim stays secondary. [n36; Section 1, Cited Findings] The National Institute of Oceanography’s onshore and nearshore survey of 1997, at Elephanta, Malvan, Vijaydurg, Sopara and Chaul, found coins and pottery “of different cultural periods”, and its abstract says nothing of prehistoric or pre-Mauryan finds. [n24; Tripati & Gaur 1997] No radiocarbon date or stratified layer earlier than the Mauryan period has been reported from Sopara. [n24; Section 5, Gaps] The “fifth to sixth century BC Sopara port” of popular accounts rests on later Pali, Buddhist Sanskrit and Jain texts, not on excavated levels. [n24; Section 5, Inferences] And yet, the notes add, “the stronger the case for Sopara as an Ashokan centre, the more likely earlier Iron Age use”; it is only that the Konkan’s own Iron Age is unexcavated. [n24; Section 5, Inferences] An emperor does not usually set up his edicts in an empty place. Who was there to read them, the evidence does not say.

What is not known

The silences of this chapter are, if anything, longer than those of the last. They are worth setting down together.

  • Farmers on the Konkan. The literature reports no Neolithic or Chalcolithic site anywhere on the Konkan, and no Jorwe site on the coast. Whether this is an absence of farmers or of archaeologists is not known. [n24; What is not known] [n23; Section 6, Gaps]
  • Early crops. No rice, millet or other crop evidence from the Konkan before the Early Historic period. [n24; Section 4, Gaps]
  • Harappan or Bronze Age contact. No Harappan, Mesopotamian or Bronze Age find on the Sopara–Vasai coast. The Ophir identification has no archaeology behind it. [n36; Section 1, Gaps] [n15; Inferences]
  • The 4.2 ka event here. No Konkan-specific record. The estuary cores cited for mid-second-millennium aridity are not named. [n23; Section 5, Gaps] [n24; Schug]
  • The Karal shell. The 3,250-year date near Nala Sopara has no published location or context. [n24; Gaps]
  • The Iron Age and megaliths. None reported from the north Konkan. [n24; Section 4, Gaps]
  • Ancient DNA. None from the Konkan, Mumbai or the Maharashtra coast; none of the large studies includes a person from Maharashtra. [n24; Section 7, Gaps]
  • Language. No evidence of what was spoken here before the Prakrit of the edicts. [n22; Gaps]
  • The texts. Whether the Sunaparanta of the Pali canon, the Aparanta of the edicts and chronicles, and the Shurparaka of the epics are the same place; the dates of the Mahabharata passages, the Jatakas and the Punna story. [n24; What is not known; Earliest texts, Gaps]
  • Sopara before Ashoka. No pre-Mauryan occupation layer; no primary report of the 1992–93 excavation; when Sopara became a port. [n24; What is not known]

The stone-cutter’s island

So, at the end of these twenty-seven centuries, the picture is this.

Across the mountains, farmers had come and gone. On the Gujarat coast, Harappan sailors had built harbours and traded with the Gulf. Ancestries had mixed across the subcontinent, and iron had come into use. In the Ganges valley, new cities had risen, the Buddha and Mahavira had taught, and a road had been built toward the south. On the coast of Vasai itself, the sea had stood near its present level, the creeks had begun, very slowly, to silt, and a shell on a dune near Nala Sopara had died about 3,250 years ago. That is all the Vasai-specific evidence there is. The people who must have lived on the island, fishing the creeks, perhaps growing a little grain, perhaps trading shell and salt up the Ghats, left nothing that has yet been found.

And then, some time in the middle of the third century BCE, a stone-cutter stood on the island of Sopara with a slab of the local basalt before him and a text in front of him that had come from the Ganges, and began to cut, letter by letter, in the Brahmi script, the words of the Beloved of the Gods. Some of what he cut would be found by a scholar beside the Bhatela pond in 1882, and some by a schoolboy at Bhuigaon in 1956.

With those words, the coast stops being only geology and inference. It has a name, a script, a language and a place in the history of an empire. The next chapter begins at that slab.
