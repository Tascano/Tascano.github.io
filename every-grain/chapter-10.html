Part Five — Marathas and the Company

After 1739 the walls became Bajipur, the Peshwas’ town, and in 1818 the Company took the coast. These four chapters follow the Maratha years, the British district, the sugar and plantain economy, the railway that made Virar and the plague that came with it, and the long movement toward independence.

Period covered: 1739 – 1818

In this chapter. The Marathas take the fort and rename it. This chapter follows the Hindu restoration, the Christians who stayed, General Goddard’s capture of the fort and the Treaty of Bassein of 1802.

Just inside the sea gate of the fort, on the left as you come up from the water, stands a small temple of Hanuman. Gerson da Cunha, who walked through the ruins in the 1870s, did not think much of it. He called it “mean architecture”. But he understood what it meant. It was a Maratha memento, he wrote, a sign that “the monkey-god has kept his ascendancy”. [n32; BG 38-39; DC 214]

Local tradition gives the temple a precise birthday: its image, with a carved moustache, is said to have been consecrated on 27 July 1739, about ten weeks after the Portuguese garrison marched out. [n10; durgbharari] The date is tradition, not document. But the place is well recorded. Anyone coming up from the river jetty passed the teak doors of 1720, still studded with their iron spikes, and the first sacred thing they met inside was no longer a cross. It was the god of strength and loyalty, the companion of Rama.

This chapter covers the seventy-nine years between that consecration and the day the British took the coast for good. It is a shorter record than the Portuguese one. The Portuguese wrote down their captains, their friars and their tombstones in Latin and Portuguese, and the nineteenth-century antiquarians who wrote the sources used here could read them. The Marathas kept their records in Modi script in the Peshwa’s archives at Pune. Those archives hold, by one quoted estimate, about four crore pages, and little of that has reached this book. [n22; Wikipedia: Modi script; Free Press Journal on Peshwa records] What survives in the notes is mostly in stone: temples, inscriptions, a fort, bells. Then, at the end, comes a treaty whose consequences reached far beyond this coast.

1739 – 1740: Bajipur

The conquerors gave the city a new name. Baçaim became Bajipur, “Baji Rao’s city”, after the Peshwa Baji Rao I in whose reign it fell. [n32; BG 34; n15; Thana Gaz. XIV, p. 34] It was the headquarters of a governor whose charge ran from the Bankot river in the south to Daman in the north. Da Cunha calls this province the Sar Subha, and its governor was the Sarsubhedar. [n32; BG 34; DC 149] The change of name came with a change of people at the top. The Portuguese administration left on 23 May 1739, the day the saffron flag went up. [n04; n18] With it went the landholders of the 1727–30 Tombo: the fidalgos and widows who held Gris, Sandor, Bolinj, Vatar, Mardes, Wagholi and Virar on three-life leases, the Jesuit procurators who held Chulne and Maroy, the captain who farmed the toddy rents. [n39; n40; Tombo] The capitulation gave them eight days. [n32; DC 148-149] The villages they had leased did not go anywhere. Their cultivators now owed their dues to the Peshwa’s officers instead of to an absent Portuguese lessee, and the new state rewarded its own allies with patilships and inams on the same land. [n41; Enthoven III, p. 161] No document of that transfer, village by village, has been found. [n34, Gaps]

The forms of the name tell the history of the place: Basai under the Gujarat sultans, Baçaim under the Portuguese, Bajipur or Bajipura under the Marathas, then Bassein under the British, and finally Vasai. [n22; Wikipedia: Vasai] In this chapter the town is Bajipur when the Marathas are speaking, and Bassein when the British are. For the land and its people, this book uses Vasai.

The first Maratha governor was Shankarji Keshav Phadke, the first subhedar (Sarsubhedar) of Bassein. [n04; Wikipedia: Vajreshwari Temple; n25] The Gazetteer calls him “the celebrated Maratha Sarsubhedar of Bassein”. [n10; Gazetteer 1882, p. 367] Some modern sources call him Shankarji Pant, the officer who had urged Chimaji Appa to attack Arnala and who then built its new fort. [n13; Wikipedia: Arnala fort] His exact dates are not documented in any source this book used. [n25, Gaps] But his name is on more of Vasai’s sacred landscape than any other person’s, and he is the real protagonist of this chapter’s first half.

A naval town and a Brahman town

Bajipur was more than a temple-builder’s seat. It was a naval station. After the death of the great admiral Kanhoji Angre in 1729, the Maratha fleet had split, and the Peshwa’s share of it, according to Wikipedia’s summary, was headquartered at Vasai. [n36; Wikipedia: Kanhoji Angre] The Gazetteer confirms the shape of this from the English side: “Bassein was made the head-quarters of the governor”, and around 1780 “the governor of Bassein, one of the Peshwa’s admirals”, was known for attacking English ships. [n36; Gaz XIII-II pp. 498-499, 504 n.3] Most Maratha vessels were built of Bombay-area teak. The notes find no record of Peshwa-era shipbuilding at Vasai itself; the yards of Agashi and Bassein, so busy under the Portuguese, fall silent in the record until 1837, when the Gazetteer notes that “very good fishing boats and coasting craft” were again being built at Agashi and Sopara. [n36; Gaz XIV pp. 2, 323, Gaps] The heritage survey of 2008 suggests that ship-building declined at Bassein and Agashi as “modern shipping requires newer technology” and the ports silted, and that the district turned to agriculture. [n40; CRIT Vol I pp. 6-8]

It was also a town of officials, and the officials were Brahmans. The Gazetteer gives one family history that shows how the new administration was staffed. Two Brahmans of the Kudale family migrated to the north Konkan about 1728, while the coast was still Portuguese. One settled at Thana and was made a Sardar. The other, Yeshwantrao, settled in Bassein, and his son became Atrekar, a clerk or karkun, of Bassein fort. [n35; Gaz XIII-I p. 63 n.2] Whole villages were reorganised around the new order. Papdi, “a village about a cannon-shot from Bajipura”, was by da Cunha’s time “chiefly inhabited by Brahmans of Chitpawan, Karada, and Deshastha divisions, as well as Palshas, Sonars, and other inferior castes of the Hindus”, and yielded an annual revenue of Rs 1,80,630. [n40; DC p. 157] The heritage survey adds that Papdi’s present fabric “shows palimpsests from the 18th century when it was given as a jagir to the community which transported goods, supplies and food to the Maratha army”. [n40; CRIT Vol II, Papdy Talav Precinct] The survey does not name that community. The research notes suggest it may have been the cart-owning Christian Vadvals whom the 1882 Gazetteer describes as the district’s best carters, but that is an inference, not a finding. [n40, B2 Notable]

At Agashi, the old shipbuilding port, the Marathas made a local base after 1737. Its heritage precinct still holds the Khanolkar House, described as a Peshwa guest house, and until about 2019 it held the Phadkewada, the mansion built by “Subhedar Phadke”, which the survey records as “lost to high-rise construction”. [n40; CRIT Vol I items 43-46] Chalpeth Road at Agashi was lined with Maratha-era houses. [n40; Heritage Chronicles 2019] Those buildings were the nearest thing the Maratha province had to a court: not a palace, but a governor’s family mansion and a guest house beside a temple tank.

The conqueror did not live to see much of his conquest. Chimaji Appa died on 17 December 1740, about nineteen months after the surrender. One source gives 1741. [n04; Wikipedia: Chimaji Appa] He was the younger brother of the Peshwa and had led the Konkan campaign, and with it he had ended Portuguese rule north of Goa. [n25]

The rewards of the siege

Before he died, the Peshwa’s court paid its debts to the local men who had helped. The best documented are the Pachkalshis, the farming caste whose Bassein branch were the Vadvals. Three government texts, written decades apart, agree on the reward. The Bombay City Gazetteer of 1909 says: “As late as 1739 AD two Panchkalshis, named Bugaji and Gangaji Naik, were granted the village of Anjur in inam with the title of Naik Sarpatel by the Peshwa in return for help accorded to the Marathas against the Portuguese.” [n41; Bombay City Gaz. I, p. 238] Enthoven, in 1922, adds that “the Naik family of Anjore, among others, obtained the title of Sarpatil and received inam lands from the Peshwa’s Court”, and that “to the siege of Bassein and other events of the great Maratha-Portuguese struggle are traced back the patelships of many families of the Vadval division”. [n41; Enthoven III, p. 161] The 1882 Thana Gazetteer, in a footnote by the Collector W. B. Mulock, says the Peshwa made one family chief Patils of Salsette, another proprietors of Angaon in Bhiwandi, and a third proprietors of Anjar in Bhiwandi. Its date, “1743”, is almost certainly a slip for 1739. [n41; Gaz XIII-I p. 130 n.]

Anjur lies in Bhiwandi taluka, east of the Ulhas, not on the island of Bassein. [n40; B12] But the patilships matter for Vasai’s own villages. A patil was a village headman, responsible for revenue and order, and the office was hereditary and honoured. The reward to the Vadval families was not a caste title; it was patilships and inam, rent-free land. That reward underlies the landed standing of Vadval patil families in the generations that followed. [n41, Inferences] The Peshwa’s original sanads, the grant deeds, are cited from the Peshwa archives in a Marathi edition of 1935 but have not been seen by this book’s researchers. [n41, Gaps]

That 1935 edition of the Sashtichi Bakhar, edited by Gajanan Govind Naik of Anjur, quotes an older source naming “Gangaji Naik and Chuba Naik of Anjur”, and lists helpers from many communities, among them Somvanshi Kshatriya Chaudharis of Umele, Raut men, Bhandaris, Kolis, Agris and Christians. [n41; Naik 1935, pp. 15, 320 ff.] The editor himself disputes whether Gangaji Naik belonged to the “Pathare Kshatriya” or “Somvanshi Kshatriya” community, a sign of how much these honours still mattered to caste identity two centuries later. [n41, Inferences] The Wikipedia article on the Pachkalshis adds that the Chogle family built ships for the Maratha navy and was rewarded with land near Borivali; that detail is unsourced and should be held loosely. [n41; Wikipedia: Pachkalshi]

For the Pachkalshis the conquest was therefore a rise in fortune. The 1882 Gazetteer’s phrase for it, “the disturbances of the eighteenth century aroused the old warlike spirit of the Pachkalshis”, is a colonial compliment, but the land and headmanships were real. [n41; Gaz XIII-I p. 130 n.]

The English at Vasai, June–July 1739

The English of Bombay had sold the Portuguese powder and grenades during the siege, according to Kelkar. After the fall, the Bombay Board sent Bhiku Shenvi to congratulate Chimaji Appa with velvet and cloth, and then Captain Inchbird to negotiate. According to the Peshwa’s day-book, Inchbird was at Vasai for 23 days, from 20 June to 13 July Old Style (1 to 24 July New Style). [n63 §7] Chimaji set aside Inchbird’s draft and sent nine articles of his own, among them that the English must not talk to Shahu without the Peshwa’s consent, and that ships wrecked on the Maratha coast belonged to the Marathas. Inchbird wrote to the Company that the Marathas meant to take Chaul and Daman as they had taken Vasai, and urged Bombay to begin fortifying at once: the Marathas, he thought, resented English naval power and would find a pretext to destroy it. [n63 §7]

The treaty was agreed at Vasai on 12 July Old Style (23 July New Style) between Chimaji and Inchbird, and accepted by the Bombay Government on 20 July Old Style. Kelkar’s list has eleven articles. [n63 §8] The English were to give passes to Bombay merchants but not to foreign ships, and foreign ships bearing Maratha passes were not to be hindered. Ships without the Peshwa’s pass were not to carry goods and cattle, and the Peshwa’s fleet could seize those that did, unless the Bombay men proved the cargo theirs. People of the lands from Vasai to Daman who had taken shelter in Bombay in the war, labourers, carpenters or any others, could go home with their property and boats. The Peshwa’s fishing boats between Mahim and Versova were to have two English warboats as escort. The English could sell the Marathas iron, lead, saltpetre, sulphur and tar, but not guns, powder, hand grenades or shot, at no extra duty. The English kept what they had won from the Portuguese between Bombay and Mahim and no more, and fishing and trading boats in the creek were to go unhindered. Neither side was to help the other’s enemies, “even a friend”. Fugitive debtors could be pursued across the border, but slaves were to be returned. Wrecked vessels were to be helped; Company ships wrecked on Maratha shores would share half the cargo with the Marathas, and the same rule applied the other way. And the Marathas would not stop any vessel within a kos of the boundary post buried at Mahim. [n63 §8]

It was a treaty of neighbours, not of allies, and it fixed a border in the creek between Bombay and the Maratha coast. It also shows what the Marathas now held: the island of Bassein and its ports, with a fleet to enforce passes. The next chapter returns to the creek, and to the English. [n63]

The bells

From the churches of the conquered province, the Marathas took the bells. They were carried off as trophies of war. A later account says they went on elephants. [n04; n09; Goanet 2020] They hang today in Hindu temples across Maharashtra, far from the sea. No one knows how many there were, and the local researchers who have traced them say one or two are still being sought. [n09; Vasaiker, “Bells of Vasai Fort”]

The list, as far as the notes take it:

  • Naroshankar Temple, Panchavati, Nashik. The “Naroshankarachi Ghanta”, about 2.5 feet high and 10.25 feet round. It is said to have been taken in 1739 not from Vasai but from the church of Our Lady of the Immaculate Conception at Mount Poinsur, Borivali. [n09; Vasaiker]
  • Bhimashankar Temple (Bhorgiri, near Khed). Cast in 1729, with a cross and an image of Jesus. A plaque says it arrived in 1729, which is wrong: it came after 1739. [n09; Vasaiker]
  • Meneshwar Temple, Menavali (near Wai). Cast in 1707. A 650-kilogram five-metal (panchadhatu) bell bearing a relief of the Madonna and Child. [n09; Vasaiker; Atlas Obscura]
  • Durga Devi Temple, Murud. A bell said to come from Vasai fort. [n09; Vasaiker]
  • Baneshwar Temple, Pune. Cast in 1683, with a cross. [n09; Vasaiker]
  • Bhairavnath Temple, Loni Bhapkar (Baramati). A church bell, known from a photograph. [n09; Vasaiker]
  • Nimgaon Khandoba (Pune district). A bell with a crucifix. [n09; Vasaiker]
  • Ballaleshwar, Pali (Raigad), one of the Ashtavinayak temples. One bell is said to hang here, though it is not in the main list. [n09; Goanet 2020]
  • Khandoba temple, Jejuri, and Tulja Bhavani temple, Osmanabad, as reported by the Hindustan Times in December 2018 and cited by Wikipedia. [n04; Battle of Vasai, Wikipedia]

Wikipedia’s article on Chimaji Appa says that he dedicated church bells to the Vajreshwari temple, the goddess of his vow; that article was not read in full, and no bell at Vajreshwari appears in the Vasaiker list. [n41; Wikipedia: Chimaji Appa] Other bells are claimed for temples at Ramlinga, Bhuleshwar and Shirval, but this book could not verify them. [n09, Gaps] The inscriptions on the bells have not been transcribed in any source reached, and no bell has yet been traced to a particular church inside the fort. [n09, Inferences; n04, Gaps]

The dates cast into the metal are the best evidence the bells carry: 1683, 1707, 1729. They were made in the last half-century of Portuguese rule, in the decades of Fryer, Careri and Hamilton, and were rung for mass in Christian churches on the coast. Now they are rung for aarti in temples on the Deccan plateau. Nobody melted them down. They kept their crosses and their Madonna, and they now serve another god’s house.

1739 – 1760: Rebuilding the gods

When the Portuguese took the coast in the 1530s, they pulled down temples and mosques. In Baçaim itself, the Gazetteer records, “certain Musalman mosques were pulled down and in their place a cathedral of St. Joseph was built.” [n10; Gazetteer 1882, Bassein] At Nirmal, “the Portuguese had pulled down the temples and destroyed the ling.” [n10; Gazetteer 1882, pp. 292-293] The great goddess of Gunj had lost her temple, and her worship had been moved inland. [n10; Gazetteer 1882, p. 105]

After 1739 the Marathas rebuilt, and the 1882 Gazetteer is the best dated record of what they built. A pattern runs through it. Nearly every major Hindu shrine in Vasai tells a story from the Puranas, a landscape shaped by Parashurama. Nearly every one was destroyed under the Portuguese. And nearly every one was rebuilt between about 1739 and 1760 under Shankarji Keshav, with Peshwa money and, later, British grants that kept it going. [n10, Takeaway] Most of the exact “founding dates” given today by temples and blogs are tradition. The Gazetteer’s dates are the ones that can be checked. [n10]

Vajreshwari

The first temple belongs to the story of the siege. At Vadavli, on a spur of the Gumtara range in the Tansa valley (in Bhiwandi taluka, outside Vasai), the temple of Vajrabai or Vajreshwari stands at the top of a flight of steps above hot springs. Tradition says the springs are the blood of a demon the goddess killed. [n10; Gazetteer 1882, pp. 373-374]

The Gazetteer records that the goddess’s original temple stood at Gunj, six miles to the north, and “after its destruction by the Portuguese, was removed to Vadavli”. [n10; Gazetteer 1882, p. 105] The new temple was “said to have been built about the beginning of the last century by the well-known Shankarji Keshav, the Peshwa’s Sarsubhedar, to commemorate a victory over the Portuguese”. [n10; Gazetteer 1882, pp. 373-374] The phrase “beginning of the last century” is loose; the man and the victory place it after 1739. Wikipedia gives the completion as 1739 and names Chimaji Appa as its creator, after the vow he is said to have made while camped at Vadavli. [n04; n10; Wikipedia: Vajreshwari Temple] One account says Chimaji ordered his new subhedar, Shankar Keshav Phadke, to build it. [n10; search summary] That would reconcile the two traditions, but it rests on a single weak source.

The image itself, in 1882, was “a rude stone female figure holding… a short Roman-looking sword”. [n10; Gazetteer 1882, pp. 373-374] The temple was richly endowed. It held six inam (rent-free) villages, three of them in Bassein. Later a ruler of Baroda, Khanderao Gaikwad, added a timber hall with a picture gallery and a grant of Rs 450. By 1882 its Chaitra (April) fair drew about five thousand people “of all castes, from Thana, Bombay, Poona, and Nasik”. [n10; Gazetteer 1882, pp. 373-374] The Gazetteer also notes that Vajreshvari “is also the name of a Buddhist goddess”, a hint of older layers beneath the Maratha one. [n10; Gazetteer 1882, p. 373 n.]

The Marathi Vishwakosh gives a third version. Chimaji Appa, it says, meant to build the temple of Vajreshwari with its Vajrayoginis, but did not finish it; Nanasaheb Peshwa completed it in 1753–54. [n62] That is the only account that gives a specific later date and a named builder. It sits between the Gazetteer’s loose “beginning of the last century” for Shankarji Keshav and Wikipedia’s completion in 1739. The encyclopedia does not say whether it means the temple at Vadavli, in Bhiwandi taluka, or one in Vasai fort; it lists the Maruti and Shiva temples in the fort separately. [n62]

Kelkar, working from the Peshwa’s day-books, gives the vow itself. Chimaji, he says, vowed during the campaign to build a temple to Vajrayogini of Vajreshwari. On 6 August 1739 Old Style (17 August New Style) the day-book records Rs 1,283 and 2 annas spent on the gold vowed to the goddess at Vadavli, paid through Ramchandragiri Gosavi and others. Chimaji died soon after, without building the temple, and his nephew Nanasaheb Peshwa fulfilled the vow; the entries are in Nanasaheb’s day-book. A note in the day-books records a levy for the building on soldiers, clerks, boatmen and others at a fraction of a rupee a head (the OCR is unclear). [n63 §7] That is the source behind the Marathi Vishwakosh’s “1753–54”. [n62; n63]

Some modern guides say Chimaji Appa built a Vajreshwari temple inside Vasai fort. The Gazetteer’s 1882 list of temples in the fort does not include one. [n10, Conflict]

Nirmal

Six miles north of the fort lies Nirmal. The Gazetteer called it “one of the most sacred places in the district, having a much venerated ling, and being, according to tradition, the burying place of one of the great Shankaracharyas”. [n10; Gazetteer 1882, pp. 292-293] Its founding legend, the Nirmal Mahatmya, tells how the demon Vimal harassed Brahmans near the Vaitarna. Parashurama could not kill him, because Shiva kept restoring his limbs, and won only with Shiva’s help. The pardoned demon fell, and Parashurama set up a linga there called Vimaleshvar. “From Vimal it came to be called Nirmal or stainless.” [n10; Gazetteer 1882, p. 292 n.]

The Gazetteer then gives the Maratha history in three sentences:

“The Portuguese had pulled down the temples and destroyed the ling. On the fall of Bassein (1739) Nirmal was purified, prints of the feet of Shri Dattatraya took the place of the ling, and a reservoir was built.” And: “There are eight temples at Nirmal all built about 1750 by Shankarji Keshav, Sarsubhedar of Bassein.” [n10; Gazetteer 1882, pp. 292-293]

The third sentence is the one this book finds most striking: “The stones which the Portuguese had built into Christian churches were probably used to raise the temples of the older gods.” [n10; Gazetteer 1882, pp. 292-293] The word “probably” matters, and the claim cannot be pushed further than the Gazetteer pushes it. But the image is hard to forget. Two centuries before, Portuguese builders had used the stones of temples for their churches; in Thana, the Franciscan António do Porto is said to have raised a church “out of the stones of twelve temples”. [n25; TG-XIV] Now the stones may have gone back the other way. Elsewhere in the taluka, carved temple stones were still built into Christian houses at Bolinj and Gass in 1882. [n11, Inferences] As rulers changed, the same stones changed hands.

The eight temples the Gazetteer listed in 1882, with their yearly grants, show how carefully the Maratha state had endowed them, and how the British had kept the endowments going. There was the Shankaracharya Swami (Rs 421 a year), Mahavishnu (Rs 118), Vimaleshvar Mahadev, “a large substantial building” (Rs 100), Bhuteshvar west of the lake (Rs 60), Ganpati, Durga and Hanuman east of the lake (Rs 35), and Siddheshvar Swami north of the lake. That last was supported with Rs 25 a year by Shankar Keshav’s own descendants. There were alms-houses for Brahmans (Rs 454) and for Bairagi ascetics (Rs 148). The Gazetteer reads these state grants as showing that “the temples were built from public not from private funds”. [n10; Gazetteer 1882, p. 293]

Modern tradition tells the Nirmal story differently. It credits Chimaji Appa with renovating the samadhi temples, sometimes “on the guidance of Swami Vidya Shankara Bharati”, and says Brahmin families were resettled there: Konkanastha, Karhade, Devrukhe and four Gujarati Shukla Yajurvedi families. [n03; n10; Heritage Chronicles] These are blog-level accounts, and on the builder they conflict with the Gazetteer, which credits Shankarji Keshav. [n11] The caretakers today are the Pandya family, Gujarati Brahmins. [n10; Heritage Chronicles 2019]

What makes Nirmal unusual is that the restoration did not drive the Christians out. The village kept its Christian parish of the Holy Cross, the mother parish of the Samvedi Christians, whose descendants are called Kuparis today. The Portuguese church had been destroyed in the war, in 1737 by Hull’s account or in 1739 by the plaque on its bell tower. The plaque adds a detail that tells what the next century was like for the parish: “From 1739 to 1856 the present Cemetery was pro tempore their Church.” [n40; EI Nirmal; n38; Hull I, p. 5] So for 117 years, while Shankarji Keshav’s eight temples rose around the lake and their grants were paid, the Christians of Nirmal heard mass in their graveyard. The parishioners rebuilt their church in 1856, at their own cost. [n40; Gaz XIV p. 293]

By the 1880s the Hindu and Christian faces of the village sat side by side in the same Gazetteer entry. Nirmal’s Kartik fair drew six or seven thousand pilgrims, “Hindus, Musalmans, Christians, and a few Parsis”, and its stalls sold brass and copper vessels, dried plantains, sweetmeats, cloth and cattle. [n40; Gaz XIV p. 292] The research notes call this a documented coexistence, not just a remembered one: Hindu temples, Marathi Brahman officials and a Christian parish shared one village from 1739 onward. [n34, Inferences]

The Gazetteer’s general verdict on the district places Nirmal in a wider pattern: “On the decline of Portuguese power (1740) Brahmanism revived, and except those that are more modern, most of the present Hindu temples date from the eighteenth century.” [n37; Gaz XIII-I p. 66]

Tungareshwar

To the east, on the long ridge of Tungar hill above the Bassein creek, stood another holy place with a demon in its story. In the Tungar Mahatmya, which belongs to the Padma Purana, the same Vimal is driven into the sea by Parashurama but settles on Mount Tungar. There he wins Shiva’s favour by penance and receives immortality, “and had his hill honoured by a holy pool and a lingam”. [n10; Gazetteer 1882, p. 366 n.]

In 1882 the temple was reached from Satavli through the Varli hamlet of Dhondvira. It was “a group of four square buildings… said to be of great age, and were rebuilt about a hundred and thirty years ago by Shankarji Keshav, the celebrated Maratha Sarsubhedar of Bassein”. That puts the rebuilding at about 1750. Under a canopy with Nandi were Dattatreya’s footprints. [n10; Gazetteer 1882, p. 367] “A plot of ground about three miles square was granted to the shrine by the Peshwa,” the Gazetteer adds, and the British continued it. [n10; n12; Gazetteer 1882, p. 367]

Agashi

At Agashi, the old shipbuilding port north of the island where Antonio do Porto had founded his orphanage, the Gazetteer records “a large temple of Bhavanishankar which was built in A.D. 1691 (Shak 1613) by Shankarji Keshav Phadke”, with a grant of Rs 50. Close to it lay “a holy bathing place, or tirth, supposed to cure skin diseases”. A second temple, to Hanuman, “was built by the same Shankarji Keshav”. [n10; Gazetteer 1882, p. 2]

The date cannot be right as printed. In 1691 Agashi was Portuguese, and Shankarji Keshav governed only after 1739. The printed “Shak 1613” may be an OCR or printing error. One of this book’s notes suggests Shak 1673 (A.D. 1751), but that is a guess, not a reading. [n10, Inferences; n25] A local heritage site dates the Bhavanishankar temple to 1739–1750 and calls Shankaraji Keshav Phadke Chimaji Appa’s commander for the Agashi–Arnala region. It says the temple stands on the site of an older fort named in the Mahikavati Bakhar, and that large stones and old idols were found when the lake was dug. [n11; Durg Bharari] A blog gives “c. 1691” for the Shiva and Hanuman temples, repeating the Gazetteer’s problem. [n11; Heritage Chronicles] And another modern account says the tank was built in 1691 “by a relation of the ruler of Miraj”, after a Patwardhan of Sangli was told in a dream to build it and bathe there for six months. [n10; Heritage Chronicles] The conflict is unresolved. The 2008 heritage survey labels the Bhavani Shankar and Hanuman temples “18th century, patron Peshwa”, which fits the post-1739 reading better than the printed 1691. [n40; CRIT Vol III assets 43a, 43d]

At Agashi, too, there is a memorial to the builder himself. The same local site says the hero stone and samadhi of Shankaraji Phadke and his relatives lay neglected for more than a century, until volunteers led by Dr Shridutt Raut cleared them. [n11; Durg Bharari] It is the nearest thing to a grave that the notes give him.

Arnala and the fort

On the island of Arnala, the Maratha fort that Baji Tulaji had rebuilt in 1737 held two temples by 1882. Trimbakeshvar’s received a yearly government grant of Rs 45, and Bhavani’s Rs 91. Inside the same walls stood the tomb of two Muslim saints, Shahali and Hajiali, endowed with Rs 80 a year, beside “a stone-lined eight-cornered pond and many wells”. [n10; n11; Gazetteer 1882, Arnala Fort] The notes do not say when the saints lived or when their tomb was built. In 1882 a Hindu fort held both a Shiva temple and a dargah, each with its government grant.

Inside Vasai fort the Gazetteer found, besides the Hanuman temple at the sea gate, a temple of Trivikram. Its yearly government allowance of Rs 1,061 was the largest temple grant in the taluka. There was also “a modern Hindu temple of Mahadev” beside the ruined Misericórdia. [n10; n32; BG 37, 40] A local site adds Shri Nageshwar, destroyed under the Portuguese and rebuilt after the conquest beside the old Nagesh Mahatirtha pond, and a newer Hanuman temple at the Bhui Darwaza. [n10; durgbharari] The notes give no date for the Trivikram temple or for the Nageshwar rebuilding. Mrs Heber, writing in the 1820s, saw a pagoda in the fort “with the sacred bull well carved at its entrance”. [n32; DC 211]

The god of the footprints

A small thread runs through these rebuildings. At Nirmal after 1739, the footprints of Dattatreya took the place of the destroyed linga. At Tungareshwar, Dattatreya’s footprints sat under the Nandi canopy. And at the Chakreshvar lake in Sopara, an eleventh- or twelfth-century image of Brahma, dug out of a field about 1850, “is still worshipped as Dattatraya” in 1882. [n10; Gazetteer 1882, pp. 293, 338, 367] One of this book’s notes suggests that the footprints, which carry no image, may have been a deliberate way of reconsecrating sites the Marathas regarded as polluted. That is a hypothesis for a historian to test, not a finding. [n10, Inferences]

Shrines in the villages

The great temples carry the Gazetteer’s dates. Smaller shrines across the villages carry only the heritage survey’s period labels, but together they show how far the Maratha restoration reached into ordinary places. In Sandor, the Christian Vadval village whose church of St Thomas had survived the war, the survey lists a Dev Talav and a Hanuman Mandir, “both 18th century, Maratha era”. [n40; CRIT Vol I items 99-103] In Papdi the Ram Mandir, rebuilt in the early twentieth century, stood on the site of an original whose building “marked Maratha dominance 1739-1818”. [n40; CRIT Vol I items 36-42] On Arnala a Ram Mandir is dated 1806. [n40; CRIT Vol III asset 51] The Muslims of the coast built too. At Pali, a village of Christian farmers, the survey records a sixteenth-century dargah with a step-well and an eighteenth-century masjid in a mixed style of Islamic, Portuguese and wada features. [n40; CRIT Vol I items 107-109; Vol III] The Gazetteer’s entry for Arnala fort, with its Shiva temple and its tomb of the two Muslim saints, each on a government grant, belongs to the same world.

None of these survey labels is a document, and they should be read as period estimates. [n40, Gaps] But the pattern they make is clear. Under the Peshwas, Hindu shrines returned to Christian villages, and the Christian churches that survived stood beside them.

Christians under the Peshwas

What became of the Christians of Vasai, the converts and their descendants who made up nearly all of the province’s counted population in the early 1700s? [n15; Thana Gaz. XIV, p. 33]

The first version of this chapter began with an admission that the capitulation’s religious clauses had not been found. Newer research has found them, in Hull’s Bombay Mission History of 1927, which summarises the terms from an earlier collection of documents. Noble families, Christians who wished to leave, all the religious orders and the priests were given free passage. The Christians who stayed were to “enjoy liberty of worship”. The privileges of three churches in Bassein, one in the district and one in Salsette, were preserved, with their curates “subject to the Primate of India”, the Archbishop of Goa. And the images in the churches were to remain. [n37; n38; Hull I, pp. 75-76, citing Materials I, p. 213] The Gazetteer confirms the number: the Portuguese secured “only five churches, three in Bassein city, one in Bassein district and one in Salsette”. [n37; Gaz XIII-I p. 203] The printed treaty in the Bombay Quarterly Review has still not been read in full, so the exact wording of these clauses remains second-hand. [n32, Gaps]

Who left, and who stayed

The terms decided who would go. “All the Portuguese ‘reinóis’ and ‘descendentes’ fled or were expelled,” Mendiratta writes, meaning both the Portuguese-born and the locally born of Portuguese descent, “leaving only the Indian converted population behind.” [n34; Mendiratta pp. 216-218] The Jesuit traveller Tieffenthaler, who passed in 1750, put it in one sentence: the Portuguese nobles and all the regular clergy left for Goa; “the ordinary people remained, a native priest taking charge of their souls with the approbation of the Mahrattas”. Most of the families who could afford to, he added, later left for Bombay. [n38; n37; Hull I, p. 76]

So the fidalgos of the 1727–30 Tombo, the Pereiras and Melos and Meneses with their three-life leases, went south. The friars and the Jesuits went with them. What remained were the people who had worked those leased villages: the Christian Samvedis of the northern gardens, ancestors of the Kuparis; the Christian Vadvals of Sandor, Manickpur and Chulne; the Koli fishermen of Mercês and the coast; Bhandaris, Kunbis and others. Mendiratta describes the Indian Christians who stayed in Salsette as “overwhelmingly either fisherfolk or farmers, mostly of Koli, Kunbi or Bhandari castes”. [n34; Mendiratta p. 218] The Bassein countryside was the same mix with a larger share of Samvedi and Vadval gardeners.

How many were they? Anquetil-Duperron and Tieffenthaler, as Hull reports them, say the town of Bassein “never counted more than 3,500 inhabitants”, though 30,000 lived in the country around. [n37; Hull I, p. 76] Early in the nineteenth century, when the British took the Bassein subdivision, a British report counted “eight functioning churches and a Catholic population estimated at 5000”. [n34; Mendiratta p. 219, citing Further Papers 1828-36] Set against the 58,131 native Christians that da Cunha and the Gazetteer give for the province around 1720, those figures suggest a very large fall. [n15; n36; Thana Gaz. XIV, p. 33; DC pp. 145-146] But the areas counted are not the same (the 1720 figure covers a whole province reaching to Karanja, the later one only the Bassein subdivision), and none of these numbers is a census. The honest conclusion is that the Christian population shrank, by flight, by reconversion and by the loss of Salsette and Bombay from the count, without the sources allowing anyone to say by how much. [n34, Inferences]

The priests who stayed

That the community survived at all is owed to a decision the Portuguese had made two centuries earlier: to train Indian priests. The Marathas, Mendiratta says, accepted only non-white priests, so Goan priests, ordained by the secular clergy or by the Oratorians, took over the parishes. Reorganisation began in 1740 from Kurla, in Salsette, where the Vicar-General of the North now had his seat. [n34; Mendiratta pp. 216-218; n38; Hull I, p. 6] The Gazetteer gives the result: under a Vicar-General at Kurla, “the native or … Kanarin vicars managed the churches and kept the bulk of the people from forsaking Christianity”. [n34; n37; Gaz XIII-I p. 203] Kanarin was the Portuguese word for these Indian priests, and the Gazetteer notes that it “was considered a term of reproach”. [n34; Gaz XIII-I p. 203 n.1] The men the Portuguese had looked down on were the ones who held the parishes together.

Jurisdiction stayed with Goa. The capitulation had made the curates “subject to the Primate of India”, and Bassein’s parishes remained under the Archbishop of Goa through the Padroado, the Portuguese Crown’s right of patronage over the Church in its old territories. [n38; Hull I, pp. 75-76] That would matter later. When English Bombay fell into a long quarrel between priests loyal to Goa and priests appointed by Rome’s Propaganda Fide, with a division of the Bombay churches in 1794 that created “two rival jurisdictions”, Maratha Bassein stood outside it. [n38; Catholic Encyclopedia, Goa; Inferences] Its parishes stayed with Goa until 1886. [n38]

What the notes do not record is any name. No Kanarin vicar of a Bassein parish between 1739 and 1818 is named in the sources this book used. [n04; n25, Gaps]

The end of the Inquisition’s reach

One thing left with the Portuguese: the Inquisition’s machinery in the north. Its commissary had worked from the Jesuit college inside the fort, and its prisoners had waited in the jail near the Captain’s palace for the fleet to Goa. [n37; DC pp. 235-236] With the fort in Maratha hands, the commissary, the jail and the fleet were gone. For the Christian villages, that was a real change. The Inquisition had tried baptised people for straying from orthodoxy, and the secret offerings to Ganpati and Gauri that the Gazetteer later heard of in outlying villages would once have been its business. [n34; n37; Gaz XIII-I pp. 202, 207-210] Under the Peshwas, no court was looking.

The Goa Inquisition itself was suppressed in 1774, restored around 1779, and finally abolished in 1812. [n37; DC p. 235; Gaz XIII-II p. 463 n.3] One case of these years is recorded on a stone. A Goan scholar in 1840 found a stone of 1786 marking the site of a razed house whose family, “dogmatists of the said sect”, had been “burnt in the Auto de Fé celebrated on December 30, 1747”, their houses “demolished and ploughed with salt”. Priolkar, who reports it, connects it with Bassein, but the notes do not identify the family or say where the stone stood, and a Bassein case eight years after the Maratha conquest is hard to explain unless the family lived in territory still under Portugal. [n37; Priolkar pp. 60-61, Gaps] It is best read as a sign of what the Inquisition still did elsewhere, not as evidence about Maratha Bassein.

The pilgrimage it had banned came back. At the healing tank near Our Lady of Remedies, Hindus once again came to bathe, and by da Cunha’s time they held a jatra there. [n37; DC p. 163] A church and a Hindu pilgrimage stood side by side again, as the church and the temples did at Nirmal.

Churches destroyed. The churches of the conquered coast were looted and unroofed, and their bells carried off. [n09; Vasaiker; Wikipedia: Vasai Fort] Our Lady of Mercy at Mercês, founded in 1606, was destroyed in 1739 and its priests fled. [n09; Goanet 2020; Vasaiker] St Bonaventure at Erangal, across the creek, built in 1575, was destroyed in 1739 and not repaired until the 1950s. [n09; n07; FPJ 2026] The great churches inside the fort were never rebuilt. A century later, travellers found them roofless under trees. Hull says the churches in the fort were “left to ruin”, while those in the district were, “within the last century”, rebuilt or restored. [n38; Hull I, p. 6] The parish histories add the countryside’s losses: St Michael’s at Porim (Manickpur), the Jesuit church of 1606, was “the first to be demolished by the conquering Maratha army”; St James at Agashi was burned; the Franciscans left Pali; and at Nirmal, as told above, the parish worshipped in its cemetery for more than a century. [n40; EI Manikpur; CRIT Vol III assets 44, 107; EI Nirmal]

Rebuilding. Some churches were rebuilt surprisingly soon. The heritage survey says St James at Agashi was “rebuilt 1760”, about twenty years after it burned. [n40; CRIT Vol III asset 44] At Manickpur a new church was built on the same site, “presumably in 1783”, a date taken from an old cross found by the parish historian Fr Conti; another source says it was built “immediately” on the old foundations, and the Gazetteer dates the church standing in 1882 to an 1851 rebuilding. [n40; EI Manikpur; CRIT Vol II asset 19a; Gaz XIV p. 37] The three dates cannot all be right. In Salsette, the Gazetteer adds, “many of the Salsette churches were built or rebuilt about this time (1760)”. [n37; Gaz XIII-I p. 203] Mercês, its Augustinian priest gone, was looked after by the vicar of Remedy, and had no vicar of its own until 1862. [n40; EI Merces] Papdi’s church, which had escaped destruction, was served after the Jesuits left and got its own vicar only in 1839. [n40; EI Papdy] Nandakhal, which had also escaped, received priests from Goa. [n40; Wikipedia: Holy Spirit Church Nandakhal] A Maratha government that permitted a burned church to be rebuilt within a generation was not trying to stamp the faith out.

Churches surviving. Not every church fell. The Holy Spirit church at Nandakhal, built by Franciscans in 1573, survived the attacks of 1737–39, and is therefore called the oldest surviving church in North Vasai. [n09; Wikipedia: Holy Spirit Church Nandakhal] Most of the other village parishes of the Portuguese foundation (Nirmal, Remedi, Sandor, Agashi, Papdi, Pali, Manickpur and Mercês) are still in use today, though most of their present buildings date from British-era rebuilding. [n09; n11; Gaz. 1882 pp. 36-37] So the parishes survived even where the buildings did not.

Pressure and taxes. Wikipedia’s summary of the historian Machado (1999) says that after 1739 Christianity faced “severe threat from the Peshwa Brahmins” and only a few churches remained. A heavy, discriminatory religious tax led many to convert to Hinduism. [n04; Machado 1999, p. 108, via Wikipedia: Bombay East Indians] The 1882 Gazetteer and da Cunha are more specific. After the conquest, the Marathas offered rent-free land grants to settle Hindus, and they levied a tax to pay the Brahmans who “purified” Christians so that they could re-enter caste. [n32; BG 34; DC 149] A weaker source adds that the Peshwas brought in priests to purify converts who were willing to return to Hinduism. [n04; Military Wiki]

The fullest statement is da Cunha’s, and it gives the measure a date. Madhavrao Peshwa, he writes, “also instituted a tax in support of Brahmans to purify the native Christians, whom they regarded as polluted Hindus, before receiving them into their former castes. Both these measures [the land grants and the purification tax] brought down a crowd of Hindus from Maharashtra and Gujarat, especially Prabhus.” [n34; DC p. 149] Madhavrao ruled from 1761 to 1772, so if da Cunha is right the purification tax was a policy of the 1760s, a generation after the conquest, not of 1739 itself. [n34, Inferences] The Gazetteer’s wording is more general: “To restore a Hindu population grants of rent-free land were offered, and a tax was levied for the support of Brahmans who were brought to purify the Christians and make them fit to take their place in their old castes.” [n37; Gaz XIV p. 34]

The logic of the policy is worth stating plainly, because it explains a great deal. A Christian convert, in Brahman eyes, was a Hindu who had been polluted, by eating forbidden food, by marriage, by baptism, and so had lost caste. Purification by Brahmans, with a fee and a ritual, restored the person to the caste of his ancestors. [n34; DC p. 149] The Maratha state did not force anyone through that rite, so far as the notes show. It paid Brahmans to perform it, offered land to Hindus who would settle, and so made returning attractive. It was, in its way, the mirror of the Portuguese policy of the 1560s: incentives and status, rather than the sword. Mendiratta’s judgement is careful: “The Marathas probably managed to bring some Catholics back into the Hindu fold through purifying rites, and economic pressure.” [n34; Mendiratta pp. 216-218]

Shivaji had done the same in the 1660s, the Gazetteer recalls, taking “much care that the people should be purified by Brahmans and brought back to Hinduism”. [n37; Gaz XIII-I p. 202] What had been a frontier policy then was a state policy in Bajipur now.

Return. How many Christians went back to Hindu caste, and how freely, is disputed. A polemical modern article by a local Hindu writer says reconversion under the Marathas was voluntary. It points to “Fatherwadi”, a once-Christian village that is now largely Hindu. [n07; Swarajya] The 1882 Gazetteer records whole groups that came back: the Kirpals among the Bhandari palm-tappers were “once Christians”, and the Urap or Varap Agris were Christian Agris of Salsette and Bassein who reverted. Those reversions came later, in 1820 and 1828, under the British, and kept surnames like Gomas, Soz, Fernan, Frutad and Minez. [n08; Gazetteer 1882, pp. 117, 149-150] The process the Maratha conquest began went on for decades after the Marathas themselves had gone.

One group’s return is dated to the Maratha years by an official source. The Census of 1911, in its Bombay report, says the Kirpal Bhandaris, the toddy-tappers, “were forcibly converted to Christianity by the Portuguese, but soon after the conquest of Bassein by the Marathas (about 1739) they were given the choice of reverting to Hinduism, which they accepted”. [n34; Census 1911 Bombay Part I, para 136] The Gazetteer records the same people in local story: many Christians were purified, “among them probably the Bhandaris known as Kirpals”. [n37; Gaz XIII-I p. 202] The phrase “given the choice” is the census-takers’ summary of a community memory, written down 170 years later, but it fits the policy da Cunha describes.

Who stayed Christian, and why. The more striking fact is how many did not go back. The Gazetteer, compiled by British officials with no love for the Portuguese clergy, concluded that the native Christians “showed more constancy and the Marathas more toleration than could have been expected”. [n37; Gaz XIII-I p. 203] It also said that “the Marathas allowed them the freest exercise of their religion, their processions and festivals were respected”. [n37; n34; Gaz XIII-I p. 203]

The notes suggest several reasons for that constancy, though none of them is stated as a cause in any single source. The first is the native priesthood: Indian priests, trained in Bassein’s own seminaries and in Goa, could stay when the Portuguese could not, and the Gazetteer credits them with keeping “the bulk of the people from forsaking Christianity”. [n37, Inferences; Gaz XIII-I p. 203] The second is the parish itself. By 1739 the village churches of Nirmal, Agashi, Nandakhal, Sandor, Papdi, Pali and the rest had been the centre of village life for 140 to 180 years, through five or six generations; baptisms, weddings, burials and feasts had made the parish part of the family. [n09; n40] The third is land and standing. The Samvedi and Vadval Christians were landholding gardeners, not landless dependants; the Gazetteer later found that “wealthy families who are village headmen and owners of rich garden lands often bring up one or more of their sons for the Church”. [n34; n41; Gaz XIII-I pp. 205-206] A family that owned its plantain and betel gardens did not need a new patron. The fourth is purification’s cost. Return to caste meant paying for a rite and accepting a place, often a low place, in a Hindu hierarchy that might not welcome the returner; when the Urap Agris reverted in 1820, they “had to pay £120 (Rs 1200)”, and their Palshe priest was put out of his own caste for a time for admitting them. [n37; Gaz XIII-I p. 117] Those figures come from after 1818, but they show what the price of return could be.

Two sides of the record. The sources on this question come from different places, and each has its leanings. Machado’s “severe threat from the Peshwa Brahmins” and his heavy, discriminatory tax come from a historian of the East Indian Catholic community, writing in 1999 and known to this book only through Wikipedia’s summary. [n04; Machado 1999 via Wikipedia] Da Cunha, who records the purification tax, was a Goan Catholic writing in 1876. [n34; DC p. 149] The Gazetteer’s praise of Maratha “toleration” was written by British officials of the 1880s, who had their own reasons to contrast the Marathas favourably with the Portuguese clergy they distrusted. [n37; Gaz XIII-I p. 203] The Swarajya writer who insists that reconversion was voluntary is a modern Hindu polemicist. [n07; n41; Swarajya 2019] None of them is a Maratha document, and no Marathi source from the Hindu side on these questions has been found. [n37, Gaps] What the sources share is enough to say: the Maratha state paid for purification and offered land to Hindus, some Christians returned to caste, most did not, and the churches that remained were allowed to keep their processions and feasts.

Returning to the reasons for constancy, the fifth is the Maratha state’s own restraint. The Peshwas wanted revenue, order and the restoration of their gods. A Christian village that paid its dues and kept its processions was not, on the evidence of the notes, a problem they set out to solve by force.

Survival. The community did not disappear. According to Machado, it was restored by the native clergy under a Vicar General at Kurla. No names of those priests have been found. [n04; n25; Machado 1999, p. 108] One eyewitness from 1760, given below, shows how much survived.

The Christians were not the only people the conquest affected. The Palshe Brahmans, the old priestly class of the coast, “suffered much since the Maratha conquest of western Thana (1740)”, the Gazetteer says, “at the hands of… the Deccan and Chitpávan Bráhmans”. The Gazetteer thought the insulting folk etymology of their name, palā-áshi, “flesh-eater”, was probably the work of those rivals. [n08; Gazetteer 1882, p. 81 n.] The Peshwas were Chitpavan Brahmans from the Konkan, and the new order brought its own priests and families. A Wikipedia page assumes that some Chitpavan, Karhade, Devrukhe and Gujarati Shukla Yajurvedi surnames in Vasai arrived under Peshwa rule. [n08; Wikipedia: Samvedi] By 1876 Papdi, “a village about a cannon-shot from Bajipura”, had Chitpavan, Karhada and Deshastha Brahmans, Palshes and Sonars among its people. [n32; DC 156-157] Some communities were rewarded. The Pachkalshi gardeners, whose help at the siege had earned the Naiks of Anjur their inam village and other families their patilships, prospered under the new rulers. [n08; n41; Gazetteer 1882, p. 130 n.; Bombay City Gaz. I, p. 238]

“The jaws of the Mahrattas”: Portuguese plans to retake Bassein

The Portuguese did not accept the loss. Pissurlencar reads their letters for the thirty years after 1739, and they give a sidelong view of life in the Maratha province. [n65 §5] In January 1743 the acting Governor of Goa told the King that the Maratha captains of Thana and Bassein forts would hand over their forts for a bribe of one lakh rupees each; Lisbon answered that doing so would be dangerous. On 3 November 1744 the new Viceroy wrote that “the people of Bassein are eager to get out of the jaws of the Mahrattas”, and that he received letters from the local people, Hindus as well as Christians, asking to be freed. Caetano de Sousa Pereira, the defender of 1739, thought the captains’ offers “all a fraud” and said only force would do: 600 Portuguese soldiers, a thousand native soldiers and a good fleet. In March 1747 Nanasaheb Peshwa wrote of the Portuguese: “They are dishonest and must be uprooted, but it is not possible just now.” [n65 §5]

Plots followed. A spy sent from Bassein to Goa by Shankarajipant was sentenced to death in 1751 and refused conversion to save his life. In 1752–53 the Portuguese were drawn toward Bussy and Salabat Jung with the promise of Bassein. On 23 January 1757 the Governor of Goa reported a plan in which, if the Portuguese helped Tarabai and the Angria take Vijaydurg, they would receive one lakh rupees and, afterwards, Bassein, Kalyan and Bhiwandi. About 1760 Lisbon told the Viceroy to live in peace and not to take Bassein even if it could be had by negotiation. The Peshwa’s papers record two spies from Goa, Galvão of Bassein and Duma Koli of Agashi, beheaded, while the Portuguese looked after Galvão’s family at Bassein. [n65 §5] In 1766 the municipality of Daman appealed to the Viceroy to win back Bassein territory.

The other side of the story is the Maratha navy. A Portuguese officer wrote on 19 January 1769: “At Bassein there are 14 palas and over forty galvetas. At Versova, there are three palas and over 50 galvetas, but all of them are small.” A Portuguese work of 1772 says the Marathas raised their fleet at Bassein and Arnala after taking the province, that it had over a hundred ships, some with three masts, and that the Peshwa drew Rs 6 lakh from each of Bassein, Arnala and Vijaydurg. [n65 §5] When the Portuguese bargained for the return of a captured ship, the Santana, one of their conditions was that “the fine teakwood in Bassein province should not be given to anybody else but to the Portuguese”. [n65 §5] Pissurlencar sums up: the Portuguese economy “became shaky after Bassein was captured by Chimaji Appa”, and the Portuguese “were unable to take revenge for it”. [n65 §5]

The new Brahmans and the old

The conquest changed the Hindu world of the coast as well as the Christian one, and the clearest change was among the priests.

Before 1739 the priests of the coast’s Hindus had been the Palshes, or Palshikars, an old Yajurvedi Brahman community whose own tradition said they had come from Paithan on the Godavari with the legendary King Bimb in the thirteenth century. The Gazetteer argued instead for a Gujarat origin, from their White Yajurveda and their Gujarati marriage rules, and one Palshe grant says their priest came from Pattan. [n35; Gaz XIII-I pp. 61-62; Enthoven I, pp. 248-249] Whatever their origin, they were the household priests of the Pachkalshis, the Pathare Prabhus and the Mangela fishermen, and they held “the privilege of fixing the auspicious time for marriage and thread ceremonies for all classes”. [n35; Gaz XIII-I p. 81; Enthoven III pp. 160, 250]

The Peshwas were Chitpavan Brahmans, from the southern Konkan, and their government brought its own Brahmans with it. The Gazetteer states it plainly: after the Maratha conquest, priestly Chitpavan and Deshastha Brahmans “settled as priests, and to a great extent ousted the Palshis and other older classes of priests”. [n35; Gaz XIII-I p. 63] “Of Maratha power the chief relics are priestly Brahmans of the Konkanasth and Deshasth classes,” it says elsewhere. [n35; Gaz XIII-I p. 63] The Chitpavans had only begun calling themselves “Konkanasths”, Brahmans of the Konkan, around 1715, when the first Peshwa, Balaji Vishwanath, rose to power. [n35; Enthoven I, pp. 243-245] Their arrival in Bassein as a ruling class was a generation old when Bajipur was founded.

The Palshes “suffered much since the Maratha conquest of western Thana (1740)”, the Gazetteer says, “at the hands of … the Deccan and Chitpávan Bráhmans”. The Chitpavans refused to dine with them, and the insulting folk etymology of their name, palā-āshi, “flesh-eater”, was, in the Gazetteer’s view, probably the rivals’ work. [n08; n35; Gaz XIII-I p. 81 n.; Enthoven I] The Palshes defended their rank with sanads, grant deeds from the Peshwa era itself, and much later the Smarta Shankaracharya recognised them. [n35; Enthoven I, pp. 248-249] The research notes draw the obvious inference: the Palshes’ insistence on a Paithan origin and on their rank as Deshastha Brahmans was a defence against the newcomers, and the conquest of 1739–40 is why the argument mattered. [n35, Inferences]

The Gazetteer names one more relic of the Peshwas: the Pandharpeshas, “a privileged class of land-holding Brahmans and Prabhus”. [n35; Gaz XIII-I p. 63] They were the families who held land on favourable terms from the Maratha state, the Hindu counterpart, in a sense, of the Portuguese fidalgos with their three-life leases.

The coast’s Hindu Samvedi Brahmans, farmers rather than priests, appear in none of these disputes. They stayed on their gardens beside their Christian kin. Nirmal’s Shankaracharya temple, by one tradition, was served by Samvedi temple musicians; the heritage survey records that story, but it is tradition only. [n40; CRIT Vol III asset 82a]

And the old priests had one role left that would matter in the next century. When groups of Christians sought to return to Hinduism under the British, in 1820 and 1828, it was Palshe priests, Ramchandra Baba Joshi and Vithal Hari Naik Vaidya, who admitted them, at the price of being put out of caste themselves for a time. [n35; n37; Gaz XIII-I p. 117; Enthoven I] The ousted priesthood became the door back into caste for those the Church had lost.

The villages under the Peshwas

The Maratha record does not describe villages one by one. But by putting together the 1727–30 Tombo, the 1882 Gazetteer, da Cunha’s 1876 survey, the parish histories and the 2008 heritage survey, the research notes can trace what changed in each of the island’s main villages between the Portuguese and the British.

Agashi, the old shipyard at the island’s north end, became the Marathas’ local base after 1737, with the Phadke mansion, a Peshwa guest house, the Bhavani Shankar temple and its healing tank. [n40; CRIT Vol I items 43-46] Its Christian church of St James, burned in 1739, was rebuilt by 1760, and it was there that Anquetil saw the crowds going to the feast. [n40; CRIT Vol III asset 44; DC p. 164] So Agashi under the Peshwas was a mixed town: a Maratha official’s seat, a Hindu temple town and a Christian parish, with plantain gardens around it. Its land, which in 1727–30 had been leased to Dona Domingas Pereira de Azevedo with the fisheries of Agashi and Arnala, now answered to the Peshwa’s officers. [n40; Tombo fl. 41-42]

Nirmal had its eight new temples and its Christian congregation worshipping in a cemetery. [n40; Gaz XIV pp. 292-293; EI Nirmal]

Nandakhal, whose church of the Holy Spirit had escaped destruction, received priests from Goa after the Franciscans left. [n40; Wikipedia: Holy Spirit Church Nandakhal] Da Cunha, a century later, still found it “the only one standing in its primitive state” among the rural churches. [n40; DC p. 161]

Vatar, Wagholi, Mardes, Bolinj, Koprad and Umrale, the plantain villages of the Sopara garden belt, held in 1727–30 by Varjão da Fonseca, Abreu de Lima, Almeida de Andrade, Coelho da Silva and others, appear in the Maratha years only through travellers. Anquetil passed “Outar”, that is Vatar, and “Vagholi with a creek of its own” in 1760. [n40; n11; Gaz XIV pp. 322, 385 n.1] When these villages reappear in the record in 1882, they are farmed by Hindu Samvedis, Hindu Pachkalshis and Christians side by side, drying the best plantains in the district. [n40; Gaz XIII-I pp. 292, 395] The likeliest reading is that the Portuguese lessees left and the cultivators stayed, now paying the Maratha state; but no document of the transfer has been found. [n34, Gaps]

Papdi became a Brahman settlement and a jagir village of the army’s carriers, with a Ram Mandir marking the new order, while its church of Our Lady of Grace, which had escaped destruction, continued as a parish of poor farmers. [n40; DC p. 157; CRIT Vol II; EI Papdy]

Sandor, the Christian Vadval village, kept its St Thomas church, “the oldest church building that presently exists outside the Vasai Fort”, and gained a Maratha-era Hanuman temple and tank. [n40; CRIT Vol III asset 103; Vol I items 99-103] Its parish tradition says three altars in the church came from one of the churches inside the fort, presumably carried out when the fort’s churches were abandoned; when that happened is not recorded. [n40; EI Sandor]

Manickpur, still called Porim, lost its Jesuit church in 1739 and perhaps rebuilt it by 1783. [n40; EI Manikpur] Its Christian Vadval carters, with their big carts and strong cattle, may be the “community which transported goods, supplies and food to the Maratha army” that the heritage survey connects with Papdi’s jagir; that link is an inference, not a source. [n40, B2]

Sopara, the ancient port, gained its Lad Vani traders and Gujarati Brahmans in the 1760s, and in 1802 a palm-tree stockade to guard the Peshwa. [n35; Gaz XIII-I p. 63; n15; Thana Gaz. XIV, p. 35] Its creek was still silting. Da Cunha’s survey called it “once a famous bandar, now almost abandoned”. [n32; DC 156-157]

Arnala was a Maratha island fort with temples, a dargah and, by 1806, a Ram Mandir. [n10; n40; CRIT Vol III asset 51]

Bajipur itself, the old walled city, emptied. The Christian population the Portuguese had kept inside the walls left with the garrison; the Maratha administration used the fort, but the great houses were not reoccupied. By 1818 the British found “scarcely a house habitable”. [n28; G-XIV] The town that later grew up as Bassein lay half a mile inland, in the village of Malonde, outside the walls. [n32; BG 28, 36]

Da Cunha’s 1876 survey of the Maratha-era divisions, as they came down to the British, counts 161 villages in the taluka, four of them held in inam, the rent-free grants by which the Peshwas had rewarded allies and endowed temples. [n32; DC 156-157] Vajreshwari alone held six inam villages, three of them in Bassein. [n10; Gazetteer 1882, pp. 373-374]

The Kuparis’ ancestors and the Vadvals under the Peshwas

For the two communities this book follows most closely, the Maratha years were a turning point in opposite ways.

The Christian Samvedis of the northern gardens, the people later called Kuparis, lost their Portuguese landlords and their Franciscan and Jesuit priests, and gained Indian priests from Goa and a Maratha government that tolerated their processions. They lived in the villages, Nirmal, Agashi, Nandakhal, Vatar, Koprad, Bolinj, Sopara and the rest, where Hindu temples were being rebuilt beside their churches, and where their Hindu Samvedi cousins farmed the next garden. [n34; Gaz XIV p. 315; Gaz XIII-I p. 395] No Maratha-period document names a Samvedi Christian village, and no source says how many of them returned to Hindu caste through purification. [n34, Gaps] The 1882 evidence, seventy-five Christian families “originally Samvedis and Pachkalshis” beside eighty-five Hindu Samvedi families at Agashi, Vatar and Koprad, shows that both communities came through the Maratha century, and that the split made by conversion held. [n34; Gaz XIII-I p. 395]

The Vadvals were divided by the same line, but the conquest treated the two halves differently. The Hindu Pachkalshis gained patilships and inam villages for their help at the siege. [n41; Enthoven III, p. 161] The Christian Vadvals of Sandor, Manickpur, Chulne and Gokhivare kept their parishes, their carts and their gardens under a government that had rewarded their Hindu kin. [n41; Gaz XIII-I pp. 97-98] Whether any Christian Vadvals returned to Hinduism after 1739 is not recorded; the notes find reconversion documented for Bhandaris and Agris, not for Vadvals. [n41, Gaps]

One small sign of how the old Christian devotions fared survives. The cult of Gonsalo Garcia, the Bassein-born martyr of Nagasaki, “lapsed from about 1739”, the Catholic Encyclopedia says, and was revived only later, with an annual feast drawing pilgrims from Bassein, Salsette and Bombay. [n38; Catholic Encyclopedia, Gonsalo Garcia] His Jesuit school was inside the fort, and the fort was empty; his memory waited, like the Nirmal parishioners in their cemetery, for better times.

Work, feasts and fairs

Daily life in the villages shows through only in fragments. The gardens grew sugarcane, coconuts and betel, which went out by sea to Bombay and Gujarat. [n36; Gaz XIII-II pp. 512-513] The Christian feasts went on, with processions the Marathas “respected”, and with neighbouring priests joining the parish priest on the feast day, as at Agashi in December 1760. [n37; Gaz XIII-I p. 203; n40; DC p. 164] The Hindu fairs returned. By 1882, the Nirmal fair in Kartik drew six or seven thousand people of every faith, and Vajreshwari’s Chaitra fair about five thousand; both were held at temples Shankarji Keshav had built, and both had probably grown up in the Maratha century, though the notes give no date for their beginnings. [n40; Gaz XIV p. 292; n10; Gazetteer 1882, pp. 373-374] And the healing tank near Our Lady of Remedies, where the Inquisition had banned pilgrimage “under very severe penalties”, drew Hindu pilgrims again; by da Cunha’s time they held a fair there. [n37; DC p. 163] Under the Portuguese the ban had been enforced by the Church; under the Peshwas, nothing stood in the way.

1760: A Frenchman on the road north

In November 1760, twenty-one years after the conquest, the young French scholar Abraham Hyacinthe Anquetil-Duperron travelled up this coast. His notes are the best eyewitness account of Maratha Vasai the sources hold.

He admired the fort as a soldier might. “Except Goa I have seen no town better placed for trade. The fort, in warlike hands, could offer a strong resistance. It is a regular hexagon…” Its bastions, he said, carried nine guns on each face, and a square bastion protected many curtains. The south gate was open, but the Marathas had “condemned the south-west gate”, closing it off. [n15; n32; Thana Gaz. XIV, p. 34 n.; BG 34 n.2] His “hexagon” conflicts with the ten-sided wall the Gazetteer measured in 1882, and was probably a loose description. [n32] Inside, in a magazine, later visitors saw stone cannonballs about six inches across. [n32; DC 148 n.]

On 27 November 1760 he passed “Sopara, or Sipala, though fallen to insignificance”. [n15; Thana Gaz. XIV, p. 322] He noted “Vagholi with a creek of its own”, and the village of “Outar”, which the Gazetteer identified as Vatar, the Vattaraka of a Shilahara inscription six centuries older. [n11; Gaz. 1882 pp. 322, 341 n.] He was travelling through landscapes far older than any of the empires that had fought over them.

At Agashi he wrote the sentence that best answers the question of what happened to Vasai’s Christians. He “found the lanes full of people going to church as freely as in a Christian country”. [n15; n11; Thana Gaz. XIV, p. 2]

Da Cunha, who read the French original, adds the circumstances. Anquetil reached Agashi on the church’s feast day, 16 December 1760, having come “par les plus beaux vergers du monde”, through the finest orchards in the world. The parish priest was entertaining the “Canarin” priests from the neighbouring parishes who had helped him with the service. [n40; DC p. 164] The scene is precise: a village feast, a procession of people, a priest’s table with his fellow Indian clergy around it, and all around the village the plantain and coconut gardens the Frenchman thought the loveliest in the world. That is what Christian life under the Peshwas looked like on a good day in the northern garden belt, the country of the Kuparis’ ancestors. Twenty-one years after the bells had been carried off, under a Brahman government that taxed and pressured them, the Christians of Agashi were still walking to mass in crowds. The sources contain both his sentence and Machado’s “severe threat”, and both have to stand.

Around the same year, new traders arrived. Lad Vani Gujaratis came to Sopara from Cambay about 1760, the Gazetteer says, “fleeing the exactions of Momin Khan II”. By 1882 they and the Naita Muslims were the traders of Sopara’s crowded weekly market. [n11; n31; Gaz. 1882 p. 315] The coast that the Portuguese had closed to Hindu merchants at night, when the evening drum sent the “Banyans” to the suburbs, was now open to them. [n15; Fryer/Crooke I]

The Lad Vanis from Cambay

The Gazetteer’s population volume tells their story more fully. “About the middle of the eighteenth century (1760-1766) a considerable number of Cambay Vanis, chiefly of the Lad sub-division, and with them several Gujarat Brahmans settled in Supara, Bassein, and other coast towns to avoid the exactions of Momin Khan II (1748-1783).” [n35; n36; Gaz XIII-I p. 63] Momin Khan II was the Nawab of Cambay, the Gujarat port at the head of its gulf, and his taxes drove its traders south by the old sea road. By 1882 there were about 120 families of Lad Vanis in the district: about forty in Thana, thirty-five at Sopara, and the rest at Bassein, Agashi, Nala, Papdi and Dahanu. [n35; Gaz XIII-I pp. 112-113]

They were not entirely strangers. The Gazetteer points out that the Arab geographer Masudi, writing in 915, had found “the Lar language” spoken in the coast towns as far south as Chaul, which “makes it probable that from very early times Lad Vanis had settled along the Thana coast for purposes of trade”. [n35; Gaz XIII-I pp. 112-113] Lad takes its name from Lat-desh, the old name of south Gujarat. [n35; Enthoven III, p. 428] So the refugees of the 1760s were following an ancient trading route, and perhaps rejoining distant kin.

Their way of life shows the mix the Maratha coast had become. They spoke Gujarati among themselves and Marathi with others. They worked as shopkeepers, moneylenders, landholders and merchants. They were Vaishnavs of the Vallabhacharya sect who “observe Shaiv fasts and feasts” because of their “close connection with Maratha Brahmans”. They took brides from Cambay, Jambusar and Bombay. [n35; Gaz XIII-I pp. 112-113] The research notes call them the best documentary precedent for the later Gujarati and Jain presence in Nallasopara and Virar: they arrived by the Cambay–Bassein sea route, 120 years before the railway. [n35, Inferences]

Why did they come to Sopara rather than to Bombay? The sources do not say. Sopara under the Peshwas was, in Anquetil’s word, “fallen to insignificance” as a port, but it was still a market town in the garden belt, and its small creek landings at Gass, Bolinj and Vagholi still sent molasses, rice and betel to Surat, Broach and the Gujarat ports. [n37; Gaz XIV pp. 315-316] For traders from Cambay, a Gujarat-facing market in friendly Maratha territory was perhaps nearer to home than the English island. That is an inference only.

Parsi money arrived too, as told below, and in the north Gujarati was slowly spreading as a language. [n35; Gaz XIII-I pp. 64-65] The coast of the Peshwas was multilingual in a new way: Marathi in the Brahman offices, written in Modi script; Gujarati in the Vanis’ shops; the mixed Marathi-Gujarati speech of the Samvedi and Vadval villages; Marathi with a scatter of Portuguese church words among the Christians; Latin at the altar; and, among the Konkani Muslims such as the Naitas of Sopara, a Marathi base layered with Urdu, Persian and Arabic words. [n34; n35, Cross-cutting]

1760 – 1774: The quiet years

After Anquetil-Duperron, the record for Vasai goes quiet for almost twenty years.

This is one of the silent periods the book has to name. The notes record no subhedar of Bassein after Shankarji Keshav, no revenue figures for the Maratha province, no list of its mamlatdars, and no description of how the district was run. [n04, Gaps; n25, Gaps] The evidence probably survives in the Peshwa daftar, in Modi script. Wikimedia Commons holds scans of Peshwa-era Modi letters, including some of Chimaji Appa’s. This book could not verify any reading of them. [n22; Commons] A record from much later, da Cunha’s survey of 1876, gives the outline of the Maratha-era divisions as they had come down to the British. The taluka had 161 villages, four of them held in inam. Khani Vadem had a small bandar. The Manikpura mahal lay in the south-east, with Agashi in the north, Tungar hill, Nirmala, and “Supara, once a famous bandar, now almost abandoned”. [n32; DC 156-157] That is a picture from the end of the Maratha system, not from its working years.

A few events of these years do reach the notes, from other directions.

The purification tax, if da Cunha is right to credit it to Madhavrao Peshwa, belongs to this decade, since Madhavrao ruled from 1761 to 1772. [n34; DC p. 149] So does the arrival of the Lad Vanis from Cambay, between 1760 and 1766. [n35; Gaz XIII-I p. 63]

Among the Pachkalshis, the community the siege had raised, a caste meeting in 1779 passed a resolution against marriage between two of its divisions, the Sashtikars of Salsette and the Ashtagarkars of the southern coast. Enthoven, who records it, adds that such intermarriage was “not frequent, though not rare”. [n41; Enthoven III, p. 161] It is a small entry, but it shows a community confident enough, a generation after the conquest, to hold meetings and set its own rules. The Vadvals, the Bassein branch, had separated from the rest “some time in the fourteenth century”, by their own account, and married only among themselves; their right to the name Panchkalshi would be disputed, and defended, in the nineteenth century. [n41; Enthoven III, pp. 161-162]

What can be said is how the wider world pressed on the coast. Vasai lay between the Peshwa’s government in Pune and the English island of Bombay just across the water. Bombay had refused help to the Portuguese in 1739. [n32; DC 147-148] Thirty-five years later, the English came to the Peshwa’s coast on their own account.

They came first to Salsette, the island just south of the Bassein creek, which the British took in 1774. Mendiratta counts about 20,000 Catholics there at that time, with at least ten churches. [n34; Mendiratta p. 219] So from 1774 the old Province of the North was divided: Salsette under the British, Bassein under the Peshwa, with the creek between them. The Christians on the two sides would live under different rulers for the next forty-four years, and their churches would be drawn into different quarrels. In Salsette, the Gazetteer notes, the British gave Christians “no special encouragement”, and the earliest English accounts mention them “in terms of contempt”. [n34; Gaz XIII-I p. 203]

1775 – 1782: Goddard at the walls

The First Anglo-Maratha War began with a quarrel over who should be Peshwa. In 1775 the East India Company made the Treaty of Surat with Raghunathrao, a claimant to the office. [n25; Wikipedia: Vasai] In 1779 a Company army under Colonel Egerton was defeated, and the Governor-General, Warren Hastings, rejected the Convention of Wadgaon that followed. [n25; Wikipedia: Vasai] The notes give these events only in outline, and only as background.

Then the war came to Bassein. A Bengal army under General Thomas Goddard besieged the fort from 13 November to 11 December 1780, according to the FIBIS wiki, which cites Grant Duff’s history of the Marathas. Other sources call it a twelve-day siege. [n04; n05; FIBIS: Siege of Bassein Fort 1780; Wikipedia: Vasai] Either way, the fort the Marathas had needed three months and a hundred thousand men to take, by the Portuguese count, fell to the British within a month. The notes do not name the Maratha commander or give the casualties. [n04, Gaps]

One reason the Company wanted the fort is given by the Gazetteer: around 1780 “the governor of Bassein, one of the Peshwa’s admirals”, was attacking English ships. [n36; Gaz XIII-II p. 504 n.3] Bajipur was the Peshwa’s naval base on the coast nearest Bombay, and a governor-admiral there threatened the Company’s shipping at its own door. [n36; Wikipedia: Kanhoji Angre] Taking Bassein removed that threat, at least for the length of the war. The notes do not say whether the Peshwa’s ships were in harbour when Goddard came, or where they went.

The news travelled far. The Persian chronicle Seir Mutaqherin recorded in 1781: “General Goddard after having taken the fortress of Bessi, which is one of the strongest and most important fortresses under the Mahratta power…” [n15; Hobson-Jobson, “Bassein”]

Arnala held out longer. In 1781 its commandant refused to yield until the British prepared a bombardment from Agashi, across the water. [n15; n11; Thana Gaz. XIV, pp. 10-11] Wikipedia dates the British capture of Arnala to 18 January 1781; the Incredible India government page gives the same day. [n11; n39; Wikipedia: Arnala Fort; Incredible India]

For the Christians of the island, the British occupation of 1780–83 was a brief interval under a third set of rulers. The notes record nothing of how they fared, whether any priest or parish petitioned the British, or whether any Christians left with the Company’s army when it gave the fort back. [n04, Gaps] The cross found at Manickpur with the date 1783, if it belongs to a rebuilt church, would fall at the very end of this interval, just as the Peshwa’s officials returned. [n40; EI Manikpur]

Then the diplomats undid the war. The Treaty of Salbai was signed on 17 May 1782, ratified by Hastings in June 1782 and by the Peshwa’s minister Nana Phadnavis in February 1783. The British kept Salsette and Elephanta and gave back their other conquests, Bassein among them. Arnala went back too. [n04; GKToday; First Anglo-Maratha War (wikiwand); n11] The sources disagree on some details of the treaty. [n04] What matters here is the result. Bassein was Maratha again, but only because the British had handed it back.

1782 – 1802: Two decades of quiet

The record for these twenty years is thin again. In 1780, the year of the siege, a Parsi named Lavji Sorabji Lakdawala had built a rest-house in the town, which still stood in 1882. [n32; n11; BG 37] Parsi merchants from Bombay were already putting money into the Maratha town.

The great famine of 1790 is remembered in the Thana district record as a long-remembered disaster. The notes do not say how it touched Vasai itself. [n28; G-I, Famines] Beyond that, until the last weeks of 1802, the notes have almost nothing on Vasai.

The silence can be partly filled from what neighbouring places were doing, and how it would have touched this coast. Across the creek, the English island was growing fast. The Bombay Dockyard, founded in 1735 and run by the Wadia family of shipbuilders from Surat, built 159 ships of over 100 tons between 1736 and 1821, of Malabar teak. [n36; Gaz XIII-II p. 513 n.3; Wikipedia: Lovji Nusserwanjee Wadia] By 1800, the Gazetteer says, “almost the whole of the foreign trade” of the coast passed through Bombay. [n36; Gaz XIII-II p. 513] The yards of Agashi and Bassein, which had built ships for Europe two centuries before, now looked across the water at a rival that had overtaken them completely. Bassein’s role became that of a feeder port, sending the produce of its gardens and fields to Bombay and Gujarat in small local boats. [n36, Takeaway section 5]

The Church on both sides of the creek was drawn into Bombay’s quarrels too. In 1794 the churches of English Bombay were divided between priests loyal to the Padroado of Goa and those under the Vicar Apostolic appointed through Rome’s Propaganda Fide, creating “two rival jurisdictions”. [n38; Catholic Encyclopedia, Goa] Maratha Bassein, its parishes still under the Archbishop of Goa by the terms of 1739, stayed outside that dispute. [n38, Inferences]

The Portuguese letters of these years show the Peshwa’s subhedar of Bassein at work. After the English restored Bassein to the Marathas with Salbai, the Governor of Goa wrote on 11 December 1783 that the English had made peace and restored “Baçaim with the lands and villages of its jurisdiction up to the limits of Damão, reserving Thana and the province of Salsette”. Under an agreement made at Poona on 6 January 1780, the Peshwa had promised the Portuguese villages worth 12,000 rupees a year, and in 1783–85 sixty-two villages of the Nagar Haveli pargana, with six more and the customs houses on 22 July 1785, making seventy-two in all, were handed to the governor of Damão. The orders came to Damão from the “Subedar General of Baçaim”, named in the Portuguese register as Anand Rão Bicagi in 1783 and as Givagi Copal in 1785, who passed on the Peshwa’s sanads and had the villages’ administrator, Sadashiv Pant, give them up. [n67 §2] It is one of the few places where the Peshwa’s officer at Bassein is named, and it shows that Poona’s business on this coast, even with the Portuguese of Daman, went through him.

1802: The Treaty of Bassein

At the end of 1802 a Peshwa came to Bassein as a fugitive.

Baji Rao II had been Peshwa since 1796. [n25] In 1802 the Maratha chief Yashwantrao Holkar defeated him at Poona, and Baji Rao fled to the coast. [n04; n05; Wikipedia: Treaty of Bassein (1802); Vasaiker] On 17 December 1802, according to the Gazetteer, he “reached Bassein from Suvarndurg”, the sea fort down the coast. At Bassein, then a British stronghold, he was “met by Colonel Close and Mr. Elphinstone his Assistant, and on December 31st the Treaty of Bassein was concluded”. [n15; n28; Thana Gaz. XIV, p. 35; G-II] While he was there, “a considerable palm-tree stockade was built at Sopara”, at the Sopara bridge, to guard the passage to Bassein island. [n15; n28; Thana Gaz. XIV, p. 35]

The terms of the treaty, signed on 31 December 1802 between the East India Company and the Peshwa, were these:

  • About six thousand Company troops were to be stationed permanently with the Peshwa.
  • To pay for them, he ceded districts yielding 2.6 million rupees (Rs 26 lakh) a year.
  • He could make no war and no treaty without the Company.
  • He gave up his claims on Surat, and agreed that his differences and claims against the Nizam and the Gaekwar would be settled by British arbitration; with the Nizam he was to keep to the treaty of Mhar. [n61; Duff III, ch. 12]
  • He was to employ no European “of a nation hostile to the English”. [n61; Duff III, ch. 12]
  • If war came, two battalions of the Company’s infantry, of not less than a thousand each, were to stay near the Peshwa’s person; the rest were to be joined by 6,000 of his infantry and 10,000 horse. Goods for the Company’s troops passed duty free. [n61; Duff III, ch. 12]

[n04; n15; n28; Wikipedia: Treaty of Bassein (1802); Britannica; G-II]

The Peshwa stayed on at Bassein for four more months. A letter in the Asiatic Annual Register, headed “Camp Soopara, March 26, 1803”, reads: “We have just been paying a formal visit to his highness the peishwa.” [n15; Hobson-Jobson, “Supara”] Ancient Shurparaka, the port of the Periplus and the Ashokan edicts, which Anquetil-Duperron had found “fallen to insignificance”, gave its name to a British camp guarding a Maratha prince. On 27 April 1803 Baji Rao left Bassein and marched under escort, by way of Kalyan and the Bor pass, to Poona. [n28; G-II] There, on 13 May 1803, he was restored as Peshwa, now as a client of the Company. [n04; n15; Wikipedia: Treaty of Bassein (1802)]

The road to the treaty, 1795–1802

A popular chronology on the Sify history pages (an unsigned web article, “1802: Treaty of Bassein”, supplied by the author) and James Grant Duff’s History of the Mahrattas give the lead-up. [n61] On 25 October 1795 the young Peshwa Madhavrao II Narayan killed himself, tired, the article says, of the dictatorship of his minister Nana Phadnavis. After plots and counter-plots, Baji Rao II, son of Raghoba, became Peshwa on 4 December 1796, with Nana as his chief minister, and the Nizam recovered the lands he had lost to the Marathas at Kharda. [n61; Sify] Lord Wellesley arrived as Governor-General on 26 April 1798 and set about the Subsidiary Alliance, in the conviction, the article says, that the best way to safeguard English interests was to reduce the whole country to military dependence on the Company. [n61; Sify]

Nana’s death on 13 March 1800 removed the one man who foresaw it. Duff records the remark of Colonel Palmer, the British Resident at Poona: “and with him … has departed all the wisdom and moderation of the Mahratta government”. The Sify article gives it in slightly different words. [n61; Duff; Sify] The great chiefs Daulat Rao Sindhia and Jaswant Rao Holkar fought for mastery at Poona, and the Peshwa, favouring Sindhia, became his puppet. The article says the Peshwa had Holkar’s brother Vithuji killed in April 1801, which drove Holkar into open rebellion; Holkar defeated the combined armies of Sindhia and the Peshwa at Poona and took the city. [n61; Sify] The article dates the battle to 23 October, but histories and encyclopedias give 25 October 1802, so its date is probably wrong. [n61] Holkar then set up Vinayak Rao, the son of Amrit Rao, as Peshwa, and Baji Rao fled to the coast. [n61; Duff]

What happened after he landed at Bassein, Duff tells with a British officer’s economy. Colonel Close met him on the evening of the day he landed, and the preliminary of the treaty, which an agent had already offered on the day of his flight from Poona, was at once acknowledged. The articles were taken up on 18 December and completed on 31 December. [n61; Duff] The Gazetteer’s date for the landing, 17 December, fits: the Peshwa landed, met Close that evening, and the discussion began the next day. [n61, Inferences; n15] Duff’s judgement was that by the treaty the Peshwa “sacrificed his independence as the price of protection, but it was the only course he could pursue to save himself from becoming, more than ever, a pageant in the hands of one or other of the contending chiefs”. He adds that the Peshwa “had scarcely ratified the treaty when he began to waver”, regretting that Sindhia had not been consulted. [n61; Duff] When the Peshwa went back, Duff says, he was escorted by 2,300 infantry, 1,200 of them Europeans, and took his seat on the throne again on 13 May 1803. [n61; Duff] Sindhia and Raghuji Bhonsle, the article adds, refused to accept the treaty, and that refusal brought on the Second Anglo-Maratha War. [n61; Sify; Duff]

The ports in the treaty years

The treaty years happen to be the first for which the Bassein ports have trade figures since the Portuguese revenue lines of the 1720s, because the British in Bombay kept returns of what came and went. The Gazetteer, citing those returns and the trade writer Milburn, gives the trade of the Bassein ports with Bombay and Surat as “about three and a half lakhs” of rupees in 1802, of which about two lakhs were exports and one and a half imports; nine lakhs in 1805; and an average of “about nine lakhs of rupees … of which about five lakhs were exports” for the five years to 1806. [n36; Gaz XIII-II pp. 512-513]

The goods of 1805 are listed. Exports were piece-goods (cloth), grain, iron, sugar, coconuts, betelnut, dates, pepper, turmeric and treasure. Imports were ivory, oil, timber, hemp, piece-goods and betelnut. [n36; Gaz XIII-II pp. 512-513] The Gazetteer itself found the list odd: iron and ivory, it thought, suggested either a direct trade with Africa or a quirk of recording. [n36; Gaz XIII-II p. 513] Sugar, coconuts and betelnut were the produce of the island’s own gardens, the same plantations Anquetil had ridden through. Dates and pepper were goods in transit.

The figures are small beside Bombay’s, and they describe a feeder, not an entrepôt. [n36, Inferences] But they show the coast’s gardens at work. Under the Peshwas, the Christian and Hindu gardeners of the island were growing cane, coconut and betel for the markets of Bombay and Gujarat, and the Lad Vanis and other traders were shipping it. The notes name no trader, boat-owner or merchant house of these years. [n36, Gaps] The boats were probably the lateen-rigged local craft that the Gazetteer would describe eighty years later, the machvas and mhangiris, built mostly in the Bassein subdivision by Marathas, Christians, Gujarat carpenters, Kolis and Pachkalshis; but that description is from the 1880s, and its use for 1805 is an inference. [n36; Gaz XIII-I pp. 342-343]

What the treaty meant

Historians have called the treaty “a decisive step in the dissolution of the Maratha Empire”. [n04; Wikipedia: Treaty of Bassein (1802)] Its logic is plain from its terms. A sovereign who cannot make war or peace without a foreign company, who pays for that company’s army out of his own land, and who may not hire anyone else’s officers, is no longer fully sovereign. The other great Maratha chiefs saw that, and the treaty set off the Second Anglo-Maratha War. [n04; n15] The Company’s eventual annexation of the Peshwa’s territories in 1818 followed from it. [n04; n15; Wikipedia: Treaty of Bassein (1802); Britannica]

A sentence often quoted about the treaty says that “the Treaty of Bassein, by its direct and indirect operations, gave the Company the Empire of India”. It is commonly credited to the Victorian editor Sidney J. Owen. This book’s research found it only as an unattributed examination question, and it should not be credited to Owen without checking. [n15, Gaps] The judgement it expresses is close to the plainer one historians make: the treaty was the step from which the Company’s mastery of western India followed.

The treaty also closes a circle on this coast. In December 1534 a ruler under pressure, Sultan Bahadur Shah of Gujarat, signed a treaty aboard a galleon off this shore that gave the coast to Portugal. In December 1802 another ruler under pressure, the Peshwa Baji Rao II, signed a treaty at Bassein that began the end of Maratha power. [n04, Inferences; n18, Inferences] Two treaties of Bassein, 268 years apart, mark the beginning of one empire’s hold on India and the beginning of another’s.

One source muddies the dates. The Catholic Encyclopedia of 1907 says simply that “the English took it from them in 1802”. That conflicts with the record that Bassein stayed in the Peshwa’s territory until 1817–18. [n04; Catholic Encyclopedia] The likeliest explanation is that the encyclopedia mistook the treaty signed at Bassein for a transfer of Bassein. The Gazetteer and the other sources date the British takeover to 1817–18. [n15; n28]

1803 – 1818: The last Maratha years

The Thana district suffered “much distress” from famine in 1803 and 1804. The record does not single out Bassein. [n28; G-II]

The palm-tree stockade at Sopara, built in December 1802 to guard the Peshwa, also says something about the land. It stood at the crossing onto the island, the same shallow backwater that a Portuguese officer had warned about in 1728, when he complained that the creek between Sopara and Bolinj was fordable at low water and that the bridge at Gokirva was unprotected. [n15; Thana Gaz. XIV, p. 35; n36; Gaz XIV p. 322] Seventy-four years later, the British were still guarding the island’s eastern edge with timber, because the moat that had once made Bassein an island was silting into fields. Whether the 1802 stockade stood on the site of the old Portuguese works the notes do not say. [n36, Gaps] By 1882 the Gazetteer would find the creek at Sopara dry. [n36; Gaz XIV pp. 314-315]

The client Peshwa did not stay a client quietly. The notes do not tell the story of his break with the Company, only its end on this coast. Under the Treaty of Poona of June 1817, “Bassein with the rest of the north Konkan passed to the British”. [n15; n16; n28; Thana Gaz. XIV, p. 35; Thana Gazetteer, History] The day is uncertain. The Gazetteer’s OCR reads “18th June 1817”; other histories give 13 June 1817. [n28] Arnala became British the same year. [n11; Gaz. 1882 pp. 10-11] In 1818 the Company annexed the Peshwa’s remaining territories. [n04; Wikipedia: Treaty of Bassein (1802)] From 1818 the fort belonged to the East India Company, and from 1857 to the Crown. [n04; n16; Wikipedia: Vasai Fort]

The British took over a fort they had little use for. In 1818 a British report described Bassein as of no military value, with “scarcely a house habitable”; a small detachment was kept there for a time. [n28; G-XIV] The same report of forts on the north Konkan coast ranked Arnala “next to Bassein in size and strength”. [n15; n11; Thana Gaz. XIV, pp. 10-11] The Court of the North, which Fryer had called “strong enough against the Indians”, which the Marathas had taken at the cost of so much blood, and which Goddard had taken in a month, ended as a ruin with a caretaker.

The new rulers soon took stock of the Christians, too. When the British took over the Bassein subdivision, their records counted “eight functioning churches and a Catholic population estimated at 5000”. [n34; Mendiratta p. 219] Eight churches is close to the nine parishes the Gazetteer would list in 1881 (Sandor, Papdi, Pali, Manikpur, Davli or Remedy, Nirmal, Koprad, Agashi and the Bassein church of Our Lady of Mercy), so the parish map of the Portuguese countryside had come through the Maratha century nearly whole. [n34; Gaz XIII-I p. 206 n.3] The British were wary of what they found. A letter of 1819, just after this chapter’s end, said of the Goa-linked clergy that “they form a sort of separate caste in the middle of the community”. [n34; Mendiratta p. 220] The cholera that would strike in 1818–20, and the first recorded group reconversions that followed it, belong to the next chapter. [n37; Gaz XIII-I p. 203]

Goa’s jurisdiction continued without a break. The parishes that had been “subject to the Primate of India” under the Peshwa remained under the Archbishop of Goa under the Company, and would stay so until 1886. [n38; Hull I, p. 6] A Bassein parish’s priest and church did not change when the flag over the fort did.

What the Marathas left

The Maratha years left Vasai no palace and no wada that the notes can name. [n18, Inferences] What they left instead is visible across the taluka. There are the eight temples of Nirmal around their lake, the four buildings of Tungareshwar on the hill, the Bhavanishankar temple and its curative tank at Agashi, and the Trimbakeshvar and Bhavani temples inside Baji Tulaji’s walls at Arnala. Inside the sea gate of the old Portuguese city stands the small temple of Hanuman. In the Deccan hang the bells, still wearing their crosses. And in the landscape the Marathas left a new arrangement of peoples. Brahman families came from the Deccan and the Konkan, Gujarati traders were at Sopara, Parsi money was in the town, and the Christian villages of the west, smaller and poorer but still there, still walked to mass.

The Marathas left a memory as well. A memorial to Chimaji Appa, the Narveer Chimaji Appa Smarak, with a statue and a garden, stands near the fort today. Every May the town keeps the Vasai Vijayotsav, the victory festival. Its 2025 celebration was counted as the 287th, which treats 1739 as the first. The modern civic festival is very probably much newer than that count implies; its origin has not been verified. [n18; FPJ: Vasai Vijayotsav 2025] On 12 May 2025, after heavy rain shortened the programme to one day, a procession went from the Tehsil office to the fort, with an 11 a.m. tribute at the statue. [n18; FPJ] Local tradition also remembers a fallen hero of the siege. A memorial behind a lawyer’s bungalow in the town honours Balajipant More, who, the legend says, kept fighting after the Portuguese beheaded him. [n13; vasai.com blog] It is a legend, and this book tells it as one.

The Maratha century also settled the shape of the coast’s communities for the next two hundred years. The Konkanasth and Deshastha Brahmans who came as officials and priests stayed as landholders, the Pandharpeshas. [n35; Gaz XIII-I p. 63] The Palshes, ousted from much of their priestly work, kept their rank by sanad and argument. [n35; Enthoven I] The Pachkalshi patil families held the headmanships the siege had earned them. [n41; Enthoven III, p. 161] The Lad Vanis of Cambay ran the shops and lent the money at Sopara, Agashi, Nala and Papdi. [n35; Gaz XIII-I pp. 112-113] And the Christians, their Portuguese landlords and friars gone, became a community of Indian farmers, gardeners, carters and fishermen with Indian priests, under a Hindu state, the shape in which the British would find them and in which their descendants, the Kuparis and the Christian Vadvals among them, would enter the modern age. [n34; Mendiratta pp. 216-218]

Languages followed the same lines. Government business was conducted in Marathi, written in the Modi script of the Peshwa’s daftar. [n22] The Lad Vanis kept Gujarati at home and used Marathi in the market. [n35; Gaz XIII-I pp. 112-113] The Christians spoke the Marathi of their neighbours, keeping a handful of Portuguese words for God-parents, father, mother and the Mass, and heard Latin at the altar; Portuguese itself was fading to a language of a few families. [n34; Gaz XIII-I p. 204; LSI VII] The Samvedi and Vadval villages kept the mixed Marathi-Gujarati speech that Grierson would record in 1905. [n34; n41; LSI VII]

For the Christians of the west, the same May marks the end of a world, the day their churches lost their bells. For the Hindus of the east and north, it marks the return of their gods to Nirmal and Tungar. Vasai has lived with both memories ever since, often in the same villages.

On a British map of 1818, the island of Bassein was a new possession in the Collectorate of the north Konkan. It had a ruined fort, a sugar-cane plain, gardens of plantain and betel, and a mixed population that no one had counted for a century. The new rulers soon started counting: acres, rupees, schools, Christians, Hindus and the dead of the cholera years. With them, for the first time since the friars, the record of this coast becomes a ledger again.
