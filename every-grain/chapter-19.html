Period covered: the rock, creeks and gardens of Vasai as the Portuguese, the Marathas and the 1882 Gazetteer knew them, through the sugar, betel, plantain, salt and boat economy of the nineteenth century, to the floods, water schemes, industrial estates and municipal budget of 2026

In this chapter. The land, water and living of Vasai through time: rock, creeks, rain, floods, and the economy of rice, plantains, salt and boats.

In 1852 a gang of workmen began digging foundations inside the ruined church of Nossa Senhora da Vida, within the walls of the old Portuguese city of Bassein. The church was one of the oldest in the fort; a Neapolitan traveller, Careri, had counted three altars in it in 1695. Now it was to become a sugar factory. Between the church and the ruin of the Misericórdia next to it, the builders dug a tank to feed the sugar machinery, and near the sea they walled a place with steps where sea water was stored. In one trench they found a grave, and in it the bones of a man and of a horse, with a rusty rapier: a burial, the Portuguese historian José Gerson da Cunha supposed, of a general with his charger. [n32; Gaz XIV pp. 36, 40; Da Cunha pp. 226–227] The factory did not last. By the Gazetteer’s account of 1882 it “did not pay, and has been closed”. [n53 A5; n32; Gaz XIV p. 36]

A hundred and seventy-four years later, on 4 July 2026, after days of rain, the headquarters of the Vasai-Virar City Municipal Corporation stood in water, inside and all around. [n53 A2; FPJ, 4 July 2026] The building from which the city is governed had flooded, like the streets it governs.

Between those two scenes lies the subject of this chapter: the ground on which Vasai stands, the water that runs over it, under it and into it, and the many ways the people of Vasai have made a living from both. The soldier and his horse were buried in a city built of basalt quarried from the hills behind it, on the north bank of a creek, in a country of gardens; the factory that disturbed them was one of a long line of attempts to turn the sweet water and sandy soil of Vasai into money. The corporation that flooded in 2026 governs the same creek, the same gardens, now largely built over, and the same rain.

This chapter tells Vasai: the old fort and town, Manickpur and Vasai Road, Papdi, Sandor, Merces and the parishes round the fort, the green-belt villages of the south-west, the creek shore from Killa Bandar to Naigaon and Panju, and the eastern side from Gokhivare, Sativali and Waliv to Vasai Phata and the foot of Tungar. Its twin, Chapter 30, tells the same subjects for Virar and the northern villages, and Chapter 24 tells them for Nallasopara; where a story belongs to the whole belt, such as the bavkhal ponds, the kawadi vendors or the July 2026 flood, it is told here from Vasai’s side and the reader is pointed to the others. The deep history of the rock is told in Chapters 01 and 02; the walled city, its trade and its decline in Chapters 07, 08 and A1; the villages one by one in Chapter 21; the decades of the republic in Chapters 14, 14 and 15; the coast and beaches in Chapter 32.

The evidence is of two kinds, a long way apart. The first is the Gazetteer of the Bombay Presidency, Volume XIII (Thana) in two parts and Volume XIV (Places of Interest), compiled under James Campbell and published in 1882, read from the scanned text on the Internet Archive; its prose is reliable, its tables often garbled by the scanner, and where a figure is uncertain this chapter says so. [n53, note on sources; n21] The second is the newspaper, the activist report, the planning document and the court order of the last thirty years, many of them read only as a search engine’s summary, and marked as such. Between them lie Da Cunha’s Notes on the History and Antiquities of Chaul and Bassein of 1876, a handful of scientific papers, and the census. The two kinds of evidence describe the same land, and they agree about it more often than one might expect.

The rock: “as fine and hard as granite”

Vasai stands on basalt, though little of it shows. The dark volcanic rock of the Deccan, laid down in the eruptions that Chapter 01 describes, underlies the whole coast; the Gazetteer called trap “the lowest rock” of the district. [n48 §2; Gaz XIII-I, geology] On the plain the basalt is buried under silt, sand and black soil. It comes to the surface in the hills to the east, in a few knolls and dykes on the plain, and in the stone of the old city.

That stone was famous. “In the beginning of the seventeenth century (1607),” the Gazetteer wrote, “Bassein was a great place for ship-building and had a large trade in timber and building stone, which was as fine and hard as granite, and was used in all the Goa churches and palaces.” [n36 §4; Gaz XIV pp. 30–31] Large basalt columns went from Bassein to Goa, and the Portuguese viceroy Dom João de Castro remarked on the basalt columns across from Bassein as early as 1538. [n36 §4; Gaz XIII-II pp. 465–468] The great Franciscan church of Santo António inside the fort was built of dressed stone “with basalt dressings”. [n32; Gaz XIV pp. 41–42] The stone of Vasai built not only Vasai but the capital of Portuguese Asia.

The plain has its own outcrops. The Gazetteer’s tour of the Sopara country began at the Manikpur bungalow, near what is now Vasai Road station, and named the hills that rise from the salt waste and rice ground: Vajirgad, four miles west of the bungalow, with the ruins of a Portuguese fort; Brahma hill, a bush- and palm-covered knoll; and Tulinj, the Rakshi Dongar, to the north, with its columnar dyke. [n53 A1, B1; Gaz XIV pp. 315, 342] Tulinj and its basalt pillars belong to Nallasopara and are told in Chapter 24. What matters for Vasai is that the hard rock lay close at hand, and that the people of the plain used it. Old footways of broken basalt blocks crossed the muddy hollows of the island, and the older houses of the garden villages stood on basalt plinths. [n53 B1; n11; Gaz XIV pp. 315, 342]

No geological survey specific to Vasai taluka was found in the research for this book. The Central Ground Water Board’s report on Thane district exists but was not read, and there is no citable map of where the basalt lies near the surface and where the silt is deep. [n53 A1 Gaps] What the record holds is the Gazetteer’s description and the evidence of what people built.

Tungar and its laterite cap

East of the railway, across the old salt waste and the rice flats, the ground rises to Tungar, the long flat-topped hill that is Vasai’s eastern skyline. The Gazetteer described it as “about thirty miles north of Bombay”, 2,200 feet high, about ten miles from the sea. “Like most Konkan hills, Tungar is trap, capped with a layer of iron-clay or laterite.” [n12 §7; Gaz XIV pp. 366–367] Laterite is the red, iron-rich rock that forms when basalt weathers for ages under a hot, wet sky; elsewhere in the same volume the Gazetteer described it as “red, of very coarse grain”, soft when quarried and hardening on exposure. [n48 §2; Gaz XIII-I, Minerals] The north and east faces of Tungar carried “magnificent forests”; the south and west slopes, the slopes that face Vasai, were gentler, with bamboo-dominated forest. The Vaitarna washed the hill’s foot to the north and “the Bassein river”, the Ulhas, to the south. [n12 §7; Gaz XIV pp. 366–367]

At the southern end of the ridge stands Kamandurg, “the finely rounded peak”, which travellers in the 1880s saw from Sopara’s Brahma hill at the end of “the level line of Tungar”. [n12 §6; Gaz XIV] (The Gazetteer gives Tungar 2,200 feet in its Places volume and 2,800 feet in the general description that Chapter 30 quotes; the two figures are as printed.) [n12 §7; n48 §2]

The foothills are where Vasai’s hard-rock economy lives today: the quarries and stone crushers of the villages around Tungareshwar, told below. They are also where the city’s drinking water once came from, and where its last leopards live. The foot of Tungar was reached from Vasai Road by a government road built in 1869–71, “bridged, drained, embanked, and metalled” from the station to Gokirva (Gokhivare), and then up the hill; the Gokirva-to-top stretch cost £855, or Rs 8,555. [n12 §4; Gaz XIV pp. 366–367] The road was built for a hill station that was never made, the “poor man’s Matheran” whose story is told in Chapter 12. At Gokirva in 1882 there were “a shrine of Mahadev, a pond, and the remains of an old Portuguese stockade”. [n12 §4; Gaz XIV pp. 366–367]

The garden island and its sand

Between the hills and the sea lies the plain, and the Vasai part of it was, for most of recorded history, the southern end of an island.

“Under the Portuguese the whole of this rich strip, with its patches of salt marsh, was known as the island of Bassein, and, before Portuguese times, as the island of Sopara.” [n11 Q1; Gaz XIV pp. 314–315] The strip was about three miles broad and ran from the Bassein creek in the south to the Vaitarna in the north. It was cut off from the mainland on the east by a backwater that left the Bassein creek “a little above the railway bridge” and wound north to the Vaitarna. The people of 1882 divided the island into three gardens, agar: the agar of Agashi in the north, the agar of Sopara in the middle, and the agar of Bassein in the south. [n11 Q1; n48 §1; Gaz XIV pp. 314–315] The southern garden was Vasai’s.

What made it a garden was its soil and its water. The Gazetteer divided the land of the district into “sweet” and “salt”: the black shet rice fields and red hill slopes on one side, the salt marsh on the other. “In the garden lands of Bassein and Mahim,” it noted, “the black soil is lighter and more sandy than in the interior.” [n53 A1; n48 §1; Gaz XIII-I pp. 280–281] The light sandy soil drained well and took water from shallow wells, and on it the cultivators had made something remarkable. “The most hardworking and skilful husbandmen are the Christians of Bassein,” the Gazetteer said, who grew sugarcane and plantains and had “turned the light sandy country about Bassein into an evergreen garden”. [n21 §2; Gaz XIII-I p. 300] The district’s statistical account described the same thing in plainer terms: a coast strip about three miles broad of rich alluvial soil, watered by Persian wheels from shallow wells, with plantains, sugarcane and coconuts the chief products. [n21 §2; Gaz XIII-II p. 679]

The brief for this chapter speaks of a “sand belt” along the shore, and the beaches of Vasai, Suruchi, Rajodi, Bhuigaon, Kalamb and the rest, are its visible edge; Suruchi beach takes its name from its suru, casuarina, trees. [n53 A7; n11 Q2] But no source in the research gives the width or extent of the sand belt, and no survey of the coastal dunes or their loss was found. [n53 A1 Gaps] What can be said is that the Gazetteer’s “light sandy” garden soil and its “long sandy spits running into muddy shallows” along the shore describe a coast where sea sand and river silt met, and that the gardens were made on the sandier, better-drained ground between the beach and the salt marsh. [n53 A1; Gaz XIII-I pp. 2–3, 280–281]

Inland of the gardens, and between them and the creeks, lay the salt land. In the words of the Gazetteer’s general description, the shores of the district were “flat, with long sandy spits running into muddy shallows”, divided “by wide wastes of salt marsh”, with “slightly rising ground, covered by palmyra trees” toward the hills. [n53 A1; Gaz XIII-I pp. 2–3] The city development plan of 2010 gives the same ground a modern name and a number: the coastal strip, the kharpat, runs from Chikhal-Dongri in the north to Naigaon in the south, “hardly 3 to 4 ft. above sea level”, and the average elevation of the whole area is 1.5 to 2 metres. [n48 §3–4; CDP pp. 24–27] That figure, a metre and a half to two metres above the sea, is the single most important fact about Vasai’s land. Everything in the flood story below follows from it.

The creeks

Vasai is bounded and shaped by two creeks, one great and one small.

The Vasai creek

The great one is the Vasai creek, the old Bassein creek or Bassein river, which the Portuguese knew as the river of Baçaim. It is one of the two mouths of the Ulhas river; the other is the Thane creek. Between them lies Salsette island, the island of Bombay’s northern suburbs, and the Vasai creek forms Salsette’s northern boundary, with Vasai on its north bank. [n53 A1; Wikipedia: Vasai Creek] The Gazetteer described the rivers of the district generally as meeting the tide and winding “among low mangrove-covered salt marshes, along channels of mud, with occasional bands of rock”, navigable at high water “for boats of from five to sixty tons”, and noted that most had two names, one for the fresh upper river and one for the salt lower creek. [n53 A1; Gaz XIII-I p. 8] The Vaitarna and the Ulhas estuaries, it said, “form the northern and southern limits of the Bassein sub-division”. [n53 A1; Gaz XIII-I p. 9]

The creek was Vasai’s harbour, its moat, its fishery and its salt field. The Portuguese city was built on its north bank, facing it, and the sea gate of the fort opened onto it. The salt works of 1881 were “widely scattered on the north bank of the Bassein creek”. [n21 §3; Gaz XIII-I p. 364] The fishing villages of Killa Bandar and Pachubandar stand on it. And from 1864 the railway crossed it.

The first railway bridge over the Vasai creek, carrying the Bombay, Baroda and Central India line from Bhayandar to the Bassein side with Panju island in the middle, opened in 1864 and was rebuilt in the 1920s; it is now a non-operational heritage bridge, with two newer rail bridges in service beside it. [n53 A1; Wikipedia: Vasai Creek (secondary)] Da Cunha noted that an embankment across the Kalyan creek to Bassein island had been sanctioned by the Court of Directors in 1840, and that by 1876 an iron railway bridge connected Bassein with Bombay. [n32 §5; Da Cunha p. 157] Road traffic waited longer. The first road bridge over the creek on the Mumbai–Ahmedabad highway, at Ghodbunder and Naigaon, was built in 1968; as it weakened, a second, four-lane bridge was begun alongside in 2018 and opened on 27 March 2023, a cantilever span of 918 metres with 1.33 kilometres of approach roads, at a reported cost of over Rs 247 crore. [n12 §5; Mumbai Live; Swarajya; FPJ] In February 2024 a roll-on, roll-off ferry between Vasai and Bhayandar was launched as a three-month pilot, carrying a hundred passengers and thirty-three vehicles over about three and a half kilometres of water; it was halted on 25 February after the vessel hit the Vasai jetty, and its later status was not confirmed. [n36 §8; ThePrint; Lokmat Times] The creek that had carried Portuguese galleys was, briefly, a ferry route again.

Panju

In the middle of the creek, where the old railway bridge touches down, lies Panju island, with two smaller islands near it. [n53 A1; Wikipedia: Vasai Creek] Panju is an Agri island, and its story, as told by The Better India in 2017, is a small history of the creek in itself. In the years of the freedom struggle it had at most three hundred residents, twenty-one of whom are remembered as freedom fighters. Piped water reached it only in 1983, and electricity in 1989. In 2017 it had roughly a hundred fishing boats and sand barges, many of them derelict, and a single old boat linked it to Naigaon station about every fifteen minutes. [n53 A1; The Better India, 4 May 2017] The census of 2011 counted 1,358 people on Panju, one of the fifty villages of Vasai taluka still classed as rural. [n12 §1; census2011.co.in] No source gives Panju’s area. [n53 A1 Gaps] Its boats, half fishing and half sand, tell the two economies of the creek that this chapter returns to below.

The Manickpur creek

The small creek is the southern arm of the backwater that made the island. In 1882 it was “locally known by three names, the Manikpur creek in the south, the Sopara creek in the centre, and the Bolinj creek in the north”. [n53 B1; Gaz XIV pp. 314–315] The Manikpur, or Manickpur, creek was the stretch nearest Vasai, leaving the Bassein creek just above the railway bridge. It had once been “a considerable arm of the sea overflowing a large breadth of land on both banks”. But “with the mud-laden Vaitarna on the north and the Bassein creek or Ulhas river on the south, silting has been rapid, especially since the long railway embankment has dammed the flow of water from the south.” [n53 B1; n36 §6; Gaz XIV pp. 314–315] Boats of ten or twelve tons still came up from the south as far as Gass in 1882, but no farther. [n53 B1]

The silting had been going on for a long time. A Portuguese report of 1728 already complained that the creek between Sopara and Bolinj was fordable at low water for about four thousand feet, and warned that unless it were dug “as deep as the height of a man” it would soon fill “to a level with the fields”. The same report noted that General Louis de Mello Pereira had built watch-towers of coconut-palm stems and a bridge across the creek at Gokirva, Gokhivare, and complained that the bridge was unprotected. [n12 §1; n36 §6; Gaz XIV p. 322] By 1882 the creek was crossed “only by the railway and the bridges at Bolinj and Gokhivare”. [n53 A1; Gaz XIII-I p. 11] The bridge at Gokhivare stood on the old Portuguese crossing.

The Manickpur creek gave its name to the railway station. In 1882 Bassein Road station was also called Manikpur, and Da Cunha described the Manikpura station as crossing “a bridge over the channel separating the island from the mainland”, four and a half miles from the fort. [n32 §9; n11 Q2; Da Cunha p. 157] The story of the creek’s death, and of what the railway embankment did to Sopara, belongs to Chapter 24. For Vasai the consequence is the same: the drain that once carried the island’s monsoon water out to the sea at both ends had been dammed, silted and finally built over, and the railway towns of Vasai Road and Nallasopara sit on its bed. [n12 §1 inference]

Mangroves and salt flats

Where the creeks met the land, the mangroves grew. The Gazetteer’s rivers wound “among low mangrove-covered salt marshes”. [n53 A1; Gaz XIII-I p. 8] Behind the mangroves lay the salt flats, the tidal mud that the sea covered at spring tides and that people had turned, over centuries, into salt pans and, where they could keep the sea out, into embanked rice fields.

The struggle between land and sea was still visible in 1882. Embanked rice land, the Gazetteer said, took “twenty or twenty-five years” to sweeten; the sea still flooded “a large area at spring tides”; and since 1724 “four villages have been submerged” at the mouth of the Vaitarna, north of Vasai. [n53 A2; Gaz XIII-I p. 281] It was a coast where the line between land and water moved within living memory.

The salt flats of Vasai have been made into three things in turn. In the nineteenth century they were salt pans, worked on the north bank of the Bassein creek; their story is told below. In the planning documents of the late twentieth century they became “salt pan land” and “no development zone”. And in the twenty-first century they became building land, and floodplain. By the sanctioned development plan of 2007, salt pans covered 1,065 hectares of the urbanisable zone of the Vasai–Virar sub-region, 11.4 per cent of it, and the low-lying lands along the coast and the two creeks were “marshy, khazan lands”, some of them, “mainly to the south of Nallasopara Railway Station”, still used for salt. [n48 §3–4; CDP pp. 28, 66] The Conservation Action Trust put the salt pans of Vasai at about two thousand acres; Mumbai had about 5,379 and Palghar another two thousand or so, though the sources differ. [n17; n53 A6; Asian Age; CAT]

The mangroves themselves were never counted for Vasai alone. A weakly sourced blog repost of a 2009 newspaper story claimed that about two hundred acres of mangroves had been cleared in one year on the Mira–Bhayandar side of the creek, and that the creek’s mangroves, about three thousand hectares along forty-seven kilometres, were half destroyed; it is quoted here only as a claim. [n17; blog repost, 2009] Residents objecting to the regional plan in 2018 said that mangroves had been cleared from over a hundred acres for buildings, and “that the sea moves further onto land every day”. [n17; n53 A2; The Quint, 7 Mar 2018] No hectare figure from a survey was found. [n53 A4 Gaps] The mangrove fights of the 2020s, over railway and power lines, are told below under living things.

Rain, wind and the bad years

Vasai’s climate is the monsoon’s. Almost all the year’s rain falls between June and September; the south-west monsoon arrives in the first week of June; July is the wettest month. The city development plan of 2010 gives an annual average of about 2,200 millimetres, “2200–2500 mm”, with July averaging about 800, and says ninety-five per cent of the rain falls in the monsoon months; winter, from October to January, has mean maxima about 28°C and minima about 16°C, and summer, from February to May, about 34°C and 26°C. [n48 §3; CDP pp. 26–27] A local figure quoted by 101Reporters for the Arnala–Vasai coast is about 1,586 millimetres a year. [n17] The Gazetteer of 1882 recorded that Bassein’s rainfall had averaged 71.87 inches, about 1,825 millimetres, in the ten years to 1881. [n21 §10; Gaz XIII-II p. 678] The three figures are not reconciled, and no official rain-gauge series for Vasai taluka was found. [n53 A2 Gaps]

The rain was the farmer’s capital, and its failure was his ruin. The Gazetteer recorded the bad seasons. In 1824–25 the rain failed and crops were very scanty in Dahanu, Bassein and Salsette; grain sold at famine rates, though no one died of starvation, and the government spent £1,550 clearing reservoirs. In 1837 the crops failed in Sanjan and Bassein for want of rain, and remissions of about £4,500, Rs 45,000, were granted. In 1838–39 crops failed across the whole district. In 1848 most of the salt rice-land crop failed. In 1850 the rainfall was much below average and the coast suffered drought, with remissions of £694 on sweet rice land and £1,103 on salt rice land. And in 1854, in Bassein, the salt rice crops “were partly injured by worms”. [n21 §10; Gaz XIII-I p. 303] Those are the dry years. The wet years, the floods, are hardly recorded before the twentieth century, perhaps because a coast of gardens, ponds, paddy and salt marsh absorbed the water that now runs off concrete.

The sea brought storms as well as trade. In 1618 a cyclone blew a boat into a house at Agashi and wrecked “provision boats” at Bassein. [n36 §7; Gaz XIV pp. 1, 31] Three and a half centuries later, Cyclone Tauktae, in May 2021, damaged fifteen to twenty boats at Vasai, at about Rs 2 lakh each, by a summary of local reports. [n53 A6; FPJ/HT (summary)] And the sea brought disease: the cholera of 1881 “began among the fishermen of Kelva Mahim in April and prevailed in Bassein from May to July”. [n21 §4; Gaz XIII-II pp. 665–667]

The floods, 2005–2026

On a plain a metre and a half above the sea, rain that cannot drain away stands. For centuries it drained into the creeks, the salt marsh, the paddy fields that stood under water each monsoon, and the hundreds of ponds of the garden villages. As those were filled, silted and built on, the water had nowhere to go but the streets. The dated floods of modern Vasai are a sequence, and it begins with the great Mumbai deluge.

26 July 2005. The deluge that put 944 millimetres of rain on Santacruz in a day and killed more than four hundred people across the Mumbai region is remembered in Vasai as the benchmark flood, the one later floods are measured against: a Vasai resident told the Deccan Herald that a later flood was worse than 2005. But no Vasai-specific rainfall figure, death toll or damage account for 2005 was found. [n53 A2; Deccan Herald (summary)] Chapter 15 tells the deluge for the region.

March 2018, looking back at 2017. Before the great floods of July 2018, residents objecting to the Mumbai Metropolitan Region Development Authority’s draft regional plan for 2016–36 wrote that during the previous monsoon a village had stood under water for five or six days and at least six people had drowned. [n17; n53 A2; The Quint, 7 Mar 2018] Their objection, told below under salt, was that the plan would build on exactly the land that flooded.

July 2018. On 10 July 2018 Mumbai recorded 184 millimetres in twenty-four hours, its highest one-day total in four years; the National Disaster Response Force rescued about 1,500 passengers from trains stranded in the floods, at Nallasopara among other places, with the Navy evacuating commuters. [n53 A2; Business Standard/ANI; NewsBytes (summary)] On 13 July, the fifth day, residents of Vasai and Nallasopara were still stranded. The corporation’s commissioner, Satish Lokhande, blamed sludge choking the drains. [n53 A2; NewsBytes, 13 July 2018 (summary)] The environmentalists did not blame the drains. Debi Goenka of the Conservation Action Trust said that wetland reclamation in Thane and Vasai had “shrunk the natural drainage”; Stalin Dayanand of Vanashakti called Vasai–Virar a natural floodplain of wetlands and salt pans, unsuited to townships on raised fill; and more than sixty per cent of Thane’s wetlands, they said, were gone. [n17; Asian Age via CAT, 13 July 2018] A report by the National Environmental Engineering Research Institute and IIT Bombay followed; in 2025 the Deccan Herald said its recommendations had not been carried out. The report itself was not found. [n17; Deccan Herald, 2025]

2019. A Free Press Journal report, on a page stamped 1 June 2019 but describing a monsoon rescue, probably the deluge of early July 2019, said the Vasai fire brigade rescued twenty-one sand workers at Chandip and five people at Khaniwade, and that the villages of Navsai, Usgaon, Shiravali, Bhatane and Parol, home to about thirty thousand people, were “always under flood threat”; ground-floor flats in Nallasopara societies at Tulinj flooded. [n53 A2; FPJ (date unverified)] These are the villages of the Tansa and the eastern foothills, and they show that Vasai’s eastern side floods from the river as well as from the sea.

June and August 2025. In June 2025 an overnight downpour waterlogged Gala Nagar circle, by a search summary. [n53 A2 (summary)] Western Railway blamed waterlogging on its tracks that year on unplanned building on salt-pan land and on mangroves and waterholes in Nallasopara and Virar, on choked culverts, and on overflow from the Pelhar dam. [n17; PropNewsTime] On 18 August 2025 heavy rain brought the corporation’s largest relief operation up to then: about 497 residents rescued, more than ten thousand food packets distributed, fifty-four suction pumps run and thirty-four relief centres opened. [n53 A2; FPJ, 19 Aug 2025]

The Khanivade dam

The first water crisis of 2026 came not from rain but from the sea. On the Tansa creek, in the eastern villages, a dam built in 1984 at Khanivade held back fresh water for irrigation. In January 2026 the Free Press Journal reported that the dam was leaking, and that the tides were pushing saline water through it into the irrigation reservoir behind. The summer crops of the farmers who drew on it were at risk. Grouting done four years earlier, the report said, had only partly worked. [n53 A2; FPJ, 24 Jan 2026] The newspaper placed the dam in Vasai East; Khanivade itself, a village of 2,386 people in 2011, lies on the Vaitarna–Tansa side, and Chapter 30 tells the Tansa villages. [n12 §1]

The Khanivade leak is a small story with a long shadow. The whole agricultural history of this coast, from the embanked rice lands that took twenty-five years to sweeten in 1882 to the irrigation dam of 1984, is a history of keeping salt water out of fresh. The leak of 2026 was the sea finding a way back in. The research found no report of whether the dam was repaired, or of what the summer harvest of 2026 was. [n53 A2]

July 2026

Then came the rain. On 1 July 2026, after three days of downpour, 150 millimetres fell in twenty-four hours. At Rajavali Waghralpada in Vasai East a drain swept away five vehicles, two cars and three two-wheelers, and residents confronted the corporation’s commissioner, Prithviraj B.P., in anger. [n53 A2; FPJ, 2 July 2026] The next day the corporation’s divisions recorded an average of 78.25 millimetres; the headline figure of “690 mm” that the newspapers carried was the sum of the readings across eight divisions, not a rainfall anyone experienced. The Viva College area, Kumbharpada Road, Shriprastha Road and Sun City Gas Road in Virar were under water, with many places in Nallasopara, and the Mumbai–Ahmedabad highway was waterlogged. [n53 A2; FPJ, 1 July 2026]

On 4 July the corporation’s own headquarters flooded. [n53 A2; FPJ, 4 July 2026] Over that weekend, by the account of one outlet, about 750 millimetres fell between Saturday and Monday, and “about 70% of the urban conglomeration” was submerged; the main substation of the state electricity company at Vasai went under water and the supply was cut across the region; the National Disaster Response Force and the fire brigade rescued about 250 people; auto-rickshaws charged ten to fifteen times the fare. [n53 A2; Khaleej Times, 8 July 2026] The 750 millimetres and the seventy per cent come from a single outlet and are unverified. On 6 and 7 July the suburban trains between Vasai Road and Virar were suspended at the evening peak on both days, and tractors carried people to the stations for Rs 100 to 150 a head; that story, and the Western Railway’s decision to survey raising the track, belong to Nallasopara and Virar and are told in Chapters 24 and V4. [n53 B2; FPJ, 8 July 2026]

By 13 July nine people had died in the rains in Vasai–Virar. Among them were a body found in the Waliv pond in Vasai East and one behind the Vasai West crematorium. [n53 A2; The Quint, 13 July 2026] The pond at Waliv, in the industrial belt, was one of the old water bodies of the plain; in July 2026 it held a drowned man.

The relief of September 2026

Floods are counted afterwards, in forms. The revenue officers of Vasai tehsil drew up panchnamas, the itemised records of loss, for the flooding of 5 to 10 July 2026, and found about nineteen thousand residents affected. By 22 September, the Free Press Journal reported, fifteen thousand of them had been paid Rs 20,000 each, a total of Rs 30 crore, and about four thousand cases were pending; a camp to clear them was held at the Vasai tehsil office from 21 to 25 September. [n53 A2; FPJ, 22 Sept 2026] (The relief window, 5 to 10 July, differs from the 4 to 8 July of the rain reports; both are as printed.) [n53 A2]

Rs 20,000 a household is a measure of what the state judged a flood to cost a family. The Gazetteer’s remissions of 1837, Rs 45,000 for a failed harvest, were the same instrument in another century: the state paying the cultivator for weather. What had changed was who was paid, and for what. In 1837 it was the farmer, for the rain that did not come; in 2026 it was the flat-dweller, for the rain that came and could not leave.

The pattern of the floods, from 2017 to 2026, is the land’s pattern. The plain is low. Its old drains, the creeks, the salt marsh, the paddy, the ponds, have been silted, filled or built on. The railway embankment that dammed the Sopara creek in the 1860s still runs across the line of the water’s escape, now carrying more tracks. And the rain is the same rain the Gazetteer measured. The causal link between filled wetlands and floods is argued by activists and by the railway itself; no published study proving it for Vasai was found. [n17 Inferences; n53 A2 Inferences]

Water under the ground and in the ponds

Under the plain lies water, and pressing in on it from the sea, salt. Vasai’s water history is a layered one: the wells and ponds of the gardens, the brackish groundwater that the creek feeds, and, since the 1990s, the long search for drinking water from farther and farther away.

The two reservoirs of 1882

In 1882 the Bassein sub-division had, by the Gazetteer’s count, only two reservoirs worth the name. One was the Nirmal lake, 4,488 feet by 1,551, holding water for about eleven months, ten feet deep at most; it belongs to Chapter 24. The other was in Vasai town itself: the Dhavpani-tirth at Malonde, 800 feet by 275, perennial and fifteen feet deep. [n53 A3; Gaz XIII-I pp. 12–13] Malonde was the village in which the modern town of Bassein stood, outside the fort walls; the municipality of 1864 included “the old fort and most of the modern town which stand in the village of Malonde”. [n21 §9; Gaz XIV p. 36] So the town had, at its heart, the only perennial reservoir of the sub-division, a tirth, a holy bathing place, that never ran dry. No recent report of the Dhavpani-tirth’s condition, or even whether it survives under that name, was found. [n53 A3 Gaps]

Inside the fort there were wells. A news report of the excavation at Balle Killa, the citadel, described a “secret room”, artefacts and several wells that had been a major water source; it was read only as a search-engine summary. [n32 §3; DNA (summary)]

Persian wheels and unbuilt wells

The gardens drew their water from the ground. “Those in Bassein and Mahim, which are much the best in the district, are watered by Persian wheels from unbuilt wells,” the Gazetteer said: unlined pits in the sandy soil, worked by bullocks walking round a wheel that lifted a chain of pots. [n53 A3; Gaz XIII-I p. 281] The garden island in 1882 was full of them; its ponds and lakes were “fringed by high pile-supported Persian wheels”. [n53 B3; Gaz XIV p. 314] The district’s statistical account counted, for the Bassein sub-division in 1881–82, 191 ponds and 2,624 wells, twenty-five of them with steps. [n21 §2; Gaz XIII-II pp. 678–679] No source describes when the Persian wheels stopped turning, or what replaced them; by the time the modern sources begin, the talk is of bore wells and pumps. [n17 Gaps]

The bavkhal

Beside the wells were the ponds, and the ponds of the Vasai gardens had a name and a law of their own. A bavkhal (also written bawkhal or bhowkal) was an irrigation pond, dug to hold the monsoon and fed through the dry months by the unconfined aquifer below; it was never used for drinking or washing. Each was owned by a family group of five to sixty people. [n17; n53 A3; Vikalp Sangam, Sept 2019] Marcus Dabre, editor of the Vasai Times, explained that the bavkhals hold rainwater and feed the fields through underground connections. [n17; 101Reporters, Jan 2023] Macanzy Dabre of the Paryavaran Samvardhan Samiti put the hydrology in a sentence: “Though close to the sea, water bodies hold brackish water, linked subterraneously.” [n53 A3; Vikalp Sangam]

That is the key to the whole system. Under the garden villages, the groundwater is a thin layer of fresh water floating on salt. The bavkhals, filled each monsoon, pressed fresh water down into the ground and kept the salt beneath at bay. As long as there were enough ponds, and enough rain, the wells stayed sweet. [n53 A3 Inferences]

The numbers show how many there were. A Minor Irrigation survey of 2007 counted 479 traditional water ponds in twenty-seven gram panchayats. A citizen study, Water Crisis in Vasai, counted 108 lakes within a sixty-kilometre span from Arnala to Malonde and from Achole to Umelle, of which only twenty-eight survived. [n17; n53 A3; Vikalp Sangam]

The Kupari ponds: Bhuigaon, Nandakhal and Giriz

The pond story is told most fully from the villages of the Kuparis, the Samvedi Christians whose garden villages lie in the green belt north and west of Vasai town, and whose history is the subject of Part Seven of this book. Bhuigaon, Nandakhal and Wagholi, the three villages where the pond survey of 2013 was done, are all named by the community’s own writers among its villages. [n11 Q2; n17; Rajan D’Mello blog, 2009]

In 2013 the Yuva Vikas Sanstha, a volunteer group, walked these villages and counted their ponds, and found far more than the official records showed. At Bhuigaon the records listed sixteen ponds; the volunteers found thirty-nine. At Nandakhal the records said thirty-two; there were fifty-five. At Wagholi the records said sixteen; there were fifty-four. [n17; n53 A3; Vikalp Sangam] The undercount is itself a finding. The ponds were private, family property, maintained by custom; the state had never fully known they were there.

From 2011 the volunteers desilted eight ponds in five villages, and the results showed in the wells around them: the total dissolved solids in nearby wells, a measure of salt and minerals, fell from 750 parts per million to 300. [n53 A3; Vikalp Sangam] Fresh water poured back into the ponds pushed the salt down again, as it had for centuries.

The ponds had been in decline since the boom. They decayed after the mid-1990s, the volunteers found, “as the paddy fields and the family orchards gave way to high-rise buildings”, and many became garbage dumps that contaminated the groundwater beneath them. [n17; Vikalp Sangam] At Bhuigaon a blogger who walked the village in 2011 found about forty large irrigation wells, bhowkal, still kept by the women of the village, watering vegetables, hybrid bananas and flowers and holding back monsoon water for paddy. The extended Marti family acted as the village’s “territorial protectors”, and a stainless-steel cross, the Madikar Cross, in the western quarter served as “the local equivalent of a Gaondevi”, a village goddess; houses carried a cross at the front door, and the dialect was Kadodi, the Kupari speech. [n11 Q2; Mumbai Paused blog, 2011] Bhuigaon’s Holy Family church had been founded only in 1946, for families who had walked far to the parish at Nirmal. [n11 Q2] The pond revival at Bhuigaon has a name attached to it: Sachin Marti, who from about 2010, The Better India reports, began reviving the village ponds when he saw villagers hauling water despite the ponds near them. [n53 B3; The Better India (summary)] In August 2025 the Times of India carried a piece on reviving Vasai’s traditional ponds to resolve its water woes, known to this research only by its headline. [n53 B3; AKDN listing]

At Giriz, the next Kupari village, the pond is in the name of a place to eat. A home-chef couple, Regina and Bernard, serve visitors potato chops, fugias and sukeli, the dried banana of the old gardens, “opposite Giriz Talao”, the Giriz pond, after a walk through the village. [n50 §3; Homegrown guide (commercial listing)] The parish of Giriz was itself built by the villagers’ own labour: its church of St Francis Xavier was begun on 14 February 1917 and finished in 1918 “without foreign aid”, so that the people of Giriz need no longer walk to distant parishes in the monsoon. [n11 Q2; Wikipedia: St Francis Xavier’s Church, Giriz]

What the Kupari record adds to the pond story is the human side of the turn. The community’s own writers say the Kuparis depended mostly on farming until the late 1960s and early 1970s, and then moved into professions and emigration. [n11 Q3; Rajan D’Mello blog, 2009] The ponds needed hands to desilt them each dry season, and the hands left for offices in Bombay and the Gulf; the land around them was sold for buildings. The bavkhal did not fail because the hydrology changed; it failed because the people who had kept it for centuries stopped being farmers. The revival, where it has happened, has been the work of those who stayed, and of volunteers. No source counts how many of Bhuigaon’s thirty-nine or Nandakhal’s fifty-five ponds survive in 2026. [n53 A3 Gaps]

Salt in the wells

Where the ponds were lost, the salt came in. From the early 1990s, builders and tanker operators drew groundwater from the green belt for the new colonies, and the farmers’ wells turned saline; the Harit Vasai movement led by Fr Francis D’Britto won a ban on tankers drawing water there. [n29 §1; Sabrang India] By 2012 about thirteen lakh people depended on tankers and bore wells, and tankers moved about fifteen million litres a day from bore wells off the Mumbai–Ahmedabad highway. [n17; n53 A3; Vikalp Sangam] The city plan of 2010 counted thirty-four tube wells and 275 bore wells at Vasai, and warned that “over drawal of water from underground is resulting into the ingress of salinity”, so that the “potability of water is adversely affected”. [n48 §3; CDP p. 94]

Science confirms the residents. A conference paper by D. Chandrasekharam and colleagues, of about 2000, identified “two saline groundwater zones” in the Vasai–Palghar coastal belt and attributed the contamination to salt-panning; the Central Ground Water Board’s Thane report described irrigation groundwater of “medium to high salinity”; and studies of coastal Maharashtra generally put salinity intrusion at two to ten kilometres inland. All three were known to this research only through search summaries. [n53 A3 (summaries)] A Lok Sabha reply, likewise known from a summary, said the Central Ground Water Board’s studies in the Vasai–Virar area “have indicated possible sea-water ingress”, a “localized phenomenon”. [n48 §3 (summary)] No village-by-village salinity map for Vasai was found. [n53 A3 Gaps]

Water from the hills and beyond

As the local water turned salt, the city reached outward for water, first to its own hills and then much farther.

Pelhar, Usgaon and the dry 1990s

The first piped schemes drew on small dams at the edge of the hills. The Tungareshwar forest holds the catchments of the Pelhar dam and the Juchandra tank, “which supply water to Vasai and Nallasopara”. [n53 A3; DNA, 25 Apr 2017] An environmental summary of 2014 listed the existing supply of the sub-region: Pelhar at 14 million litres a day, Usgaon at 24.5, Papadkhind at 1.5, and a “Maswan” source of 100, a figure that looks high and is given as reported. [n53 A3; Surya EIA executive summary, 2014]

None of it was enough. When the green belt was opened to building in 1988, the infrastructure did not follow. Between 1991 and 1996, by the account kept on the TIFR “Bombay” site, power was available two days a week; there was no piped water and no central drainage; less than ten per cent of the area had motorable roads; and in 1996 some residents got as little as ten litres of water a day. [n17; n53 A3; TIFR] Ten litres is a bucket. The population had grown by 150 per cent between 1981 and 1991 across the region, and by 290 per cent in Vasai, Virar, Sopara and Sandor, driven by commuters to Mumbai. [n17; TIFR] The people came before the pipes.

Surya

The answer, when it came, came from a river forty kilometres north. The Surya Regional Bulk Water Supply Scheme of the Mumbai regional authority was planned at 403 million litres a day in all: 218 for Mira-Bhayandar, fifteen for the rural villages, and 170 for the Vasai–Virar corporation, earlier set at 185. [n53 A3; Surya EIA executive summary] It was commissioned in 2023, reached the corporation in late 2023, and was dedicated by the Prime Minister on 12 January 2024. [n12 §7; n06 §4; Indian Infrastructure] A 2023 report put the corporation’s need at 372 million litres a day against 230 received from all sources. [n53 A3 (summary)] On one occasion a transformer failure halted a 140 million-litre flow, by a search summary. [n53 A3 (summary)] A dam at Kholsapada, more than eighty per cent complete in June 2026, was expected to add twenty-two million litres a day, and the regional authority’s Deharji project more. [n12 §7; FPJ, 14 June 2026]

So Vasai’s water, which in 1882 came from the Dhavpani-tirth at Malonde, the unbuilt wells of the gardens and the family bavkhals, comes in 2026 through a pipe from the Surya, pumped by electricity that a flood can cut. In March 2026 the corporation proposed to double the water tax. [n53 A7; FPJ, 11 Mar 2026] On 15 November 2026 a Marathi daily warned that reservoir levels were falling and scarcity was feared, and that Vasai–Virar’s historic lakes were choked with garbage and water hyacinth. [n06 §4; eSakal, 15 Nov 2026] The supply figures across sources conflict, and no current figure for what Surya actually delivers was found. [n53 A3 Gaps]

What goes out

The water that comes in must go out, and much of it goes out dirty. The Maharashtra Pollution Control Board’s action plan for the Pelhar river, of July 2019, traced the non-perennial river nine or ten kilometres from the Pelhar dam to the Sopara creek, fed by three drains: the Sopara nallah, the Tungar nallah at Sativali, and the Waliv nallah. The biochemical oxygen demand, a measure of pollution, was 98.6 milligrams a litre in the Waliv nallah, 76.0 in the Sopara nallah and 26.6 in the Tungar nallah. Vasai–Virar generated 105 million litres of sewage a day and treated thirty, in one plant at Bolinj; seven more plants were proposed. The city dumped 630 tonnes of solid waste a day. [n48 §2; MPCB, Pelhar action plan, 2019] The Waliv nallah, the dirtiest of the three, drains Vasai’s industrial belt; it is told below.

Living things

Mangroves and the railway

The mangroves of the Vasai creek are the coast’s first defence against the sea and its nursery for fish, and in the 2020s they stood in the way of two of the region’s largest projects. The Bombay High Court allowed Western Railway to fell 2,612 mangroves for its fifth and sixth lines between Borivali and Virar, at bridges 73 and 75 over the Vasai creek, ordering 7,823 to be planted in their place; the replanting was later moved to Rajodi, on the coast north of Vasai, rather than at the creek itself. [n53 A4; FPJ (summary)] The court also allowed Adani Electricity to fell 209 mangroves near the Vasai creek for a transmission line, against an initial estimate of seventy-nine. [n17; n53 A4; FPJ (summaries)] In 2025 the river and wetlands network SANDRP recorded assaults on mangroves and wetlands at the Vasai creek, among other sites. [n17; SANDRP, 8 Feb 2026] A joint committee of the National Green Tribunal noted that the land adjacent to the creek is in the Coastal Regulation Zone and needs mapping at 1:4,000 to settle its status. [n53 B4; NGT OA 201/2023 (summary)] And in August 2024 environmental groups wrote to the chief minister alleging extensive mining along the Vasai creek. [n53 A4; FPJ (summary)]

Flamingos at the salt pans

The salt pans of Naigaon and Vasai draw birds in winter. A local website claims two to three hundred flamingos at the Naigaon–Vasai salt pans each winter; it is not a scientific count and is unverified. [n53 A4; vasaivirar.org] No bird survey of the Vasai creek itself was found; the rich literature on the flamingos of the Thane creek and Sewri does not transfer. [n53 A4 Gaps]

The leopards of Tungareshwar

East of the city, the slopes of Tungar are a wildlife sanctuary. The Tungareshwar Wildlife Sanctuary was declared protected on 24 October 2003 (one source says November); its area is given as about 85 square kilometres, 85.7 by Wikipedia, and 95.24 in one outlying newspaper report. [n12 §7; n17; n53 A1; DNA; Wikipedia] It is a corridor between the Sanjay Gandhi National Park to the south and the Tansa sanctuary to the north-east, and the forests the Gazetteer called “magnificent” on Tungar’s north and east faces are its core. [n12 §7]

The first systematic count of its leopards was made between February and July 2016, by the Wildlife Conservation Society–India with the Sanjay Gandhi National Park, using camera traps at twenty-nine locations; the study was led by Nikit Surve. It confirmed five leopards, two males, two females and one of unknown sex, against an estimated thirty-five in the national park. It found a low base of the herbivores leopards prey on, and recorded the pressures on the forest: encroachment, illegal felling, illicit liquor breweries and hunting. [n17; n53 A4; DNA, 25 Apr 2017; CAT] The sanctuary’s recorded life runs to about six hundred plant species, more than 250 bird species, 150 butterflies and thirty-six reptiles and amphibians; its mammals include wild boar, barking deer, langur, bonnet and rhesus macaques and the black-naped hare. [n17; Nature in Focus]

In 2024 a leopard was trapped at Vasai Fort, more than fifteen kilometres from the sanctuary, by a summary of reports from the Conservation Action Trust and the Hindustan Times. [n53 A4 (summary)] A leopard among the ruins of the Portuguese city is the clearest sign of how the corridor has narrowed, and of how far an animal will travel along what green is left.

An eco-sensitive zone round the sanctuary was notified in 2018–19, by a gazette of 24 December 2018 cited in a petition by quarry owners; the details of a Supreme Court exemption of 1 December 2022 are known only from a summary. [n53 A4, A7 (summaries)] Inside and around it, the Collector named Sativali, Chinchoti, Pelhar and Mandvi for illegal construction, and issued a stop-work notice on 1 October 2019. [n53 A7; Business World; FPJ (summaries)] The sanctuary’s edge is where the city’s hunger for stone and land meets its last forest; that story continues below, under quarries.

The economy through time

The rest of this chapter follows the work. It begins with the Portuguese city and its taxes, passes through the trade returns of the Maratha and Company years, and settles on the 1880s, when the Gazetteer recorded the economy of Vasai in more detail than any document before or since: its rice and plantains, its betel nut and sugar, its port and boats, its salt and its palm juice. Then it follows each of those trades into the twentieth century, and turns to the new economy that replaced them: the industrial estates of Vasai East, the commuter train, the building boom and the municipal budget, and the visitors who come to the fort.

Baçaim’s revenue: what the Portuguese taxed

The first detailed picture of what Vasai’s people produced is a tax list. In 1535, the year after Sultan Bahadur of Gujarat ceded Bassein “with all its revenues” to Portugal, the Portuguese took the first half-year’s revenue of the Bassein district: 158,475 fedeas, a small coin of twelve reis. It came from taxes and duties on coconut oil, opium, cotton, toddy, vegetables, fish, sugarcane and betel, and on the professions of butchers, dyers, Koli fishermen and shepherds. [n36 §4; Da Cunha pp. 158–159] The list is almost a census of livelihoods. Coconut, toddy, sugarcane and betel are the garden; vegetables and fish are the market; cotton and dyers are cloth; the Koli fishermen are named as a taxed profession in their own right; and opium is the trade that passed through. The same treaty of 1534 kept the 5,000 larins formerly paid from Bassein’s revenues to mosques. [n36 §4; Danvers I p. 416]

Over the next two centuries Bassein became the “Court of the North”, a timber, stone and shipbuilding city more than a trading one; the Gazetteer said it was “never so busy a trade centre as Chaul”. Its timber trade was chiefly in the hands of the captains of the forts and other officials, who in 1587 were accused in a royal letter of supplying timber to the king’s armada at prices “far higher than their value in the market” because they traded on their own account. [n36 §4; Da Cunha p. 144; Gaz XIII-II p. 465] Bassein built ships: between 1550 and 1600 “great ships built at Agashi and Bassein made many voyages to Europe”, and in 1636 two pinnaces built at Bassein for the English factory at Surat, with two from Daman, formed the nucleus of what became the Bombay Marine. [n36 §4; Gaz XIII-II pp. 468–469; Da Cunha] Its exports were rice, pulse, vegetables, coconuts and betel nuts, teak and the basalt columns already described. [n36 §4; Gaz XIII-II pp. 465–468] There was no royal customs house; a private house was rented for the purpose at eighty-four pardaos a year, and the ferry boatmen of the Bassein river were paid two hundred reis a month. [n32 §5; n36 §4; Da Cunha pp. 222–223] The revenue of Bassein rose, by Da Cunha’s figures, to 194,748 xerafins in 1709 and 310,779 in 1718–19, though the digits in the scanned text are uncertain. [n36 §4; Da Cunha pp. 145–146]

That city, its captains, its nobility and its ruin are the subject of Chapters 07, 08 and A1. For this chapter, two things matter. The first is that the Portuguese economy rested on the same land as every later one: rice, coconut, sugarcane, betel, vegetables, fish and stone, the products of the sandy garden, the salt flat and the basalt hill. The second is that by about 1700, by the English traveller Hamilton’s account, “Bassein was a place of small trade, its riches dead and buried in the churches”. [n36 §4; Gaz XIII-II p. 485]

The Maratha town and the Company’s returns

After the Maratha conquest of 1739, Bassein became the headquarters of a Maratha governor, Bajipur, and a base of the Peshwa’s navy; one governor of Bassein, about 1780, was “one of the Peshwa’s admirals”, who “used to attack English ships”. [n36 §5; Gaz XIII-II pp. 498–499, 504] Maratha Vasai is told in Chapter 09. Its economy appears in the record chiefly through the temples its governors built and endowed, at Nirmal and on Tungar, with grants of land. [n12 §7; n11 Q2]

Under the British, from 1802 to 1818 a district of the Company’s, the trade of the Bassein ports appears in the Bombay customs returns. In 1802 their trade with Bombay and Surat was about three and a half lakhs of rupees, two in exports and one and a half in imports; in 1805 it was nine lakhs; and over the five years to 1806 it averaged about nine lakhs, of which about five were exports. In 1805 the exports were piece-goods, grain, iron, sugar, coconuts, betel nut, dates, pepper, turmeric and treasure; the imports ivory, oil, timber, hemp, piece-goods and betel nut. The Gazetteer itself remarked that iron and ivory were odd items, suggesting either a direct trade with Africa or a quirk of recording. [n36 §5; Gaz XIII-II pp. 512–513] Sugar, coconut and betel were already leaving Bassein in quantity two generations before the Gazetteer described the gardens that grew them.

The survey and the rent of a garden

The Company measured the land before it taxed it. “The survey was begun in 1858–59 and finished in 1861–62,” the Gazetteer recorded, and rates were fixed in 1861–62 for thirty years in eighty-eight government villages of the sub-division. Garden land was assessed in three classes, at top rates of Rs 8, Rs 7 and Rs 6 an acre; rice land at much lower rates. Garden rates had been cut by the Revenue Commissioner, Williamson, in 1836–37, because they were too high and there was no market; the survey of 1862 raised them again, “as capital had accumulated”, and garden rental rose from £2,277 to £2,988, while rice rents rose only about three per cent. By 1880 the land revenue of the sub-division was £12,671, about Rs 1,26,710, from 8,064 holdings; the average acre rate on occupied land was about nine annas on dry land, Rs 2-14-9 on rice land and Rs 6-1-9 on garden land. [n21 §7; Gaz XIII-II pp. 609–612, 679] An acre of Vasai garden was worth to the state more than twice an acre of rice. That ratio is the economic shape of the island in one number.

Of about 221 square miles in the sub-division, 64,098 acres, 46.4 per cent, were arable in 1880–81, and 70,635 acres, 51.2 per cent, were village sites, roads, ponds and river beds, with 2,859 acres of unarable land and 328 of grass. [n21 §2; Gaz XIII-II p. 678] (The OCR of these tables is rough, and the large share of “village sites, roads, ponds and river beds” probably includes the salt marsh and creek beds.) [n21 §2]

Rice

The first crop of the plain was rice, as it had been for as long as there is any record. In 1880–81, of about 36,541 acres under tillage on government land in the Bassein sub-division, grain crops covered 31,885 acres, 87 per cent; of these, rice took 29,687 acres, ragi (nachni) 1,846, and other grains a few hundred more. Pulses covered 1,555 acres and oilseeds 575. [n21 §2; Gaz XIII-II p. 680] The district’s rice land was of two kinds, bandhni, banked and floodable, and malkhandi, open; and of two waters, sweet and salt. [n48 §1; Gaz XIII-I pp. 280–281] Salt rice land, the embanked fields reclaimed from the tidal flats, was the land that failed in the bad years of 1848 and 1850 and that worms attacked in 1854. [n21 §10; Gaz XIII-I p. 303]

Rice was the food of almost everyone. The Gazetteer’s caste chapters, read in Chapter 43, give the daily food of community after community as rice: the Agris lived on nagle, coarse rice, and fish; the Pachkalshis on “coarse and fine rice, rice bread, pulse, vegetables, and fish”; the Bhandaris on “rice, rice bread, and rice broth”; the Samvedi Brahmans on “rice pulse and vegetables”. [n50 §1; Gaz XIII-I pp. 81, 116, 129–130, 150] Rice was also an export: husked rice led the list of exports from the three Bassein ports in 1881–82. [n36 §5; Gaz XIII-I pp. 358–359]

Paddy survived in the green belt long after the other crops changed. In 2019 a volunteer account recalled that “paddy was widely grown”; 101Reporters in 2023 recalled kharif paddy grown until the land went to housing. [n17; n11 Q3] At Bhuigaon in 2011 the women’s wells still held back monsoon water for paddy. [n11 Q2] The city plan’s land-use table of 2007 still counted 58 per cent of the urbanisable zone, 5,437 hectares, as agriculture; how much of that was rice, and how much survives in 2026, is not recorded. [n48 §4; n53 A5 Gaps] No series for Vasai’s rice acreage after 1881 was found.

Plantains and the garden belt

The gardens’ great crop was the plantain. The Gazetteer of 1882 named nine varieties grown in Bassein: basrai, mutheli, tambdi, rajeli, lokhandi, sonkeli, bankeli, karanjeli and narsingi. About a thousand basrai plants were set to the acre but only 550 tambdi. The plants were manured with oilcake, then fish; fish manure was cheaper and gave the best return but bred worms. Tambdi fruited in the tenth month, the others in the eighth. [n21 §2; Gaz XIII-I pp. 291–292] The Gazetteer’s table of sales, about 300,000 bunches a year in total, mostly basrai, is the best measure of Bassein’s plantain output in the 1880s, though it counts sales rather than acres. [n21 §2 Inferences]

The famous product was the dried plantain, made from the rajeli. Its home was the northern gardens, Agashi above all, with Vagholi, Vatar, Bolinj, Koprad, Nala, Umrale, Rajodi and Murdes; it was dried by about eighty-five Samvedi Brahman families, fifteen Pachkalshi families and about seventy-five families of Native Christians, and it is told in Chapters 30 and N3. [n21 §2; Gaz XIII-I pp. 292, 395] The agar of Bassein, Vasai’s own garden, grew the fresh fruit, and the sugarcane.

The garden was a layered thing. Under coconut and brab palms grew sugarcane and plantains; among them stood mango, tamarind, banyan, pipal and karanj trees; between the gardens ran sunken lanes “four or five feet below the level of the fields”, crossed by runnels from the garden wells. [n11 Q1; Gaz XIV p. 315] In 1880–81 the sub-division had 1,188 acres of sugarcane and 1,297 acres of “fruits, vegetables and other garden crops”, out of miscellaneous crops of 2,485 acres, 6.8 per cent of the tilled area. [n21 §2; Gaz XIII-II p. 680] By acreage the garden was small; by value and by fame it was the heart of the economy, and the source of nearly all the goods that left the ports.

The plantain’s later history in Vasai is a story of varieties. A study of banana marketing, known only from a summary, put commercial banana in old Thane district in the Vasai and Palghar tehsils, on about 911 hectares, with the safed velchi sold through contractors and commission agents. [n53 A5 (summary)] Farmers told 101Reporters in 2023 that about thirty-five years earlier they had grown velchi, the miniature banana that “sold at a premium”, until it was hit by blight and replaced by the tissue-culture Grand Naine. [n11 Q3; n17; 101Reporters] Vasai’s sweet yellow bananas were chosen for naivedya, the temple offering, and for shikaran, banana in cardamom milk; dried banana, suki keli or sukeli, became the signature Vasai snack. [n17; India Food Network] Chapter 43 tells the sukeli.

Betel nut and the Vanis of Papdi

Betel, the leaf and the nut chewed together across India, was the other great garden crop, and Vasai had a particular share of it. The betel-nut or areca palm, the Gazetteer said, was grown “chiefly in garden lands at Bassein and Bombay-Mahim”. The trees bore from the fifth or seventh year for twenty to twenty-five years, each yielding between 150 and 1,250 nuts a year, about three hundred on average; seedlings cost half an anna to an anna and a half. A betel garden cost, over its first five years, Rs 1,276 for every thousand plants. The growers were “partly Christians (converted Malis) and partly Chavkalshi” or Pachkalshi Hindus. [n21 §2; Gaz XIII-I pp. 298–300]

The nuts were sold wholesale to the Vanis of Papdi, a village about two miles from Bassein, who sorted them into six classes, phulbardi, tambdi, chikni, lavangchuri, pandhri and dagdi or kapkadi. “Bassein is famous for its phulbardi betelnuts.” At Papdi they fetched Rs 21 to 36 for a maund of eighty pounds, and were exported to Bombay, Poona and Gujarat. [n21 §2; Gaz XIII-I pp. 298–300] Papdi was a village of traders and Brahmans: Da Cunha in 1876 described it as “about a cannon-shot from Bajipura”, with Chitpavan, Karhada and Deshastha Brahmans, Palshes and Sonars among its people. [n32 §9; Da Cunha pp. 156–157] Its parish church of Our Lady of Grace, built in 1865 for Rs 18,000 raised by subscription, with a parish school of fifty-six pupils and a violin master, became the cathedral of the Diocese of Vasai in 1998. [n11 Q2; Gaz XIV p. 37] So the betel trade of Vasai had three hands in it: Christian and Pachkalshi growers in the gardens, Vani sorters and dealers at Papdi, and the buyers of Bombay, Poona and Gujarat.

The betel leaf, the pan or nagvel, was by the Gazetteer’s account a chief watered crop of Mahim, to the north, rather than of Bassein; but the leaf too left the Sopara landings and Agashi in quantity. [n21 §2; Gaz XIII-I p. 292; Gaz XIV pp. 2, 316] In the twentieth century, by one account, Vasai’s betel leaf was exported to Pakistan, a trade that declined as relations between the two countries soured; it is the only modern record of the Vasai betel trade found. [n17; n11 Q3; Vikalp Sangam; 101Reporters] No acreage of betel vine for Vasai was found for any date. [n21 §2 Gaps]

Sugar: from the roller mill to Lingard’s factory

Sugarcane was the gardens’ most valuable crop, and the one that drew the most ambitious attempts to industrialise them.

The village sugar industry was a seasonal craft. In the Bassein sub-division, the Gazetteer recorded, raw sugar and molasses were made by Pachkalshis, Malis, Native Christians and Samvedi Brahmans, in a season from February to June. The cane was crushed in a wooden roller mill that cost Rs 7 or 8, and the juice boiled down in copper pans at Rs 3 or 4 each; women and children carried the cane. [n53 A5; n21 §2; Gaz XIII-I pp. 390–392] Four communities shared the work: the Pachkalshis, whose Christian branch is the Vadvals of Sandor and the parishes round the fort; the Malis, gardeners, some of them converted; the Native Christians, among them the Kuparis; and the Samvedi Brahmans. About 750,000 canes a year left Bassein, chiefly for Bombay, Surat and Broach. [n21 §2; Gaz XIII-I pp. 291, 338] Molasses was among the chief exports of the Bassein ports, and Agashi’s molasses was prized in Gujarat for gudaku, the paste of tobacco and molasses smoked in the hookah. [n36 §5; Gaz XIII-I pp. 338, 358–359] The cane itself was manured with oilcake brought from the Gujarat ports. [n53 B5; Gaz XIV pp. 315–316]

Into this world came the factory men. The sources give two versions of their story, and they are told here side by side.

The fuller version is in the Gazetteer’s crafts chapter. In 1829 a Mr Lingard obtained a forty-year rent-free lease of about eighty-three acres on the esplanade of the fort, with an advance of £2,300 from government, to start a sugar factory. He died in 1832. In 1841 the firm of McGregor, Brownrigg and Company took a perpetual lease of about 115 acres; in 1843 they reported that “the poorness of the soil and the want of shelter” were bad for cane. In 1852 the ruined church of Nossa Senhora da Vida inside the fort was turned into a sugar factory, the factory whose builders found the soldier and his horse. In 1860 Macfarlane and Littlewood took a thirty-year lease at £27 2s, Rs 271, a year as the “Bassein Sugar Company”; it was sold in 1861 to Lawrence and Company; and by 1881 the company “has long ceased to exist”. [n21 §2; n48 §7; Gaz XIII-I pp. 394–395]

The shorter version is in the Places volume. In 1830, it says, “Lingard’s sugar factory failed from the death of Mr. Lingard the promoter”, and the factory of 1852 in the church of N. S. da Vida “did not pay, and has been closed”. [n53 A5; n32; Gaz XIV pp. 35–36] The same volume elsewhere says the church factory closed in 1874; Da Cunha, writing in 1876, says it had “recently closed”. [n32 §2; Gaz XIV p. 40; Da Cunha] The dates for Lingard (1829, 1830, 1832) and for the closure (1874, or simply “closed”) do not agree, and this chapter does not choose between them. [n53 A5]

What the record shows clearly is that for half a century, from about 1829 to the 1870s, men with capital tried to turn Vasai’s cane into a factory product, on the esplanade and inside the ruins of the old city, and that every attempt failed: from a promoter’s death, from poor soil and want of shelter, from lack of profit. Meanwhile the village roller mills and copper pans of the Pachkalshis, Malis, Christians and Samvedis went on making molasses for Gujarat. The factory failed; the craft did not. And the sugar factory left one physical mark: the tank dug between the Misericórdia and N. S. da Vida for its machinery, and the walled sea-water store, which Da Cunha saw. [n32 §3; Da Cunha p. 227]

A Littlewood appears again a decade later, at the other end of the creek. In 1871 “Messrs. Johnson and Littlewood” started a paper mill at Sasunavghar, told below under industry. [n12 §6; Gaz XIV] Whether he was the Littlewood of the Bassein Sugar Company, the sources do not say.

The port of Bassein in the 1870s

All this produce left by sea, and in the 1870s the port of Bassein was still busy. In the five years to 1878–79 its exports averaged £51,414, Rs 5,14,140, a year, and its imports £22,520, Rs 2,25,200. The figures swung widely: exports from £20,710 in 1876–77 to £97,480 in 1875–76; imports from about £17,200 to about £33,500. [n21 §6; n53 A7; Gaz XIV p. 36] A road to the landing place had been cut through the old town in 1856, and the port had “a good landing place and custom house”. [n32 §5; n11 Q2; Gaz XIV pp. 36, 39]

The district’s trade chapter grouped Bassein with Agashi and Navghar as the three ports of the Bassein customs division, and described them in 1881–82. Their trade was worth £108,868: imports £27,127 and exports £81,741. They exported husked rice, molasses, coconuts and plantains to Bombay and Gujarat, and firewood and tiles to Bombay; they imported wheat, pulse and ghee from Bombay and Surat, timber from Bombay, piece-goods, oil and oilcake, and lime. [n21 §6; n36 §5; Gaz XIII-I pp. 358–359] The Bassein division was about five per cent of the sea trade of the whole district, three of its thirty-three ports. [n36 §5 Inferences]

The traders were a mixed company: “Musalmans, Marathas, local Vanis, and a few Brahmans”, about half of them natives of Bassein, the rest from Kalyan, Bhiwndi, Bombay and Gujarat, who stayed “from December to June”, the sailing season. Visiting boats came from Gujarat, Cutch, Kathiawar, Diu, Daman and the south Konkan, anchoring twenty-five to two hundred feet from the landing at Bassein. [n36 §5; Gaz XIII-I pp. 358–359] The trade of the port had long been in Gujarati hands: in 1837 the market town’s shops were mostly held by Gujarati Banias, by Vaupell’s account. [n32 §6; Gaz XIV pp. 35–36]

East along the creek, at Sasunavghar, “about six miles east of Bassein”, was a smaller port, which in the five years to 1878–79 averaged Rs 46,610 of exports and Rs 10,070 of imports; its exports swung from Rs 1,180 in 1874–75 to Rs 1,08,270 in 1876–77. [n12 §6; Gaz XIV] A system of credit served the trade. Bills of exchange of the old hundi kind were still cashed in 1882 at Bassein for sums up to Rs 20,000, and in the villages of Nala, Agashi, Sopara and Navghar up to Rs 10,000. [n48 §1; Gaz XIII-I] Bassein was a credit centre as well as a port.

The boats and the boatbuilders

The port built its own boats, and the Bassein sub-division was the boatyard of the whole Thana coast. The Gazetteer listed the local coasters, the balav for fishing, the machva or suval, the padav or cotton boat and the mhangiri, a large padav, and said that while some were built in Bombay, Versova, Dantivra, Mahim, Karanja and Daman, “most [are built] in the Bassein sub-division, chiefly at Bassein”. [n36 §5; Gaz XIII-I pp. 342–343] The Bassein ports’ own vessels, mhangiris, machvas, paddvas and batelas of fifty to seventy tons, were “built locally by Marathas and Native Christians”, with crews from Bassein and the villages round it. [n21 §4; n36 §5; Gaz XIII-I pp. 358–359]

The district-wide list of builders and owners is a roll-call of the coast’s communities. The builders were “Marathas, Christians, Gujarat carpenters, Cutch Musalmans, Kolis, and Pachkalshis”; the timber was teak and ain from Bombay, Bassein and Jawhar. The owners were “Gujarat and Marwar Vanis, Brahmans, Agris, Bhatias, Lingayats, Bhandaris, Machhis, Kolis, Khojas”. The boats lasted twenty to thirty years; sailors were paid by a share of the profit, the captain, the tandel, a double share; they steered “almost entirely by land-marks, and… by the stars”, and hauled their craft ashore from early June until Coconut Day, Narali Purnima, at the end of the monsoon. [n36 §5; Gaz XIII-I pp. 342–343] Every community of the coast is in that list, from the Brahman and the Vani with capital to the Koli and the Christian with the adze.

The railway takes the trade

Then the railway came. The Bombay, Baroda and Central India line reached Bassein Road in 1864, and suburban trains from Grant Road to Bassein Road began on 1 November 1865. [n31 §1; n36 §5] “Along the coast,” the Gazetteer wrote, “the opening of the Baroda railway has destroyed the old cart traffic, and has reduced the sea-trade with Gujarat and Bombay.” [n36 §5; Gaz XIII-I pp. 341–342] The figures for Bassein Road station show the shift beginning. Its passengers rose from 86,478 in 1873 to 140,837 in 1880; its goods, oddly, fell, from 5,292 tons to 3,278. [n21 §6; Gaz XIII-I p. 340; Gaz XIV p. 36] The goods of the island went north and south by rail from Virar rather than from Bassein Road, as Chapter 30 tells; Bassein Road’s growth was in people. At Bhayandar, across the creek, the goods traffic rose from 2,627 tons in 1873 to 19,770 in 1880, mostly salt from the pans nearby. [n21 §3; Gaz XIV p. 45]

The port lingered as a local feeder for perishables and bulk goods too cheap for railway rates. [n36 §5 Inferences] No port statistics for Bassein after 1882 were found. [n36 §5 Gaps; n21 §6 Gaps] By the twentieth century the produce went by train, and in the twentieth century’s last great carrying trade, it went on the shoulders of the farmers themselves.

The kawadis: Vasai on the four o’clock train

For most of the twentieth century the gardens of Vasai sent their vegetables to Bombay on the train, carried by the people who grew them. The villages of the Vasai–Virar sub-region supplied vegetables to Mumbai’s suburbs “until the early 1980s”, by a 2019 account. [n17; n53 A5; Vikalp Sangam]

The farmer-vendors loaded their produce on a kawad, a shoulder pole with a basket hanging from each end, and took the four o’clock train to Victoria Terminus. They sold at Bora Bazaar and near the Kabutarkhana at Dadar, and the city’s housewives called them “Vasaiwale mama” and “Vasaiwale maushi”, uncle and auntie from Vasai. What they carried followed the seasons. In the monsoon there were garlic chives, cilantro, kantol (teasel gourd) and banana flower, kelphul; in winter, field beans, ghevda and toor; and through the year light-green okra, spiny brinjal, colocasia leaves for alu wadi, round bottle gourd for dudhi halwa, the sweet yellow bananas and the dried ones. [n17; n50 §3; n53 A5; India Food Network, 6 Oct 2015]

The kawadis faded, the 2015 account says, for three reasons: the younger generation took office jobs; contractors tied the remaining farmers to supermarket chains; and produce from Nashik and Pune took over the Mumbai market. A Vasai food writer, Apolina Fos, whose family farmed there, said only a few of her cousins still farmed. [n17; n50 §3; India Food Network] The sources do not say which communities the Vasaiwale vendors came from; the vegetables they sold came from the same garden villages, Christian and Hindu, Kupari, Vadval, Samvedi and Pachkalshi, that had sent molasses and plantains by sea a century before. Chapter 14 tells the kawadis’ last decades.

The vegetables have not quite gone. Vasai’s white onion still reaches the Mumbai market, sold by the bunch at Rs 140 to 170 by a Marathi report, and avocado has been tried as a new crop. [n20; Lokmat; Pudhari] The garden belt keeps producing for the city, but in pockets, and through traders rather than on the shoulders of its own people.

Flowers

The last great cash crop of the green belt is flowers. A study of urban agriculture in the Mumbai region, by Vazhacharickal and Buerkert in 2011, found that traditional farming with crop rotation and intercropping persisted in Arnala, Virar, Vasai and Nallasopara, and that the flower system peaked at Ganesh Chaturthi, Diwali and Navratri; ninety per cent of the flower growers named price swings as their main constraint, with prices rising up to twenty-fold at festivals. [n17; Vazhacharickal & Buerkert 2011] By 2023 101Reporters counted about five hundred families growing flowers in about twenty-five villages of the corporation area, on plots of four gunthas to three acres, along an eighteen-kilometre stretch from the Arnala coast to Vasai. [n17; n11 Q3; 101Reporters, Jan 2023]

The earlier favourite was mogra, the Arabian jasmine, sold at premium prices at the Dadar flower market. From the late 1980s it gave way to sonchafa, a grafted champak from a nursery in Sindhudurg that reached Arnala through a farmer named Moreshwar Bhoir. A box of a hundred flowers sold at Dadar for about Rs 150, rising to about Rs 700 at Ganesh Chaturthi. [n17; 101Reporters] Among the growers the report named were Subhash Bhatte of Satpale, who grew about four hundred plants on an acre, and Robert D’Britto, a veteran grower; both had won the state’s Vasantrao Naik Shetimitra award. [n11 Q3; 101Reporters] Then the weather turned: unseasonal rain and the humidity and fungus after Cyclone Mandous in December 2022 roughly halved the yield. Flower growers have no crop insurance and no Kisan Credit Card loans, and waterlogging has worsened as open fields have vanished around them. [n17; 101Reporters] The flower economy’s centre is on the Virar coast, at Arnala and Agashi, and Chapter 30 tells it in full; its southern end reaches Vasai, and its growers’ bavkhals are the same ponds as the vegetable growers’. [n17]

Fishing: Killa Bandar, Pachubandar and Naigaon

Vasai’s sea was worked long before its gardens were recorded; the Portuguese taxed “Koli fishermen” as a profession in 1535. [n36 §4] In the nineteenth century the fishing people of the Bassein coast were mainly Son Kolis; but, the Gazetteer noted, “the Uraps and other Christian Kolis in Bassein… are professional fishermen”. [n21 §4; Gaz XIII-I p. 55] Vasai’s Kolis are both Hindu and Christian. [n50 §3] The fishing castes counted in the sub-division in 1881 were 2,375 Mangelas, seventy-seven Machhis and sixteen Bhois, river fishers. [n21 §4; Gaz XIII-II p. 680]

The state had long taxed them. Before Act XIX of 1844, Kolis, Mangelas and Vaitis paid a poll tax, the day-dene, of a rupee and a quarter for the privilege of fishing. In 1851 a survey counted 129 salt-water fisheries in the district, twenty-two of them in Bassein, and 101 fresh-water fisheries, eighteen in Bassein; the fresh-water fisheries of Kalyan and Bassein together paid about Rs 130. [n21 §4; Gaz XIII-I p. 54] The fishing of the coast was done with the dol, the great bag net held open by the tide, whose divisions the Gazetteer named, the bombil mar, the vagti mar, the mandil mar; large fish, singhali, kajra and pomfret, were caught in the net’s mouth. Dried bombil, Bombay duck, sold at Rs 6 to 12 a bundle of four thousand, and bombil and mandeli were “the fish chiefly consumed by the agricultural classes”, who carried dried fish inland and bartered it for grain. [n50 §1; Gaz XIII-I pp. 55–59] Along the coast as far as Bassein, fishermen had taken to jolly-boats of fourteen to twenty-five feet. [n21 §4; Gaz XIII-I p. 351]

The fishing villages of Vasai itself are, in the written record, almost silent. Killa Bandar, the village by the fort jetty whose name the buses still carry as “Killa Bunder”, has no reliable published account of its boats, its families or its catch; the research for this book found none. [n32 §5; n11 Q2] Pachubandar appears in the sources as a road name in property listings; Vasai’s Koliwada as a name in a list of Mumbai-region Koli settlements; Naigaon as a church and a railway station. [n11 Q2; n12 §5] There are no fleet sizes and no catch series for any of them. [n17 Gaps] What the record does hold is conflict, and it is a revealing one.

Sand against fish

The fishers of Killa Bandar and Pachubandar have spent the twenty-first century fighting the dredgers. Sand dredging in the Vasai creek was banned by the Collector in June 2006, partly to protect the railway bridge, but it continued: sixty to eighty boats a day, working in two shifts, by a DNA report. The Koli Yuva Shakti, a Koli youth organisation, opposed it for ten years. On 11 June of a year the report does not show, about eighty police entered Pachubandar and Killa Bandar, in what the paper called a “season of protests for Vasai fishermen”. [n53 A6; DNA] Fishing groups say that sand mining destroys the creek’s fishing; the planners of 2010, writing of the Vaitarna creek to the north, said approvingly that dredging “helps the creek to remain desilted”. [n48 §5; CDP p. 26; DNA (summary)] The two views of the same mud are the two economies of the creek, and Panju’s boats, half fishing and half sand, sit between them. [n53 A1]

A one-fish town

The wider Vasai fleet tells of a shrinking sea. Vasai fishers once caught many species in their bag nets and now depend largely on pomfret, which is also falling; trawlers and purse-seiners are blamed, and the number of dol-net boats in Vasai reportedly doubled in a decade to about a hundred. [n17; Citizen Matters] Large trawlers fishing during the monsoon ban, when the traditional boats are ashore, are a standing complaint of the Uttan and Vasai fishers. [n53 A6 (summary)] A 2007 study, known only from a summary, counted 5,007 active fishermen in Vasai taluka in 1997, and the taluka had eleven primary fishermen’s cooperatives. [n36 §7, §8 (summaries)] Cyclone Tauktae in 2021 damaged fifteen to twenty boats at Vasai, against more than 750 in the fishing villages of Uttan, Pali, Dongri, Bhatte Bunder and Chowk across the creek. [n53 A6 (summary)] The Vadhvan port, north of Arnala in Dahanu taluka, is the newest threat to the whole coast’s fishers; about three thousand fishing vessels are registered between Palghar and Vasai, and Chapters 30 and 15 tell the port’s story. [n48 §5; n17; Mongabay India]

The Central Marine Fisheries Research Institute’s census of 2016, and its fifth census of 2025, hold village-level counts of boats and fishers for Palghar district; neither was opened for this book, and the fishing families of Killa Bandar, Pachubandar and Naigaon wait for their historian. [n53 A6 Gaps]

Salt: from 1,439 acres to the objections of 2018

The salt pans of the Vasai creek are among the oldest industries of the coast and the most fought-over land of the modern city.

The salt works of 1881

In 1880–81 the Bassein salt division had seventeen works, arranged in six groups, with 11,374 pans on 1,439 acres and thirty owners. “The Bassein works are widely scattered on the north bank of the Bassein creek; a few are in the interior on the banks of rivers.” [n21 §3; Gaz XIII-I p. 364] Bassein ranked third in the district by area, after Uran with 3,241 acres and Ghodbandar with 1,616, and had about eighteen per cent of the district’s salt-pan area. [n21 §3 (derived)] The salt tax was Rs 2 a Bengal maund of 82½ pounds, and the ground rent in Bassein Rs 1½ an acre. [n21 §3; Gaz XIII-I p. 365] Bassein and Ghodbandar salt together commanded about three-quarters of the trade of Uran, the district’s greatest salt port, and since 1876, “when the salt trade was thrown open to private enterprise”, the quality had improved and Bassein salt competed in local markets and along the railways. [n21 §3; Gaz XIII-I p. 370]

The workers came from several communities. Agris worked the pans in Bassein, as in Ghodbandar, Panvel and Uran, and Kharvas in Bassein, Trombay, Panvel and Uran. Bassein workmen were paid by a unit of eighty Bengal maunds, at Rs 5 to 8 a unit; salt-makers across the district earned between £2 and £5, Rs 20 to 50, for the whole season from January to May. [n21 §3; n48 §1, §5; Gaz XIII-I pp. 365–366] And in the 1870s a salt proprietor named Manekji Kharshedji brought three or four hundred Dublas and Dhodias, Adivasi labourers, from Surat “for the manufacture of salt at his pans in Bassein”, producing much better salt than the district had made before. The Dublas, the Gazetteer said, “work only on the salt-works near Bassein Road station”. [n21 §3; n48 §1, §5; Gaz XIII-I pp. 365–366, 370] (Manekji Kharshedji’s name is a Parsi one, but the Gazetteer does not state his community.) So the salt of Vasai in the 1880s was made by Agri and Kharva hands and by Dubla and Dhodia migrants from Gujarat, on pans owned by thirty proprietors, one of whom imported his labour by the hundred.

The 1881 census counted 114 Dublas in the sub-division. [n21 §1; Gaz XIII-II p. 680] Whether the Dublas of Bassein Road stayed, returned to Gujarat or merged into the coast’s other communities is not recorded. A district table gives a Bassein row in its salt output figures, but its columns cannot be separated in the scan, so Bassein’s own output and revenue in 1881 are unknown. [n21 §3]

The salt pans in the plans

Through the twentieth century the salt pans shrank and changed hands, and no figures survive in the notes for their revenue or their workers. [n17 Gaps; n21 §3 Gaps] By the sanctioned plan of 2007 they covered 1,065 hectares of the urbanisable zone; “Umela is the only place where salt is produced on mass scale”, the city plan said of the Naigaon side, and salt was still made south of Nallasopara station. [n48 §3, §5; CDP pp. 28, 47, 66] Salt pans are protected under the Coastal Regulation Zone rules; the Conservation Action Trust and Down To Earth, in summaries, describe the Vasai pans as zoned CRZ-IB and note that a former planner of the metropolitan authority said Vasai’s own development plan could override the regional plan. [n53 A6 (summaries)]

The fight came in 2018. The metropolitan authority’s draft regional plan for 2016–36 proposed to turn a block of 1,560 acres that had been salt pans until 2016, and lay in the no-development zone, into a “growth centre”, with residential, sports, educational and commercial uses. It would also allow fifteen-metre buildings in most of the green zone and twenty-four-metre buildings in the village gaothans. [n17; n11 Q3; The Quint, 7 Mar 2018] The residents of Vasai answered with paper. The Quint reported at least forty thousand objections from Vasai–Virar and more than 62,000 across the metropolitan region; the activist Samir Vartak put the local objectors at about 63,000. [n17; n53 A2; ICSF/Quint] Fr Francis D’Britto, the Franciscan who had founded the Harit Vasai movement, called the draft “builder-driven”. [n11 Q3; ICSF] The residents’ argument was the land’s: this ground floods every monsoon; a village had stood under water for five or six days the previous year; over a hundred acres of mangrove had already been cleared; and the green zone, they said, was why Vasai was less polluted than Mumbai. [n17; The Quint] They wrote to the chief minister, Devendra Fadnavis. [n17 Inferences] The final status of the 1,560-acre growth centre in the sanctioned regional plan was not confirmed in the research. [n17 Gaps]

The researchers’ observation is worth keeping. Salt, fish and plantains were all “coast edge” trades in 1882, and all three have shrunk; the salt-pan edge has become the city’s floodplain and its political fight. [n48 §5 Inferences] When Western Railway looked for the cause of its waterlogged tracks in 2025, the first thing it named was buildings on salt-pan land. [n17; PropNewsTime]

Palm juice: the Bhandaris

One trade of the gardens has left almost no modern trace. The Bhandaris were the coast’s palm-juice drawers, who climbed the coconut and brab palms with a tapping knife and a gourd and drew the toddy that was drunk fresh, fermented, or distilled into the spirit the Gazetteer called phul and, distilled twice, bevda. [n50 §1; n21 §5; Gaz XIII-I p. 150] The census of 1881 counted 8,334 Bhandaris in the Bassein sub-division, though the scanned figure might be 3,334. [n21 §1, §5; Gaz XIII-II p. 680] Toddy had been taxed since the Portuguese list of 1535. [n36 §4] Under the British the retail monopoly of palm and country liquor in the Sivan, Naman and Manikpur divisions of Bassein was auctioned each year, and a palm-tree cess from 1877 pushed many Bhandaris into farming; the Bassein Bhandaris, the Gazetteer said, “continued unruly and discontented” after the government’s concessions. [n21 §5; n50 §1] In 1882 the Bhandaris were also among the husbandmen of Sopara, and among the owners of the coast’s boats. [n53 B5; n36 §5]

No count of tapped trees and no toddy revenue for Bassein were found, and no modern source on toddy tapping in Vasai. [n21 §5 Gaps; n17 Gaps] The palms still stand in the gardens; who taps them, if anyone, is not recorded.

Industry: from the paper mill to the galas of Waliv

The paper mill at Sasunavghar

Vasai’s first factory after the sugar works was a paper mill, and it failed in the same way. “A paper-mill was started here in 1871 by Messrs. Johnson and Littlewood,” the Gazetteer wrote of Sasunavghar, the small port on the creek six miles east of Bassein. The buildings, the English machinery and a dam across the nearby stream cost £8,000, Rs 80,000. The mill began working in 1877 and made paper from grass and rice straw, “but none was ever sold although many dealers had approved of the samples”. The manager died in an accident in 1880 and the mill closed. “The project failed through want of funds to buy new machinery”; it needed another boiler to make a ton of paper a day. [n12 §6; Gaz XIV] Whether the mill’s site survives was not checked. [n12 §6 Gaps]

The pattern of the nineteenth century is clear. Two factories, the sugar works and the paper mill, both set up by men from outside with English capital and machinery, both on raw materials the land produced, cane and rice straw; both failed. The crafts of the villages, the roller mill, the copper pan, the boatyard, the plantain-drying booth, did not.

The industrial belt of Vasai East

Modern industry came to Vasai a century later, and to the other side of the railway. The city plan of 2010 explained why it was small. “Due to the industrial location policy for the MMR, there are few small scale industries located in this sub-region mainly in Valiv-Gokhivare area. Vasai Municipal Council has also developed its own small-scale industrial estate… few small-scale industries have come up to the east of Vasai-Road Railway Station… The MMRDA had prepared a plan for Waliv-Gokhivare-Sativali Industrial Complex.” [n48 §7; CDP p. 27] Industry covered 155.4 hectares of the urbanisable zone in 2007, 1.7 per cent of it, and the plan described its profile as “mainly service industry in small scale sector & not heavy manufacturing industry”. [n48 §4, §7; CDP pp. 49, 66] The student researchers of the Tata Institute of Social Sciences add that small-scale industrial zones were set beside the green zone in the 1970s, with little planned housing for the workers. [n53 B6; TISS (summary)] When Waliv became an industrial zone is not firmly dated. [n12 §4 Gaps]

The belt runs through Navghar, Gokhivare, Waliv, Sativali and Gauraipada, east of Vasai Road station and along the road to Vasai Phata, the junction with the Mumbai–Ahmedabad highway. Its units are galas, small industrial sheds in multi-storey estates with names that recur in property listings: Tara, Samruddhi and Pride on Sativali Main Road, Pinnacle, Pushpraj, Anmol; at Gokhivare the Nova Industrial Estate was registered under the real-estate regulator on 24 January 2023. [n12 §4; ghar.tv; geosquare; cityair] The pollution board’s inventory lists units at Valiv Phata and at Sativali, among them the Khokhani Estate. [n53 A7; MPCB (summary)]

How big the belt is, nobody official says. An activist told the Free Press Journal that Navghar, Gokhivare, Valiv and Sativali held about 2,500 units, employing directly and indirectly more than fifty thousand people, in a report about industrialists opposing the Virar–Alibaug corridor; a Deccan Herald flood report, probably of 2018, put the units across Navghar, Gokhivare, Waliv, Sativali and Gauraipada at more than twelve thousand. Both figures are single claims, read from summaries, and they differ by a factor of five. [n53 A7 (summary); n48 §7 (snippet)] The district counts, across all of Palghar, one industrial estate of the state corporation, three cooperative industrial colonies, 5,757 registered small industries and 427 large and medium ones. [n12 §4; Palghar district] No official count of units, workers or turnover for Waliv, Sativali, Gokhivare or Vasai Phata was found. [n53 A7 Gaps]

The pollution board’s river plan gives the belt’s environmental measure. The Pelhar river’s catchment held 144 effluent-generating industries, each with its own small treatment plant, together discharging about a third of a million litres a day, none connected to a common treatment plant, and “no major polluting industry”. [n48 §2, §7; MPCB 2019] But the Waliv nallah that drains the belt was the dirtiest of the three drains measured, at a biochemical oxygen demand of 98.6 milligrams a litre. [n48 §2] Small fires in the galas and scrap yards make the news; in June 2026 frequent unannounced power cuts were reported to be disrupting the industrial units of Vasai–Virar. [n48 §7 (snippet)]

The contrast with 1882 is complete. The industries of the old Vasai, sugar, plantain, boatbuilding and salt, all came from the sweet-water garden and the creek, west of the line. The industry of the new Vasai sits east of the line, on the old rice flats and the dry foot of the hills, and makes things that have nothing to do with the land under it. [n48 §7 Inferences]

The Vadval axemen of Gokhivare

Gokhivare, at the heart of the industrial belt, had an older industry. In 1882 it was the home of Christian Vadval timber cartmen, “the best axemen in the district”. [n49 §3c; Gaz XIII-I pp. 97–98] The Vadvals, the Christian Pachkalshis of Sandor and the parishes round the fort, are best known as gardeners; the Gokhivare cartmen show that some of them worked the forest of Tungar, felling and hauling its timber to the creek. Gokhivare grew into a census town of 19,772 people by 2001, and now lies inside the corporation. [n12 §4; Wikipedia: Gokhivare] Its axemen’s timber came down the same road the Company built to Tungar in 1869; its galas now line it.

Quarries, sand and brick

The eastern edge of Vasai, where the plain meets Tungar, supplies the city with the materials it is built of, and the work is done largely by Adivasi labour.

The quarries and stone crushers of the villages around Tungareshwar cut the basalt that the Portuguese once shipped to Goa. When the eco-sensitive zone was drawn round the sanctuary, the Paye-Brahmangaon Mining and Stone Crushers Owners’ Association asked for four villages to be excluded; its members supply “hard Black Rock” to Mumbai, Thane and Kalyan, and said their Adivasi workers had no other income. [n53 A7; n48 §6–7; Business World (summary)] No count of the quarries was found. [n48 §6 Gaps]

Sand comes from the creeks. Illegal sand pits are raided: at Khanivade the police seized seventy brass of illegally extracted sand from a sand port, a brass being a hundred cubic feet. [n53 A7; FPJ (summary)] In 2018 the state told the High Court that fifteen sand sites, eleven on the Tansa and four on the Vaitarna, had been destroyed. [n48 §5; HT, 10 Sept 2018 (summary)] The twenty-one workers rescued by the fire brigade at Chandip in the 2019 flood were at a sand site. [n53 A2]

The bricks are burned in kilns on the Tansa side. The People’s Archive of Rural India followed a group of eighteen Katkari Adivasis who migrated to the brick kilns at Bhatane, in the Vasai block, during the COVID-19 period; across Palghar district kiln owners have been booked for forcing tribal families into bonded labour, through the bayana, the advance payment whose debt binds a family to a kiln. [n17; PARI; Deccan Herald; ThePrint; NewsDrum] No count of kilns in Vasai taluka was found. [n48 §7 Gaps]

The Warlis and Katkaris of the hills, who in 1882 came down to the Saturday market at Virar and lived in hamlets on the paths up Tungar, such as the Warli hamlet of Dhondvira on the path from Sativali to the Tungareshwar temple, are in 2026 the labour of the quarry, the kiln and the sand barge. [n12 §4; n48 §1; Gaz XIV p. 367] Vivek Pandit’s Shramajeevi Sanghatana, founded in rural Vasai in 1982, began as a fight against exactly this bondage; Chapter 14 tells it. [n26 §2]

Markets: from the Robertson market to the station

The market of the old town of Bassein was a municipal building with an office on top. In the 1880s the Robertson vegetable and fruit markets, named after the Collector, James Walker Robertson, stood opposite the public garden that government had granted the municipality in 1877; the municipal office occupied their upper storey. [n21 §9; n32 §6; Gaz XIV pp. 36–37] Market fees were among the municipality’s four sources of income. [n21 §9] The town’s shops, in 1837, were mostly held by Gujarati Banias. [n32 §6] Under the Portuguese, the great square at the end of the street from the sea gate, beside the State House, had been the city’s market. [n32 §2; Gaz XIV p. 39; Da Cunha p. 218]

Curiously, the Gazetteer’s list of weekly markets in the Bassein sub-division, four of them, attended by five or six hundred people each, names Agashi, Sopara, a place the scan reads as “Dhovh”, and Virar, but not Bassein town; the town had its daily municipal market instead. [n48 §7; Gaz XIII-I] For Vasai’s markets after 1882, the record is thin. The market at Parnaka, in Vasai West, is known to this research only from bus-stop listings; no description of it was found. [n53 A7 Gaps; n32 §9] What the sources do show is that the trade centre moved, as everywhere on this coast, from the old town by the creek to the station: in 2026, the station markets at Vasai Road, Nallasopara and Virar are where the city shops, and the flower market and main market at Nallasopara station flooded on 13 September 2026, the night before Ganpati. [n53 A7; n29 §4; FPJ, 13 Sept 2026] A present-day study of any Vasai market, its traders and its volume, was not found.

The commuter town

The defining economic fact of modern Vasai is the train. The suburban service began on 1 November 1865, from Grant Road to Bassein Road. [n31 §1] By 1880 Bassein Road station handled 140,837 passengers a year. [n21 §6] By the 2000s the whole sub-region had become what its own plan called “a growing dormitory town to the Mumbai city”: “About 60% of workers commute to Mumbai in the morning & return in the evening putting pressure on Public Transport system.” [n48 §8; CDP p. 23] In 2001 the sub-region’s census found 166,924 workers among 702,723 people, of whom 1,627 were cultivators and agricultural labourers: about one per cent. [n48 §8; CDP Tables 6.7–6.8] The economy of the gardens had become, in sixty years, the economy of the platform. Chapter 30 sets out the census tables in full; the same tables cover Vasai.

The population figures show when the change came. In 1981 the whole region had fewer than 250,000 people, with Vasai town at 34,900 and Virar at 23,303; Agashi was the only village with more than ten thousand people, and many places had lost people in the decade before. Between 1981 and 1991 the region grew by 150 per cent, and Vasai, Virar, Sopara and Sandor by 290 per cent, driven by commuters to Mumbai. [n17; TIFR] The census of 2011 counted 1,222,390 people in the corporation, in 291,229 households, on 319 square kilometres. [n12 §1; census2011.co.in] The garden villages were inside it, and the census gives them no separate figures. [n11 Q1]

The roads followed the trains, slowly. The Vasai creek road bridge of 1968 and its successor of 2023 tied Vasai to Thane and the highway; flyovers and corridors, told in Chapter 16, followed. And in July 2026, when the trains stopped, auto-rickshaws charged ten to fifteen times the fare, and the commuter city discovered how completely it depended on its railway. [n53 A2; Khaleej Times, 8 July 2026]

Land as livelihood: the building boom

Since 1988 the most valuable crop of the Vasai plain has been the plain itself. The sequence is told in Chapters 14, 14 and A1: on 31 August 1988 the regional plan for the Vasai–Virar sub-region was sanctioned, and the state “de-reserved this region from agricultural use, contrary to the recommendation of the MMRDA”; private builders began at once; in May 1990 the plan was amended to convert about 8,500 hectares from the green zone to urbanisable land, for a city of twelve lakh people by 2011. [n17; TIFR; VVCMC draft DCR 2013; Scribd] The planning authority changed from the metropolitan authority to CIDCO in 1991. [n17; TIFR] Harit Vasai’s members point to 1988 as the year the plans that ended the green belt were drawn. [n17; Karmayog]

What the land became is the city. Its cost appears in the courts. The illegal-building case of 2024–26, in which the Enforcement Directorate alleged a fraud of about Rs 9,000 crore, seized about Rs 32 crore in raids in May 2025 from a corporation town-planning official, Y. S. Reddy, and others, raided twelve locations in July 2025 linked to the former commissioner Anil Kumar Pawar, finding about Rs 1.32 crore in cash, and arrested him in August 2025 with a town-planning official and a former corporator, centred on forty-one buildings put up on land reserved for a dumping ground and a sewage plant at Achole in Nallasopara East. [n53 A7; n48 §8; ThePrint; ED press release, 3 July 2025; FPJ; Deccan Herald (summaries)] These are the Directorate’s allegations; the notes record no court verdict. Chapter 24 and Chapter 23 tell the buildings and the families who bought them; Chapter 16 tells the case.

The market for flats is measured only in asking prices. Listing sites in 2026 put the average around Virar at about Rs 8,000 a square foot and Nallasopara East at about Rs 10,000; these are advertisements, not sales. [n48 §8; Squareyards (summary)] No price series for Vasai West or Vasai East, and no rent series, was found.

The municipal purse: from Rs 7,141 to Rs 4,209 crore

The municipality of Bassein was established in 1864, by Government Resolution 621 of 15 March, and covered the old fort, the modern town in Malonde, the survey villages of Dhauli, Sandore and Mulgaon, “and many gardens and fields between Bassein and Papdi”. [n21 §9; Gaz XIV p. 36] Its income in 1880–81 came from octroi, the tax on goods entering the town, from house and privy taxes, tolls and market fees, and amounted to £714, Rs 7,141; it spent £664, Rs 6,637, of which £117 went on scavenging, £46 on lighting and £77 on roads. [n21 §9; n53 A7; Gaz XIV p. 36] The census of 1872 had given it an income of Rs 6,579, though the scanned figure is uncertain. [n21 §9]

A hundred and sixty-two years after its founding, the municipality’s successor, the Vasai-Virar City Municipal Corporation, formed on 3 July 2009 from the councils of Vasai, Virar, Nallasopara and Navghar-Manikpur and fifty-three villages, presented its budget for 2026–27. [n12 §1; VVCMC] On 9 March 2026 the commissioner, Manoj Kumar Suryavanshi, laid out spending of Rs 4,208.99 crore, against a revised Rs 3,194.71 crore for 2025–26, an increase of Rs 1,014.28 crore, with a surplus of Rs 2.12 crore. Capital grants doubled, from Rs 320.78 crore to Rs 642.61 crore. Collections from property tax, town-planning fees and market fees had fallen short, so property tax was to rise and the water tax was proposed to double. [n53 A7; FPJ, 11 Mar 2026] Other outlets gave the total as about Rs 4,206 or 4,207 crore. [n53 A7 (summary)] Among its projects was a 400-bed hospital at Achole. [n48 §8]

The two budgets, Rs 7,141 and Rs 4,209 crore, are separated by a factor of about six hundred thousand, and by a change in what a municipality lives on. The municipality of 1881 lived on octroi and market fees: on trade. The corporation of 2026 lives on property tax and town-planning fees: on building. When building slows, so does the budget; and the town-planning fees are the same fees whose irregular collection the Enforcement Directorate alleged. Market fees appear in both. [n53 A7; n21 §9]

The fort and its visitors

The last livelihood of Vasai is visitors, and they come above all for the fort. The walled city covers about 109 acres, with a perimeter of about 3,300 metres. [n53 A7; FPJ, 4 June 2026] Its protection, neglect and controversies are told in Chapter 17, and its sights in Chapter 44.

In June 2026 the Mumbai circle of the Archaeological Survey of India announced a plan to conserve the ruins of three of the fort’s seven churches, St John the Baptist, St Dominic and St Augustine, with gates, fencing and better visitor facilities and security; the Franciscan church and the church associated with St Gonsalo Garcia had already been restored, the report said. [n53 A7; n32 §4; FPJ, 4 June 2026 (fetch summary)] No footfall figures for the fort and no confirmed entry fee were found. [n53 A7 Gaps] Nearby, the parish community’s restoration of the cathedral of Our Lady of Grace at Papdi won a UNESCO Asia-Pacific Award of Merit in 2025. [n11 Q2; Outlook Traveller; FPJ] Each year the fort fills for the feast told in Chapter 17, Killa cha Sann.

Beyond the fort, the visitors go to the beaches of the west, Suruchi, Rajodi, Bhuigaon, Kalamb and Navapur, named in the travel guides, and to the hills of the east. [n53 A7 (summary)] The Chinchoti waterfall in the Tungareshwar foothills draws monsoon picnickers and has killed many of them: three in twenty-four hours in July 2023, two college students from Goregaon in July 2025 despite restrictions, others besides; the authorities periodically close it. [n12 §5; Mid-day; Lokmat Times; FPJ] The city plan of 2010 named “the Chinchoti fall, Tungareshwar Temple and thick forest” as offering “good tourism potential” in the east, with holiday resorts along the highway. [n48 §9; CDP pp. 25, 64] The Tungareshwar temple on the hill, rebuilt about 1750 by Shankarji Keshav, the Maratha Sarsubhedar of Bassein, with a grant of about three square miles from the Peshwa, is a pilgrimage of its own. [n12 §7; Gaz XIV p. 367]

The fort, the waterfall and the temple are all day trips from Mumbai. The visitors who come to the ruins of the Court of the North arrive, like the commuters who leave Vasai each morning, by the railway.

Every community at work

Set side by side, the sources for this chapter put every community of Vasai somewhere in its economy, though rarely in the same place for long.

In 1882 the Christians of Bassein, Vadval and Kupari alike, were the “most hardworking and skilful husbandmen”, growers of sugarcane, plantains and betel nut, makers of molasses, builders of boats and, at Gokhivare, axemen; the Kupari villages kept the densest networks of bavkhal ponds; and the community’s own writers date their move from the gardens to the professions and emigration to the late 1960s. [n21 §2; n36 §5; n11 Q3] The Pachkalshis, the Hindu kin of the Vadvals, grew betel nut and cane, made molasses and built boats. [n21 §2; n36 §5] The Samvedi Brahmans made molasses and, in the northern gardens, dried plantains. [n21 §2] The Chitpavan, Karhada and Deshastha Brahmans and the Palshes lived at Papdi in 1876, with Sonars, and Brahmans were among the port’s traders and boat owners. [n32 §9; n36 §5] The Vanis of Papdi sorted and sold betel nut; Gujarati Banias held the town’s shops in 1837; Gujarat and Marwar Vanis and Bhatias owned boats. [n21 §2; n32 §6; n36 §5] Muslim traders, Cutch Musalman boatbuilders and Khoja owners worked the port; the 1534 treaty kept the endowment of its mosques. [n36 §4, §5] The Kolis, Son Kolis and Christian Uraps, fished the creek and the sea and fight the dredgers still; the Mangelas, Machhis and Bhois fished with them. [n21 §4; n53 A6] The Agris and Kharvas worked the salt pans, and the Agris of Panju live between fishing and sand. [n21 §3; n53 A1] The Bhandaris tapped the palms. [n21 §5] The Dublas and Dhodias came from Surat to make salt. [n21 §3] The Parsis kept rest-houses in the town, one built by Lavji Sorabji Lakdavala in 1780 and another by Dadabhai and Mancherji Pestanji Wadia in 1836. [n11 Q2; Gaz XIV pp. 36–37] The Warlis and Katkaris of the hills work the quarries and kilns. [n17; n12 §4] And the migrants of the last forty years, from every part of India, are the commuters, the gala workers, the builders and the buyers of the city’s flats. [n17; n48 §8]

What the sources do not give is the twentieth century in between: who left the gardens when, which families ran the boats after the port declined, who worked the salt pans of the 1950s, who opened the first galas at Waliv. Those are the gaps listed at the end of this chapter.

From the rapier to the rain gauge

Put the two ends of the record side by side, the Gazetteer of 1882 and the newspapers of 2026, and the change is plain.

In 1882 the land of Vasai was a garden of light sandy soil on the south end of an island, watered by Persian wheels from unlined wells and by hundreds of family ponds, with a perennial holy reservoir at Malonde in the middle of the town. It grew rice in the monsoon and sugarcane after it, nine kinds of plantain, betel nut that the Vanis of Papdi sorted into six grades, and coconuts and palms that the Bhandaris tapped. Its molasses went to Gujarat for the hookah; its canes went to Bombay, Surat and Broach; its nuts went to Poona. Its port sent out five lakh rupees of goods a year in boats that its own Christians and Marathas built. Its salt pans covered 1,439 acres on the north bank of the creek, worked by Agris and Kharvas and by Dublas brought from Surat. Its Kolis fished the creek. Two factories, a sugar works and a paper mill, had been tried with English capital, and both had failed. Its railway station, sixteen years old, carried 140,837 passengers a year. Its municipality lived on Rs 7,141 of octroi, tolls and market fees.

In 2026 the same land holds part of a city of more than a million people, most of whose workers go to Mumbai on the train. Its water comes from the Surya through a pipe, its groundwater is turning salt, and the ponds of its villages survive where volunteers have dug them out. Its salt pans are a floodplain and a planning fight; its creek is fished by Kolis who fight the sand dredgers; its mangroves are felled for railway lines and power lines; its last leopards walk to the fort. Its industry is a belt of small galas on the far side of the railway, its quarries and kilns are worked by Adivasi labour, and its gardens survive in pockets, growing flowers for Dadar. Its corporation spends Rs 4,209 crore, raised largely on building. And in July the water that the Gazetteer saw “flooded at spring tides” comes back to the low ground, fills the corporation’s own headquarters, drowns a man in the Waliv pond, and is paid for in September at Rs 20,000 a household.

The soldier and his horse in the church of Nossa Senhora da Vida were buried on this ground when it was the capital of the Portuguese north; the sugar factory that disturbed them was the first of many attempts to make the ground pay in a new way. The ground has not changed. The rock is the same basalt, the soil the same sand and silt, the creek the same creek, the monsoon the same monsoon. What changed was what the people on it lived by. The next chapter on this ground, Chapter 24, crosses the old creek to the island of Sopara, where the same story was told first, and where the sea left earliest.

What the record does not hold

  • Geology and soils. No Vasai-specific geological survey; no map of the sand belt, its width or its loss; no laterite extent; no area for Panju island. The CGWB Thane report was not read. [n53 A1 Gaps]
  • Rainfall and floods. No official rain-gauge series for Vasai; the 1882 average (71.87 inches), the local 1,586 mm and the planning 2,200 mm are unreconciled. No Vasai-specific figures for the floods of 2005 or 2024; the NEERI–IIT Bombay report of 2018 was not found; the rainfall totals of July 2026 conflict between outlets, and the relief window (5–10 July) differs from the rain dates. [n53 A2 Gaps]
  • Khanivade. Whether the dam’s leak was repaired, and what the 2026 summer harvest was. [n53 A2]
  • Water. The present state of the Dhavpani-tirth at Malonde and of the Papdi tank; how many of the bavkhals counted in 2007 and 2013 survive; a current figure for Surya supply; ward-wise supply. [n53 A3 Gaps]
  • Biodiversity. No bird survey of the Vasai creek; no hectare figure for mangrove loss; no count of quarries round Tungareshwar; the Supreme Court’s 2022 exemption known only from a summary. [n53 A4 Gaps]
  • Crops. No acreage or output series for rice, banana, betel or flowers in Vasai after 1881; no betel-vine acreage at any date; the end of the Persian wheels. [n53 A5 Gaps; n17 Gaps]
  • Sugar. The conflicting dates of Lingard’s factory (1829, 1830, 1832) and of the church factory’s closure (1874 or “recently”). [n53 A5]
  • Port. No port statistics for Bassein after 1882; no named trading family, shipowner or salt merchant beyond Manekji Kharshedji. [n36 §5 Gaps]
  • Fishing. No history, fleet count or catch for Killa Bandar, Pachubandar, Koliwada or Naigaon; the year of the police entry at Pachubandar; the CMFRI 2016 and 2025 census tables were not opened. [n53 A6 Gaps; n11 Q3 Gaps]
  • Salt. Bassein’s 1881 output and revenue (the table cannot be read); salt revenue under the Portuguese, the Marathas and the twentieth century; the acreage still producing salt; the final status of the 1,560-acre growth centre. [n21 §3 Gaps; n17 Gaps]
  • Toddy. Tree counts and toddy revenue; any modern account of tapping in Vasai. [n21 §5 Gaps]
  • Industry. No official unit, worker or turnover figures for Waliv, Sativali, Gokhivare, Navghar or Vasai Phata; the year Waliv became an industrial zone; whether the Sasunavghar mill site survives. [n53 A7 Gaps; n12 §4 Gaps]
  • Markets. No description of the Parnaka market or of any modern Vasai market. [n53 A7 Gaps]
  • Work and money. The 2011 census worker tables; household incomes; a sale-price or rent series; the original 2025–26 budget figure. [n53 A7; n48 §8 Gaps]
  • Tourism. Fort footfall and entry fee; visitor counts for the beaches and Chinchoti. [n53 A7 Gaps]

The 1982 Thane District Gazetteer, the Census of India’s printed tables, the CMFRI censuses, the CGWB district reports, the corporation’s development plan, the fishing cooperatives’ own records and the Marathi press, Lokmat, Loksatta and the Vasai Times, are where most of these gaps would close; and the families of Killa Bandar, Pachubandar, Papdi and the garden villages hold the rest in memory.
