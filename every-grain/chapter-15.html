Period covered: 1990 – 2009

In this chapter. In 1988 a green belt is opened, and the coast becomes a city. This chapter follows the years from 1990 to 2009: the migrants, the builders and the protests.

On Republic Day, 26 January 1992, the Vasai grounds filled with people. The account we have, written eight years later by a magazine sympathetic to the organisers, puts the crowd at about one hundred thousand. They had come from the garden villages of the old island of Bassein: from Nirmal and Nandakhal, from Gass and Bhuigaon, from Papdi and Sandor, from the betel and banana country between the creek and the Vaitarna. They had come with a petition. About one hundred thousand signatures were gathered and sent to the Governor of Maharashtra, P. C. Alexander, and to the Prime Minister. The source names the Prime Minister as V. P. Singh, which cannot be right, since Singh had left office in November 1990; the error is the magazine’s, and it is a useful reminder that even the best account of these years is a partisan memory written down after the fact. What the crowd wanted is not in doubt. They wanted the state to stop turning their orchards, paddy and ponds into a city. [n29; Sabrang India, “A people’s priest”, 30 Nov 2000]

At the head of the movement that called them there was a Franciscan priest and Marathi writer named Francis D’Britto, the editor of a church monthly called Suvarta. Behind it stood a church that had been on this coast for four and a half centuries, and a farming people, Catholic and Hindu, who had built the wadis by hand. Against it stood a plan drawn in Bombay, a law that allowed the state to name a planning authority, a market of commuters who needed somewhere cheap to sleep, and, by the reckoning of the movement itself, a new kind of local power that was rising in Virar at exactly the same moment.

This chapter is the story of the twenty years that followed. It is the story of how a market-garden belt that had fed Bombay by train for a century became, by 2009, a municipal corporation of more than a million people. It is also a story told almost entirely from modern sources: planning documents, court records seen through newspaper summaries, election tables, environmental-clearance papers, community blogs, and a handful of good journalism. For the first time in this book the problem is not too few witnesses but too many voices, most of them interested. Where the evidence is a claim, it is told as a claim. Where a court has decided, the decision is stated.

Prologue to the decade: August 1988

The fight that filled the Vasai grounds in 1992 had begun four years earlier.

On 31 August 1988 the state sanctioned a Regional Plan for the Vasai-Virar sub-region. The plan, as later development-control regulations describe it, divided the land into zones, and one of them was a Green Zone. It was modified in 1990 and again in 1992. [n17; VVCMC draft DCR 2013] What mattered was what happened alongside it. The Tata Institute of Fundamental Research’s long-running “Bombay” pages, a careful secondary account, put it plainly: “In 1988 the state government de-reserved this region from agricultural use, contrary to the recommendation of the MMRDA.” The Mumbai Metropolitan Region Development Authority, the regional planner, had advised against it. Private builders began at once. [n17; TIFR, “The Vasai-Virar Region”] Sabrang’s account, from inside the movement, dates the decision to August 1988 and describes it as a conversion of the Vasai-Virar green belt to residential use “without infrastructure”. [n29; Sabrang 2000] Harit Vasai members would later point to 1988 as the year the plans were first drawn. [n17; Karmayog; India Environment Portal, “Green Vasai to become concrete jungle”]

There is an even earlier strand. The obituary of Fr D’Britto in the Free Press Journal, written in 2024, says that in the 1980s residents had already campaigned against making CIDCO, the state’s City and Industrial Development Corporation, the planning agency for a projected population of two and a half million. It does not give a date for that plan. [n29; FPJ obituary, 25 Jul 2024]

What did the coast look like on the eve of this? The 1981 census had found fewer than 250,000 people in the whole area. Vasai town had 34,900 people and Virar 23,303. Agashi, the old plantain-and-betel market of the north, was the only village with more than ten thousand people. Many places had actually lost people in the decade before 1981. [n17; TIFR] The census series that Wikipedia gives for the city area is 219,868 in 1981. [n06; Wikipedia: VVCMC] Most of that population lived in the way the previous chapter described: in garden villages on the sandy western strip, in fishing villages along the shore, in rice-and-forest hamlets under Tungar hill to the east, and in a few small railway towns strung along the Bombay, Baroda and Central India line that had run north since 1865.

On 1 April 1989 Fr D’Britto convened a meeting of public figures. The same year the Harit Vasai Saurakshan Samiti, the “committee to protect green Vasai”, was formed. On 1 October 1989 it held a rally of about 35,000 people. Among the speakers were the playwright Vijay Tendulkar and the activist Kisan Mahta. [n29; Sabrang 2000] An academic case study, which this research could only see through a search summary, also dates the Samiti to 1989 and notes that sources differ on the details of its origin. [n29; IDRC case study, “City Building and Regime Creation in the Peripheries for Mumbai”]

And on 9 October 1989, eight days after that rally, a thirty-one-year-old builder named Suresh Dube was shot at point-blank range in daylight on the platform of Nallasopara station. [n26; FPJ, 24 May 2023; Lokmat Times; ThePrint] The killing, and the long prosecution that followed, hangs over the whole of this chapter. We will come back to it.

So the decade opened with three things already in motion: a plan that had moved the land from farm to future city, a church-led movement to stop it, and a murder on a railway platform that the police would come to blame on a gang in Virar.

The land in 1990

Before the story of the plan, the land itself, because the plan was a plan about land, and the land had its own habits.

The Gazetteer of 1882 had described the western strip between the Vasai creek and the Vaitarna as an island, the “island of Bassein”, earlier the “island of Sopara”. A backwater cut it off from the mainland: the Manikpur creek in the south, the Sopara creek in the middle, the Bolinj creek in the north. Even in 1882 that backwater was silting fast, “especially since the long railway embankment has dammed the flow of water from the south”. Small boats could still come up to Gass, about a mile from Sopara, and to Bolinj; the old Sopara landing at Dedar was dry. [n31; Gazetteer XIV, pp. 314–316] The island was divided into three garden belts, the agar of Agashi in the north, Sopara in the middle and Bassein in the south. [n11; Gazetteer 1882]

East of the railway lay a different country. The Gazetteer called much of it salt waste, flooded and impassable in the rains, with rice land and then the forested mass of Tungar. [n31; Gazetteer 1882] In 1990 that eastern ground was still largely what it had been: paddy, salt pans, creeks fringed with mangrove, and the low hamlets of Achole, Tulinj, Gokhivare, Waliv and Sativali. Harit Vasai would later say that about half of Vasai’s land is low-lying or marshy. [n06; CAT; The Quint] Annual rainfall is about 1,586 millimetres, almost all of it in four months. [n17; 101Reporters, 7 Jan 2023] Any city built here would have to decide what to do with that water.

The western gardens had their own water system, and it is the key to everything that follows. The farmers called the ponds bavkhal (also written bawkhal or bhowkal). They were dug for irrigation, fed by an unconfined aquifer just below the sandy surface, and never used for drinking. Each was owned by a family group of anywhere from five to sixty people. [n17; Vikalp Sangam / Village Square, 2019] Marcus Dabre, editor of the Vasai Times, explains that the bavkhals hold rainwater and feed the fields through underground connections. [n17; 101Reporters] In Bhuigaon, a 2011 village walk recorded about forty large irrigation wells, maintained mostly by women, watering vegetables, bananas and flowers. [n08; Mumbai Paused blog, 2011] The ponds, the sand and the rain made a closed loop: the monsoon filled the ponds and the aquifer, the dry season drew them down, and the brackish sea was held back by the pressure of fresh water underneath the gardens. Macanzy Dabre of the Paryavaran Samvardhan Samiti describes the coastal water bodies as brackish and linked underground. [n17; Vikalp Sangam] Take out the ponds, or pump the aquifer hard, and the salt comes in.

What grew on this land in 1990? Modern sources list betel leaf, velchi bananas, paddy and sugarcane as the former crops. [n17; 101Reporters] “Paddy was widely grown, betel leaf was exported to Pakistan, and velchi kela plantains sold at a premium,” says a 2019 report on pond restoration, and the villages of the sub-region supplied vegetables to Mumbai’s suburbs until the early 1980s. [n17; Vikalp Sangam] The betel trade to Pakistan declined as relations between the two countries worsened. [n08; Mumbai Paused] Vasai’s sweet yellow bananas were the ones Bombay households chose for naivedya, the temple offering, and for shikran, banana in cardamom milk; the dried banana, sukeli, was the coast’s signature snack. [n17; India Food Network, 6 Oct 2015] Marathi sources add that sukeli is made by sun-drying ripe bananas, mostly of the Rajeli variety, and that Agashi was its centre. [n20; Marathi Wikipedia; Vishwakosh “केळ” (snippet)]

That was the land the plan of 1988 had reclassified. On paper it was now a city in waiting.

May 1990: eight thousand five hundred hectares

The decisive paper change came in May 1990. A revised land-use document, available to this research only through a copy posted online, says: “In May 1990, the Regional Plan was amended to convert about 8500 ha of land from G-Zone to urbanisable zone.” The area was to develop as a city of twelve lakh people, 1.2 million, by 2011. [n17; Revised Land Use Plan document (Scribd)]

Eight thousand five hundred hectares is eighty-five square kilometres. For comparison, the whole of the municipal corporation that would eventually be formed covers a little over three hundred square kilometres. [n29; n12; census2011.co.in] In one amendment, something like a quarter of the land of the future city was moved from the column marked green to the column marked urbanisable. The target population, 1.2 million by 2011, turned out to be almost exactly right. The 2011 census would count 1,222,390 people in the corporation. [n12; census2011.co.in] The plan was not wrong about how many people would come. The fight was about where they would live, on what land, with what water, and who would profit.

In the same month the state named a new planner. The 2001 development-control regulations for the sub-region record that the City and Industrial Development Corporation of Maharashtra, CIDCO, was appointed Special Planning Authority under Section 40 of the Maharashtra Regional and Town Planning Act by a notification dated 14 May 1990, numbered TPS-1287/2753/CR-228-87/UD-12. [n29; VVCMC sanctioned DCR (search summary)] Sabrang, too, puts CIDCO’s appointment in 1990. [n29; Sabrang 2000] Other sources, including the TIFR pages, say MMRDA remained the planning authority until 1991, when CIDCO replaced it. [n17; TIFR] The research notes resolve this in favour of 14 May 1990 as the date of appointment, the best-documented date, with 1991 probably the year CIDCO’s role first began to bite on the ground. [n29, §5]

CIDCO was the agency that had built Navi Mumbai across the harbour. Its arrival meant that Vasai-Virar was now officially being treated as the next satellite city, a place to be planned from above for Bombay’s overflow. In September 1990 CIDCO released an interim draft development plan. Harit Vasai opposed it, saying it put holding ponds and dumping grounds inside the green zone. [n29; Sabrang 2000] A 1990 plan modification also added about 2,000 hectares to the urbanisable zone; after objections that addition was deleted and merged back into the green zone, except for land along a stretch of three roads in Virar town. This comes from a search summary of academic and Harit Vasai accounts, not from the plan itself. [n29; IDRC case study (search summary)]

The order of events, then, is: a sanctioned plan with a green zone (August 1988), a state decision to de-reserve agricultural land against the regional planner’s advice (1988), a large conversion of green zone to urbanisable (May 1990), a new planning authority (14 May 1990), an interim draft opposed for putting dumping grounds and holding ponds in the green belt (September 1990), and a partial retreat on one 2,000-hectare addition. None of the primary notifications for the 1988 and 1990 changes was read by this research; all of it comes through later regulations, an activist magazine, and secondary summaries.

Fr Francis D’Britto and Harit Vasai

Who was the priest at the head of the crowd?

Francis D’Britto was born on 4 December 1943, though some reports give 1942. [n38; Wikipedia: Francis D’Britto; n27] He was a Franciscan, of the order that had first planted churches on this coast in 1534. He was a Marathi writer of more than fifty books, and from 1983 to 2007 he edited Suvarta, the Catholic Marathi monthly that the diocese would later call its premier Marathi publication. [n27; n13; Wikipedia; ThePrint] His home parish was Nandakhal, one of the garden villages of the old Sopara agar, and he would die there. [n26; n38]

That he was a Marathi writer matters. The Catholics of Vasai were not, in their own eyes or their neighbours’, a foreign community. They spoke dialects of Marathi, Kadodi among the Kuparis of the north and Vadvali among the Vadvals of the south, and the standard Marathi of the schools. Their ancestors, the Gazetteer of 1882 had said, were “chiefly converted Brahmans, Prabhus, Pachkalshis and Kolis”. [n34; Gazetteer XIII, p. 63] A Franciscan who edited a Marathi magazine could speak to Hindu farmers, Marathi playwrights and the Bombay press in their own language. That is exactly the coalition that appears at the October 1989 rally, with Vijay Tendulkar on the platform.

The movement’s name, Harit Vasai Saurakshan Samiti (also written Sanrakshan), means “committee for the protection of green Vasai”. Its convenor was D’Britto. [n06; Sabrang] The church supported it. [n06; Sabrang] Later accounts describe it as fighting “to protect villages, farms, forests and fishing grounds from urbanisation”. [n38; FPJ 2024; Sabrang 2024] The first bishop of the diocese, when it came, would also speak publicly about the building boom. [n38; AsiaNews]

The tanker war

The first concrete battle was over water, and it turned ugly.

As builders put up housing colonies near Vasai, Naigaon, Nallasopara and Virar stations, the new buildings needed water that the old municipal councils could not supply. The answer was the tanker: trucks that drew groundwater from wells in the green belt and sold it to the new flats. The builders’ groundwater extraction in the 1990s raised salinity and damaged farm produce, Sabrang says. [n29; Sabrang 2000] Given what we know of the bavkhal system, the mechanism is plain: pump the shallow fresh aquifer under the gardens, and the brackish water underneath rises into the wells and the fields.

On 20 May 1991 the Times of India, as quoted by Sabrang, reported violence against the area’s Christian residents linked to this well-water agitation. [n29; Sabrang 2000] We do not have the original report, and we do not know who did what to whom. What we know is that the conflict over water had become a conflict that the press described in terms of community. After talks at ministerial level, in which the Samiti took part, tankers were banned from drawing water from the green belt. The date of the ban is not given. [n29; n06; Sabrang]

Republic Day 1992

Then came the great rally of 26 January 1992 with which this chapter opened: about a hundred thousand people at the Vasai grounds, about a hundred thousand signatures to the Governor and the Prime Minister. After it, Sabrang says, CIDCO revised its plans to preserve the green belt. [n29; Sabrang 2000] The Regional Plan’s second modification is dated 1992 in the development-control regulations, which fits. [n17; VVCMC draft DCR 2013]

What exactly was saved? The best testimony is retrospective. When D’Britto died in 2024, the advocate Noel Dabre, who had worked in the movement, told the Free Press Journal: “The greenery that you see now is because of the movement.” The same report says the state limited development to 1.5 kilometres on either side of the railway line. [n29; FPJ, 25 Jul 2024] Another advocate in the movement said that “the development plan was changed and villages were excluded from the urbanisation plans”. [n38; FPJ 2024]

If that is right, the shape of the modern city was decided in these years: a dense urban strip along the railway, from Naigaon through Vasai Road and Nallasopara to Virar, with the old garden villages of the western island and the forest belt of the east held, more or less, as green. Anyone who has looked at the map of Vasai-Virar today will recognise the shape: a spine of towers along the tracks, and to the west a darker green of coconut and banana with church spires showing through. That shape is the movement’s monument, and also its limit. It protected the west. It did not stop the strip.

1995: an election code and a claim

By 1995 the movement had taken on a second, more political role. In February 1995, before the state elections, UCA News, a Catholic news agency, reported that a priest was promoting an election code in Maharashtra, and that Harit Vasai’s main activity was opposition to “Thakur’s land acquisitions, unauthorized, ill-planned construction and illegal land transactions”. [n29; UCA News, 1 Feb 1995 (search summary)] That is the Samiti’s claim, as reported; it is not a finding by any court. But it shows that by the middle of the decade the green belt movement saw its opponent not only as a plan in Bombay but as a family in Virar.

The research notes draw the obvious inference, and it is worth stating with the same caution they use: the same forces, peripheral urbanisation, land and water, drove both the Christian-led Harit Vasai movement and the builder-linked political base of the Thakurs, and the press recorded the conflict between them as early as 1995. [n29, §1 Inferences]

The Thakurs

Hitendra Vishnu Thakur was born in Virar on 3 October 1961. He studied at Vartak College in Vasai, the institution founded by the family of the Congress leader Bhausaheb Vartak, whose long hold on the Bassein seat in the 1960s and 1970s the previous chapter described. In 1988 he became president of the Vasai Taluka Youth Congress. [n26; Wikipedia: Hitendra Thakur]

In the assembly election of March 1990 he won the Vasai seat for the Congress, aged twenty-nine, beating Dominik John Gonsalvis of the Janata Dal by 46,468 votes. [n29; Wikipedia: Vasai Assembly constituency, citing ECI] The man he beat bore a Vasai Catholic name; the seat had been held in 1985 by a Dominic Gonsalves of the Janata Party, very probably the same man, though the sources do not say so. [n26] In 1980 it had been held by Tarabai Vartak. [n26] Thakur’s victory in 1990 marks the end of one political era on this coast and the start of another that would last until November 2024.

The Dube killing and the TADA case

Thakur’s elder brother, Jayendra Thakur, is known universally as “Bhai”. The newspapers that reported his acquittal in 2023 describe him as the Vasai-Virar “strongman” and a former gangster. [n26; Lokmat Times; FPJ] It was Bhai Thakur’s gang that the prosecution blamed for the murder of Suresh Dube.

What follows is a summary of a prosecution, not of facts proved, and the reader should hold that distinction throughout.

Dube was killed on 9 October 1989 at Nallasopara station. [n26; FPJ 2023] The case was prosecuted under TADA, the Terrorist and Disruptive Activities (Prevention) Act, the harsh special law of those years, which allowed confessions to police to be used in evidence and was used against organised crime as well as terrorism. In 1992, seventeen accused were arrested. [n29; Supreme Court, S. N. Dube v. N. B. Bhoir (search summary)] A Designated Court first acquitted all of them, finding the confessions inadmissible and the eyewitness evidence unreliable. Dube’s brother and the state appealed to the Supreme Court. According to a search summary of the judgment, which this research could not open, the Supreme Court later convicted six of the accused. The same summary says the judgment lists Hitendra Thakur as accused number nine, and names Bhai Thakur’s brothers Hitendra and Deepak among the gang’s members. [n29; casemine (search summary)] The date of the Supreme Court judgment was not captured.

Two headlines from the period survive in the notes without their articles. One, from India Today in 1994, reads: “Administrative delay allows TADA detainee, Hitendra Thakur to get away.” The other, from the Times of India, reads: “TADA Acquits Thakur.” [n26; Wikipedia: Hitendra Thakur] What charges these refer to, and what the final outcome for Hitendra Thakur was in the Dube case, is not clear in any source this research read. His continuing political career shows that he was not convicted; his own 2014 election affidavit listed eight criminal cases, with charges framed in seven and cognizance taken in one, and no convictions. [n26; n29; Wikipedia]

The case against Bhai Thakur himself lasted far longer. The Free Press Journal names an IPS officer, Sudhakar Suradkar, in connection with his arrest, but the date and details of the arrest were not found. [n29] The verdict did not come until 24 May 2023, when a special TADA court in Pune, Judge S. R. Navander presiding, acquitted Jayendra “Bhai” Thakur and others of the Dube murder for lack of evidence. The Free Press Journal called it the last TADA case in India; Lokmat’s headline said “after 34 years”. Dube’s daughter Neha said the family would appeal to the Supreme Court. Reports differ on how many co-accused were acquitted with him, two or three, and on their names. [n26; n29; FPJ; Lokmat Times; ThePrint] That outcome belongs to the next chapter, but it must be stated here, because for thirty-four years the case was an allegation and nothing more, and in law it ended as an acquittal.

One further piece of characterisation belongs to a newspaper, not to a court: the Free Press Journal wrote that Bhai found it cost-effective to make his brother and nephew MLAs. [n26] It is a line from the press. It is quoted here as the paper’s view.

The independent years

Between 1990 and 1995 Hitendra Thakur left the Congress. None of the sources explains why. [n29, Gaps] In 1995 he won Vasai as an independent, beating Deepak Gawhankar of the Shiv Sena by 33,575 votes. In 1999 he won again as an independent, beating Gavankar (the same man, spelled differently in the tables) by 50,428. In 2004 he won a third time as an independent, beating Vivek Pandit, then a Shiv Sena candidate, by 67,917. [n29; Wikipedia: Vasai Assembly constituency] Each margin was larger than the last.

An independent who wins three times against a major party, with a rising margin, is not riding a party wave. The research notes say it directly: Thakur’s 1990 win for the Congress followed by independent wins in 1995, 1999 and 2004 shows a personal, not a party, vote. [n29, §1 Inferences] He built an organisation around himself. Wikipedia says that he helped the actor Govinda, who had grown up in a Virar chawl and studied at Vartak College, win the Mumbai North seat in the 2004 Lok Sabha election for the Congress, and that he helped Vasant Davkhare win the post of Deputy Chairman of the Legislative Council three times. [n26; n29; n27; Wikipedia] The local vehicle was first called the Vasai Vikas Mandal, the “Vasai development circle”; it became the Bahujan Vikas Aaghadi, the BVA, the “front for the development of the many”. Wikipedia gives 2008 as the year Thakur became its president. [n29; Wikipedia] Its symbol, the whistle, would become one of the most familiar marks on the coast.

The family’s wider interests appear in later sources. Wikipedia says it controls the Viva Group and the Viva Trust, which runs Viva College in Virar, though that line is flagged as needing a citation; Lokmat describes the Viva Group as owned by Bhai Thakur’s family. [n26] In the 2015 civic election Hitendra Thakur declared assets of 158 crore rupees, the highest of any candidate, according to the Association for Democratic Reforms. [n26; ADR] A Marathi television profile of him that this research did not open carries the title “दहशत आणि विकास”, “Terror and development”. [n20; TV9 Marathi] That title is the broadcaster’s framing. It captures, in two words, the two stories that people on this coast have told about the Thakur years, and the book records both without choosing.

The Kala-Krida Mahotsav

There is another side of the period that should not be lost in the crime reporting. Some time around 1988 to 1990 a festival began that would become one of the largest of its kind in the state: the Vasai Taluka Kala-Krida Mahotsav, an annual festival of the arts and sports held over the last days of December. Its exact founding year is not stated in any source found. It is inferred: the 37th edition was being prepared in October 2026, the 2020 edition was cancelled for COVID, and a former mayor of Virar, Mukesh Save, who died in November 2025, was called its “शिल्पकार”, its architect, and “for the last 37 years the main pillar and guide” of the festival. [n20; eSakal, 30 Nov 2025; eSakal, 18 Oct 2026] By its 36th edition it ran eighty-five competitions with more than sixty thousand contestants, organised by the Young Stars Trust and the Vasai Taluka Kala-Krida Vikas Mandal. [n13; FPJ] Attribution of its founding to the Thakur family is common in conversation but unsourced in the notes, and the 2026 office-bearers named in the Marathi press do not include Hitendra Thakur. [n20] At the festival’s 25th edition, Save’s show “Mahi Vasai”, on the coast’s traditions and past, was staged. [n20] The festival is the closest thing the new city had to a common civic ritual: tens of thousands of schoolchildren from every community competing in dance, drawing, kabaddi and athletics in the last week of every year.

The numbers: one hundred and fifty, two hundred and ninety

When the census-takers came in 1991 they found a place transformed.

The TIFR account, summarising the 1991 census, says that the population of the Vasai-Virar area had risen by about 150 per cent over the decade, and by 290 per cent in Vasai, Virar, Sopara and Sandor, “driven by commuters to Mumbai”, people who worked in the city and moved here to live. [n17; n35; TIFR] The census series in Wikipedia gives the city area as 219,868 in 1981, 365,480 in 1991, 693,350 in 2001 and 1,343,402 in 2011. [n06; Wikipedia: VVCMC] Those totals give a growth of about 66 per cent for 1981–91, about 90 per cent for 1991–2001 and about 76 to 94 per cent for 2001–11, depending on which 2011 figure is used; those are the research notes’ own calculations, not a source’s. [n06, Inferences] The 150 per cent figure and the census-series figures do not agree; the difference probably lies in what area each counted. A Business Standard piece of 2014 gives yet another series: 194,262 people in 1971, and decadal growth of 32 per cent in the 1970s, 58 per cent in the 1980s, 70 per cent in the 1990s and 221 per cent in the 2000s. The 221 per cent figure does not fit the census totals, and the notes advise treating those rates with caution. [n06; Business Standard, 11 Apr 2014]

Whichever series one trusts, the shape is the same. Something under a quarter of a million people in 1981. Something over a million and a quarter in 2011. Six times as many people in thirty years, with the steepest climb in the twenty years this chapter covers. The 2001 census put the population of Nallasopara alone at 184,664. [n29; n31; Wikipedia: Nallasopara]

What the new people found

They found a place that was not ready for them. The TIFR account describes the infrastructure of 1991–96 in four plain facts. Power was available two days a week. There was no piped water and no central drainage. Under ten per cent of the area had motorable roads. In 1996 some residents got as little as ten litres of water a day. [n17; TIFR] Ten litres is a single bucket, for drinking, cooking and washing, for a whole day.

Many of the buildings that went up in these years were unauthorised and were later regularised. [n17; TIFR] That sentence, in a careful secondary source, explains a good deal of what followed in the next twenty years. If a building put up without permission is later made legal, the lesson for the next builder is that permission is something that can be had afterwards.

Why did people come to such a place? Because it was cheap, and because the train was there. The western line had been carrying the Vasaiwale farmers south since the nineteenth century; now it carried the new residents south every morning to offices, mills, shops and building sites in Bombay. The plan had moved the land from green to urbanisable; the railway gave the new land a value; Bombay’s own land prices drove people outward. The TIFR summary calls the result a commuter boom. [n06; n17; TIFR] A 2014 Business Standard profile called it an affordable housing market. [n06]

Who came: migrants and chawls

The census has never published, for this coast, the tables that would tell us where each new resident came from. The migration tables of the 2011 census were not reached by this research, and no primary study separating origins by state was found. [n06, Gaps; n35] What we have are fragments, and an old precedent.

The precedent is in the Gazetteer of 1882. Among the “recent settlers” of Thana district it lists Pardeshis from Upper India working as messengers and servants, Kachis who were market gardeners, halwais from Sindh, Gujarat Brahmans and traders, Marwar Vanis and Lingayat Vanis. It notes that Gujarati was superseding Marathi in the extreme north of the district. [n35; Gazetteer XIII-1, pp. 64–65] At Sopara the traders were Muslims and Gujarat Vanis of the Lad caste who had come from Cambay about 1760. [n31; Gazetteer XIV, p. 315] The research notes put it well: the 1882 list of Pardeshi servants, Gujarati traders and Marwari moneylenders is the same cast of migrants as today, 140 years earlier. The modern arrivals from Uttar Pradesh, Bihar and the south are a third wave, after the sea-borne Gujarat wave and the Maratha-era Konkanasth and Deshastha wave. [n35, Inferences]

The fragments are these.

A student research project of the Tata Institute of Social Sciences, called “Make Break”, studied Nallasopara East. Its page could not be opened by this research, so what follows comes from a search summary. It found that many people in Makarandnagar had been displaced from Sanjay Gandhi National Park, the forest park to the south whose slum clearances pushed families out of Mumbai. It found that in the 1970s industrial zoning had led entrepreneurs to build chawls for workers. And it found that after 2000, landowners converted farmland into chawls for migrants, many of them from Mumbai itself. [n31; TISS “Make Break”] In one Nallasopara settlement, Sai Society, the scrap dealers and recyclers all came from a single village in eastern Uttar Pradesh. [n08; n35; TISS]

A chawl, on this coast as in Bombay, is a row of single rooms, usually opening on a common passage, with shared toilets and taps. In Nallasopara East the low-rise “baithi chawls”, the single-storey kind, were later being replaced by four-storey blocks, according to a student project from the Technical University of Delft that this research saw only in summary. [n31; TU Delft repository] The chawl was the first rung on the ladder: a room for a family of five, rented from the farmer whose field it had been.

Two famous lives show the pattern. The actor Govinda’s family, after financial setbacks, moved from Bandra to a 300-square-foot chawl in Virar; he studied at the Annasaheb Vartak Smarak Vidyamandir in Virar and took a commerce degree at Vartak College in Vasai, and seeing Disco Dancer in a Virar cinema is said to have inspired him. Sources differ on whether he was born in Virar or moved there as a child; his own accounts put his youth in a Virar chawl. [n27; n13; WION; ETV Bharat; DNA India] And on 9 November 1999 a boy named Prithvi Shaw was born in Virar to parents who had come to the city as economic migrants from Gaya, in Bihar. He trained at an academy in Virar under coach Santosh Pingulkar before the family moved to Bandra in 2006. [n27; n26; Wikipedia: Prithvi Shaw; Business Standard/PTI] Eighteen years later he would captain India to an under-19 World Cup.

The languages tell the same story. In 2001, among Nallasopara’s minority speakers, Wikipedia records Hindi at 22.92 per cent, Gujarati at 17.82 per cent and Urdu at 12 per cent. [n31; Wikipedia: Nallasopara] By the 2011 census, across the whole city, Marathi was the mother tongue of only 46.49 per cent of people, Hindi of 26.58 per cent, Gujarati of 9.26 per cent, Bhojpuri of 3.10 per cent, Urdu of 2.72 per cent and Marwari of 1.78 per cent. [n22; Wikipedia: Vasai-Virar] Those are city figures from a secondary source, not checked against the census tables. But they are startling against the long history of this coast. For the first time since the Marathi language emerged here in the Shilahara grants, Marathi speakers had become a minority in the city of Vasai-Virar.

The notes record no study of how the older communities received the newcomers in these years, and no sourced account of tension in this period between them. The silence is itself a finding: the political weight of the new voters would show only later, in the elections of 2024 and 2026. [n06, Gaps]

Nallasopara

Of all the places on this coast, the one that changed most completely in these twenty years was the one with the oldest name.

For two thousand years the record calls it Shurparaka, Supparaka, Soupara, Soparaka, Sopara. In 1882 the Gazetteer still called it only “Sopara”, a town of about 1,700 people, “the capital of the Konkan from about B.C. 1500 to A.D. 1800”, three and a half miles from Bassein Road station and three and a half from Virar. [n31; Gazetteer XIV, p. 314] It was then “not more than three-quarters of a mile from the line of railway”, yet “badly off for communications”; the Gazetteer’s writer wished for “a station at Sopara or a made road to the town from the Agashi high road”. [n31; Gazetteer XIV, p. 316] So in 1882 there was no station that he knew of. The earliest trace of the new name is a list of halts on an early Back Bay to Virar service, given in a 2022 newspaper article that draws on a 1923 railway magazine: it includes “Neela or (Nalasopara)”. [n31; FPJ, 12 Apr 2022] A popular account, with no source and no dates, says the name arose when two villages, Nala east of the line and Sopara west of it, came to be treated as one place. [n31; Lokshahi] Wikipedia gives 1865 as the station’s opening year on the strength of one Western Railway heritage book, which conflicts with the Gazetteer’s complaint. [n31] The exact year of the change of name remains unresolved, and the book leaves it so.

By 1990, whatever its origin, the name Nallasopara belonged to a railway town, and the railway town was about to swallow the old one. The station sat east of the old Sopara village. Westward lay the old village with its mohallas, Nawayat, Kharkhandi and Bazar Peth, and the localities still called Sopara Gaon and Nale Gaon; eastward lay More Gaon and Achole Gaon. [n31; Wikipedia mirror] West, too, lay the antiquities: the Bhatela pond, 240 by 146 yards, with Muslim graves on its banks and the remains of a Portuguese jetty and customs house; the Chakreshwar lake and temple; the stupa mound that Bhagwanlal Indraji had opened in April 1882. [n31; Gazetteer; Wikipedia]

East of the line was the old salt waste and rice land of Achole and Tulinj, the ground the Gazetteer had called flooded and impassable in the rains, where the railway builders had quarried the six-sided basalt pillars of Tulinj hill for their embankment. [n31; Gazetteer] It was here, on cheap low land close to the station, that the new town rose. The TISS project noted that the low-lying, flood-prone land overlapped the zones marked for urban development. [n31; TISS (search summary)] The research notes draw the inference that frames the next chapter: the same eastern low ground that the Gazetteer called salt waste is where flooding now recurs. The geography has not changed. Only the buildings on it have. [n31, Inferences]

The council that governed the new town was the Nallasopara Nagar Parishad, one of four municipal councils, with Vasai, Virar and Navghar-Manikpur, on this stretch of line. The date of its founding was not found. [n31; n06] By 2001 Nallasopara had 184,664 people, a hundred times the population of the Sopara of 1882. [n29; n31]

What of the old Sopara inside the new? The stupa was cared for. A newspaper analysis says, sharply: “While the Stupa has been looked after, Nalasopara’s historical significance is nowhere to be seen.” [n02; DNA] Another account says that “no traces of the ancient port remain”, only a few protected mounds and a dried-up lake. [n02; LiveHistoryIndia] The Chakreshwar temple, rebuilt, stands beside an Akkalkot Swami math, and a jogging track now rings the lake where, in Hindu belief, Krishna’s discus dug the tank and, in Jain belief, the yakshini Chakreshvari presides. [n10; Inditales; Heritage Chronicles] The Jain pilgrims who come to the Munisuvrat Swami temple at Agashi Chalpeth still tell the story of an idol that surfaced from a pond at Chakreshvar, “the lake of Nalasopara”. A newer derasar stands on Tulinj Road in Nallasopara East. [n10; n08; jainmandir.org] The parish church of St George serves Nallasopara West. [n31; Kalyan Diocese]

In 2006 a set of buildings began to go up in Nallasopara East, at a place called Agarwal Nagari, on survey numbers 22 to 30 at Achole, about thirty acres of land reserved in the plan for a sewage treatment plant and a dumping ground. Over the following years forty-one buildings would stand there, and thousands of families would buy flats in them. [n29; n31; n06; Land Conflict Watch] Their fate belongs to the next chapter. Their beginning belongs to this one, because it shows how the city of these years was made: on land the plan had reserved for something else, by builders, for buyers who did not know.

In 2008 the delimitation of assembly constituencies created a new Nallasopara seat. [n29; Wikipedia: Nallasopara AC] The town that had been a halt called Neela was now big enough to send its own member to the legislature.

The loss of the wadis and the bavkhal

Meanwhile, in the gardens, the old economy was ending, and it was ending in stages.

The first stage had come before 1990. The Vasaiwale farmer-vendors who had ridden the four o’clock train to Victoria Terminus with their shoulder poles, selling at Bora Bazaar and near Dadar Kabutarkhana to the cry of baaju-baaju, Vasaiwale aale, were fading. A 2015 article that went looking for them gives three reasons: families moved into office jobs; contractors tied farmers to supermarket chains; and produce from Nashik and Pune took over the Bombay market. [n17; India Food Network, 2015] The supply of vegetables from the sub-region’s villages to Mumbai’s suburbs had lasted until the early 1980s. [n17; Vikalp Sangam] The research notes see the decline as three stages: the kawadi trade and vegetables to Mumbai, until the early 1980s; then the 1988 de-reservation and the mid-1990s building boom, which ate the paddy and the wadis; then climate and urban-heat stress on the flowers that remained, in the 2020s. [n17, Inferences] This chapter’s twenty years are the second stage.

The pond-restoration report of 2019 says it in a single sentence: the ponds decayed after the mid-1990s real-estate boom, “as the paddy fields and the family orchards gave way to high-rise buildings”. Many became garbage dumps that contaminate groundwater. [n17; Vikalp Sangam] The numbers it gives come from two surveys. A 2007 Minor Irrigation survey counted 479 traditional water ponds in 27 gram panchayats. A 2013 survey by a local group, the Yuva Vikas Sanstha, found more than the official records showed: 39 in Bhuigaon where the records said 16, 55 in Nandakhal where they said 32, 54 in Wagholi where they said 16. A study called Water Crisis in Vasai counted 108 lakes within a sixty-kilometre radius, of which only 28 survived. [n17; Vikalp Sangam]

Bhuigaon, Nandakhal and Wagholi are Kupari villages, Catholic villages of the old Sopara agar. [n11; n20] It is no accident that the best counts come from them: they were the villages where the movement was strongest and where the wadi held on longest. Even there, the official record had lost track of most of the ponds.

Why did the ponds go? The sources point to the boom, and to what it did to every part of the system. Fields were sold or built on. Groundwater was pumped for tankers and new buildings, raising salinity. [n29; Sabrang] Ponds that no longer watered fields were not desilted and became dumps. The family groups that owned each pond, five to sixty people each, scattered into salaried jobs. Each of these is a plain consequence of the facts in the notes; no single source measures them all.

The Kuparis leave the land

For the Kuparis, the Samvedi Christians of the northern villages, the move away from farming had begun a little before the plan. Two community blogs, one from 2007 and one from 2015, both uncited and both written from inside the community, say that farming dominated until the late 1960s and early 1970s, and that people then moved into engineering, medicine, banking and information technology, and abroad, to the United States, Europe, the Gulf and Australia. [n34; Vasai Kupari blog, 10 Sep 2007; Post in Mumbai, 25 Mar 2015] They put the community at “about 40,000 to 45,000” or “around 60,000+”, about 97 or 95 per cent Roman Catholic. No census counts Kuparis; the name itself does not appear in any of fifteen primary sources searched, from the 1882 Gazetteer to the 1931 census, and its first attestations are modern. [n34] So these numbers are self-report, and the book gives them as such: tens of thousands.

Education and the Church had been the way out of the fields since the nineteenth century, when the Gazetteer noted that wealthy Christian families sent sons to St Mary’s or St Xavier’s in Bombay and “often bring up one or more of their sons for the Church”. [n34; Gazetteer XIII, pp. 205, 215–216] By the 1990s that path was a highway. The son of Bhuigaon who became the diocese’s first bishop, Thomas Dabre, born there on 23 October 1945, is one example; no source says he is Kupari, but his birthplace is a Kupari village. [n34; Catholic-Hierarchy] The Kupari villages were not abandoned, but their young people increasingly went to work in Bombay and abroad, and the gardens were tended by the old, or leased, or sold.

What survived was the culture in a new form. The Kupari songs of Nandakhal, about a bride leaving her parents, the father’s goodbye, women fetching fodder, the groom’s first shave in the days of child marriage, and a daughter coming home after the monsoon rice planting, are, a community site says, “rarely heard as lifestyles have changed”. [n13; Vasaiker] The red cap and the red lugda sari that marked Kupari men and women are, by a 2026 account, “largely absent from daily life but remain part of community identity”. [n34; All About Vasai Virar, 29 Jun 2026] The 1882 Gazetteer had recorded red broadcloth caps among Hindu Samvedi men too, so the red head-cloth was older than conversion and shared across the line of faith. [n34; Gazetteer XIII, p. 82] It was in these decades that scholars inside the community began to write the culture down. A Nandakhal teacher, Paul Rumav, edited collections of Samvedi folk songs, Samvedi Lokgeete, and a book of proverbs of the Vasai area, as well as Madhachya Ghagari (“Pots of honey”) and a study of the Samvedi Christian community; the dates of these books are not given in the notes. [n20; Marathi Wikipedia: सामवेदी बोली]

The Vadvals and the wadi

The Vadvals of the south had the oldest claim to the word wadi itself. The Gazetteers of 1882 and 1909 consistently describe the Vadvals and Pachkalshis as gardeners and husbandmen of coconut, plantain, sugarcane and betel gardens, with some working as carpenters, boat-builders, carters and axemen; their women sold vegetables. [n41; Gazetteer XIII-1, p. 130] They are split along the line of faith: Hindu Vadvals, who call themselves Somvanshi Kshatriya Pathare or Panchkalshi, and Christian Vadvals, who are part of the wider East Indian community. [n41, §13] The Gazetteer had recorded Christian Vadval cartmen of Chulna, Manikpur, Gokhivra, Sandor and Barhanpur in Bassein, “the best axemen in the district”, travelling in gangs of five to thirty carts by night. [n41; Gazetteer XIII-1, pp. 97–98]

Every one of those villages, Chulne, Manikpur, Gokhivare, Sandor, lies on the southern edge of the island, close to Vasai Road station, exactly where the boom of the 1990s hit hardest. Manikpur gave its name to one of the four councils. Gokhivare had become a census town with 19,772 people by 2001. [n12; Wikipedia: Gokhivare] There is no economic survey of who owned the wadis that were sold; the notes found none. [n41, Gaps] But the geography makes the inference hard to avoid: the Vadval south was swallowed first.

The Vadval dialect, Vadvali, which Grierson had recorded in 1905 as spoken by 3,500 people in the coastal talukas of Bassein, Mahim and Dahanu, and which modern linguists identify with Phudagi, was by 2000 counted at about 2,500 native speakers, its use as a first language falling among the young. [n41; LSI VII, p. 145; Wikipedia: Phudagi] Community leaders began recording and printing oral folk songs because they feared the language would disappear. [n41; indiandistricts.in, relaying PLSI 2017] The Somvanshi Kshatriya Samaj Sangh organised sports festivals, and community websites later offered directories, matrimonial listings and blood-donor services. [n41; vadvalsamaj.com] The pattern is the same as the Kuparis’: the gardens shrank, the speech thinned, and the identity moved into associations, print and festivals.

The best-known Vadval politicians of the century, if the community lists are right, include Bhausaheb Vartak; and Wikipedia’s list of Somvanshi Kshatriya Pathare people includes Hitendra Thakur himself, though his own page does not state his community and this research could not confirm it. [n41, §12] If the list is right, the man whose rise this chapter has traced, and the movement that opposed him, both came out of the old garden communities of the island: one Hindu and Vadval, one Catholic and largely Kupari. That would make the fight over the green belt, among other things, a fight inside the coast’s oldest families about what their land was for. It is an inference, offered as one.

Mogra and sonchafa

Not everything died. One crop not only survived but changed, and its story is told by the growers themselves.

The flower belt runs along an eighteen-kilometre stretch from the Arnala coast to Vasai, through about twenty-five villages, where about five hundred families grow flowers on plots from four gunthas to three acres. [n17; 101Reporters, 7 Jan 2023] A 2011 study found that traditional farming with crop rotation and intercropping persisted in Arnala, Virar, Vasai and Nallasopara, and that Arnala and Agashi had an extensive flower system peaking at Ganesh Chaturthi, Diwali and Navratri, with prices rising up to twenty-fold at festival times; ninety per cent of flower growers named price swings as a constraint. [n17; Vazhacharickal and Buerkert 2011]

The earlier favourite was mogra, Jasminum sambac, the white jasmine that fetched premium prices at the Dadar flower market, about an hour and a half away. [n17; 101Reporters] Then, in the late 1980s, came a newcomer. The sonchafa, Magnolia champaca, the golden champak, in a variety called “Velankar chafa” after Uday Velankar’s nursery at Kudal in the south Konkan, was brought to Arnala by Moreshwar Bhoir, grandfather of Kiran Patil, who would later chair the Arnala Farmers’ Cooperative, a society of 1,100 members. [n17; 101Reporters] The timing is striking: the sonchafa arrived in the same years as the plan that de-reserved the green belt. The research notes suggest, without any source to confirm it, that sonchafa may have been an adaptation, since it needs little water as the ponds failed; they flag it as a plausible story beat, not a fact. [n17, Inferences]

What the sources do give is the economics, from a 2023 report looking back. Each shrub gives 150 to 200 flowers a day. A box of a hundred sells for about 150 rupees, rising to about 700 at Ganesh Chaturthi. One grower, Subhash Bhatte of Satpale, produced about 11,000 flowers a day and earned about 75,000 rupees a month from one acre. [n17; 101Reporters] For a family on a small plot, that is a living, and it is one of the few ways left in which the land of the island still paid for itself as land.

The sea, the salt and the mangroves

The sea side of the coast changed more slowly, and its records are thinner.

The fishing villages, Arnala, Pachubandar and the Naigaon Koliwada, remained among the main fishing harbours of the district. [n20; eSakal, 19 Jan 2023] Their people are Kolis, Hindu and Christian, the Christian Kolis being, as Marathi Wikipedia puts it, “Kolis who were originally Hindu, converted to Christianity by the Portuguese of Vasai”. [n20; mr.wikipedia: ख्रिस्ती कोळी समाज] The Mangela Kolis count Pachubandar, Vasai and Arnala among their villages. [n08; Wikipedia: Mangela Kolis (search summary)] In 2011 Arnala village had 15,331 people and the fort island, Arnala Killa, 2,566. [n11; census2011.co.in]

What changed at sea in these years was the method of fishing. “A few decades ago, Vasai’s fisherfolk used to catch a variety of fish with their traditional dol (bag) nets,” a fisheries collective reports; Vasai “has become largely a one-fish town,” dependent on pomfret, and even that is falling. Overfishing by trawlers and purse-seiners is blamed, and dolnet boats in Vasai reportedly doubled to about a hundred in a decade. Creek pollution chokes breeding grounds, and educated young people are leaving fishing. [n17; n08; Citizen Matters; Goya; ICSF; Outlook] Exact dates for these changes are not in the notes, and no catch statistics for Arnala, Vasai, Pachubandar or Naigaon were found. [n17, Gaps]

The salt pans, too, were still there at the end of the period, about two thousand acres of them in Vasai by one count. [n17; Asian Age; Business Standard, 2007] Salt had been in Bassein island’s “brisk trade to Gujarat and Bombay” in 1882. [n17; Gazetteer 1882] Salt-pan land, flat, open and close to the railway, was exactly the kind of land a builder or a planner would look at next, and the next chapter tells how they did.

The mangroves of the Vasai creek were already under attack. A 2009 blog repost of a newspaper story claimed that about two hundred acres of mangroves had been cleared in one year on the Mira-Bhayandar side of the creek, and put the creek’s mangroves at about 3,000 hectares over 47 kilometres, of which about half had been destroyed. The sourcing is weak, and the notes advise verifying it against the original. [n17; blog repost, Jul 2009] It is given here as the alarm that was being raised at the end of the decade, not as a measurement.

On the eastern side, the forest of Tungar received legal protection. The Tungareshwar Wildlife Sanctuary was notified on 24 October 2003 (another source says November 2003), covering about 85.7 square kilometres of hill forest that links Sanjay Gandhi National Park to the south with the Tansa sanctuary to the north-east. [n12; Wikipedia; DNA; KBA factsheet] In 1869–71 the colonial government had considered making Tungar a cheap hill station “for poor families who cannot avail themselves of the expensive accommodation of Matheran”. [n12; Gazetteer 1882] In 2003 it became the green wall at the back of the new city, with some six hundred plant species, more than 250 bird species and 150 butterfly species recorded since. [n17; Nature in Focus]

The Adivasis of the eastern belt, Warlis, Katkaris and Thakurs, had their own struggle in these years, and its leader was the man who would end Hitendra Thakur’s run in Vasai. Vivek Pandit, born in Dahanu on 23 August 1957, had left Mumbai with his wife Vidyullata to work in Dahisar village in Vasai taluka, and in 1982 founded the Shramajeevi Sanghatana against bonded labour. [n26; n08; Wikipedia: Vivek Raghunath Pandit] In 1998 the organisation challenged the state’s claim that there had been no bonded labour since 1992. [n08; India Environment Portal (Indian Express, 1998)] In 1999 Pandit received an International Anti-Slavery Award. [n26] The Gazetteer of 1882 had noted that Varlis who pledged their land were employed in tillage in the rains, an early sign of debt bondage to moneylenders. [n08; Gazetteer 1882, p. 183] The struggle Pandit led ran from that moneylender bondage of the nineteenth century to the brick-kiln advance-payment system of the twenty-first. [n08, Inferences]

Water: Usgaon, Pelhar and the tanker

If one question defined daily life in the new city it was water, and the answer, for twenty years, was not enough.

The old towns had small schemes of their own. When an environmental-clearance document for a new regional water project was written in 2014, it listed the supply that existed before: the old Pelhar scheme, 14 million litres a day; the Usgaon scheme, 24.5 million litres a day; Papadkhind, 1.5 million; and a figure of 100 million litres a day from Maswan (or Masvan), which the research notes flag as odd and in need of checking. [n29; n12; Surya project executive summary, 30 Sep 2014] Pelhar and Usgaon are dams; their years of construction and their full capacities were not found. [n06, Gaps; n12, Gaps]

Set those figures against a population that passed 693,000 in 2001 and was heading for a million and a quarter. The same document says that most residents relied on tanker water. [n29; Surya summary] The 2019 pond-restoration report gives the scale as it stood around 2012: about thirteen lakh people, 1.3 million, depended on tankers and bore wells, and tankers moved about fifteen million litres a day from bore wells along the Mumbai-Ahmedabad highway. [n17; Vikalp Sangam] So when the Samiti won its tanker ban in the green belt in the early 1990s, the tankers did not disappear; they moved east, to the wells along the national highway.

The Pelhar dam has a second place in this story. When the railway later blamed flooding on the Vasai-Virar tracks on construction over salt pans, mangroves and waterholes, and on choked culverts, it added one more cause: overflow from the Pelhar dam. [n06; PropNewsTime] The same small reservoir that was supposed to supply the new city could, in a heavy monsoon, help to drown it.

26 July 2005

On 26 July 2005 the monsoon broke over the Mumbai region with a force no one living had seen. In the Mumbai municipal area alone more than 419 people died. [n29; Deccan Herald (search summary)] For Vasai, the sources are frustrating. A Deccan Herald article, undated, carries the title “When Vasai turned waterworld”, and a 2021 piece on new floods says the rains “bring back images of July 2005”. Residents of Vasai still call it the benchmark against which every later flood is measured. But this research found no Vasai-only death toll and no local rainfall figure for that day. [n29; Deccan Herald (both via search summary)]

What can be said is what kind of land the water fell on. By 2005 the low eastern ground of Achole, Tulinj, Gokhivare and Waliv, the salt waste of the Gazetteer, had been built on for fifteen years. The ponds of the west were silting. The creeks were narrowed by fill. The water that had once spread across paddy and salt pan and soaked into bavkhal now had fewer places to go. Activists would make this argument explicitly after the floods of 2018, and the railway would make it after 2025; for 2005 no study ties the two together, and the book makes the connection only as the notes do, as an argument made by others. [n17, Inferences]

The railway north

The railway itself was changing, slowly. Through these years Virar was the end of the line for the electric multiple-unit locals, sixty kilometres from Churchgate; beyond it, MEMU and passenger trains ran north towards Dahanu. In 2002 Western Railway announced that it would extend EMU services from Virar to Dahanu Road. [n29; Wikipedia: Western line (search summary)] The extension would take eleven years to arrive. The Virar fast local, the train that would become a byword for crowding across Mumbai, was the daily experience of hundreds of thousands, but the notes found no sourced history of the first Churchgate-Virar fast services or the quadrupling of the line in these years. [n12, Gaps] The four o’clock train that had carried Vasai’s bananas south now carried Vasai’s clerks north.

The Diocese of Vasai

In the middle of these years the Catholic community of the coast received a gift that it had, in a sense, been waiting for since 1739.

When the Marathas took Bassein, the seat of the Vicar-General of the North, who had governed the Church from Bassein since the sixteenth century, moved away to Thana, Kurla and finally to Bombay. [n38; Hull I, p. 6] For more than two and a half centuries afterwards, the parishes of Vasai were governed from somewhere else: from Goa under the Padroado until 1886, from the new diocese of Daman from 1886 to 1928, and from Bombay from 1928. [n38; Hull I; Archdiocese of Bombay; Diocese of Vasai (snippet)] Pope John Paul II had made Vasai the first stop of his Bombay visit on 9 February 1986, calling it the place “where the Christian faith was first proclaimed and Christian communities were first established”, as the previous chapter recounted. [n38; Vatican: Address at Vasai, 9 Feb 1986]

From 1990 the Archdiocese of Bombay had an auxiliary bishop with special charge of Vasai: Thomas Dabre, born at Bhuigaon in 1945, ordained in 1971. [n38; n34; Catholic-Hierarchy] On 22 May 1998 John Paul II signed the decree Cum ad aeternam, erecting the Diocese of Vasai out of the Archdiocese of Bombay. It was announced on 28 June 1998, and on 15 August 1998, Indian Independence Day, Dabre was installed as its first bishop in the church of Our Lady of Grace at Papdi, which became the cathedral. [n38; Archdiocese of Bombay; Wikipedia: Diocese of Vasai; n11] Papdi’s church had been built in 1565 and rebuilt in 1864; in 1882 it had a congregation of 1,294 and a parish school with fifty-six pupils and a violin master. [n38; n11; Hull I, p. 5; Gazetteer XIV, p. 37] It was now the mother church of a diocese.

The diocese took as its principal patron St Gonsalo Garcia, the Franciscan lay brother born at Bassein about 1556, crucified at Nagasaki on 5 February 1597 and canonised in 1862, the saint regarded as the first Indian-born saint of the Catholic Church. [n38; n26; Catholic Encyclopedia; Diocese of Vasai] The diocese itself traces Christianity in the area back to the apostle Bartholomew, a claim of tradition that most scholars doubt. [n38; Wikipedia: Diocese of Vasai]

How big was this Church at the end of the period? A Vatican Radio report from November 2009, written when the second bishop was named, gave about 122,000 Catholics, 139 priests, 499 religious, 33 churches and 56 convents, with 4 hospitals, 3 senior and 2 junior colleges, 23 high schools, 56 primary schools, orphanages, homes for the aged, dispensaries and leprosy homes. [n38; Vatican Radio archive, 11 Nov 2009] Other counts differ widely, and the notes advise comparing like with like. [n38] St Gonsalo Garcia College, Vasai, had been founded in 1984, affiliated to the University of Mumbai. [n38; Wikipedia] The Cardinal Gracias Memorial Hospital at Bangli, Sandor, is a Catholic hospital of 147 beds. [n38; search snippet] By the census of 2011, Christians of all kinds made up 8.25 per cent of the taluka, about 110,800 people: a share far higher than anywhere else in the Mumbai region, and a reflection of the Portuguese-era community. [n06; n34; Wikipedia: Vasai taluka]

The diocese gave the community something the green-belt movement could not: an institutional voice of its own, rooted on the coast, with a bishop who had been born in a Kupari village. The first bishop spoke on the building boom. [n38; AsiaNews] Dabre led the diocese until 2009, when he was transferred to Poona; the diocese’s own history says 2008, which conflicts, and the notes prefer 4 April 2009. [n26; n38] He was succeeded by Felix Anthony Machado, born at Remedy in Vasai in 1948, who had spent a decade at the Pontifical Council for Interreligious Dialogue in Rome, helped organise the 2002 Assisi prayer meeting, and served as Bishop of Nashik. Machado was named to Vasai on 10 November 2009 and installed on 19 December 2009. [n26; n38; GCatholic; Wikipedia: Felix Machado]

D’Britto’s Bible

In the same years, the priest who had led the green-belt crowd turned to a different labour. From about 1997 to 2010, Fr Francis D’Britto worked on a Marathi translation of the Bible, with introductions and about two hundred illustrations, which would be published as the Subodh Bible, the “easily understood Bible”, in 2010. Reports differ on whether it took thirteen or fifteen years. [n38; Wikipedia; Vatican Radio, 3 Aug 2010] It would win the state’s translation award in 2013 and the Sahitya Akademi award in 2014, reported as the first Bible translation to win a national award. [n38] It belongs to a line that runs back on this coast to Thomas Stephens’s Kristapurana of 1616, the great Marathi Christian epic, of which a Devanagari edition in the East Indian dialect had been printed at Vasai in 1922. [n38; Wikipedia: Krista Purana]

East Indians and the state

One more step in these years shaped the community’s legal identity. In 2006 the state of Maharashtra issued a government resolution placing “East Indians” on its list of Other Backward Classes, at serial number 341, after a campaign led by Oneil Kinny, Prem Moraes and the Maharashtra Christi Boomputra Bandhav Sangathana, with the Bombay East Indian Association as a party. [n34; east-indians.com] The name East Indian goes back to 1887, when the Catholics of the Bombay coast, then called “Portuguese Christians”, renamed themselves for Queen Victoria’s Golden Jubilee. [n38; Wikipedia: Bombay East Indians] The resolution does not name Kupari or Samvedi Christians; the notes’ reading is that a Kupari family would obtain a certificate as an East Indian, but the text of the resolution was unreadable to this research. [n34, Inferences]

The calendar that held

Through all of this, one thing held: the coast’s religious year. The sources for it are modern, most of them newspaper reports from the 2010s and 2020s, and none dates a particular festival to these twenty years. But they describe rhythms that are old, that ran through the boom, and that the newcomers joined.

In late autumn, on Kartiki Ekadashi, the yatra of Nirmal begins and runs for about fifteen days. On the first day a procession of schoolchildren goes out, a dindi, followed by a palanquin carrying the image of the Shankaracharya whose samadhi the shrine holds. Pilgrims bathe in the Vimal tank, where rites for the dead are performed. Stalls line the road from Nirmal Naka to Bhuigaon Phata selling toys, sweets and dry fruit, and among them Vasai sukeli, dates, pedhe, water chestnuts and groundnuts. [n20; Lokmat (snippet)] Nirmal is the place where the Gazetteer recorded the legend of the demon Vimal and Parashurama’s linga, and from which, it said, the place took its name, “Nirmal or stainless”. [n10; Gazetteer 1882] It is also, a few hundred yards away, the site of the Holy Cross church, one of the oldest Catholic foundations on the coast. [n38] The Hindu fair and the Catholic parish share a village.

At Dussehra and through Navratri the crowds climb the hill at Virar to Jivdani, the goddess on Jivdhan hill, whose fort Chimaji Appa’s men took on 31 March 1739. [n20; mr.wikipedia: जिवदानी देवी] The temple’s age is disputed: the district website says about 150 years; one Marathi account says the idol’s consecration was performed by devotees in 1956; popular sites claim far more. [n10; n20] The old fort has become a seven-storey temple reached by about 1,375 steps, and a funicular was planned for the hill; a 2020 report said it would open in January 2021. [n20; n12; Onmanorama 2020] Jivdani is the family goddess of the Mangela, Koli and Bhandari communities, and worshipped by Agri and Dhiwar people too. [n08] In the new city she became something more: a pilgrimage for anyone on the Western line.

In January, on the first Sunday nearest the neap tide after Christmas, the Catholics gather in the ruined Jesuit church of the Holy Name inside Vasai Fort for the feast of St Gonsalo Garcia, the Killa cha Sann, “the fort’s festival”. Families arrive the evening before, camp in enclosures and cook East Indian dishes, moile and stew; once they came by bullock cart or by boat. [n38; n09; FPJ, 11 Jan 2026; DNA India, 12 Jan 2014] Devotion to the saint had lapsed after 1739 and been revived. [n38; Catholic Encyclopedia] His modern shrine stands at Gass, the church built in 1942 by Msgr Louis Caetan D’Souza and renovated in 1957, which the diocese calls its major shrine. [n38; Indian Catholic Matters; Catholics India]

Between them, Nirmal, Jivdani and the fort mark out the coast’s sacred geography: a Shaiva tank of the Puranas, a goddess on a hill above a Maratha fort, and a Portuguese church in ruins where a saint born on this coast is honoured. All three outlasted the boom. All three are now reached by rickshaw from a railway station.

Languages at the turn of the century

By the end of these twenty years the coast spoke more languages than at any time since its days as an international port.

At the bottom of the layers lay the old local dialects, now all endangered. Kadodi, or Samvedi, the speech of the Samvedi Brahmins and the Kupari Christians, a Marathi-Konkani dialect with Gujarati and Portuguese elements, oral, written in Devanagari when written at all, was spoken by perhaps 50,000 to 60,000 people in sixteen villages of north Vasai, from Bhuigaon, Wagholi, Kofrad and Gass to Umrale, Bolinj, Vatar and Mardes. [n22; n20; Wikipedia: Kadodi; Saamana, 22 Mar 2026] Its signature is the change of s to h, so that sukeli, the dried banana, becomes hukeli. [n20; mr.wikipedia] Vadvali, or Phudagi, spoken by the Panchkalshis and Chaukalshis, had about 2,500 speakers in 2000. [n41] The East Indian Marathi of the Christian villages kept its Portuguese words, mai and pai for mother and father, kadair for chair from cadeira, zanel for window from janela. [n13; FPJ, 14 Mar 2024] The Koli speech of the fishing villages, which the Gazetteer had found so curious in accent, and the Agri, Warli, Katkari and Thakur speech of the east, carried on, uncounted, folded by the census into “Marathi”. [n22, Gaps]

Above them lay standard Marathi, the language of the state, the schools, the Church’s monthly and D’Britto’s Bible. And arriving on top of all of it, carried by the train, came Hindi, Gujarati, Bhojpuri, Urdu and Marwari. When the 2011 census was taken, Marathi would be less than half. [n22]

2008 and 2009: the corporation

The end of the period came in a rush of reorganisation.

In 2008 the delimitation commission redrew the constituencies. Nallasopara got its own assembly seat, as did Boisar to the north; Vasai remained. [n29; n26] A new Palghar parliamentary constituency was created, taking in the Vasai, Nallasopara and Boisar segments. [n26; Wikipedia: Palghar Lok Sabha] In the same year Hitendra Thakur became president of the Bahujan Vikas Aaghadi. [n29; Wikipedia]

On 3 July 2009 the state created the Vasai-Virar City Municipal Corporation. Its own website says it was established that day as a municipality formed from four municipal councils, Vasai, Virar, Nallasopara and Navghar-Manikpur, and fifty-three villages. [n29; VVCMC, About] Some Wikipedia pages and two environmental-clearance documents give 2010 instead; the notes take 3 July 2009 as the constituting date, with 2010 probably reflecting the first elected body or the effective date of the notification, a guess that needs the government resolution to settle. [n29, §5; n06]

The inclusion of the villages was bitterly contested. Wikipedia’s page on Hitendra Thakur says that on 3 July 2009 the state notified the inclusion of fifty-three villages, and that forty-nine of them opposed it. [n29; Wikipedia] The villages were the green belt: the garden and fishing villages that the movement of the 1990s had kept out of the urbanisable zone. To be inside a municipal corporation meant urban taxes, urban building rules, and, the villagers feared, urban development. Vivek Pandit led the opposition through the Gao Bachao Andolan Samiti, the “save the villages movement committee”. [n26; Wikipedia: Vivek Pandit] Wikipedia also says that Thakur allegedly influenced the police to arrest and assault Pandit during the protest. That is an allegation, recorded on Wikipedia, and there is no finding on it. [n29; n26] A local blog of 2011 later reported a state notification excluding twenty-nine of the villages, with a court case following; a 2022 governance study gives the village count variously as 53, 59 or 49 coastal villages “excluded”. The notes treat these only as pointers. [n29; n12; vasairoad blog, Sept 2011; Praja, Nov 2022]

Then came the October 2009 assembly elections, and with them a result that no one in the Thakur camp could have wanted. In the new Nallasopara seat, Kshitij Thakur, Hitendra’s elder son, won for the BVA with 89,284 votes, 52.84 per cent, beating Shirish Chavhan of the Shiv Sena by 40,782 on a turnout of 48.06 per cent. He was called the youngest MLA in the state. [n29; n26; Wikipedia: Nallasopara AC; Wikipedia: Kshitij Thakur] In Boisar the BVA’s Vilas Tare won. [n26] In Palghar the BVA’s Baliram Jadhav was elected to the Lok Sabha, the only time the party ever held the parliamentary seat. [n26; Wikipedia: Palghar Lok Sabha]

But in Vasai itself, Vivek Pandit, standing as an independent with Shiv Sena support, beat the BVA’s candidate, Narayan Mankar, by 16,798 votes. [n29; n26; Wikipedia: Vasai AC] The seat that Hitendra Thakur had held since March 1990, through one Congress victory and three as an independent, went to the leader of the Adivasi bonded-labour movement, the same man who had led the villages against their inclusion in the corporation that summer.

The research notes sum up the moment: the family held the region’s politics in 2009 through two seats, one held by the son, even as the father lost Vasai, and the creation of the corporation changed the contest into a civic one. [n29, §2 Inferences] The father had lost a seat. The family was about to gain a city.

The coast in 2009

Stand back and look at what twenty years had done.

In 1990 the coast had a quarter of a million people, or not many more, in a dozen small towns and a hundred villages. In 2009 it had something over a million in one corporation. Hindi and Gujarati and Bhojpuri were spoken on every platform. The towers of the railway strip ran almost unbroken from the creek to Virar. Nallasopara, the halt once called Neela, was a town of nearly two hundred thousand at the 2001 count, and still growing on the flood-prone ground east of the line.

The old island was still there, and it was green, because a priest and a hundred thousand people had made the state redraw a plan. But its ponds were silting, its young people were working in offices in Bombay and the Gulf, its dialects were spoken mostly by the old, and its families were selling land. Mogra and sonchafa still went to Dadar in the mornings. The Kolis of Arnala and Pachubandar still went to sea, catching less. The church of Papdi had become a cathedral; the diocese had a new bishop from Remedy; and the priest who had led the crowd was finishing a Bible.

Water came in tankers. Floods came every monsoon. Buildings rose on land reserved for other things, and some of them were made legal afterwards. The man who had held the Vasai seat for nineteen years had just lost it, and his family now stood ready to run a municipal corporation with fifty-three villages that did not want to be in it.

On 28 March 2010 the new corporation would seat its first mayor. What that city would become, through flood and pandemic, fire and collapse, demolition and election, and how the oldest names on this coast would fare inside it, is the subject of the last chapter of this part of the book.
