A reader's guide

How this book is arranged

Every Grain tells the story of one stretch of the Konkan coast, about forty miles north of Mumbai, in three different ways. They answer three different questions, and the book is arranged so that each answer sits where a reader will look for it.

Book One, The Coast Through Time, asks what happened here. It runs in order from the cooling of the planet to November 2026: the lava that made the ground, the sea that drowned and returned, the port of Shurparaka and its Ashokan edicts, the Shilahara kings, the sultans and the Portuguese, the walled city of Baçaim and its fall in 1739, the Marathas, the Company, the railway and the republic. If you read only one book, read this one.

Book Two, Three Towns and a Shore, asks what the same story looks like from the ground. Vasai, Nallasopara and Virar each have their own history, their own water, their own peoples and their own villages, and the sea-edge between them has its own. Where Book One goes through a century in a page, Book Two walks a single street.

Book Three, The People and the Threads, asks who lived through it and what ran through all of it. It follows the Kuparis, the Catholic community whose villages are the heart of this country, from the Samaveda Brahmins of the eleventh century to their descendants today, and then picks up five threads that cross every period: the sea and its traders, the way each community and its language formed, how each settlement was born, the Catholic story, and food.

A short Epilogue brings the grains of rice back to the table, and the Appendices hold the working tools: a directory of people, places and sacred sites, a note on languages and calendars, the open questions, and the sources.

Names

The same place has had many names. The island of Sopara is Shurparaka in Sanskrit, Suppara in Greek and Sopara today. The creek city is Basai to the Gujarat sultans, Baçaim to the Portuguese, Bassein to the English, Vasai to the Marathas and to the present. The book uses the name that the period used, and says so when the name changes. Vasai is also the modern municipal city; where the difference matters, the text says which is meant.

Dates and spellings

Dates are given in the calendar of the source and, where the two differ, in both. The Maratha records Y. N. Kelkar quotes use the Old Style (Julian) calendar, eleven days behind the New Style used by Portuguese and modern sources; the surrender of Bassein is therefore 16 May 1739 in one reckoning and 5 May in the other. Portuguese names are given as the sources print them, with the cedilla where it belongs. Translations from Portuguese, Marathi and Latin are the author’s own and are marked where they matter.

Citations, evidence and doubt

Every statement of fact carries a tag such as [n83 §2]. It points to a numbered research note, which sets out the source, the exact wording found, and any doubt. In this printed edition the tags are set in small grey type so that they do not interrupt the story, but they have not been removed: a reader who wants to check a claim can follow it to the note. The notes label what the sources say separately from what the author infers; the text keeps the same distinction, and uses words such as “perhaps” and “the likeliest reading” only where the evidence stops. Where a source is a Gazetteer of 1882, a Portuguese chronicler writing sixty years after the event, or a Wikipedia summary with weak footnotes, the text says so.

What the book cannot do

Large parts of this coast’s past are not written down, above all the lives of the ordinary families who ploughed, fished and prayed here. The book does not invent them. The chapter called The Silences in the Prologue and the Open Questions appendix list what is missing, and the Epilogue invites readers who hold family papers, parish books or oral histories to add them.
