Period covered: 1600 – 1739

In this chapter. For a century and a half Baçaim was the capital of the Portuguese North. This chapter follows its great years, the storm of 1618, its slow decline and the siege of 1737–39 that ended it.

Above a church door inside the walls of Baçaim, a stonecutter carved a sentence that someone could still read in the 1870s. Its Portuguese runs: No anno de 1601, sendo Arcebispo Primaz o Illmo. Sr. Dom Frei Aleixo de Menezes, e vigário o Pe. Pedro Galvão Pereira, se reformou esta Matriz. “In the year 1601, the Most Illustrious Lord Dom Frei Aleixo de Menezes being Archbishop Primate, and Father Pedro Galvão Pereira vicar, this mother church was rebuilt.” [n32; DC 214-216; BG 38-39]

The lintel is a good place to begin, because it shows the city as it saw itself at the turn of the seventeenth century. Two lifetimes after Sultan Bahadur Shah signed the coast away aboard the São Mateus, Baçaim had no need to fortify itself in haste. It could rebuild its cathedral in dressed stone and put an archbishop’s name over the door. The new Matriz of St Joseph stood where an earlier church of about 1546 had stood. That church had been founded under King João III, whose letter of March 1546 ordered the money once spent on the town’s mosques, 3,000 pardaos, to go to its clergy instead. Inside the rebuilt church, to the right of the main altar, a black stone with a Latin inscription later recorded that the bones of Pedro Galvão, who had “managed and enlarged this temple”, had been brought there. He died at Goa on 19 March 1618. [n32; DC 214-216; BG 38-39]

That date of death falls two months before the worst day in the city’s history before its last one. This chapter runs from that confident lintel to a breach in the wall of São Sebastião, and to a sentence a British official wrote 143 years after the fall: “Thus Bassein fell as falls a stately tree never to rise.” [n15; Thana Gaz. XIV, p. 34]

1600 – 1617: The city at its height

By 1600 the Portuguese called the place Baçaim. Duarte Barbosa had written it “Baxay” in 1514, and the Gujarat sultans knew it as Basai. The Marathas would later rename it, and the British would call it Bassein. In this chapter it is Baçaim, the name its own inhabitants used, and “Bassein” only when an English traveller is speaking. [n22; Wikipedia: Vasai, Fort Vasai; n04]

The fortifications that framed the city were, in a sense, brand new in 1600. Fort-building had begun again in 1590, the 1882 Gazetteer says, and was finished “about the close of the century”. A description from the late 1590s, quoted by the Gazetteer from the Cronista de Tissuary and from Gerson da Cunha, counts eleven bastions mounting ninety pieces of artillery (twenty-seven of them bronze) and seventy mortars (seven bronze), with twenty-one gunboats of sixteen to eighteen guns each. The same Gazetteer, describing the ruin in 1882, counts ten bastions, so the number is uncertain even inside one book. Modern Wikipedia editors also flag the artillery figures as poorly sourced. [n32; BG 30, 34 n.8; n04; Wikipedia: Vasai Fort]

The physical outline is clearer. The wall ran about a mile and a half round, an irregular ten-sided figure of stone thirty to thirty-five feet high, rising to forty-five feet on the west, and “not more than five feet thick”. At each corner stood a four-sided bastion. The Gazetteer’s footnote gives their names, a litany of saints and one animal: Nossa Senhora dos Remédios, Reis Magos, São Tiago, São Gonçalo, Madre de Deus, São João, Elefante, São Pedro, São Paulo and São Sebastião. The last of these, built under Captain Francisco de Sá and dated by its stone to 22 February 1554, will carry the end of this chapter. There were two great double gateways, the Porta do Mar (sea gate) and the Porta do Campo (land gate), and a postern. [n32; BG 38 and n.2, 42-43]

The walls, gate by gate

The fort is better described in the nineteenth-century books than in anything the Portuguese themselves left in print that this book could reach, so its shape has to be pieced together from ruins measured by British officials and a Goan doctor. They did not fully agree. The 1882 Gazetteer, walking the circuit, saw an irregular decagon with ten bastions, one at each corner, and listed their names in a footnote. Its own historical section, drawing on the late-1590s description, says eleven. And the French scholar Anquetil-Duperron, who saw the fort in 1760, a generation after it fell, called it “a regular hexagon”, with bastions carrying nine guns on each face and a square bastion covering many lengths of curtain wall. [n32; BG 30, 38 and n.2, 34 n.2] The safest statement is the Gazetteer’s own field description: about a mile and a half of wall, ten bastions, two double gateways and a postern. The “eleven” and the “hexagon” are variants the reader should know exist. [n32, Inferences]

Nobody in the sources gives the bastions’ positions on a plan, their sizes, or their building dates, with two exceptions. [n32, Gaps] One is the oldest stone in the fort, the 1536 inscription naming Garcia de Sá as the first captain who built the fortress “by command of the Governor Nuno da Cunha”. Da Cunha found it in the 1870s with the help of Mr J. H. Littlewood, the man who then leased the fort’s interior, set in a ruined bastion near the citadel. [n32; DC 217; BG 39] The other is the stone of São Sebastião, dated 22 February 1554, which names King João III, the Viceroy Dom Afonso de Noronha and Captain Francisco de Sá. By 1882 that stone had been prised out and lay neglected near the land gateway. [n32; BG 42-43] Which of the ten was the “Elefante”, and which the “Madre de Deus”, no source in the notes says. [n32, Gaps]

The gates are clearer. The Porta do Mar, facing the river and the landing, had massive teak doors cased in iron bars and spikes; the date carved on one of those doors, 1720, belongs to the end of this chapter. The Porta do Campo, the land gate, faced the fields and the creek; by 1882 its woodwork was broken. The postern was a small door behind the cloisters of the Franciscan convent, and it was closed off by the bastion of São Sebastião, which stood beside it. [n32; BG 38-39; DC 213-214] That small fact matters later. The bastion through which the Marathas broke in, in May 1739, guarded the fort’s back door.

Inside, the space was not evenly used. The seaward half of the enclosure was built up with churches, palaces and houses; the landward third was empty ground. [n32; DC 213; BG 38] The chapter returns to that empty third below, because three different explanations have been offered for it and none is proven.

An inscription catalogue

What the fort lacks in surviving plans it makes up for in carved stone. Read in date order, the inscriptions that da Cunha and the Gazetteer transcribed form a skeleton of the city’s history: 1536, the first captain’s stone; 1554, São Sebastião; 1558, the tomb of a royal councillor in the Franciscan church; 1591, the tomb of Isabel de Aguiar in the Jesuit church; 1601, the rebuilt Matriz; 1606, the citadel portal and the tomb of Dona Francisca de Miranda; 1617, the shell-studded bath-house; 1618, the bones of Pedro Galvão; 1628, the tomb of Dona Filipa da Fonseca; 1631, the portal of St Francis Xavier; 1636, the Jesuit college door and the jail of the same years; 1676, the tomb of Antonio Teles de Menezes; and 1720, the sea-gate door. [n32; BG 38-43; DC 213-232, 237+] A caution comes with the list. The nineteenth-century scans of both books are badly damaged at exactly these points, and the research notes warn against quoting any inscription’s exact wording without checking the printed plates. [n32, Summary of corrections]

A walk from the sea gate

The Gazetteer’s compilers walked the ruins in a set order, from the sea gate to the land gate, and following them is the nearest a reader can come to entering the living city of the 1600s. [n32; BG 38-43]

Just inside the sea gate, on the left, a small Hindu temple to Hanuman stood in 1882. It was not Portuguese at all. Da Cunha dismissed its “mean architecture” and read it as a Maratha memento: “the monkey-god has kept his ascendancy”. [n32; BG 38-39; DC 214] In 1650 the space it occupied was simply the entrance street of a Christian city where, as Fryer would say, no one but Christians could sleep.

Beyond it rose the Matriz of St Joseph, the church whose 1601 lintel opens this chapter. Da Cunha calls it a “cathedral wrongly so called, in reality a matriz”, a mother church but never a bishop’s seat. Its tower stair had decayed by his time; two steps crumbled under him when he tried to climb it. At its west end lay the half-buried tomb of Antonio de Almeida de Sampaio. [n32; BG 38-39; DC 214-216; n37; Hull I, pp. 4-5] To the left of the sea-gate street opened the great square and market, with the arcaded State House where the captain met the nobles, whose morning ritual Fryer describes later in this chapter. [n32; BG 39; DC 218]

Farther on stood the citadel with its 1606 portal, the palaces of the General of the North and of the Captain, the factory and the great rice granary. [n32; DC 216-218, 220; BG 39-40] Along the river-side wall ran the long cloistered pile of the Misericórdia, the church and hospital of the charitable brotherhood, its front finely hewn with a Maltese cross and dragons on the escutcheon. Two tombstones there named a Cabral de Navais and his son Hieronimo. Da Cunha found the hospital’s budget: 140 pardaos a month, or 504,000 réis a year, plus 79,200 réis for rice for the poor. [n32; BG 40; DC 225-226] Those figures are the nearest thing the notes hold to a welfare budget for the Court of the North: food and care for the city’s sick and poor, paid out of the same revenues that kept the captains.

Next to it stood Nossa Senhora da Vida, “one of the oldest churches in Bassein”. The Vasai diocese’s own history, known to this book only through a search snippet, names it as the first Latin church in the fort, of 1536. [n32; BG 40; n38; dioceseofvasai.com snippet] Its ground held a strange burial. When nineteenth-century workmen dug foundations there for a sugar factory, they found the bones of a man and a horse with a rusty rapier: a general buried with his charger, da Cunha thought. [n32; DC 226-227] The man’s name is lost.

The Jesuit church and college came next, with the handsomest façade in the city. Its foundations had been laid in 1548 by Father Melchior Gonçalves, after the Jesuits arrived the year before; St Francis Xavier had visited Bassein three times, once in 1544 and twice in 1548. From 1560 a Commissary of the Inquisition worked from here. [n32; BG 41; DC 228-230; n38; Hull I, p. 4] The Franciscan church of Santo António, the oldest and one of the largest, stood near the land gate. Its great arch by the baptismal chapel was built in the style of the “flat arch” of Belém near Lisbon, in dressed stone with basalt dressings, with four side chapels and cloisters. Xavier had lodged here on his visits. In its floor lay a Councillor of His Majesty who died on 24 August 1558, with his wife Dona Luiza da Silva. [n32; BG 41-42 and n.1; DC 237+] The Dominicans’ São Gonçalo, of 1583, kept its walls, tower and chief chapel into the 1880s, with the ruined tomb of its patron on the gospel side. The Augustinians’ chapel of Nossa Senhora da Anunciada had a double-arched front and a vaulted roof with painted mouldings. [n32; BG 42-43]

The Gazetteer’s general count, across all the Portuguese years, was five convents, thirteen churches, an orphanage and a hospital; Hull, copying da Cunha, counts five convents, the Matriz, six other churches and “one asylum for orphans and maidens”. [n32; BG 32 n.7, 38; n37; Hull I, pp. 4-5] The differences come from counting different things at different dates, and from chapels that came and went.

Inside, the streets ran straight between large squares, and the houses were “stately two-storied dwellings”. The Gazetteer, summarising the Portuguese chroniclers, says Baçaim was “next to Goa the largest and richest of the Portuguese settlements”, with “more than 100 families of the highest in India and proverbially rich”. Its district reached from the Agashi river in the north to Karanja in the south, taking in Thana, Salsette and the islands around Bombay. [n15; Thana Gaz. XIV, pp. 30, 33] Da Cunha’s survey of the old records lists the units of this “Capital of the North”: the Saibana de Baçaim, the Caçabe de Tana, the Ilha de Salcete, the Ilha de Caranja, the Ilha de Bellaflor de Sambayo, the Praganas of Manora and Asserim, and the Ilha de Bombaim. The Portuguese borrowed the local words. A caçabe was the Persian kasaba, a market town; a pragana was the Mughal pargana. The custom-house was the mandovim, and the orchards were hortas. [n32; DC 157-158] The Baçaim district itself stretched about ten miles north to the Agashi river, about twenty-four miles east to Asserim and Manora, and about forty miles south to Karanja. [n32; BG 30 n.5] Wikipedia’s summary, marked “citation needed”, calls this the Province of the North: roughly a hundred kilometres of coast between Daman and Chaul, thirty to fifty kilometres deep. [n04; Wikipedia: Vasai Fort]

People called it “a Corte do Norte”, the Court of the North. It was a resort of fidalgos, the Portuguese gentry, and of rich merchants. A great man of the city might be styled “Fidalgo ou Cavalheiro de Baçaim”. [n04; Wikipedia: Vasai Fort; n18; Vasaiker] The captaincy, the city’s chief office, was held for three-year terms, and Dr John Fryer would later note that it went to men from certain families. Around 1550 the captain had earned 600,000 réis a year. [n32; BG 30 and n.8; DC 218-219] The chief revenue officer, the Tanadar-mor, did not live inside the walls at all. He lived at “Baçaim de Cima”, Upper Baçaim, outside them. [n32; DC 221-222; BG 31]

What paid for all this? The Treaty of 1534 had made every Gujarati ship bound for the Red Sea take a cartaz, a Portuguese pass, at Baçaim and pay duty on its return. That made the city a customs gate as well as a fortress. [n04; Military history of Bassein, Wikipedia] There was trade in the city’s own products too. In 1607 the traveller Pyrard de Laval found it “a great place for ship-building”, with “a large trade in timber and building stone”. The stone, he said, was “as fine and hard as granite”, and was shipped south to raise the churches and palaces of Goa. [n32; BG 30-31; n15; Thana Gaz. XIV, p. 31] Earlier travellers had noted the rice, wheat and timber of the district, and the trade in corn and rice. [n15; Federici c. 1565; Fitch 1583]

The administration was sometimes less grand than the city. Da Cunha found that there was no royal customs house at all. The crown rented a private house for the purpose at eighty-four pardaos a year. He also found a small entry that brings the creek to life: the ferry boatmen of the Bassein river were paid two hundred réis a month. [n32; DC 222-223] The notes give no figures for timber exports, rice or sugar revenue, or customs yields in these years. Those numbers, if they survive, are still in the Portuguese archives and the full Gazetteer volumes. [n04, Economy gaps]

The sea and its traders

Newer research fills part of that gap, and it changes the picture of what kind of port Baçaim was. The Gazetteer’s own judgement, made in the trade chapter of its Thana volumes, is blunt: Bassein was “never so busy a trade centre as Chaul”, but it was “famous for its ship building”. [n36; Gaz XIV pp. 30-31] Under the Portuguese, the Gazetteer explains, “foreign trade was a monopoly of the king. Most of the local sea trade was in the hands of free-traders or interlopers, whom the Portuguese government tried to put down.” After the middle of the sixteenth century, the North Konkan ports lost their old share of the trade with Europe to Goa and Diu. What they kept was “a large coasting traffic with the Malabar, Gujarat, and Sindh ports, and a considerable foreign trade with the Persian Gulf, the Arabian and African coasts”. [n36; Gaz XIII-II p. 465]

The list of ports connected to Bassein, Mahim, Thana and Chaul in those years reads like a map of the western Indian Ocean: Cambay, Diu, Surat and Diul-Sindhi to the north; Goa, Calicut, Cochin and Quilon to the south; Chittagong in Bengal; Ormuz, Maskat, Shehr, Julfar and Kalat in the Gulf and Arabia; Socotra, Aden, Mocha and Jidda in the Red Sea; Zaila, Quiloa, Brava, Mombasa, Melinda, Mogadishu and Sofala on the African coast; Colombo; Malacca and Achin. [n36; Gaz XIII-II p. 465] The goods are listed too. Out went rice, pulse, vegetables, coconuts and betelnuts; the basalt columns “as fine and hard as granite” that Goa’s builders wanted; “great quantities of the finest teak” to Goa, Gujarat, Sindh, and sometimes to the Persian Gulf and the Red Sea; local cotton cloth and muslins to Malabar, Diu, Persia, Arabia and Africa; local blankets, silks and sandals. In came dates and raisins, Spanish wine, silks, gold from Sofala, ivory from Abyssinia, spices from Malabar, Sumatra, Ceylon, Borneo and the Moluccas, horses from the Persian Gulf and the Arab coast, elephants from Ceylon, and enslaved people from Abyssinia. Pilgrims to Mecca took ship along the same routes. [n36; Gaz XIII-II pp. 465-468] The Gazetteer’s list is for the whole Thana coast under the Portuguese, not for Baçaim’s quay alone, and it gives no quantities. But it shows the web Baçaim belonged to.

The traders changed with the century. The Gazetteer notes “the disappearance of the Chinese, and the decrease of Arabs and Turks”, and the arrival of new Europeans: Portuguese first, then English, Dutch, French and Danes. Hindu merchants of the coast, it adds, were met in the Persian Gulf, at Mocha, in the African ports, at Malacca and at Achin. [n36; Gaz XIII-II p. 468] The Portuguese had a word of their own for the high-caste Hindus of Bassein who handled so much of this business: “Parabus”, that is Prabhus. [n36; DC p. 144 n.] The Gujarati Banias that Fryer saw leaving the city at dusk were part of the same world. The notes name no individual Hindu, Muslim or Parsi merchant of Baçaim in these decades; the records that survive name the Portuguese who taxed them.

Horses are the thread that runs through every century of this coast’s trade, and they ran through this one too. The treaty of 1534 had pinned the Arabian horse trade to Bassein for three years after the fortress was finished, so that the Gujarat sultan and his vassals could buy horses there, paying duty to the King of Portugal, rather than see them taken to the Deccan, Kanara or Malabar. [n36; Danvers I, p. 416] By the seventeenth century the treaty clause was long past, but horses from the Gulf and Arabia still appear among the coast’s imports. [n36; Gaz XIII-II pp. 465-468, Inferences]

Shipbuilding was the city’s real distinction. “Between 1550 and 1600 great ships built at Agashi and Bassein made many voyages to Europe,” the Gazetteer says, citing Bruce’s Annals; and Pyrard de Laval, in 1607, wrote that “From Bassein is exported the best timber for the building of houses and ships, the greater part of which latter are built here”, and that no place had better timber. [n36; Gaz XIII-II pp. 468-469; DC pp. 143-144] Agashi, ten miles north, was the older yard. In 1630, though poor in buildings, it “had a rich timber trade, and built ships as good as Portuguese ships, able to make the voyage to Europe”. [n36; Gaz XIV pp. 1-2] Whether Agashi’s ships truly reached Lisbon the Gazetteer states from the old chroniclers De Couto and Barros, and nobody has checked it independently. [n36, Gaps] The local craft were described by the chronicler Gaspar Correa: planks either sewn together with coir thread, for keeled boats, or nailed, for flat-bottomed ones; pitched cloth and cane mats against the sea; cargo holds roofed with woven palm leaf; one large mast with a long lateen sail, steered close to the wind. [n36; Gaz XIII-II pp. 468-469]

The timber trade had a darker side, and it reached into the captain’s palace. A royal letter of 13 March 1587 complained that the captains of Bassein and Daman supplied timber for the king’s own fleet “from captains themselves, who charged far higher prices than their value in the market”, because they traded on their own account. The Gazetteer sums it up: “The Bassein timber trade was chiefly carried on by the captains of forts and other government officers.” [n36; DC p. 144; Gaz XIII-II p. 465] The three-year captaincy was a public office and a private business at once. The establishment list of about 1634 shows the officials who ran the shipping side: a sea bailiff on £2 10s, a customs storekeeper, the almoxarife dos armazéns, and a “master builder” on £3 16s. [n36; Gaz XIV p. 31]

The money of Bassein flowed outward, too. The Gazetteer records, from da Cunha, that the ladies of Bassein’s great families subscribed 200,000 xerafins, about £10,000, to build a nunnery in Goa. [n36; Gaz XIV p. 29 n.6; DC p. 246] That was charity on the scale of a principality, and it was raised in a city whose port, the Gazetteer insists, was never a great entrepôt. Its wealth came from land, rents and office more than from trade. [n36, Inferences]

Old Sopara, six miles north, still had a small part in all this. Under the Portuguese it was a fortified post with four wooden stockades. [n37; Gaz XIV p. 322] A Kutchi sea manual of the seventeenth century, cited by a 2020 survey of the site, “talks of a regular cargo trade route through the Bolinj–Sopara creek”, so small cargo boats were still using the backwater. [n36; Shah et al. 2020] And the antiquary Bhagvanlal Indraji found at Sopara about ten square white-metal coins of the Mughal emperor Shah Jahan (1625–1658), which he thought had been struck to replace Portuguese white-metal coins. [n36; Indraji 1882 p. 279] Money from the Mughal mint was passing hands in the old port while the Portuguese ruled the coast. But Sopara’s real fate in these centuries was quieter. “During the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries,” the Gazetteer writes, “the lake banks were stripped and the temples pulled down, and the stones used in building Portuguese churches, forts, and houses.” [n36; Gaz XIV p. 323] Some of the stone in Baçaim’s walls had once been Sopara.

The religious life of the city went on expanding outward. Inside the walls stood the Matriz; the Franciscans’ Santo António, the oldest and one of the largest churches; the Jesuit church and College of the Holy Name; the Dominicans’ São Gonçalo; the Augustinians’ Nossa Senhora da Anunciada; the Misericórdia church and hospital; and the old Nossa Senhora da Vida. [n32; BG 38-43; n09] Outside them, the parishes of the countryside were still being founded. Two of the nine Portuguese-era village churches still in use today date from these first years of the century. Manickpur, whose Portuguese parish name was “Porim”, and Mercês, Our Lady of Mercy, are both dated to 1606. They were the last of a series that ran from Nirmal and Remedi (1557), through Sandor (1566), Agashi (1568), Nandakhal (1573), Papdi (1574) and Pali (1595). [n09; Vasaiker; Goanet 2020; n04] A Jesuit account says the parishes of Remedy, Papdy, Palle, Merces and Porim were carved out of Sandor between 1574 and 1606. [n04; n11; Wikipedia: Sandor]

The founding of Porim, the later Manickpur, is told in the parish’s own history, published by the East Indian community, and it shows how a Bassein parish was made. Until 1606 the Catholics of Porim belonged to St Thomas at Sandor. It was “at the insistence of the Jesuit Provincial” that the Archbishop of Goa, the same Dom Aleixo de Menezes whose name is on the Matriz lintel, made Porim a parish of its own. Its first church was a simple building of mud and thatch. When that proved too small, the Jesuits of the fort built a new one “on the spacious grounds in the centre of the five pacarias of Wananapor, Devamany, Porim, Navgaon and Ullmemany”. Its jurisdiction reached east to Gocavarem (Gokhivare), Calmona, Balliam, Sattolli, Achola and Rajaoli. [n40; EI Manikpur; CRIT Vol II asset 19a] A pacaria was a revenue unit, a cluster of fields and hamlets leased as one; the parish was laid over the tax map. The church was dedicated to St Michael. A heritage survey of 2008 says it was built “as part of their Inquisition Movement”, a phrase that tells more about modern memory than about 1606. [n40; CRIT Vol II asset 19a]

Mercês, between Sandor and Remedy and about three kilometres north-west of the fort, has a disputed founding. The parish history says Jesuit reports of 1669 and the later researcher Fr Conti point to 1606, and that the Augustinians served it until 1739. Da Cunha, however, says Nossa Senhora das Mercês was “built by Dom Fr. Aleixo de Menezes, the Archbishop who fought the interesting sect of the Syro-Chaldeans”, and puts it under the Franciscans. The two attributions cannot both be right, and the notes cannot settle them. Its people were “predominately fisher folk (kolis)”. The village had been called Kaular; it took its later name from the church. [n40; EI Merces; CRIT Vol III asset 92; DC p. 161]

Sandor itself, the “Mother Church of all the churches of South Bassein”, kept its place at the head of the southern parishes. Its own tradition, recorded on Wikipedia and the parish pages, names a Jesuit, Fr Pedro de Almeida, as Pai dos Cristãos (Father of the Christians) over Sandor and Papdi in 1574–76, and says the church was damaged by Arabs from Gujarat in 1679 and by “Kakaji” in 1690. Who Kakaji was, the sources do not say. The same tradition holds that the boy Gonsalo Garcia was present at Sandor’s foundation in 1566. The dates on Sandor’s pages contradict each other (1556, 1564, 1565, 1566, 1571, 1573), so all of this is parish tradition rather than record. [n40; Wikipedia: Sandor; EI Sandor; CRIT Vol III asset 103]

The countryside and its people

Who held the land

Outside the walls, Baçaim’s power rested on a land system that the Portuguese had built in the sixteenth century and ran without much change until 1739. The Crown granted villages, called aldeias, and smaller revenue units, pacarias and hortas, to Portuguese settlers, soldiers, widows and religious orders, on small quit-rents, usually “for three lives”: the grantee, an heir and the heir’s heir. This was the foro system. “No right in the land was conceded to the husbandmen or tenants,” the Gazetteer says. [n37; Gaz XIII-II p. 460; n39; Teixeira and Pires 2007] Men of rank among the converts could share in it. “Like the Portuguese settlers and pensioners they received grants of land in Salsette and elsewhere on small quit-rents. On the other hand, those of low birth were left in a state little removed from servitude.” [n37; Gaz XIII-I p. 202] By gradual grants, about half of Salsette became church property, and by 1695 church revenue was said to exceed the king’s. [n37; Gaz XIII-I p. 201; Gaz XIII-II p. 463]

Fryer, in 1675, put it in one image: the gentry lived “like petty monarchs”, holding the people “in a perfect state of villainage”. [n37; n34; Gaz XIII-I p. 202] That is a hostile English Protestant’s phrase, but the Gazetteer, compiled two centuries later from officials’ reports, accepted it.

The land system had religious consequences that ran in both directions. In the 1560s, the Jesuit Francisco Cabral wrote from Bassein that the Portuguese citizens, who “all have villages”, had at first opposed conversion, believing that if one villager became Christian the village would empty. Later, he wrote, the same landlords came “to beg us to go and convert their villages”. [n34; Documenta Indica V, pp. 628-629] The Census of 1931, explaining the Portuguese surnames of the Bassein coast, gave the long result: “The Portuguese parcelled out the countryside among certain of their countrymen … With the advent of the missionary a great deal of mass conversion took place, not unaided by competition among the owners of these estates, and in many villages every tenant in the village was baptized and given the surname of the owner of the village, e.g., Demonte, Pereira, Ferreira, etc.” [n34; Census 1931 Vol VIII Part I pp. 363-364] That sentence was written about the fishing coast, three centuries after the fact, but it describes a mechanism the land registers of this chapter make visible.

The peoples of the island

Who lived on these lands? By 1600 the countryside of the island of Bassein, the garden strip about three miles broad between the Bassein creek and the Vaitarna, cut off from the mainland by the backwater the Portuguese called the island’s moat, was largely Christian. [n39; Gaz XIV pp. 314-315] The Gazetteer of 1882, describing what remained of “the Portuguese rule along the coast, from 1530 to 1740”, named its makers: “the important class of Christians, chiefly converted Brahmans, Prabhus, Pachkalshis and Kolis”. Its fuller list adds Chavkalshis, Sonars, Khatris, Bhandaris, Kharpatils, Kunbis, Kumbhars, Nhavis, Dhobis, Bhois, Mhars and Chambhars, and says “the bulk of them are Bhandaris, Kolis and Kunbis”. [n34; n41; Gaz XIII-I pp. 63, 200]

Two of those groups are the ancestors of the communities this book follows most closely.

The first are the Samvedi Brahmans, a farming Brahman caste “found only in Bassein and Mahim”, husbandmen and gardeners rather than priests. [n34; Gaz XIII-I pp. 81-83] Around Sopara and Agashi, in the northern garden belt the Portuguese administered as the caçabé (market-town district) of Baçaim and the subdivision of Agaçaim, many of them became Christians. The Gazetteer would later write that “the husbandmen of Sopara are Christians, many of them converted Samvedi Brahmans”, and that at Agashi, Vatar and Koprad about seventy-five families of “Native Christians originally Samvedis and Pachkalshis” lived and worked beside about eighty-five Hindu Samvedi families. [n34; Gaz XIV p. 315; Gaz XIII-I p. 395] These Christian Samvedis are the people later known as Kuparis, though the name itself appears in no source before the twenty-first century. [n34, Headline finding] No baptism register or land record names a Samvedi convert, and no source dates the conversion of any individual Kupari village. [n34, Gaps] What the record does show is that the Samvedi community was split by conversion, not wholly converted: Hindu and Christian Samvedis lived in the same villages and kept the same ornaments, the same red broadcloth caps, the same guardian spirit Sathi at a birth. [n34; Gaz XIII-I pp. 82, 205, 210-211]

The second are the Pachkalshis, the gardening and farming caste of the coast, whose Bassein branch was known as Vadvals. Their own tradition, recorded by the ethnographer R. E. Enthoven in 1922, remembers the Portuguese years as a time of flight and force: “Under the Portuguese, who succeeded the Muhammadans, there was much persecution, and this is said to have forced many members of the caste to fly to the outlying parts of the Southern Konkan and join those who were already settled in the Ashtagar. Many were then converted to Roman Catholicism by force and their descendants may still be traced at Bassein and Salsette.” [n41; Enthoven III, p. 161] The Bombay City Gazetteer of 1909 adds that others “were forcibly converted to Christianity and became the ancestors of some native Christian families now resident in Bombay, Bandora and Marole”. [n41; Bombay City Gaz. I, p. 238] Both are community or colonial-era narratives; no Portuguese document that the notes reached names the Vadvals at all. [n41, Inferences] The Christian Vadvals settled in the southern parishes around the fort: the Gazetteer of 1882 names “Christian cartmen chiefly of Chulna, Manikpur, Gokhivra, Sandor, and Barhanpur in Bassein” as “Vadvals or gardeners”, the best axemen in the district, with the biggest carts and the strongest cattle. [n41; n40; Gaz XIII-I pp. 97-98] The Hindu Vadvals, with surnames such as Raut, Vartak and Chodhri, held on further north and inland. [n40; Gaz XIII-I p. 130] So the same coastal stock was divided by religion, and the line between them ran roughly between the Jesuit parishes of the south and the Hindu villages beyond the creek.

The Christian Samvedis and Vadvals were not always separate in the record, either. At Agashi the Gazetteer’s Christians were “originally Samvedis and Pachkalshis” together. [n34; Gaz XIII-I p. 395]

Others lived on the island too. Koli fishermen worked the creeks; at Mercês they were most of the parish. [n40; EI Merces] Bhandaris tapped toddy palms. In the old port, Muslim families descended from Arab and Persian settlers, the Naitas, had been rich traders when the Portuguese came, and the physician Garcia da Orta, in the sixteenth century, had written of “Naitas of Bassein” who married local women. [n37; Gaz XIII-I pp. 216-217] Enslaved Africans served in Portuguese households; the Gazetteer notes, from Hamilton, that house slaves from Africa were “made Christians and sometimes raised to be priests”, and that “African blood” was visible among Salsette Christians. [n37; Gaz XIII-II p. 460 n.3; Gaz XIII-I p. 200 n.3]

Work, food and the year

What these people grew and made can be read from what the Portuguese taxed. The first half-year’s revenue the Portuguese took from the Bassein district, in 1535, came from duties on coconut oil, opium, cotton, toddy, vegetables, fish, sugarcane and betel, and from taxes on the trades of butchers, dyers, Koli fishermen and shepherds. [n36; DC pp. 158-159] A century later, Fryer saw the land around the city “plain, and fruitful of Sugar-Canes, Rice, and other Grain”. [n15; Fryer/Crooke I, pp. 190-193] The Tombo of the 1720s leased the toddy rents and the wild palms of Agashi. [n39; Tombo fl. 26, 44v] So the countryside of the Court of the North was a land of rice fields in the monsoon, sugarcane, coconut and betel gardens, palm groves tapped for toddy, and creeks and shore worked by Koli fishermen, with timber coming down from the forests to the yards at Agashi and Bassein.

The year turned on the monsoon. Small craft were hauled ashore before the rains and launched again afterwards; the 1618 cyclone struck in mid-May, at the very edge of that season. [n36; Gaz XIII-I p. 342; Gaz XIV pp. 1, 31] Christian villages kept the Church’s calendar, with first fruits of the rice blessed at the altar, while the Hindu villages beyond the creek kept theirs, quietly, as the law required. [n34; Gaz XIII-I pp. 207-210; Mendiratta p. 211] Two centuries later the Gazetteer would find the Christian farmers of Bassein “the most hardworking and skilful husbandmen” of the district, turning “the light sandy country about Bassein into an evergreen garden”. [n41; n36; Gaz XIII-I p. 301] That garden was being planted in this century, by the converts and their Hindu neighbours, on land the fidalgos held.

Languages of the Court and the fields

The Court of the North governed in Portuguese and prayed in Latin, but it borrowed the vocabulary of the Persianate states before it. Its revenue units were caçabés (from the Persian kasaba) and praganas (the Mughal pargana); its custom-house was the mandovim. [n32; DC 157-158] In the villages, people spoke Marathi. Two centuries later the Gazetteer could still say of the Christians that “their home tongue is Marathi, very little different from the Marathi spoken by the Kunbis and Kolis of the district”, that a few spoke and many understood “an ungrammatical Portuguese”, and that “Latin is the language of the Church”. [n34; Gaz XIII-I p. 204] The Portuguese words that entered the Christians’ speech were church and family words: padrinho and madrinha for godparents, and in Grierson’s 1905 survey pay (father), may (mother), padron and madron. [n34; Gaz XIII-I p. 210; LSI VII] They came through the parish, not through trade or settlement. [n34, Inferences] The Hindu Samvedis, meanwhile, spoke what Grierson called “a mechanical mixture of Konkan Marathi and Gujarati”, a sign of the Gujarati strand in the coast’s past. [n34; LSI VII]

The Church took Marathi seriously in this century. The Jesuit Thomas Stephens’s Kristapurana, a life of Christ in Marathi verse of 10,962 stanzas, is dated 1616, and one source says Stephens spent about a year at Bassein and learned Marathi there. [n38; Wikipedia: Krista Purana; Sahapedia] The Gazetteer of 1882 still found the “Christa Purana” “well known and much read by the people” in the home dialect of the Thana sea Kolis, though it dates the book to 1659. [n34; Gaz XIII-I p. 207 n.1]

The same year, 1606, was cut into the portal of the citadel, the inner castle near the south-west corner. It was an elaborate thing, with Corinthian capitals, a Maltese cross, a coat of arms, a sphere, and a niche carved “I.H.S.”, the monogram of Jesus. Beyond it lay the palaces of the General of the North and of the Captain, the factory, and a very large ambar, a rice granary. By 1876 the portal’s fluted shafts had been carried off to decorate a village church. [n32; DC 216-218, 220; BG 39-40] In the Franciscan church a tombstone records Dona Francisca de Miranda, who died on 10 November 1606. [n32; BG 41-42; DC 237+]

The record of the city’s captains has a long gap here. Da Cunha’s list, drawn from the archives and printed in a footnote, runs clearly from 1535 to 1554. Then, he admits, “follows a long gap, which cannot be easily filled up”. The next name is Nuno Vaz de Castelo Branco in 1611. [n32; DC 219 n.] One more domestic detail survives from these years. In the nobles’ quarter, the Gazetteer describes an “ornamental bath-room of hard cement studded with shells and porcelain”, with a confused inscription dated 1617. [n32; BG 43]

1618: “A general and diabolical storm”

The year 1618 began with plague. Then, on 15 May, a cyclone came in off the Arabian Sea, and famine followed it. [n04; Wikipedia: Vasai Fort]

Manuel de Faria y Sousa, the Portuguese chronicler, called it “a general and diabolical storm”. In the Gazetteer’s translation, it “began at Baçaim on the 15th of that month… the sea was brought into the city by the wind… two thousand persons were killed; the fish died in the ponds… At Bombaim sixty sail of vessels… foundered.” [n15; Thana Gaz. XIV, p. 31 n.]

The fifteenth of May is the very end of the dry season on this coast, the weeks when the monsoon gathers. The notes do not say why the storm came then, and it is not this book’s place to guess. What the sources do say is specific enough. The wind pushed the sea over the walls into the city. Two thousand people died. Even the fish in the ponds died. And sixty vessels went down at the dependency of Bombaim, the island that would one day swallow this whole coast.

The great buildings suffered too. A local history site, drawing on the older accounts, says the cyclone unroofed the three largest churches. It damaged the Franciscan and Augustinian churches and convents, and badly damaged the Jesuit church and house. [n09; Vasaiker] At Agashi, ten miles to the north, the Gazetteer preserves a single, terrible image: a boat blown from the sea into a house, killing a woman and her child. [n11; Gaz. 1882 pp. 1-2]

What the friars saw, 1618

The Franciscan Fr Paulo da Trindade, writing in the 1630s from the reports of friars who were there, gives the fullest account of that night. [n109 §5–§7] The sky had been clouded and windy since Tuesday 15 May, and on the Thursday night, from eight or nine o’clock, the wind grew so strong that people left their upper floors; this “first fury” lasted two hours, and after a quarter-hour’s lull it returned “with doubled fury”, with “great tremors of the earth and movement of houses”. The wind “raised the sea water and drove it through the city, with much sand from the beaches; the sea grew so much that, being neap tide and at low water, it entered by the gates of the city”, and it turned from south through east to north-east, until four in the morning. Old merchants who had known the typhoons of China said they were “a shadow” beside it. It was night, and Paulo thought it providence that it was not day, for “all the churches fell, except that of our Friars of St Francis” and the people would have sheltered in them. “There was not a house in the city that did not at least lose its roof.” The mother church lost its roof and the choir with the organs; the Augustinians’ convent fell wholly, leaving the church roofless, and the friars went about in sacristy albs; the Dominicans’ new convent came down, and the friars lowered themselves by ropes and spent the night in the mud of a marsh opposite until the people saw them at dawn; the Jesuits lost the high chapel roof and half the church; Our Lady of Life, a parish church, lost its roof; the Misericórdia only its tower roof; the Franciscans lost only tiles, the tower roof and the novitiate. Every rectory in the cassabé and in Salsette was damaged, and the Recollects’ monastery at Thana almost wholly fell. The gardens and palm groves were destroyed, “the cattle almost all dead”, trees torn up “with roots turned upward”, many poor people killed by trees or houses. At sea, “a very powerful and rich ship that had arrived four days before from Hormuz” sank in the river of Baçaim with its goods; a galliot loaded with ivory lost it; boats hauled up half a league from the sea at Corombandel broke; at Bombay sixty-two vessels were lost. A noble Portuguese with his wife and children, in a ship in the river, called to the Franciscans on the shore, who threw him a bamboo and would have swum to hear their confessions but “the waves did not allow even that”; they absolved the family from the beach, “and embracing one another they all went to the bottom with the ship”. [n109 §5]

Paulo records the portents the survivors told: giants and balls of fire on the “Griz hill” north of the city, voices and “a great trampling of horses” in the air, a boy carried across the river to Bainel unhurt and another carried two leagues through the bush; at Agashi an almadia driven inland into a house, killing a woman and her child; at the Dominican church the ruin falling on the high altar and holding the tabernacle dry with its lamp still burning; and two images of Our Lady found with their curtains knotted over their faces. Paulo notes that “all the signs of mercy, as temples, images and crosses, fell… while the instruments of justice stood: the gallows, of very old wood, on the unsheltered beach, and the stone pillory, which was left leaning on a very thin stick.” Eight days later, a second storm of lightning struck Thana, destroying the churches of St Paul and St Augustine. [n109 §6]

The same afternoon the city asked the Franciscans for a procession of penance, which the friars, the Dominicans and the Misericórdia held from three o’clock, barefoot, through every church. But, Paulo says, nobody confessed: “forgetting to clean their souls, they set about mending the houses.” So on the fifth day the guardian persuaded a lay brother, Pedro da Madre de Deus, to preach through the streets. Pedro, ill with fever after being bled ten or eleven times, went out at five in the afternoon barefoot with a thick rope round his neck, calling “Brethren, be converted to God and do penance for your sins!”, and “all that people, great and small, men and women, white and black” followed him crying “Lord God, mercy!” to the Franciscan church, where confessions were heard from six to eleven at night in church, cloister and porter’s lodge, people scourged themselves, “some white women also did, publicly, on their backs”, and persons who had not confessed for twenty, thirty or forty years did so; the fervour lasted three or four days “as if days of a plenary jubilee”, and “there remained no one in the whole city who did not confess”. [n109 §7] Pedro died in 1624. One of the stories told of him is that when the captain of Baçaim, Gaspar Pereira, could not launch a galleon he had built, the lay brother tied his cord to it and “with great ease it was launched”. [n109 §2]

1619 – 1634: Repair, a saint and a census

Recovery was slow. When the traveller Pietro della Valle came to Baçaim, he supped with the Jesuits and was a night guest at their college. He noticed that many buildings were still in ruins from the hurricane. [n15; Thana Gaz. XIV, p. 32 n.; Viaggi III. 131] The sources disagree about when he came. Da Cunha gives 29 March 1623; the Gazetteer, in one place, says 1628. [n32; DC 228; BG, Gaps] Either way, the ruins were still standing five or ten years after the storm.

Life and death went on among the families of the Court. A gravestone of 2 April 1620 marks Dom Francisco Telesforo de Menezes. [n25; TG-XIV] Gaspar Pereira held the captaincy from 1620 to 1623. [n32; DC 219 n.] In the Jesuit church, a tombstone records Dona Filipa da Fonseca, who died on 20 July 1628. [n32; BG 41; DC 228-230]

Far away, a son of the city was being remembered. Gonsalo Garcia had been born in the Baçaim area around 1556 or 1557, to a Portuguese father and a local mother, and schooled at this same Jesuit college. He was crucified at Nagasaki on 5 February 1597, one of the twenty-six martyrs of Japan. In 1627 the Pope beatified him. [n04; Catholic Encyclopedia; n25; Wikipedia: Gonsalo Garcia] (His canonisation would not come until 1862.) The notes do not record how the news reached Baçaim, or what the city made of it.

The details of that honour are disputed in a small way. Da Cunha says Pope Urban VIII’s bull of 14 September 1627 declared the martyrs blessed; the Catholic Encyclopedia, usually careful, says they were “declared venerable” in 1627, with veneration permitted universally in 1629. [n38; DC pp. 211-212; Catholic Encyclopedia, Gonsalo Garcia] His birthplace has been refined too. A record in the Lisbon national archive, as reported by modern Catholic writers, calls him “natural de Agaçaim”, a native of Agashi, the son of a Portuguese soldier and a Canarim mother, a local woman. [n38; Indian Catholic Matters 2023; DNA 2014] If that is right, the first Indian-born saint came from the shipbuilding village at the north end of the island, the same village where the Franciscans had opened their first orphanage in 1535.

In 1631 the city chose a new patron. Over the five-step portico of a large building with four pillars (perhaps the Captain’s house, perhaps the Court of Justice, perhaps, as the Gazetteer thought, the Augustinian church), an inscription records: “Governing the State of India the Viceroy Dom Miguel de Noronha, Count of Linhares, this portal was built, on which St Francis Xavier was placed as patron of this city. 10 May 1631.” A second inscription names the captain, Gaspar de Mello de Miranda, and the aldermen Gonçalo Coelho da Silva, Pero Ferreira and João Boto Machado. The day of the month is uncertain because the OCR of both books is rough, and one note in this book’s research files gives the portal’s date as 1681. The two primary transcriptions agree on 1631, which also fits Mello de Miranda’s captaincy of 1630. Da Cunha adds that the earlier patron of the city had been St Sebastian, the saint of the fort itself. [n32; BG 40; DC 219-220 and n.; n25]

The city counted: 1634

In 1634 the chronicler Bocarro described Baçaim. His account, filtered through the Gazetteer and through modern summaries, is the closest thing to a census the walled city ever had.

Inside the fortress, he counted about four hundred Portuguese families, two hundred Indian Christian families and eighteen hundred slaves. [n04; Wikipedia: Vasai Fort] The figures are worth sitting with. In the “Court of the North”, the enslaved people outnumbered the free households several times over, and the notes say nothing about who they were or where they came from. This book’s research notes put that number next to the orphan baptisms and the “children sold by their parents” of the previous century, and the honest reading is that charity and coercion lived side by side in this city. [n04, Inferences]

The religious establishment was large. Bassein had sixty-three friars: thirty Franciscans, fifteen Jesuits, ten Dominicans and eight Augustinians. [n04; Machado 1999, via Wikipedia: Bombay East Indians; n32; BG 42] The Matriz alone kept a vicar, four canons, two choir boys, a treasurer, four singers and one instrumentalist. [n32; BG 39 n.2] The whole civil establishment of the city cost 3,420 pardaos. [n32; DC 218-219]

The sixty-three religious were, Hull says, “all maintained by the State”. [n38; Hull I, p. 4] The money had an old source. From 1550 the Crown had paid the Franciscans of Bassein 2,070 pardaos a year, “derived from a source which before the conquest was solely applicable to the lighting of the mosques in Bassein”; when the Jesuits arrived it was halved and shared. In 1634, the year of Bocarro’s count, the Jesuits’ share was raised to 3,000 pardaos, entered in the royal accounts under a heading that says everything about its purpose: Conversão da Fé, the Conversion of the Faith. [n37; n34; DC p. 235]

Baçaim was also the Church’s capital for the whole north. The vast area from Daman to Chaul, Bombay included, belonged to the Archdiocese of Goa, and its deputy for the region, the Vicar-General of the North, had his headquarters at Bassein. “Down to the Mahratta invasion of 1739 his headquarters was at Bassein,” Hull writes; afterwards it moved to Thana or Kurla, and later to Gloria Church in Bombay. [n38; Hull I, p. 6] Bassein trained clergy as well as housing them. Around 1570 its rural churches had been handed to native secular priests, and “there seems also to have been a seminary for secular clergy at Bassein at an early date”. The Jesuit visitor Pimenta, in 1598–1602, founded at Bassein a seminary called the College of the Purification, which the Gazetteer describes as “a seminary for noble children, natives of those parts, who were brought up as missionaries”. [n38; Hull I, pp. 21-22; n34; Gaz XIII-I pp. 201-202] That decision, to train Indian priests in Bassein, would decide what happened to the Christian villages after 1739.

Bocarro noticed one small, telling ruin. The oldest dated stone in the fort, the one reading “The first Captain who built this fortress was Garcia de Sá, by command of the Governor Nuno da Cunha, in the year 1536”, was set in a small round bastion by the pillory yard. That bastion was already unmanned in 1634, and Bocarro remembered it as the former residence of the Moors, the Gujarat garrison of a century before. [n32; DC 217; BG 39]

The same year brought a reminder that the city’s power stopped at its edges. At Agashi, Mughal raiders from Gujarat destroyed a “handsome Dominican monastery”. [n11; Gaz. 1882 pp. 1-2] Da Cunha adds that in 1634 “there was a rich monastery of the Dominicans there”. [n40; DC p. 160]

Agashi’s customs, 1636–1643

For one northern port the accounts survive year by year, and they read like a fever chart. The Gazetteer, citing a Portuguese collection of unpublished documents, gives the yield of Agashi’s customs and town in fedeas, a small money of account worth twelve réis: 281,249 in 1636 (about £234, the Gazetteer reckons), 313,874 in 1637, then 162,822 in 1638, 176,876 in 1640, 199,400 in 1641 and 216,400 in 1642, and then a collapse to 38,800 in 1643, about £32. [n36; Gaz XIV p. 1 n.4]

The research notes read the series as the boom of the mid-1630s and the slump that followed the raid of 1634, when Mughal forces from Gujarat had come as far as the Dominican house. [n36; Gaz XIV p. 1 n.4, Inferences] Agashi’s customs house was old: when the Portuguese took the district in 1535, Agashi’s fortress already “had a custom-house”, with a captain on 30,000 réis a year and a customs clerk on 20,000. [n36; DC pp. 158-159] What the Agashi figures do not say is why 1643 fell so far. The sources give no cause for that one year, and any conversion of fedeas into modern money is unsafe. [n36, Inferences]

Agashi in these years was a port of timber and ships, with few fine buildings. Its bay, the British hydrographer Horsburgh later noted, was closed by a reef except for one channel, “full of shoals and navigable only by boats and small vessels”. [n36; Gaz XIV p. 1] The timber came down from the forests inland; the ships went out through that channel.

1635 – 1660: Stone, ships and the visitors’ gaze

The captains’ list resumes in the 1630s: Rui Dias da Cunha in 1635, and André Salema in about 1636. Between 1635 and 1639 the Viceroy Pero da Silva, Captain Rui Dias da Cunha and others built a jail outside the fort near the later travellers’ bungalow, finished under Salema. Its slab survived into the 1880s. [n32; BG 39 n.7; DC 219 n.]

The Jesuits repaired their college, and put the date 1636 over its door. The Gazetteer judged its church front the handsomest in the city: a noble arch, fluted columns, Corinthian capitals and the IHS monogram. [n32; BG 41; DC 228-230]

The same year, 1636, the shipyards of Baçaim did something whose meaning no one could have seen at the time. Two pinnaces were built at Bassein for the English East India Company’s factory at Surat. With two more from Daman, they formed the nucleus of what became the Bombay Marine, the Company’s navy. [n32; BG 30-31; DC 221 n.] A Portuguese city built the first ships of the force that would one day make the English masters of this sea.

European visitors came and went in these decades, and the Gazetteer gathers their impressions into one sentence. Mandelslo, Tavernier (1651), Thevenot (1666) and Ogilby (1670) all found “a handsome well fortified town with a convenient harbour”. [n15; Thana Gaz. XIV, p. 32]

Jean-Baptiste Tavernier saw something more interesting than the harbour. He “notices that the Indians worshipped the Virgin Mary as a representation of Sita, pulling off their shoes… If the Portuguese had allowed them they would have anointed the image and offered it fruit.” [n15; Thana Gaz. XIV, p. 32 n.] In a few lines, Tavernier records the meeting of two religious worlds at the church door. Hindu worshippers came to the Christian image, and they came on their own terms: shoes off, as at a temple, and ready to make the offerings they would make to a goddess. The Portuguese, he implies, would not allow those offerings. The habit of worship that crossed communities would last, as the next chapter shows.

Faith under pressure: the villages, 1600–1700

Why the conversions happened, and why they slowed

By 1600 the great wave of conversion was nearly over. The historian Sidh Mendiratta, writing in 2017 from Portuguese sources, puts it this way: between about 1570 and 1620 the Jesuits and Franciscans converted “a majority of the population” of Salsette and of the Bassein and Agashi subdivisions. “Whole villages were converted, normally following the conversion of the respective ‘matará’ or headman.” “Christian converts who were farmers or fishermen were meted out particular benefits, while Hindu and Muslim communities that did not convert had to revert to low-key and mostly private religious practices.” Missionary activity, he adds, “slowed down considerably from the early 17th century”. [n34; Mendiratta 2017, p. 211]

Why did people convert? The sources give several answers at once, and they come from different sides.

The Portuguese state offered incentives. Converts, “especially high caste converts, were treated with honour and distinction, and, for the first fifteen years after conversion, the poorer class of Christians were freed from the payment of tithes and first fruits”. [n37; Gaz XIII-II p. 462] Men of rank could marry into Portuguese society and receive land on quit-rent. [n37; Gaz XIII-I p. 202] The Jesuits’ own letters, from the 1550s and 1560s, are frank about the method. Gonçalo Rodrigues wrote from Bassein in 1558 that “those who come to conversion are commonly the poorest and most needy”, moved “more by hope of some interest and remedy of life than by preaching, which they do not understand”, and that the fathers “set them the snares of human things”. [n34; Documenta Indica IV, pp. 100-101] For higher-caste converts the method was pageantry: a procession with the governor and nobility, then “a sumptuous banquet”. Da Cunha, a Goan Catholic doctor sharply critical of the Jesuits, wrote in 1876: “Their bait was alluring and irresistible, and the native convert was led by the nose.” [n37; DC p. 234]

The state also applied pressure. A series of royal and viceregal orders, renewed in 1555 and “enforced in 1581”, forbade Hindu feasts, Brahman preaching and the keeping of idols; anyone found with idols could be sent to the galleys and lose his property, half of which went to the Church. [n37; Gaz XIII-II pp. 462-463] Under Philip II, king of Spain and Portugal from 1580, all toleration of Hindu worship in Portuguese territory ended. [n34; Gaz XIII-I p. 201] Christians were barred from lending money to, partnering with or sheltering “Parabus”, from employing non-Christians as clerks, interpreters, brokers or money-changers, and from renting property to them. [n37; Priolkar p. 53; DC p. 142] The Jesuit visitor Valignano’s instructions to the Fathers of the Christians described the logic plainly: conversion “does not commonly occur as a result of sermons and doctrine” but by “obstructing the idolatrous practices”, “refusing them favours which can justly be refused, and offering them to those who are newly converted”. [n37; Priolkar p. 58]

But the pressure was uneven. Mendiratta, the most recent scholar in the notes, says: “As far as we know, the ‘Provisions in favor of Christianity’ were never enforced in the Northern Province’s subdivisions closest to the mainland.” [n34; Mendiratta p. 211] That caveat matters for the northern villages, Nirmal and Agashi among them, where the Kuparis’ ancestors lived. And the Gazetteer’s editors, who read the Portuguese chronicles, concluded: “Force may have been used in some cases, but the Portuguese accounts seem to show that persuasion and interest were the chief means of conversion.” [n37; n34; Gaz XIII-I p. 201 n.2]

The communities’ own memories tell a harsher story. The Gazetteer itself records that “according to the story of the Uraps and other classes their forefathers were forced to become Christians by having pieces of flesh thrown into their wells”, and a Protestant periodical of 1859 wrote of “the Portuguese thrusting biscuit and pork into the mouths of unwilling multitudes”. [n37; n34; Gaz XIII-I p. 201 n.2] These are traditions first written down in the nineteenth century, three hundred years after the events. No Jesuit or Franciscan letter that the notes reached records them; those letters record gifts, feasts and land instead. [n34, Inferences] The Pachkalshi tradition of flight and forced conversion, quoted above, belongs to the same family of memories.

Two modern scholars stand at opposite ends of the argument. The Goan archivist A. K. Priolkar, writing in 1961, held that conviction played “a very insignificant role” and that conversion rested on the “lure of material rewards and threat of violence and force”. The Jesuit historian Fr H. Heras argued that Jesuit work came “only a posteriori … after the Hindus themselves had decided to join the Church”. [n37; Priolkar pp. 49-56] Priolkar’s evidence is mostly from Goa, not Bassein. [n37, Inferences] The fairest summary the notes allow is the Gazetteer’s own phrase, “persuasion and interest”, with the clear exception of orphans, for whom compulsion is documented. [n37, Inferences]

What it cost the countryside

There was a price, and a Portuguese-era witness recorded it. Around 1603, or in 1630 by another reckoning, a writer named Goez noted that “the persecution of the Portuguese had made many Hindus, Mussulmans and Parsees abandon their homes and live in the dominion of Shah Jehan”, so that “between Bassein and Damaun there are few natives and the greater part of the village lands being uncultivated”. The Gazetteer adds: “if this account is correct the districts soon recovered their prosperity”. [n37; Gaz XIII-II pp. 463-464; DC p. 143] Flight across the frontier into Mughal territory was an option for people who would not convert, and it emptied fields.

The holy places of the Hindus were the other cost. Before 1600, the Franciscan António do Porto had “destroyed 200 temples” across Agashi, Bassein and Thana, filled in sacred pools and built chapels over others. [n37; Gaz XIII-II p. 461] At Nirmal, the old pilgrimage place north of Sopara, the Portuguese “had pulled down the temples and destroyed the ling”, and da Cunha says it was “the Inquisition” that pulled down the temple; a church of the Holy Cross stood there instead. [n40; Gaz XIV p. 293; DC pp. 126-127] The stones, the Gazetteer thought, went into churches. [n40; Gaz XIV p. 293]

The Inquisition at Bassein

The Inquisition arrived in Goa in 1560, and “by 1580 agents of the Inquisition, called commissaries, were at work in Chaul, Bassein and Daman, collecting offenders and sending them for trial and punishment to Goa”. [n37; Gaz XIII-II p. 463] At Bassein the commissary worked from the Jesuit college. Prisoners were held in a jail near the Captain’s palace until “the fleet from the north” carried them south to Goa. An inscribed slab of the Inquisition prison still stood near the travellers’ bungalow when da Cunha wrote. [n37; n38; DC pp. 235-236]

One prisoner passed through and wrote about it. The French physician Charles Dellon was held at Bassein on his way to the Inquisition in Goa; his Relation de l’Inquisition de Goa was published in 1688. The year he was at Bassein is given as 1673 by one reading, “1683” by the Gazetteer’s Part II and “1583” in da Cunha’s damaged text, so it remains uncertain, though 1673 fits the publication date best. [n37; n38; DC pp. 235-236; Gaz XIII-I p. 202] Seventeenth-century travellers, among them Pietro della Valle, Dellon and Gemelli Careri, described the Inquisition’s rigour toward “the Christians if they strayed from the path of orthodoxy, the Hindus if they practised their religious rites”. [n37; Gaz XIII-I p. 202] Pyrard de Laval claimed that “only the rich, the confiscation of whose property profited the Inquisition, suffered, while the poor escaped”. That is one traveller’s claim. [n37; DC p. 235]

The Inquisition touched Hindu devotion too. Near the church of Our Lady of Remedies, on the road from Bassein to Agashi, was a tank believed to heal. The Inquisition “had put a stop to all pilgrimage” there “under very severe penalties”. [n37; n38; DC p. 163] The pilgrimage outlived the ban; in the nineteenth century Hindus held a fair there again. [n37; DC p. 163]

How many people from Bassein were tried, the sources cannot say. The Goa Inquisition’s records were destroyed, and Priolkar complains that “it is not possible to obtain an idea of the numbers”. [n37; Priolkar p. 60] One case connected to Bassein survives only because a stone recorded it. In 1840 the Goan scholar Filipe Nery Xavier found a stone, set up in 1786, on the site of a house the Inquisition had razed. It recorded that the family who lived there, “dogmatists of the said sect”, had been “burnt in the Auto de Fé celebrated on December 30, 1747”, and that their houses had been “demolished and ploughed with salt”. [n37; Priolkar pp. 60-61] The notes do not identify the family, the village or the place of the stone, and the date falls after the Marathas had taken Baçaim, so how the case connects to the district is unclear. [n37, Inferences and Gaps] It is the only named instance in the notes, and it stands for many that left no stone.

The life of the parishes

Against that background, ordinary Christian life went on in the villages, and the faint traces of it in the notes deserve setting down. Passion plays had come early: a society of penitents was founded in 1551, and Fr Melchior Nunes Barreto brought it to Bassein in 1552. [n34; Gaz XIII-I pp. 207-210] Village brotherhoods attended funerals with candles. Choirs were famous; a Lisbon journal of 1843 remembered that “the Thana choristers were famous singers”, and that the hamlet of “Trinity” sang sacred songs at work. [n34; Gaz XIII-I p. 207 and n.2] First fruits of the rice harvest were blessed at the church. [n34; Gaz XIII-I pp. 207-210]

And the old gods were not wholly forgotten. In outlying villages of Salsette and Bassein, the Gazetteer heard, secret offerings to Ganpati and Gauri and vows to the smallpox goddess Shitladevi were “said occasionally” to continue, against the priests’ disapproval. [n34; Gaz XIII-I pp. 207-210; DC pp. 249-250] Tavernier’s Hindu worshippers at the image of the Virgin, shoes off, ready with fruit, were the same habit seen from the other side of the church door. The line between the two faiths ran through families and through the year, not only between villages.

The captains continued to rotate. Dom Álvaro de Almeida held office in 1650, and Manuel Côrte Real Sampaio in 1653. João de Siqueira de Faria held it from 1661 to 1664, the years in which the Court of the North lost its most famous possession. [n32; DC 219 n.]

Salsette in 1630

For the island south of Baçaim, Fr Paulo da Trindade’s Conquista Espiritual gives a parish census taken from the books of Confession and Baptism “in this present year, which is 1630”. [n110 §4] He says that Salsette “means Sixty-six, because that many were the villages it formerly had”; the Hindu king, at the people’s request, divided them into 114, with two captains, Tape Malar and Tape Marol, who were given Bainel and Bandra as their principal villages. [n110 §1] The Franciscans administered 55 villages in eleven rectories: Manapacer (the royal college, five villages, including “Canary”, Kanheri), Magatana, St Thomas of Pare (Pahadi), St Blaise of Ambolim, Our Lady of Egypt (Kole Kalyan), St Anthony of Trombay, St Sebastian of Maroli, Our Lady of the Purification at Bainel (Bhayander), Reis Magos (Uttan, Gorai and Manori), St Bonaventure of Arangal and St Jerome of Cassi. They counted 10,156 Christians “of confession” (old enough to confess), 2,650 children and 794 infants; Manapacer alone had 609 of confession and 119 children, and Bainel 855 and 224. The Jesuits (six churches, including Bandra), the Augustinians (one) and the secular clergy (three) served the other 59 villages with 9,213 of confession and 1,557 children: in all about 23,600 Christians in the island. [n110 §4]

The mother house of the Salsette Franciscans was the royal college of Manapacer (Mandapeshwar). Paulo says that Fr António do Porto found there a Hindu temple cut in the living rock whose yogi, “Raiemnat”, had fifty disciples whom the villages fed; the disciples “fled very quickly to the mainland, the chief fell into the nets of the Gospel and was baptised”, and the temple became the church of Our Lady of Piety and a college for a hundred orphan boys, taught to read, write, play, sing and Latin. The King gave twelve villages and three thousand patacões a year, paid at the factory of Baçaim, half of it to the Jesuits and half to the college. [n110 §3] The documents his editor quotes show that this began in 1548 with “a fair devout hermitage” where Christians came every Sunday, grew into a rock-cut church by 1549 (the Jesuit Baltasar Gago saw “a church of Our Lady, where there will be 400 Christians, all in rock… of great capacity and with four chapels”, reached by “a league and a half of river”), and by December 1551 had a procession of Christians with trumpets for the Jesuit Gaspar Barzeu; Governor Jorge Cabral gave the village of Mandapeshwar to the Franciscans for it; and in 1557 Fr António told the King that Governor Barreto had asked him to keep “some Chaldean bishops with a companion”. [n110 §2] By 1603 the college kept “ordinarily ninety-odd collegians, Christian orphans natives of the said island”, with a secular master to teach plainchant and organ, a Franciscan to teach Grammar, and four o’clock Matins in the choir; those who married were married from the college’s alms, and it maintained “a hundred and more” poor widows and orphan girls. [n110 §3]

Paulo also tells of a village miracle in this country: at St Bonaventure of Arangal, a Christian boy named Gaspar, son of a palm-tapper, fell from a tall palm and lay broken, and his kin, “all natives of the village”, asked the rector, Fr Pedro da Madre de Deus, “Father, you preach that in our needs we should not go to the devils and sorcerers, but ask St Bonaventure. Why does he not give life to this boy?” They carried him to the church crying S. Boaventura, gitá di (“Bonaventure, give him life”), sprinkled him with holy water, and he revived. [n110 §5]

And Paulo records that Fr António do Porto once went into the labyrinth under the Kanheri caves. [n110 §6] Having heard from his converts “that there was a labyrinth to which they could never find the end”, said to run “as far as the kingdom of Cambay”, he took a companion, twenty men with arquebuses and servants with water, provisions and oil for torches, tied the end of a thread to a great stone at the mouth, “about four fathoms wide”, and walked “seven continuous days” through passages “some wide and some narrower, all cut in the living rock”, with small cells and cisterns on either side. At the end of the seventh day the provisions were gone and they turned back by the thread, “not knowing on all this way whether they climbed or descended or to what directions they walked, for they carried no needle”. Paulo reckoned six leagues a day, or forty-two leagues, in an island five leagues long, and concluded that the Hindus were right that it ran to Cambay. The reckoning cannot be right, and Paulo copies much of this chapter from Couto; but he is the only writer who says anyone went in.

1661: An island in a dowry

For 127 years, from 1534 to 1661, the seven islands of Bombay had been a minor dependency of Baçaim. [n04, Inferences] They had been listed among the dependencies the sultan ceded, along with Salsette, Bombaim, Parel, Sion, Worli, Mazagaon, Thana, Bandra, Mahim and Uran. [n04; Wikipedia: Vasai Fort] Their church affairs had been run by the Vigário da Vara at Baçaim, and they were administered within its province. [n15; Wikipedia: History of Bombay under Portuguese rule]

In 1661 a marriage treaty between Charles II of England and the Portuguese princess Catherine of Braganza included Bombay in her dowry, along with trading privileges and two million crowns. Portugal kept Salsette, Baçaim and the mainland. [n15; Wikipedia: History of Bombay under Portuguese rule; EBSCO Research Starters] The sources disagree about when the islands actually changed hands. The Vasai Fort article gives both 1661 and 1665. [n04] The Gazetteer says the Jesuits of Bandra, who belonged to the Baçaim “North”, resisted the handover of 1665–67 by claiming much of the land in Bombay as their own. [n15; Thana Gaz. XIV, p. 26] The ceremony itself is not described in the sources this book could use. [n15, Gaps]

Seen from the walls of Baçaim, the transfer probably looked small: a marshy dependency to the south given to a royal bridegroom. Seen from 2026, it is the hinge of this whole story. The island the Court of the North gave away became the city whose suburbs now cover the Court’s own land.

The 1670s: Pirates, a doctor and the first Maratha shadow

Dom António de Souto Maior was captain in 1667, Manuel Teixeira Franco in 1670, Jerónimo Manuel de Albuquerque in 1671, and Henrique da Silva da Fonseca in 1672. [n32; DC 219 n.] The short terms suggest a city still running by its old rules. The events of 1674 suggest a world changing around it.

That year, according to the historian Orme as cited by the Gazetteer, six hundred Arab raiders from Muscat “landed at Bassein, and, unopposed by the panic-struck garrison, plundered all the churches outside of the walls”. [n15; Thana Gaz. XIV, p. 32] Modern editors flag the source for this raid as weak, so its details should be held loosely. [n04; Wikipedia: Vasai Fort]

The same year a more lasting pressure arrived from inland. More Pandit, a Maratha commander, forced the Portuguese to pay a quarter of Baçaim’s revenues, the levy the Marathas called chauth. [n04; Wikipedia: Vasai Fort; n25] This book’s research notes point out what that means. The cartaz on Gujarat’s shipping and the quarter of the land revenue were both claims on the same wealth. Revenue was the prize on both sides. [n04, Inferences]

But the English historian Orme, as quoted by da Cunha, gave the Maratha demand another reason as well. More Pandit, he wrote, demanded the chauth because the Portuguese “had lately inflicted great severities on many families of the Morattoe religion … because they refused to become Christians”. [n37; DC p. 143] The quarrel over money had become a quarrel over faith.

Shivaji and the orphans

That quarrel had a sharp edge, and it was children. Portuguese law, from a royal order of 1559 onward, required that Hindu orphans with no living father, mother or grandparent be handed to the Jesuits to be raised as Christians; the Inquisitors later ordered that orphans be reported to the Father of Orphans within six days on pain of excommunication. [n37; Priolkar pp. 76-78, 128] How strictly that law ran in Bassein’s countryside the notes do not show; Mendiratta says the seizure of Hindu or Muslim orphans was enforced in the Northern Province “only in or near towns”. [n34; Mendiratta p. 210 n.5] But in the 1670s it reached the ears of Shivaji.

An English East India Company letter of 26 November 1675, preserved in the Orme manuscripts at the India Office and quoted by Priolkar, reports: “Sevagee and they daily quarrell, the chiefest cause of his hatred to them being for forcing orphans of his [caste to] turn Roman Catholicks.” The same letter records an unexpected twist. The Captain General of Bassein took the side of some orphans against the Jesuits, and forced the Jesuits to restore the orphans’ estate. [n37; Priolkar p. 129; Orme MSS vol. 114 sect. 4 p. 164] The Portuguese state and the Portuguese Church did not always pull the same way, which is also what Hamilton would complain of forty years later.

Shivaji had his own answer to conversion. In the 1660s, the Gazetteer records, he “is said to have taken much care that the people should be purified by Brahmans and brought back to Hinduism”. “After Shivaji’s death the spread of Hinduism ceased.” [n37; Gaz XIII-I p. 202] That rite of purification, which made a convert fit to return to his caste, would come to Bassein’s own villages after 1739.

The city counted its churches that year: two colleges, four convents and six churches. [n04; n09; Wikipedia: Vasai Fort; Ancient Origins]

Foreign eyes, 1580s–1670s

A few non-Portuguese visitors left a line each. [n81] The Dutchman Jan Huygen van Linschoten, who lived at Goa in the 1580s, wrote that the Portuguese held three fortified harbours on the northern coast, Daman, “Basaiin” fifteen miles on and Chaul ten miles beyond, all with “good havens, where great traffique is done”, very fruitful of rice, peas, butter and coconut oil, with “a very wholesome and temperate air”, “the soundest and healthiest part of all India”. [n81 §1] In 1609 the English captain John Jourdain found at Mocha, on the Red Sea, “Banians of Gujarat, Dabul, Diu, Chaul, Bazim, Daman and Sind” among the merchants, and some thirty-five ships from those ports that year. [n81 §2] On 7 October 1630 an English cruise off Bassein met two small boats of famine refugees from Cambay bound for the Deccan, and “let them pass clear, seeing there was no Portugals in them”. [n81 §3] Orme, from the Company’s records of the 1670s, tells that when Sivaji’s troops at Kalyan wished to cross at Thana to reach Bombay, the Portuguese governor of Bassein “came with the best of his force to Tannah, and anchored forty armed boats in the stream”, and refused the passage twice. [n81 §4]

Fryer’s walk through the city, 1675

In 1675 Dr John Fryer came to Baçaim and wrote the fullest description of the living city that survives. It is worth quoting at length, in his own spelling:

“BACEIN. Is incircled with a round Stone-wall, and has a Gate for every Wind; it is strong enough against the Indians, but not able to endure an European Foe: There are upon the Out-walls, and in the Fort, Forty two great Guns; the Fort in the middle of the City is circular; towards the Market appears a State-house Piatzed, where the Governour convocates the Fidalgos every Morning upon consultation, in which they all stand, a Chair not being permitted the Governour, though Gouty: Towards Evening they meet there to Game.” [n15; Fryer/Crooke I, pp. 190-193]

Then he describes the people:

“Within the Walls are six Churches, four Convents, two Colleges, one of the Jesuits, another of the Franciscans… The Fidalgos (for few Artisans are tolerated within the Walls) have stately Dwellings, graced with covered Balconies, and large Windows two Stories high, with Panes of Oister shell… None but Christians lodge within the City, the Banyans repairing to the Suburbs upon Tattoo. The City is a Mile and half round; it stands on an Island… The Land about is plain, and fruitful of Sugar-Canes, Rice, and other Grain; a great part of which has lately been destroyed by the Arabs of Muschat…” [n15; Fryer/Crooke I, pp. 190-193]

The passage is full of things a reader can almost see. The great square with its arcaded state-house stood at the end of the street to the left of the sea gate. There the nobles met their governor each morning, all of them standing, even the gouty governor, and came back in the evening to gamble. [n32; BG 39; DC 218] The windows of the great houses were glazed with flattened oyster shell. The Hindu merchants, the “Banyans”, had to leave the city at the beat of the evening drum and sleep in the suburbs, because only Christians could lodge inside the walls. Around the island lay sugar cane and rice, and the damage the Muscat raiders had done the year before was still visible.

Fryer also saw the Jesuit college, with a library of “historians, moralists and expositors”, fine cloisters, and a copy of Michelangelo’s Resurrection. [n32; BG 41; DC 228-230] On his way into the city, at Ghodbandar (“Grebondel”), he passed the estate of “the Richest Don on this side Goa”. [n15; Fryer/Crooke I]

His military judgement is the line that matters most for this chapter: “strong enough against the Indians, but not able to endure an European Foe”. He was wrong about which foe would come. But he was right that the walls had limits.

In 1676 Shivaji advanced near Saiwan, inland from Baçaim. [n04; Wikipedia: Vasai Fort] The same year, in the Franciscan church, a tombstone was laid for Antonio Teles de Menezes, who died on 26 October 1676. [n32; BG 41-42] Captain André Ferreira dos Reis had held office from 1675; Fernando António Souto Maior followed in 1677–78. [n32; DC 219 n.] Out in the countryside, the parish tradition of Sandor records that its church was damaged in 1679, and again in 1690. The notes do not say by whom. [n11; Wikipedia: Sandor, Palghar]

1680 – 1700: Plague, poor friars and an emptying city

The Maratha side of these decades is thin in the notes, but the Marathi Vishwakosh gives one sentence. Shivaji, it says, had no time for the Portuguese in north Konkan, though he took a firm line at times against oppressive Christian propagation, and his successors Sambhaji and Rajaram could not turn to it either. Sambhaji, however, overran some Portuguese territory around Goa and forced a treaty on them in 1684. [n62] The sentence is a single entry’s summary; the notes have no treaty text.

A new order came to Baçaim around 1681: the Hospitallers of São João de Deus, a poor, recent order who also served in the Misericórdia hospital. They had a church and monastery, probably the chapel later used as a sugar warehouse. [n32; BG 41; DC 227-228]

In 1685, according to local trekking sites (low-quality sources), the Portuguese took the hill fort of Kamandurg, above the Ulhas river trade, from the Marathas on 12 September, and then abandoned it for want of water. [n12; durgbharari; trekshitiz] If the account is right, the frontier between the two powers already ran through the hills just east of the island.

The Gazetteer preserves the revenue figures of these last decades, converted into pounds sterling: £8,646 in 1686, £9,737 in 1709, £15,639 in 1718 and £45,706 in 1729. [n32; BG 34 n.8] The notes do not explain the steep rise in the 1720s, and the figures should be read only as the Gazetteer gives them.

Da Cunha gives the same series in Portuguese money, xerafins, with digits the scanners have partly damaged: 172,920 xerafins in 1680, 194,748 in 1709 and 310,779 in 1718–19. [n36; DC pp. 145-146] The two sets of figures do not match year for year, and the 1680 date itself is an uncertain reading. What both show is a revenue that grew, not shrank, in the decades before the fall. Whatever was wrong with Baçaim in the early eighteenth century, it was not a shortage of income.

The sea was less safe. The Gazetteer’s history notes that in these years “Maskat pirates and Pendharis harassed Bassein”, the Omani Arabs from the sea and the Pindari raiders by land. [n36; Gaz XIII-II p. 484] Since the Portuguese had lost Ormuz in 1622 and Maskat itself in 1650, the Omanis had become a naval power in their own right, and the raid of 1674 was not the last time their ships came down the coast. [n36; Gaz XIII-II p. 465]

Manuel Tavares da Gama was captain in 1693. [n32; DC 219 n.] An inscription in the fort, too damaged to read with confidence, seems to name a “Brito” as captain-major in 1688, a year that falls in one of the gaps in da Cunha’s list. [n39; DC about p. 84, OCR]

Chauth, the Mughals and Sambhaji, 1659–1692

Pissurlencar’s chapters on Shivaji and Sambhaji give the earlier Maratha pressure on Bassein territory, from the Goa archives. [n65 §4] In July 1659 the Portuguese captains at Chaul and Bassein supplied grain to the Siddi, and Shivaji complained. Shivaji’s own first twenty armed ships were built with the help of Portuguese artisans from Bassein, and when the Portuguese in his service later quit they fled to Bombay and Bassein. In 1677 the Portuguese were paying chauth on the Tarapur and Saivan parganas of Bassein province to a “Chauthiya Raja”, and Shivaji demanded the same; on 10 January 1678 the Viceroy told him it would be paid once the captains of Daman and Bassein reported. In August 1678 the General of Bassein learnt that Shivaji had gathered an army near Kalyan and Bhiwandi with Salsette in view. [n65 §4]

Sambhaji’s treaty of 1684, a copy of which went to the General of Bassein, João de Sequeira de Faria, on 4 February 1684, provided that the gavkhandi and chauthai paid to the Chauthiya Raja on Bassein territory should now go to Sambhaji, who would protect the territory. By mid-1684 a Portuguese report said the enemy had left Chaul but had “greatly harmed the Bassein area”, taking Kalyan, Saivan and Manor, recapturing Asheri and fighting for Karanja, Mahim and Tarapur. In 1692 a Mughal general, Matbar Khan, attacked Bassein territory from Bhiwandi, and Goa bought peace by sending an envoy to the Mughal court. [n65 §4] These are the years in which the Marathas learned to treat Bassein’s villages as a source of tribute, and the Portuguese learned to pay it.

Gemelli Careri, 1695

In February 1695 the traveller Giovanni Francesco Gemelli Careri came to Baçaim. On 11 February he heard mass in “the parish church of Our Lady da Sé”. On a Sunday he heard mass again at the Misericórdia, which he called “the parish of the city”. [n32; DC 216, 225-226, quoting Churchill’s Voyages iv. 192]

He took the measure of the churches the way a connoisseur would. The Franciscan church had, “contrary to the custom of India”, many chapels. The Dominicans had “three well-adorned altars”. Nossa Senhora da Vida, “one of the oldest churches in Bassein”, had three altars. And one house, the Hospitallers’, was “so poor that it can maintain but three friars”. [n15; Thana Gaz. XIV, pp. 27, 33, ~39-40; n32; BG 40-42]

He also recorded a plague “exactly like a bubo”, which “carried off whole cityfuls of people” in Surat, Daman and Thana. [n15; Thana Gaz. XIV] The Gazetteer’s history calls it the “carazzo” plague of about 1690, a bubo-like disease that emptied cities. [n36; Gaz XIII-II p. 484] Da Cunha later wondered whether this plague explained a strange feature of the ruined city he surveyed in the 1870s. Inside the walls, the seaward half was built up, but the landward third was empty. Three explanations were offered. Perhaps the space was left clear as a field of fire. Perhaps it was a rice field to feed a garrison under siege. Or perhaps the city had simply been depopulated by the late-seventeenth-century plague. [n32; DC 213; BG 38] No source settles the question.

1700 – 1730: Rich gentlemen and a silting creek

In the early 1700s the province counted 60,499 people, of whom 58,131 were native Christians and 2,368 Europeans. [n15; Thana Gaz. XIV, pp. 30, 33] The figure covers the district, not just the walled city, and it shows what two centuries of missions had made: a countryside where almost everyone counted by the census was Catholic.

Around 1720 the traveller Alexander Hamilton passed by and wrote two sentences that read like an obituary written in advance. Baçaim’s riches, he said, “lay buried in the hands of lazy country gentlemen, who loitered their days in ease, luxury and pride, without the least sense of their country’s ruin”. And: “The church superintends the General of the North, which makes his government both uneasy and precarious.” [n15; Thana Gaz. XIV, p. 33 & n.] Hamilton was an outsider, and his judgement may be harsh. But his two charges are clear: a gentry living on rents without guarding the frontier, and a governor whom the clergy could overrule.

The Gazetteer’s trade history preserves a third sentence of his, sharper still: “Bassein was a place of small trade, its riches dead and buried in the churches.” [n36; Gaz XIII-II p. 485] Read beside the revenue figures, which were rising, Hamilton’s judgement is not that Baçaim was poor. It is that its wealth was locked up in land, in rents and in religious foundations, and not spent on ships, soldiers or walls. Da Cunha’s figure for the province’s people points the same way: 60,499 in 1720, of whom 58,131 were native Christians. [n36; DC pp. 145-146] The Court of the North ruled a Christian countryside of farmers and fishers, and a small Portuguese class that lived from them.

The city still invested in its walls. On one of the massive teak doors of the sea gate, cased in iron bars and spikes and partly hidden by one of them, someone carved a date: DO 20 DE NOV DE 1720, “the 20th November 1720”. [n32; BG 38-39; DC 213-214] Dom António Vasco de Melo had been captain from 1712 to 1717; Francisco Pereira Pinto held the office in 1728. [n32; DC 219 n.]

A granary, a colony and 123 families of weavers

Bragança Pereira’s survey of the Province (1930s) collects scattered evidence about how the countryside lived in these years. [n77 §4] Fr Manuel Godinho, who was at Baçaim in 1662, counted three hundred Portuguese households in the city and four hundred of native Christians, besides gentiles and Moors, and five thousand men of arms from the city and its district; in 1634 Thana had eighty white and a hundred black married men “all very good men of arms” and “five thousand five hundred and some souls”. [n77 §1] The Jesuits ran a public rice granary at Baçaim, “which that people made for that city always to be provided with food, and the poor to have it there certain and cheaper”. Under the rector Fr Teotónio Rebelo it bought villages of the jurisdiction that yielded up to 800 murás of rice, to leave the Crown 15,000 xerafins a year; the account, in Francisco de Sousa’s Oriente Conquistado (1710), says it had been in the rectors’ hands since the time of the Count of Alvor. [n77 §4] The same chronicle describes the Trindade colony near Thana, where Fr Gonçalo Rodrigues bought uncultivated woodland from its lords, settled new converts on it, and saw it grow from 500 to 3,000 people with a hundred oxen and common ploughs; the three-headed idol was smashed and the place became “the Village of the Trinity”. [n77 §4] Thana’s silk and cotton looms were famous, and in 1720, after merchants of Goga and Broach proposed to settle at Diu, “123 families” left the silk and cotton factories of Surat and Ahmedabad and settled at Thana. [n77 §4] The granite for the churches and palaces of Goa came from Baçaim, wheat came down from the Muslim lands in herds of ten or twenty thousand cattle that went back loaded with salt, and the 1662 letter of an English judge at Goa says that “Bagain” yielded “great quantity of sugars” and Karanja “much salt”. [n77 §4]

The Tombo de Baçaim, 1727–1730

In these same years the Crown did something that, by chance, has left the best picture of the countryside on the eve of its loss. Between 1727 and 1730 its officials compiled the Tombo de Baçaim, a register of every title by which the Crown’s lands in the province were held. The register survives in Portugal, and in 2007 the historians André Teixeira and Silvana Pires published its table of holders, with folio numbers, in an open-access journal. [n39; Teixeira and Pires 2007, pp. 325-363] It lists 362 titles in all: 36 in the caçabé of Baçaim, 28 in the caçabé of Agaçaim (Agashi) and 4 in that of Sopará. [n40; Teixeira and Pires, pp. 338-339] Many of the names can be matched, by sound and context, to villages that still exist, though the matching is the researchers’ inference, not the register’s. [n40, Inferences]

The holders were Portuguese fidalgos, Portuguese widows and the Jesuits. They were not the converts who worked the land. [n39; Teixeira and Pires 2007]

Pissurlencar, in an article of 1942, puts the same picture in figures from the Goa archive. [n76 §2] In Simão Botelho’s Tombo da Índia of 1545–54, he says, the holders of Baçaim’s villages included only “seven Hindus”, “all the rest being Catholics”; in the Tombo de Baçaim of 1727–30 “the holders of the villages of Baçaim were almost all Portuguese, figuring in the names of Hindus only five or six villages”. Land grants of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries carried a clause “obliging the holders to send every year certificates of the Commissaries of the Holy Office … so that it be shown how they help the propagation of the faith and labour to extirpate paganism”, and a grant was not renewed if the holder showed “little zeal”, so that some holders “preferred to lose the village rather than have in it the least gentility”. Hindu hereditary holders had mostly lost their grants in the sixteenth century: the Anjur Naiks after the conversion of Bandra, where they lived, and Antaji Raghunath Kavle of Malad, whose grant was last renewed in 1725 and was incorporated into the Treasury in 1726. The Marathi sanad he quotes has three Naiks telling the Peshwa in 1722–23 that “the Portuguese had desecrated temples and sacred places” and that they themselves had “left the province” when their watans were discontinued. [n76 §2, §5] These are the claimants’ own words, and Pissurlencar’s reading of them is his.

In the caçabé of Baçaim, the pacaria “Gris”, today Giriz, was the first title in the book, held “for three lives” by Dona Margarida Teles de Meneses at 166 pardaos and 4 larins a year. “Sandor” was held by Jorge da Silva Coelho on a perpetual lease (enfiteuse) at 435 pardaos. “Bolinja e terra Belvar”, Bolinj, went to Dona Maria Henriques Coelho da Silva, at 450 pardaos. The Jesuits held “Maroy”, “Naucer e Cascarida” and other pacarias. [n39; n40; Tombo fl. 23, 29v, 54v, 27, 37v] “Umella”, Umele, was held by Francisco Pereira de Sousa, and “Boroly Mulgão”, which may be Mulgaon, by Fernão de Brito de Melo. [n40; Tombo fl. 26v, 24v]

In the caçabé of Agaçaim, in the heart of what would become the Kupari country, “Vatar Rajoly” was held “in lives” by Manuel Varjão da Fonseca at 700 pardaos a year, the highest rent in that list. “Mardes Sirgoly”, Mardes, went to Lucas de Almeida de Andrade at 180 pardaos; “Vagoly”, Wagholi, to Manuel de Abreu de Lima, and “Vastal Vagoly” to Fernão da Silveira e Meneses at 60 pardaos. “Cholna Roba”, Chulne, was held by the Jesuits’ Procurador Geral at 130 pardaos. The pacaria of Agaçaim itself was held by a woman, Dona Domingas Pereira de Azevedo, who also held the coloria of Agaçaim and of the “Ilha das Vacas”, Arnala island, and the rent on Agashi’s wild palms. “Nala Magir”, the Nala of Nallasopara, was held by Miguel Francisco Mira de Vasconcelos. [n39; n40; Tombo fl. 45v, 47v, 41v, 45, 49, 41, 42, 44v, 43]

Farther north, among the larger aldeias, “Virar Naringuy” was held by Dom Pedro de Meneses at 2,000 pardaos, the highest rent among the aldeias; “Palle”, Pali, by Dona Maria Correia de Macedo; and “Sanvar Palle Talauly” by the Jesuits. [n39; n40; Tombo fl. 121, 172, 105]

The register also shows the small rents that were leased out like land. Francisco Pereira Pinto, the captain of 1728, held the urracas of Baçaim, the rent on toddy, the fermented palm sap the Bhandaris tapped. [n39; Tombo fl. 26] The coloria at Agashi and Arnala was another such lease, perhaps of fisheries or boats. [n39, Inferences] So the captain who commanded the fort also farmed the tax on the island’s palm wine, just as the captains of the 1580s had traded in the king’s timber.

Two entries stand out for this book. In a margin note of 7 May 1727, a licenciado (a man with a law degree) named André Toscano took over several hortas “em enfiteuse, por compra”, by perpetual lease bought outright, and the aldeia “Sircar”. [n39; Tombo fl. 136, 136v] It is a documentary anchor for the Vasai surname Tuscano, a Portuguese-side holder of that name in Baçaim in the 1720s; it does not show that any Kupari family descends from him. [n39] And among the hortas in the pacaria of Gris, one, “Vitoba das palmeiras bravas” (Vitoba of the wild palms), carried an Indian name, “Jasaman Naique”. [n40; Tombo fl. 53] A local landholder’s name, in a register otherwise full of fidalgos, is a rare trace of the people who actually lived on the land.

The register’s surnames read like the roll-call of the later Christian villages. Counting lines that mention each name, the researchers found Pereira 74 times, Melo 56, Meneses 35, Coutinho 22, Almeida 20, Brito 13, with Carvalho, Rodrigues, Dias, Miranda, Cunha, Lopes, Vaz, Fernandes, Gonçalves, Correia, Nunes, Toscano, Sequeira, Pinto, Machado and Furtado appearing fewer times. Mascarenhas, Colaço, Remédios, Rumão and Dabre do not appear at all. [n39] The 1931 Census had said that tenants took “the surname of the owner of the village”. The tombo shows who those owners were in the last years before 1739: Pereiras, Melos, Meneses, Coutinhos, Almeidas, Britos. No source ties any particular Kupari or Vadval family to any particular holder, and the same surnames are common among Goan and Mangalorean Catholics too. [n39, Inferences] But the register makes the mechanism visible.

It also shows how thin the Portuguese presence on the ground was. Most of these holders were absentees, living in the fort or in Goa, drawing rents from villages they may rarely have seen. [n39, Inferences] When the Maratha army came, it was this layer, the landlords, the Jesuit procurators, the toddy-farmers, that would leave. The villages themselves would stay.

In 1728 the factor of Bassein, André Ribeiro Coutinho, was sent to report on the fort, according to da Cunha. [n39; DC about p. 75, OCR] The same year a Portuguese officer wrote a worried report on the island’s defences, which the Gazetteer quotes; whether it is Coutinho’s report the notes do not say. The creek that cut the island off from the mainland, the backwater that made Baçaim an island, was fordable at low water for about four thousand feet between Sopara and Bolinj. General Louis de Mello Pereira had built watch-towers of cocoa-palm stems and a bridge across the creek at Gokirva. The report complained that the bridge was unprotected and that the creek could also be crossed at Timli. It warned that the creek would silt up level with the fields unless it was dug out. [n12; Gaz 1882] The same writer complained that the Nil hill near Sopara had been fortified without an engineer, with bastions too small for a four-pounder gun. [n31; Gazetteer footnote citing O Chronista de Tissuary] At Gokhirva, on the old road to Tungar, the ruins of a Portuguese stockade and of a tower built against Koli raids from Jawhar were still visible in 1882. [n10; Gazetteer 1882, p. 367]

That moat of mud and tide was Baçaim’s real outer wall, and in 1728 it was failing. Sopara, the ancient port whose decline had once let Baçaim rise in its place, was now part of the city’s weak point.

The officer’s exact words on the silt survive in the Gazetteer’s Sopara entry. He wanted the creek between Sopara and Bolinj dug “as deep as the height of a man, otherwise it would soon be filled to a level with the fields, because of the great amount of silt that was brought by the river … from its two mouths”. [n36; Gaz XIV p. 322] It is the first dated observation of the silting that would, over the next century and a half, turn the island of Bassein into dry land joined to the mainland. In 1882 the Gazetteer’s surveyors found that at Sopara itself “the creek is dry”. [n36; Gaz XIV pp. 314-315] The process the 1728 officer feared was already under way, and the Portuguese had no time left to dig.

The last captains before the end, João Barbosa Barros and João Xavier Pereira Pinto, are listed by da Cunha without dates. [n32; DC 219 n.]

1737: The ring closes

The Marathi encyclopedia explains the Peshwa’s motive in one sweep. In the reign of Shahu (1708–49), it says, reports of the persecution of Hindus kept coming to the Peshwas, and they tried to take Goa, Vasai and north Konkan. The first try, an expedition against Goa under Narayanrao Ghorpade of Ichalkaranji, failed. Chimaji Appa and the Maratha sardars under him began their efforts on Vasai in 1737. [n62] The notes cannot check this account; it is a summary written in the 1980s, but it names the motive the Marathi tradition gave.

The notes give the Maratha side of the 1730s only in outline. The Peshwa was Baji Rao I, and his younger brother Chimaji Appa led his armies into the Konkan. [n25; Wikipedia: Chimaji Appa] By 1736 the Portuguese had been working on their fortress at Thana for four years. [n04; Battle of Vasai, Wikipedia] The TIFR heritage page describes the end of Baçaim as the outcome of “a campaign of about three years”. [n04; TIFR]

The campaign began in the north, at the mouth of the Vaitarna. The island fort of Arnala guarded “the most extensive inlet in the north Konkan”. The Portuguese, who called the island Ilha das Vacas, Cows’ Island, had found a Gujarat Sultanate fort there about 1530, “with domes and Saracenic arches”. They destroyed it and gave the place to a Portuguese gentleman of Baçaim. [n15; n11; Thana Gaz. XIV, pp. 10-11] Wikipedia says a Maratha officer, Shankarji Pant, urged Chimaji Appa to attack it for its naval value. A first assault, with Govindji Kasar and Manaji Angre’s navy, failed. The second, under Chimaji Appa, succeeded on 28 March 1737. [n13; n11; Wikipedia: Arnala Fort] Da Cunha’s account is grim: the Marathas took “the small fort of Arnala” and put its commandant and garrison to the sword. [n32; DC 147-148]

The date of Arnala’s fall is not settled. The Gazetteer says “about 1737” in one place and 1738 in another. [n15; Thana Gaz. XIV, pp. 10-11, 34] A Marathi inscription over the fort’s northern gateway says it was rebuilt in the reign of Bajirao I in A.D. 1737 (Shak 1659) by an architect named Baji Tulaji. [n15; Thana Gaz. XIV, pp. 10-11] A single local source gives the completion date as 23 May 1737. [n13; Durg Bharari] Wikipedia says Bajirao ordered Shankarji Pant to build the new fort, “Janjire Arnala”. [n13; Wikipedia: Arnala fort]

What the Marathas took at Arnala was a Portuguese island fort, not the Gujarat one. After about 1530 the Portuguese gentleman of Bassein to whom the island was given built “an oblong square about 700 feet with a round tower”. [n37; Gaz XIV pp. 10-11] In the Tombo of 1727–30, ten years before its fall, the coloria of the “Ilha das Vacas” was a rent held, together with Agashi, by Dona Domingas Pereira de Azevedo. [n39; Tombo fl. 41] Arnala commanded the mouth of the Vaitarna, and with it the northern water road into the island of Bassein; its loss opened the island from the north, as the shallow creek at Sopara and Bolinj had always threatened to open it from the east. [n36; Gaz XIV p. 11; Gaz XIV p. 322]

The navy mattered. After Kanhoji Angre’s death in 1729 the Maratha fleet had split, and the Peshwa’s share of it would later have its headquarters at Vasai itself; at Arnala in March 1737 the attack was coordinated with naval forces under Manaji Angre. [n36; Wikipedia: Kanhoji Angre; Wikipedia: Arnala fort] These details come from Wikipedia and should be held loosely, but they explain why the Peshwa’s officer Shankarji Pant pressed for Arnala first. The island fort was a naval base waiting for a Maratha fleet. The man who would build Arnala’s new walls, and govern the conquered province afterwards, was the Shankarji of these campaigns; the next chapter follows him as Shankarji Keshav Phadke, rebuilder of temples.

Arnala was only the first link. Over the next two years the Marathas took Thana and Salsette. [n04] Kamandurg was back in Maratha hands during the campaign, according to the trekking sources. [n12; durgbharari] A Maratha chronicle of the 1738 siege names a place called Puri, said to be “Burhanpur”, about five hundred yards south-west of today’s Manickpur station. [n11; Gaz. 1882 p. 402] On the hill of Jivdhan above Virar, where a cave goddess was worshipped, a fort was taken. A Marathi source dates its capture by Chimaji Appa to 31 March 1739. [n20; Marathi source] By the early months of 1739, Baçaim stood almost alone, with Bandra and a few posts to the south. [n04; n32] To the north, Mahim, on the coast beyond the Vaitarna, fell on 20 January 1739, according to Wikipedia’s account of Chimaji Appa. [n41; Wikipedia: Chimaji Appa]

The countryside paid for the campaign before the city did. Several of the rural churches were damaged or destroyed in these years. Hull, the Jesuit historian of the Bombay mission, says the church of Santa Cruz at Nirmal, built in 1580, was “destroyed by the Mahrattas in 1737”; the plaque on Nirmal’s bell tower says it was destroyed “during Portuguese-Maratha war in 1739”, and that from then until 1856 the cemetery served “pro tempore” as the parishioners’ church. [n38; Hull I, p. 5; n40; EI Nirmal] The two dates may record one destruction or two stages of it.

Arnala by stealth, and the camp at Bahadurpur

Kelkar, working from the Peshwa’s papers, tells the taking of Arnala quite differently from the account above, and with village names. [n66 §1] In his version there was no assault. The island fort lay eight or nine miles north of Vasai, where the Vaitarna meets the sea, and the Marathas wanted it for three reasons: whoever held it commanded the widest creek in north Konkan; the Portuguese were supplied mainly from Daman by sea; and it stood, as he puts it, “like a strong battery on Vasai’s chest”. But the work, he says, “had to be done by stealth”. Gangaji Naik Anjurkar first sent agents into Arnala to sow division, and Shankarajipant sent the islanders an assurance through Govindji Kasar. For the boats he turned to Gavraji Patil of Bolinj, who agreed on a price: if Arnala fell, he was to have the right to levy a toll on all goods arriving by water at the two ports of Agashi and Bolinj. Shankarajipant promised him Peshwa grants. [n66 §1]

Then came a night’s comedy of errors. Shankarajipant sent a band of 400 men, Gangaji Naik Anjurkar among them, to Agashi to wait for the boats; the Bolinj boats ran aground on dry ground and did not come; and Gangaji Naik fetched Shankarajipant from his camp at Papdi to Agashi. After dark the boats came up from Bolinj, the men boarded, and Shankarajipant sailed through the creek to Arnala’s port. The salt-pan people of the agar came out to meet him, the boats went on to the island, and the post was taken without a fight. The date, on Pissurlencar’s reckoning, is 28 March 1737, the date the book already had. Shankarajipant left 400 men under Bajirao Belose and went back to Papdi. On 13 April he wrote to Chimaji Appa: “I went and took Arnala by great stratagem. The vessel I seized belongs to Rang Shenvi; I brought it and put it in the Agashi creek… The other twenty-five boats and mahangiris of the port of Bolinj belonged to Muslims, and I took them under me by arrangement and made forty seamen servants, and I am taking shipwrights into service.” [n66 §1] No fight and no death at Arnala is recorded in Marathi or Portuguese papers, Kelkar says. That agrees with the Portuguese loss of the post and not with the “first assault” of the Wikipedia version. It also suggests that the Tapkir letter above, in which the Portuguese fleet shelled the Marathas off Arnala so that they could not take the island, describes the earlier failed approach, and so belongs before 28 March, not to the “first week of April” the thesis gave it. [n66 §1, Inferences]

The astrologers of Agashi had a hand in the fort that followed. A family of Joshis there, whose petitions to the English government of 1833 and 1857 Kelkar prints, wrote that their ancestor, Govind or Girdhar Joshi, was summoned by the Peshwa to give the auspicious time for building Arnala’s fort; that the work began at that time and was finished, and the people settled well; and that for this the Peshwa took Rs 50 a year off their garden and field assessment, a charity “without service” that had run for 79 years by 1857. In 1833 they said their family had lived at Agashi, the kasba of Vasai taluka, “for three and a half hundred years”. [n66 §2] Work on the fort ran through the spring of 1738. On 21 March of that year the foundation of the south-west bastion’s wall was begun, 270 cubits from the Bhairav bastion; Baloji Chandrarao came from Papdi with 300 men; and Shankarajipant asked for sixteen more masons for the water tanks of Tandulwadi, Kaldurg, Jivdhan, Kamandurg and others. [n66 §3] The Gazetteer’s inscription gives the rebuilding as Shaka 1659; the Joshis give the muhurta as Shaka 1660, which began in spring 1738, and Kelkar’s papers put the building work in March 1738, so the dates are close even if they do not match to the year. [n66 §3] Khobrekar adds that the Marathas finished the repairs in December 1738, despite the Portuguese batteries firing on the work. [n68 §2]

At Vasai itself, the Marathas had camped since the monsoon of 1737 with Bahadurpur at their backs and a stockade only 800 cubits from the fort, where shot from the walls killed a man or two a day. In March and April 1738 Shankarajipant and Chimaji Appa planned a new stockade 800 cubits further back, enclosing the large fortified house of a Portuguese the OCR names “Martin Sarvela”, with Vajragad close behind it for support. It was to be finished in two months. The new site did not flood in the rains and had good drinking water, and three thousand men and two or three hundred horse could winter in it. At the same time Shankarajipant placed a post of 100 men on Kamandurg on 19 March, against a rumour that the Portuguese were coming, to keep the road from Mandvi to Kalyan and Bhiwandi and the rear of Arnala and Vajragad, and Chimaji began fortified houses at Ghodbunder and Ovale. [n66 §3–§4]

A Maratha letter from Sopara, April 1737

Little is known of how the Marathas took Sopara and Agashi. A doctoral thesis by Mayur Thakare at Deccan College, Pune (2018), on the defence of the Portuguese Province of the North, found one source: an undated letter from the Maratha officer Harbaji Patil Tapkir to Chimaji Appa, which the thesis dates from its contents to the first week of April 1737 and cites to Sardesai (1933, p. 41). The notes had not seen it. [n60 §3]

As the thesis reports the letter, two Maratha forces went to Sopara, one under Moraji Shinde, Shankaraji Keshav and Chimanaji Bhivrao, the other under Tapkir himself, and attacked three Portuguese bastions. Chimanaji Bhivrao took the first while Tapkir went to the port. The Portuguese on the bastions fled at the noise of the assault, and the Marathas took four cannon from the first bastion, five from the one by the bridge and four from the last, across the bridge. “The entire Sopara stands deserted,” Tapkir wrote, and at low tide the creek by the bridge was dry. [n60 §3] It was the creek the officer of 1728 had warned would silt up. From Sopara the Marathas went to Arnala, where the Portuguese fleet in the creek shelled them; unable to take the island, they turned to the Portuguese church at Agashi. The infantry had walked twelve kos and found the whole coast in excellent condition. The Portuguese, seeing them come, left in ships and fired on them. Agashi’s port held twenty or twenty-two ships. The Marathas took two merchant vessels loaded with sugar and other goods, left some men there, and went back toward Sopara. [n60 §3]

The name Shankaraji Keshav matches the Shankarji Keshav Phadke whom the next chapter follows, though the thesis does not say they were the same man. The thesis also settles a point this chapter left open: it says the officer who inspected Sopara’s defences in 1728, and recommended dredging the creek, was André Ribeiro Coutinho, citing the Gazetteer. [n60 §3] It adds that the Portuguese had also built four wooden stockades at the creeks, and that the palm-trunk watchtowers General Luís de Melo Pereira ordered were so decayed within three years that they could not bear the weight of a gun. [n60 §3] Nothing of the Sopara bastions survives. J. S. Barth’s aquatint of 1803, “An Elevated View of the Islands of Bombay and Salsette”, shows the bridge and the bastions as “Batteries”, and Captain Dickinson’s report of 1818, the last description, mentions a bridge joining the islands of Vasai and Sopara, a stockade of palm trunks and the remains of one Portuguese tower. [n60 §3]

1739: The siege

The camp and the vow

On 17 February 1739 the siege of Baçaim began, with Chimaji Appa encamped at Bhadrapur. [n04; Battle of Vasai, Wikipedia; n32; DC 148] (One Marathi heritage site says the siege ran “from March”. [n10; durgbharari])

A story has grown up around those first weeks. Camped at Vadavali, by the hot springs of the Tansa valley inland, Chimaji Appa is said to have vowed a temple to the goddess Vajreshwari if Baçaim fell. Some versions add a dream in which the goddess showed him how to take the fort. [n04; n10; Wikipedia: Vajreshwari Temple; durgbharari] The vow is widely repeated, but this book’s notes found no primary text of it; the letters that might hold it would be in the Peshwa archives. The dream is folklore. [n10, Gaps] What the record does show is that a temple to the goddess was built after the victory, and the next chapter will tell that story.

The two armies

The size of the attacking army comes from the defenders’ side. A Portuguese account gives the Marathas forty thousand infantry, twenty-five thousand cavalry, about four thousand miners, five thousand camels and fifty elephants, close to a hundred thousand men with reinforcements. [n04; Battle of Vasai, Wikipedia] Besieged garrisons tend to count their enemies generously, and the figure should be read as Portuguese testimony, not as a muster roll.

The Maratha commanders named are Chimaji Appa himself, Malhar Rao Holkar, Ranoji Shinde, Manaji Angre and Khanderao Holkar. [n04; n25; Battle of Vasai, Wikipedia] Local communities joined in too. The 1882 Gazetteer records that help given at the siege by the Pachkalshis, the gardening caste of the coast, led the Peshwa to make one family chief Patils of Salsette and others proprietors of villages in Bhiwandi. (The Gazetteer’s footnote prints the date as “1743”, probably a slip.) [n08; Gazetteer 1882, p. 130 n.]

Newer research names some of these local allies. Three independent government texts agree that the Pachkalshis, the caste of the Vadvals, helped the Maratha army and were rewarded. The 1882 footnote, credited to the Collector W. B. Mulock, says that “the disturbances of the eighteenth century aroused the old warlike spirit of the Pachkalshis”, and that for their help at the siege the Peshwa made one family chief Patils of Salsette, another proprietors of Angaon in Bhiwandi, and a third proprietors of Anjar in Bhiwandi. [n41; Gaz XIII-I p. 130 n.] Enthoven, in 1922, wrote that “they rendered material help to the Marathas during the siege of Bassein and the acquisition of adjoining country in 1739-40. In consequence of this help the Naik family of Anjore, among others, obtained the title of Sarpatil and received inam lands from the Peshwa’s Court. To the siege of Bassein and other events of the great Maratha-Portuguese struggle are traced back the patelships of many families of the Vadval division.” [n41; Enthoven III, p. 161] And the Bombay City Gazetteer of 1909 gave the names: “As late as 1739 AD two Panchkalshis, named Bugaji and Gangaji Naik, were granted the village of Anjur in inam with the title of Naik Sarpatel by the Peshwa in return for help accorded to the Marathas against the Portuguese.” [n41; Bombay City Gaz. I, p. 238]

A Marathi edition of the Sashtichi Bakhar, the chronicle of Salsette, published in 1935 by Gajanan Govind Naik, a writer from Anjur itself, goes further. It quotes an older source, “Hemadri”, saying that in 1739, “in the conquest of Vasai province from the Portuguese, Gangaji Naik and Chuba Naik of Anjur and some Somvanshi Kshatriya gentlemen of heroic character gave the Peshwa the greatest help; if they had not, the Maratha army would have been badly ruined”. It lists the helpers by community: Pathare Kshatriyas, Brahmans, Pathare Prabhus, Somvanshi Kshatriyas such as the Chaudhari family of Umele (known as Kuker) and of Ambemoda, Bhandaris, Kolis, Agris, and Christians. Among them it names Raut men, Dhaga, Gan, Laxman and Mayaji Raut. [n41; n40; Naik 1935, pp. 15, 320 ff.] The editor himself argues over whether Gangaji Naik’s clan was “Pathare Kshatriya” or “Somvanshi Kshatriya”, and the Peshwa’s original letters, which the book cites from the Peshwa Daftar, have not been seen by this book’s researchers. [n41, Gaps] Anjur is in Bhiwandi, across the Ulhas, not on the island of Bassein. [n40; B12]

Those lists change the picture of the siege. It was not simply a Maratha army from the Deccan against a Portuguese fort. Men of the coast’s own farming, fishing and priestly communities, including, by this account, some Christians, guided and helped the attackers, and were paid in villages and titles afterwards. The Christian helpers are not named. Why a Christian would help the Peshwa against the Portuguese, the sources do not say; resentment of the landlords the Tombo lists would be one possible reason, but it is not documented. [n41, Inferences]

Against them stood a garrison of about twelve hundred Portuguese soldiers and Indian auxiliaries. [n04; Monteiro and da Silva 1996, p. 148] Its captain was Caetano de Sousa Pereira, the last name on da Cunha’s list of captains of Baçaim. [n32; DC 219 n.; n04] The Viceroy at Goa was the Count of Sandomil. [n04]

Help was not coming. Da Cunha records that Luis de Botelho, governor of Salsette, retired to Karanja, leaving Pereira to defend Baçaim. A Captain Ferraz commanded Bandora (Bandra). Goa itself was in distress from an invasion by the Bhonsles. And Bombay, the island Portugal had given to England, refused help despite appeals by João de Sousa Terras. [n32; DC 147-148]

Spring: the creeks

In March Manaji Angre, commander of the Maratha navy, took Uran, Bandra, Versova and Dharavi, and then joined Chimaji in April. [n04; Battle of Vasai, Wikipedia] Da Cunha adds that the Marathas seized the islands of Versova and Dharavi and the creeks. [n32; DC 147-148] The sea lanes south to Bombay and Goa were being cut, and so was the water that had always been Baçaim’s last road out.

The notes do not describe March and April day by day. What they make clear is that the question, by the end of April, was whether Baçaim’s stone could be brought down.

May: the mines

The answer came from underground. The Marathas did not take Baçaim by climbing its walls; they dug under them and filled the tunnels with gunpowder. [n04, Inferences]

Wikipedia’s day-by-day account, drawn from Portuguese and Maratha sources, runs like this. On 1 May, ten mines were laid; four exploded and opened breaches. On 2 May more mines were sprung, and about four thousand Marathas stormed. On 3 May a mine destroyed the tower of São Sebastião. [n04; Battle of Vasai, Wikipedia]

The older Portuguese-based accounts, as given by da Cunha and the 1882 Gazetteer, tell it a little differently. The commandant Sylveira de Menezes was killed. Some twelve or thirteen mines were sprung. Two exploded and made a large breach. A third went wrong and blew up “hundreds of the assailants”. The final breach was beneath the tower of São Sebastião. [n32; DC 148; BG 34]

The two versions differ on the number of mines and of explosions, and they cannot be reconciled from the notes. On the decisive point they agree. The wall gave way at São Sebastião, the bastion built in 1554 under Captain Francisco de Sá and named for the saint who had been the city’s earlier patron. The Marathas, the Gazetteer says, “forced their way in through this bastion”. [n32; BG 42-43]

One more story clings to that bastion. In 1882 the Gazetteer recorded that “inside of the wall a passage is said to run to the river. But the air is bad and puts out lights, and the passage has never been explored.” It placed the passage near São Sebastião. [n32; BG 43] Later heritage writing turned this into a secret tunnel of 1554, 530 feet long, with poisoned arrowheads for intruders. Those details are not independently confirmed and look like retellings. [n32; n13; Live History India] The rumour of 1882 is the oldest evidence there is.

The surrender

With the walls open, Chimaji Appa sent an ultimatum: “the entire garrison would be slaughtered and the fort levelled”. [n04; Battle of Vasai, Wikipedia] A later newspaper column, known to this book only through a search summary, says Caetano de Sousa Pereira signed the surrender because most of his senior officers were dead. [n04; n25; DNA column] It is a thin source, but it fits what came before: a commandant killed, mines exploding under the defenders, and a garrison of twelve hundred that had held out for three months.

Baçaim surrendered on 16 May 1739. That is the date in the Gazetteer and in da Cunha’s main account. Da Cunha’s text also has “19th May” in one place, perhaps a misprint. The 16th is the better attested. [n32; BG 34; DC 147-148] Note 39’s reading of da Cunha gives 19 May for the capitulation of Caetano de Sousa Pereira, and Wikipedia’s Arnala article even gives 4 May for the surrender of Vasai; but da Cunha’s main account and the village research both settle on 16 May for the signed treaty. [n39; n40; n36; Wikipedia: Arnala fort] The 4 May and 16 May dates turn out to be two calendars, not two accounts; see “Kelkar’s account” below. [n63]

The terms were generous by the standards of the day. “All the garrison, as well regulars as auxiliaries” were to march out “with all the honours of war”. Inhabitants who wished to leave were given eight days to take their moveable property with them. The text of the treaty was printed in the Bombay Quarterly Review, volume iv, pages 84–85. Da Cunha and the Gazetteer do not give the clauses on religion. [n32; DC 148-149 and n.; BG 34]

Newer research found them in another book. Hull’s Bombay Mission History (1927), citing an earlier collection of documents, summarises the capitulation’s religious terms. Noble families, Christians who wished to leave, all the religious orders and the priests were given free passage. The Christians who stayed were to “enjoy liberty of worship”. The privileges of three churches in Bassein, one in the district and one in Salsette were preserved, with their curates “subject to the Primate of India”, that is, to the Archbishop of Goa. And the images in the churches were to remain. [n37; n38; Hull I, pp. 75-76, citing Materials I, p. 213] The Gazetteer’s population volume agrees on the number: the Portuguese secured “only five churches, three in Bassein city, one in Bassein district and one in Salsette”. [n37; Gaz XIII-I p. 203] Mendiratta places the one in the district in the “Cassabé” subdivision, and adds that the Marathas would accept only non-white priests. [n34; Mendiratta pp. 216-218]

So the question this chapter’s first version left open has an answer. The Portuguese did bargain for their faith at the surrender, and they won something: worship guaranteed, five churches protected, images left in place. They did not bargain for the Portuguese clergy to stay. The friars and Jesuits would go; the Indian priests trained in Bassein’s own seminaries would remain.

A Lisbon pamphlet of 1741

One account of the end of Baçaim was printed in Lisbon within two years of the event. Diogo da Costa’s Relaçam das guerras da India desde o anno de 1736 até o de 1740 (“Account of the wars of India from 1736 to 1740”) came out in 1741 and is now digitised in full. [n60 §1] Its preface calls Baçaim “the theatre of the most valiant deeds that can deserve eternal fame” and promises an account “written with all truth, and without the least hyperbole”. That is a warning, not a guarantee. This is a patriotic pamphlet. Its numbers, an army of 150,000 and 46,000 Maratha dead, are not data. What it gives is the shape of the last days as a Portuguese reader of 1741 was told them. The translations here are this book’s own, from OCR text, and the original wording is given where it matters.

It describes the place in a sentence. “This fortress of Baçaim is the key of the North” (a chave do Norte): it has “an arm of the sea” with a dockyard, a ribeira de naus, and in the lands around “good timber grows for building vessels.” Being a rich place, it “inflamed the enemy’s desire and greed all the more.” [n60 §1] The siege, in this telling, began in 1736. The general in the north, Martinho da Silveira, ordered the other fortresses’ garrisons to fall back on Baçaim, because their commanders could not hold them. Silveira was killed by an artillery ball which, after skipping on the water three or four times, entered his boat; the pamphlet says Chimaji Appa was delighted. The Viceroy named Pedro de Mello in his place. [n60 §1] The pamphlet’s order of events compresses three years and does not match the Goa archives, which the next sections give: there, Silveira took command only in January 1739 and was killed on 15 April 1739, and Pedro de Melo was killed at Thana in December 1738. [n65 §2]

Chimaji’s first assault came, the pamphlet says, at dawn on 9 June 1737, when he sent 8,000 men against the two bastions of São Gonçalo and Reis Magos and lost heavily. On 15 September of the same year, at four in the morning, twelve thousand men attacked four bastions. (The Portuguese commander’s own letters, read in the archives, make the second attack about 2,000 men, lasting an hour and a half; the dates of both attacks hold. [n65 §2]) The fort of São Gonçalo across the river was given up to the Marathas, but its Portuguese commander had mined it: more than 350 Marathas went in to take possession, and he fired the mines and escaped by boat. The Marathas, the pamphlet says, found the Portuguese brave and also cruel. [n60 §1]

The pamphlet’s account of the end agrees with the dates the chapter already gave and adds a good deal. By the last day, it says, fewer than sixty men could bear arms, and there was powder and shot for no more than two hours of fighting, with no means of sending for more. All asked the general to treat. He refused at first, then yielded. Next morning a white flag went up, and two soldiers and a priest who served as interpreter went to Chimaji’s tent. The terms, as the pamphlet lists them, were these. The soldiers would march out with drums beating, colours flying and weapons ready, and the people of the fortress would leave with all they had. They could take two mortars and four field guns. Chimaji would supply a hundred galvetas, small vessels, for the guns and the people. Those who preferred to go overland to Bombay, Diu or Chaul would have free passage and carriages for their baggage. What could not be carried, merchants sent by Chimaji would buy. Prisoners would be restored on both sides, even those taken on earlier occasions. The Franciscan convent in the fort would be kept, “at his cost”, though the OCR leaves the subject of that clause unclear. The fort was to be emptied in seven days. [n60 §1]

The agreement was signed on 16 May 1739. By 23 May the ships were ready, and a Maratha commander the pamphlet calls “Bagiraõ Pardani”, whom it identifies as the Peshwa, came with Chimaji to watch the embarkation. The other accounts in the notes do not put Baji Rao at Baçaim, so this should be held loosely. General Caetano de Souza went last, with fewer than sixty fighting men. Told that there was no one else, the pamphlet says, the Maratha commander declared it incredible that so few could have held such a fortress, and that “a single Portuguese was worth more than a squadron of soldiers.” [n60 §1]

Two points separate this account from the chapter’s earlier sources. It says seven days were allowed to leave; da Cunha and Hull say eight. And it says the garrison at the end was under sixty men able to fight, not the garrison of twelve hundred that the surrender section above describes. A pamphlet written to praise the defenders has every reason to make the end seem desperate, so neither figure is proven. The 4 May “white flag” that the notes carried as a rival date is explained in the next sections: it is the same event as the pamphlet’s, in the English calendar of the day.

The siege as the Marathi encyclopedia tells it

The Marathi Vishwakosh entry on Vasai (1980s, by G. H. Khare and Su. R. Deshpande) gives a short Maratha-side account of the campaign. [n62] Early in 1739, it says, Chimaji Appa sent forces at the same time into the lands of Daman, Vasai and Goa. About two lakh people, soldiers and camp followers, took part in the Vasai campaign. It names as commanders Pilaji Jadhav, Malharrao Holkar, Ranoji Shinde, Ganpatrao Mehendale, Ramchandra Patwardhan, Bapuji Bhivrao, Vithoji Kadam, Jivajirao Vichare and Gangoji Naik, and says Shankaraji Keshav and the Anjurkar group gave valuable help. On 6 February 1739 Chimaji took the nearby posts of Versova and Dharavi and laid siege to Vasai; the siege lasted about four months. [n62] The entry’s date for the end, “23 May 1839”, is a misprint for 1739. Seven or eight days before it, it says, talks began and the fighting stopped. [n62]

Its list of terms agrees with the pamphlet and with Hull in outline and adds some detail. The remaining troops and camp followers, and “Christian, Hindu or Muslim” townspeople, could leave the fort at will with arms and property. Ships were to be arranged, and guns and ammunition could be taken. Prisoners were to be freed on both sides. Christians who chose to stay could keep their religion and the churches were not to be harmed. And each side was to keep one of its officials, an amaldar, as a hostage until the terms were met. Chimaji, it says, hired boats to give the travellers passage. [n62] No one touched the Christian buildings under Maratha rule, the entry says, but Chimaji installed a Maruti temple in the fort, the first Hindu deity there. [n62] That is a claim about the surrender and the years after it; the notes record rural churches destroyed during the campaign itself (above), and the fort’s own churches later fell to ruin.

On the dead it gives a figure the notes did not have. Historians disagree, it says, but “Portuguese documents” report that more than 22,000 Marathas were killed and 850 of the Portuguese force were lost. [n62] This makes three Maratha figures, none of them from the Maratha side: about 12,000 killed and wounded in da Cunha and the Gazetteer, over 22,000 in the Marathi encyclopedia’s reading of Portuguese documents, and 46,000 in the pamphlet of 1741 above. The honest statement is that the Marathas lost heavily and that no source counts the dead. After the campaign, the entry adds, the Portuguese kept only Diu, Daman and Revdanda on the northern coast. [n62]

Kelkar’s account: the last assault, the terms and the calendar

The fullest Marathi study of the campaign is Y. N. Kelkar’s Vasaichi Mohim, “The Vasai Campaign”, which was cited in the notes only by title. Its text is on archive.org, and it quotes the Peshwa’s day-books and Chimaji Appa’s own letters. [n63] What follows is this book’s translation of the passages on the end of the siege, from a rough OCR of the Marathi.

The last two days. After the long siege, the Marathas prepared a large attack for 2 May and fired their mines the next day. The Portuguese had moved their guns from the half-ruined bastion of São Sebastião and put a battalion of grenadiers there, and had set a fire-trap in the gap in the Remédios bastion. When the mine blew, half of the bastion fell. The Marathas rushed the breach, were thrown back three times, and sat pressed against the rubble for the rest of the day. The Portuguese, Kelkar says, had put every white soldier in the line there, and most of them died: 130 of their officers, large and small, were killed or wounded. The Maratha dead included Dattaji Bhandavalkar, Hansu Adhav of Pune, Sakho Ganesh Sarnaik of Purandar, Baji Rao’s jamadar Kuwarsingh and Murarji Naik of the Anjurkar party, whose body Gangaji Naik carried out. [n63 §2]

The night council. That night the fort’s officers decided they could hold no longer. Hundreds were dead or wounded; the powder stores were nearly empty; the bastions and walls were down; and no help could come from Goa or Bombay. They had sold their gold and silver to buy from the English of Bombay five to seven thousand hand grenades (the OCR’s word is probably that) and powder, and could not buy more. Two or three more Maratha mines were known to be ready. At dawn on 4 May the Portuguese raised a white flag in the breach of São Sebastião. They sent a man to Chimaji’s camp for a pass, and a Portuguese commander whom the OCR gives as “Pedro Barit”, probably Pedro Barreto, came out to negotiate. Chimaji wrote that when they saw he would not lift the siege without terms, “they lost heart”. [n63 §2]

The terms. Kelkar prints twelve. The troops and camp followers were to leave with arms, band and colours; Christian, Hindu and Muslim townspeople could leave with their goods; the Portuguese ships could go with their guns and powder; Chimaji would supply boats and see that the Angres did not molest the travellers; priests could leave with their property; the Portuguese stores of powder and grain could be taken (Chimaji refused this and bought all the stores); Christians who stayed could practise their religion without hindrance; prisoners were to be returned; Chimaji would withdraw his army to Bahadurpur, and the Marathas would not enter the fort until the ships were out of cannon range; no goods were to be taken without payment; the three Christian churches in Vasai were to stand as they were, with their images and ornaments; and each side was to hold one official as hostage until the terms were met. Seven days were allowed for leaving. [n63 §3] The fort’s officers gathered everyone in the church of St Anthony and read the terms aloud, telling them that after two or three hundred years they must leave, and that even their revered churches would be taken from them. “Water came to the eyes of the speakers and the listeners alike.” Chimaji arranged a hundred boats: the Peshwa’s day-book records boat-hire of Rs 300, 155 and 90½ on 8, 9 and 10 May, and the Portuguese hired more from Bombay. Pedro Barreto went to the Peshwa’s camp as hostage; Chimaji put twenty men on every bastion; and the Portuguese went out through the breach of São Sebastião with colours lowered and a band playing, and boarded ship. [n63 §3]

The garrison and the dead. Chimaji’s letter to Shahu put the garrison at the start at about 5,000: 1,000 “Dons” and 3,000 Christians in the fort, with about 1,000 other hands. At the surrender, Kelkar writes from the Portuguese histories, there remained 350 Portuguese soldiers, 160 Christians and 300 locals: about 810 men able to fight. That is not the “fewer than sixty” of the 1741 pamphlet, which is most likely rhetoric or a count of one group. [n63 §4] On the dead, Chimaji’s own letters give about 5,000 Marathas killed, mostly under the mines, and 700 to 800 Portuguese, besides the wounded; Kelkar’s own estimate for the two days of assault is 5,000 Marathas and 4,000 of the enemy. He quotes Pissurlencar that neither side kept an official count, and warns against crude ratios: attackers always lose more than defenders. [n63 §4] The book now has four sets of figures for the same loss. Chimaji’s letters (about 5,000 Maratha and 700–800 Portuguese dead) are the nearest to the event; da Cunha and the Gazetteer (12,000 Maratha killed and wounded), the Marathi Vishwakosh (over 22,000, which comes from the Portuguese commanders’ reports; see below) and the 1741 pamphlet (46,000) all come later and all from the Portuguese or British side. [n63 §4; n62; n32]

Who was there. Baji Rao was not. Kelkar writes that the news of Vasai’s fall reached him on 16 May, when he was camped at Ranali in Janabad pargana, far from Vasai, “and great joy was had; sugar was distributed”. The 1741 pamphlet’s picture of the Peshwa watching the embarkation is therefore a Portuguese embellishment. [n63 §5] Chimaji’s letters on the victory are in the same pride as the Portuguese pamphlet’s: the Firangis “took courage and soldiered as one should call soldiering”; “never has the Maratha army endured such fire”. [n63 §6]

The calendar. Here Kelkar’s text settles an old puzzle. He gives Peshwa day-book dates in Hijri months with day-month equivalents. Three of them, 25 Safar, 24 Rabi I and 17 Rabi II of 1152 AH, convert to 3 June, 1 July and 24 July 1739 in the Gregorian calendar, but Kelkar glosses them as 23 May, 20 June and 13 July. The gap is 11 days each time, the difference between the Julian and the Gregorian calendars in 1739. Kelkar’s dates are Old Style, as the English of Bombay still wrote them, and 11 days must be added to match the Portuguese, who had used the Gregorian calendar since 1582. [n63 §1] On that reading, the white flag of 4 May Old Style was raised on 15 May New Style; the agreement was signed the next day, 5 May Old Style, which is the 16 May of the Portuguese pamphlet and the Gazetteer; and the Portuguese left on 12 May Old Style, which is their 23 May. The “4 May white flag” that the notes carried as a rival to the 16 May date is the same event in a different calendar. [n63 §1] It also follows that the “23 May” on which the book’s earlier sources have the saffron flag go up may be two different days: 23 May New Style, when the Portuguese left, and 23 May Old Style, the day the Peshwa’s day-book records the formal hoisting of the flag, which falls on 3 June New Style. [n63 §1] That second point is supported by Khobrekar’s history of the Konkan, which follows Kelkar and gives the same chain in the Maratha dates: the Portuguese came to terms on the morning of 5 May; they vacated on 12 May; and “on the 23rd, at an auspicious moment, the Marathas hoisted their flag on the walls of Bassein”. [n68 §1] It is still to be checked in the Peshwa Daftar itself, since Khobrekar is not an independent witness.

After the fall. On 9 May Old Style Chimaji issued a kaul, an assurance, to the merchants and Christian people of Vasai, in the names of a priest Antonio and others: “Stay without fear. Have no anxiety on any account. We will not harm your goods and cattle.” On 13 May the people of Vasai came to him with gifts; and that day he sent the 125 gold coins, and the money for a crown for the god Bhuleshwar, that his guru Brahmendra Swami had asked for if Vasai fell. [n63 §7] He had a homa performed and the flag formally hoisted with six goats, coconuts, vermilion and a flag dyed in ten colours. The Peshwa’s day-book records the cost. [n63 §7] “Till then,” Kelkar writes, “no Hindu deity had touched the fort.” On 27 July Old Style Chimaji installed in the fort, with rites, a Maruti image made by the stonecutter Manakoji Pathrawat. [n63 §7]

The Goa archives: Pissurlencar’s chronology, 1723–1739

The most careful account of the campaign’s order of events was made by Dr P. S. Pissurlencar, the archivist of the Goa Archives, who read the Portuguese registers, the Lisbon manuscripts and the Peshwa’s papers together. His books on the campaign were summed up in English in 1983 in Portuguese-Mahratta Relations. [n65] All his dates are New Style, and he checks them against the weekdays his sources give.

The pressure, 1723–1736. Kalyan, taken by the Marathas in 1719, was the base for raids on Bassein territory. Kanhoji Angria took about seven lakh rupees a year from the villages of the Bassein coast and made Portuguese ships from Bassein and Daman carry his passes. On 26 November 1723 Pilaji Jadhav came with 40,000 horse (Lobato gives 4,000, below) as far as Gokhirve near Bassein, and the Portuguese plundered Maratha villages in return; a treaty followed on 10 January 1724. On 5 December 1728 the officer André Ribeiro Coutinho told the Viceroy that the Portuguese could not withstand the Marathas with so small an army and should pay the Chhatrapati or a favourite of his Rs 2,500 a year. In May 1730 the Marathas invaded again, and Salsette would have fallen but for 700 soldiers the English of Bombay sent. The Viceroy blamed Ganesh Naik of the Anjurkar family, and one Anjurkar, arrested by the Inquisition at Bassein and sent to Goa, was baptised there as Inácio de Santa Teresa. Don Luís Botelo, General of Bassein from March 1733, built the castle at Thana. [n65 §1]

What the Portuguese archives add, 1723–1734 (Lobato). Alberto Lobato’s Relações Luso-Maratas (1965) prints the Goa-archive letters behind these raids. [n74] The pretext was a claim to a tenth of the Province’s revenue, based on a Mughal firman, which Bassein refused as having “no effect” in lands of the Portuguese Crown. [n74 §1] Lobato’s figure for the first raid, on 26 November 1723 through Saibana under Pilaji Jadhav, is “a troop of 4,000 horse”, with “more than 12,000” reported on the frontier two days later, where Pissurlencar’s summary above has 40,000; the sources differ by a factor of ten and are not reconciled here. [n74 §1] Pilaji wrote that the chauth did not come from “your side” and that Shahu had ordered him “to destroy”; the Bassein General, Luís de Melo de Sampaio, replied that he knew no lord of the lands but the King of Portugal and asked that the curumbins (the native cultivators) not be destroyed. [n74 §2] By 2 December the raid had reached the jurisdictions of Manorá, Mahim and Tarapur. The Marathas also gave a second cause, the Portuguese arrest of the headmen (patéis) of the Taloja pargana near Bhiwandi, which Baji Rao’s letter of 5 December calls the invasion that broke the peace. [n74 §3] The Ajuste of 12 January 1724 had eight articles about transit of the Maratha State’s goods, return of prizes and runaway slaves, and a Portuguese envoy to Satara over the tenth; the Governors told the King that the Province’s villages were “inhabited by people so timid that with any fear of invasion they abandon them”. [n74 §4] In December 1724 the Marathas devastated the Koli king’s lands beside Manorá and Asserim, hitting Portuguese villages “mixed” with them. [n74 §5]

In 1728 the Marathas also claimed a grão-canil, which Lobato glosses as a tax “earlier than the Portuguese conquest” that the Bassein lands paid to “the Koli King” and that the Portuguese had kept. The Viceroy answered that it was paid “to the king of that land” and would go to Shahu if Shahu became that king. [n74 §6] The war of 1728 ended with a Peace of Bassein in October whose text Lobato could not find. [n74 §6] In 1730–31 the Viceroy, Saldanha da Gama, wrote that the curumbins “only incline to and serve whom they fear, and so they now serve the Marathas, considering them victorious; if our arms begin to triumph they will serve us”. [n74 §7] The Marathas’ peace terms of January 1732 included two points that the Viceroy refused: that the Portuguese “consent to pagodes for the gentiles” and pay a village tribute, “alleging the example of those paid to the Choutiá king and the Cole king”. The Portuguese instructions for the treaty signed at Bombay about 30 January 1732 refused the tribute because “the lands of the Crown of Portugal never paid tribute to anyone”. [n74 §8] In March 1732 the General took back the hill posts of Chandavari (Santa Maria) and Tatamaca in the Bassein jurisdiction with detachments of 140 and 60 men. [n74 §8]

The next step was to fortify Thana. In 1733 André Ribeiro Coutinho organised labour from the island’s villages: the landholder Jacinto Landim da Guerra offered 23 boats and the curumbins of three of his villages at his own cost; “the Coles of Chandanim” were to supply sand free; one third of each village’s curumbins were to be taken, paid in money and not in rice; and the Bassein Senate said that the finta would yield only “19 thousand and odd xerafins”. [n74 §9] A critic’s chronicle said the island’s people paid a rupee a family and worked unpaid; Lobato doubts it. A Maratha letter of about 1734 reports from Kalyan that “the Portuguese made a census of all the Hindus without exception, including children of five years”, set up posts, and that “the rumour runs that they will convert the Hindus there”, causing “a very immoderate pain”. Lobato calls the rumour hearsay and the census a count of Hindu labour and levy. [n74 §9]

The hill forts, 1730–1737. Pissurlencar’s dated chronology (1942) names the hill forts of the Vasai–Virar country that changed hands. [n76 §3] In May 1730 the Marathas took Khandvi, Jivdhan, Takmak, Tandulwadi and Kamandurg. On 7 April 1731 Captain António dos Santos retook the serra of Jivdhan, and in March 1732, under the Bombay treaty, the Portuguese reoccupied Chandawadi (Santa Maria) and Takmak. In May 1737 the Marathas took Santa Cruz (11 May), Mandvi (12 May), Tandulwadi (13 May), Saibana (14 May) and Kaldurg (16 May), and Manorá fell on 30 May after a siege begun on the 17th; on 13 June they took Santa Maria again. At Sabajo (Belapor) the captain who lost the fort on 7 May 1737 was Manuel Caetano da Gamara Coutinho, “a converted Hindu”. On 9 June and 15 September the walls of Bassein were assaulted by more than 4,000 and more than 8,000 men and the attacks repulsed. [n76 §3]

Religion in the peace terms, 1731–1740. Pissurlencar also traces the Hindu clauses. A Portuguese paper of about August 1731 says that the Marathas twice offered peace on condition of “a village in each jurisdiction to make pagodes and continue freely the ceremonies of their religion; that orphans not be made Christians; that a fourth, and afterwards a tenth, of all the provinces be paid them; that they be given the pensions that are given to the Colle and Chotia”. The Viceroy believed they would not insist, and in the treaty that followed the clauses on Hindu worship were left out. In January 1740, through the Bombay captain Inchbird, Baji Rao asked for licence to practise Hindu religion in Salsette, Bardez and the island of Goa, and a contemporary document says that the Marathas wanted Hindus there free to found as many pagodas as they wished, the Inquisition abolished and the xendi capitation never collected. Marathi letters to Chimaji Appa speak of establishing “the dominion of the Gods and Brahmins” and of “Hindu raj”. The Portuguese sources, he says, give religion “secondary importance”. [n76 §4] On 10 March 1739 the Viceroy had the leading Hindus of Goa arrested and held at Mormugão, and a rich Hindu merchant of Panelim, Fondu Kamat, had his house plundered by priests of the city; the home government later made him amends. [n76 §3]

Thana and Dharavi, 1737. On Saturday 6 April 1737, at eleven at night, the Marathas took a water-tower in Thana creek and entered Salsette, and within two days held nearly all of it, leaving the Portuguese Bandra, Versova, Karanja and Bassein. The news reached Goa on 12 April, and the Viceroy ordered the General of Bassein to protect Dharavi “with all his might”. A Maratha letter says that “Dharavi is the nose of Bassein”. He dismissed Botelo and sent António Cardim Froes, who reached Bassein on 23 May. On 26 May Cardim attacked the fort the Marathas were building on Dongri hill and, as he wrote, “killed eighty of their men, exploded the fortress and beheaded their Captain”. [n65 §2] Then came the assaults on the fort itself. Kelkar, from the Peshwa’s day-books, counts three, all with ladders in the monsoon: on 29 May Old Style (9 June New Style), on 28 June OS (9 July) and on 4 September OS (15 September); Cardim’s letters, which Pissurlencar prefers, report two, on 9 June and Sunday 15 September, the same two dates as the 1741 pamphlet. [n65 §2; n106] Kelkar’s account is the fuller. On 15 May OS (26 May) Cardim, with 300 Portuguese and 500 native soldiers, had retaken the Maratha post at Dharavi, killing forty or fifty for one Portuguese dead; the Peshwa’s accounts list the wounded and dead of the Anjur Naiks, among them Fakir Mahammad of the Anjurkars and Vaijnath Parvari. [n106 §1] The first assault, at dawn on 9 June, aimed at the curtain between the bastions of Reis Magos and Ghonsal: “many Marathas died and thirty wounded were found. We took twenty-two ladders.” [n106 §2] The second, on 28 June OS in the full rain, sent four thousand men from Bahadurpur against the north wall between Ghonsal and Armazem, where “the ladders sank in the mud and would not stand”; Gunajirao Shinde, Khandoji Bagrao and Dadaji Naik Bhandari died, thirty-three bodies were left and, say the Portuguese, no Portuguese was killed. [n106 §3] The third, sent for by the Peshwa and led by Baji Bhivrao on the night of 4 September OS at eight, put three thousand men to the wall with ladders tied together; Danvers’s note counts 6,000 foot and 4,000 horse, 45 ladders and 120 bodies at the wall, Pissurlencar’s supplement 8,000 attackers, 604 bodies and 44 ladders. [n106 §5] In July the Portuguese officer Carvalho crossed the creek from the post at Murdhe and was ambushed by Maratha horse; Captain Roque Pereira de Sequeira was killed and six guns lost, and the Marathas burned the Portuguese posts at Dongri, Bainel and Murdhe. [n106 §4]

Kelkar’s Marathi account gives the same night from the other side, with names and a date that agrees (26 March Old Style, a Saturday, is 6 April 1737). [n105 §1] The Marathas who had waited in the forest under Mahuli went on the Thursday and Friday nights through the forests of Ghotwad and Tungar-Kaman, and at a council near Thana Antaji Raghunath Kavle wished to rest a day, but Bubaji Naik said that “the enemy has heard of us” and must be given no time; he had his informants in Salsette ready for that night, and the guards of the water-bastions had been given phulsara, a country liquor, “to make them careless”. At Valkhind the informants Lakshman Raut, Hansa Koli and Sahya Koli joined them, and at Kalwa a hundred men put ladders to the water-bastion called “Jeronimo”, killed a captain and six soldiers, and fired two guns as the signal; four hundred more came up at once. The Portuguese captain, Luís Botelho, sent a company with a sergeant-major to the bastion and the Marathas captured the whole company. Chimaji Appa, waiting at Badlapur, heard the guns “as the drums of victory”, reached Kalwa at sunrise, dismounted “and began himself to lay straw on the mud to put heart into the men”, and the army crossed into Salsette. Botelho, who had “only six companies of Portuguese soldiers” and an unfinished fort, was overruled by his officers, put his six companies and many families in boats, and left for Uran at two on the afternoon of Sunday 27 March (7 April). [n105 §1]

The Vasai column’s crossing was harder. Shankaraji Keshav’s men reached the Rajavali ford at night and found the Portuguese on guard: 200 men at the causeway at Gokharve, 30 at the ford, and 15 or 20 at a third post. The Sashtichi Bakhar says that a night or two earlier someone had killed villagers at Rajavali, Gokharve, Achole, Kolhe and Chinchoti, so the crossings were closed. A boat landed 150 men on the far side, but the moon set and the tide filled the creek; Shankaraji promised a gold bangle of one ser in weight to each man who would swim, and about 150 jumped in, “several lost their swords and blankets”; 250 got across, a fishing boat of Sudamba carried 150 more, and a raft of logs from Rajavali village another hundred or two. They killed the thirty men on the post, cleared the causeway, and at daybreak on Sunday 27 March the cavalry under Chimnaji Bhivrao and Barvaji Tapkir rode through into Vasai, by Papdi and Manikpur. At the lake the captain of Vasai came out with a company of grenadiers and four native companies; the Marathas waited until they were in the open, rode them down, and they “fled into the fort”. The same morning a second force took the three bastions of Sopara (thirteen guns: “all Sopara became deserted”), looked at Arnala (“a real island-fort… under a mast”) and rode to Agashi, where the Portuguese “went and sat in their ships” and two merchants’ ships were taken. On 28 March Barvaji laid the first trench at Bahadurpur; the fort’s guns killed two men at once, and in three days fifty-seven men a day were lost. Shankaraji reported that he had 2,200 men, needed twelve thousand and two hundred pioneers with tools, that the ground was beach sand and could not hold a trench, and that Dharavi across the creek fed the fort. [n105 §2–§4]

Exhaustion, 1737–1739. The Marathas besieged Mahim, Kelve and Shirgaon in October 1737 and held the Dongri hilltop and its guns until Cardim drove them off on 28 February 1738. On 17 October 1738 the Viceroy’s council counted 897 soldiers near Bassein, 521 Portuguese and 376 native, of whom 755 were fit, and 900 more in the campaign, 411 of them at Dongri. Ships from Portugal brought 478 troops on 23 and 24 October, 145 of them sick. Cardim resigned. His successor Pedro de Melo was killed by a cannon shot at Thana on 8 December 1738. Martinho de Silveira de Menezes took command on 8 January 1739; the next day the Marathas entered Mahim, with 8,000 horse under Chimaji Appa, Pilaji Jadhav and Shankarji Pant and 6,000 foot, and by the Viceroy’s council of 1 March the Mahim garrison had been beheaded. The Viceroy ordered all the forts of Bassein territory given up except Daman, Bassein, Diu, Revdanda and Karanja; the Marathas took Versova on 20 February; Pissurlencar’s earlier article says the English demolished Bandra’s fort on 5 April and that Dongri was handed over only on 25 April. [n76 §3] Meanwhile Venkatrao Ghorpade invaded Goa’s Salsette on 23 January to stop help going north, and on 6 May the Portuguese signed a treaty of friendship with the Marathas at Rai. [n65 §2]

The northern sieges of that autumn went badly for the Marathas. [n107] After the rains Shankaraji Keshav sent forces against Mahim, Kelve and Shirgaon, in the Palghar country north of Vasai. At Shirgaon, whose fort Malhar Hari described as “a quarter higher than the fort of Thana and stronger”, with four bastions, a madi and a temple, “seven guns”, and “two hundred or two hundred and fifty” men, Portuguese and Christian Bhandari and others, his first assault on 4 November (Old Style) failed because “the powder and ball given, three rounds per man, ran out”: “how can men hold with sweet weapons? There is no remedy but powder.” At Mahim the Peshwa’s general Ramchandra Hari Patwardhan took command of 12,950 men, under an order to behead runaways “be he commander or footman”, and from 17 November laid three trenches. On 7 December sixteen Portuguese galliots and a large vessel brought help from Vasai and twelve of them reached the bank; on the 24th (December, by the order of Kelkar’s days; the month is not stated), at noon, when the men in the trenches were eating, two or three thousand men came out of the fort and overran the three trenches: Ramchandra was shot through the right hand, and Mahadaji Keshav, Waghojirao Khanvilkar, Rajbarao Burudkar and some two hundred others were killed, “shut in and had no time to get out”. [n107 §2–§3] In the Portuguese forts of the north coast, Kelkar says, served “Sortis, Christian Bhandaris, Renois, Marathas and Siddis”; Mahim’s village alone had twelve hundred armed men. [n107 §3]

What Goa decided, January–March 1739. The minutes of the Goa State Council, printed by Pissurlencar in the Assentos do Conselho do Estado, show why no relief came. [n75] With the Marathas holding Goa’s own Salcete and threatening Bardez, the Viceroy asked the councillors on 29–30 January to say in writing which northern forts to give up. The Chancellor, António Freire de Andrade, voted on the 31st that “the Praça of Baçaim should be abandoned, being the only project left to the formidable power” the enemy had, since the Dongri fortification would fall and Bassein “can last only a short time for lack of provisions and relief without communication by land and sea”; the Nau Victoria was to carry its families and munitions out by February. Others would keep Bassein, Daman and Diu and abandon all the rest, or keep Diu alone. [n75 §2] In March the Marathas offered terms through the Portuguese envoy António Carneiro de Alcaçova at Rai: Bassein and Daman to be handed over, half or 40 per cent of the revenue of Goa’s Salcete and Bardez to go to the Marathas, and, in Goa itself, “the gentile rite” to be allowed as in the Bhonsle’s lands and “the gentiles not be guilty in the Inquisition”; Alcaçova put the price at twelve lakhs of rupees, “two million four hundred thousand xerafins”. [n75 §3] On 25 March the council, with a Maratha army in Salcete refusing peace, voted that “the Praça of Baçaim should be left to the enemy with the obligation of the State saving all its garrison, equipment, artillery and munitions”, the enemy evacuating Salcete and Bardez. [n75 §4] That is three weeks before Pereira’s dating of the battle from 17 April. The minutes do not say whether the garrison was told. (Inference.)

On 17 October 1738 the council had been given the detail behind the figure of 897 soldiers: sepoys were stationed at Dongri (411), “Juvem” island (77), Mahim-Kelve (144) and Karanja (78), with only 190 ready at Bassein’s own beach; the Marathas had 3,000 men at Thana, a new fort above Kelve and Shirgaon, and fortified Ghodbunder and a place called Gormal on the river between Bassein and Thana, while Manaji Angria’s three palas and the Marathas’ boats were ready to blockade the city by sea. [n75 §1] The Bombay Council had written on 3 October 1737 that the Marathas’ “apparent chief aim” was “a Sea Port for themselves”, though perhaps “hastened by the well grounded complaints of their Gentoo Brethren under the Portuguese Government” about “the Cruelty of the Inquisition”. [n75 §1]

The siege. General Silveira was killed on Wednesday 15 April 1739 by a bullet while reconnoitring from the Remédios tower, and Caetano de Sousa Pereira took his place. Pereira’s letter of 17 August 1739 describes the battle “from 17 April to its fall on 16 May”. Two lakh men were engaged in the siege, counting those behind the soldiers; in the last two days alone over 10,000 of them died. On 17 April he had warned that over 50,000 would attack the walls. Inside were 1,200 Hindus, and Pereira, with the Viceroy’s consent, turned out 1,000 of them, for fear of what they might do. [n65 §2] Chimaji had written to Gangaji Naik Anjurkar at the start that the Portuguese were not rich and, starved, would come to terms. They were starved: supplies from Goa and Daman had stopped. The Portuguese lost 500 Portuguese and 350 native soldiers; “reports of Cardim and Pereira show that 22,000 Marathas lost their lives”, and Shahu gave the same figure to an English officer at Bombay. [n65 §2] The 22,000 and the 850 are the figures the Marathi encyclopedia repeats; they are the Portuguese commanders’ estimates, confirmed by Shahu’s remark, and not counts. And the date the book has carried for the start of the siege, 17 February 1739, does not appear in this chronology: the battle of the castle itself runs from 17 April to the fall in May, and February is the month of the outposts. [n65 §2]

What the Marathas won, in the words Khobrekar takes from Danvers, was “the entire northern province from Versova to Daman with their four chief ports, three hundred forty villages” and a revenue of 2,50,000 (the OCR gives nipees, probably xerafins), and, besides Bassein, “eight cities, twenty fortresses, two fortified hills, the famous island of Salsette with the city of the same name, and the island of Arnala”. Daman, Chaul and Diu escaped. [n68 §1]

Why. The Viceroy wrote on 19 April 1737 that one reason the Hindus of Bassein had sided with the Marathas was persecution by the Inquisition, and that the captain of Salsette had told him that if they were left free to follow their religion as in Diu, they would have turned the Marathas out. Pissurlencar writes that many Hindus of Bassein joined the Marathas, though their names are not known. Marathi letters of the time give a religious motive: one writer says Chimaji invaded “because the Portuguese persecuted Hindus”, and another congratulates him that “the establishment of Hindu raj will be carried out”. A Portuguese manuscript gives territory and ports. Pissurlencar leaves both in view, and so does this book. [n65 §3]

The march-out

The last scene of Portuguese Baçaim is a procession. The garrison left with colours flying, muskets shouldered and drums beating, and went by sea to Bombay. [n04; Monteiro and da Silva, p. 154, via Battle of Vasai, Wikipedia]

The notes give no description of the faces in that column, or of the families who used their eight days to load what they could carry. It is enough to set down the route. The soldiers of the Court of the North went south across the water to the English island that had once been their own dependency, because their own side could not, or would not, take them in.

On 23 May the saffron flag was raised over the fort, and the Portuguese administration left that day. [n04; n18; Battle of Vasai, Wikipedia; FPJ] Kelkar’s Old Style dates suggest that this sentence joins two events: the Portuguese left on 23 May New Style, and the Peshwa’s day-book records the formal hoisting of the flag on 23 May Old Style, which is 3 June New Style. [n63 §1]

The dead

How many died? The Portuguese loss is given consistently as about eight hundred. [n04; n32; DC 148; BG 34]

The Maratha loss is harder. Wikipedia, citing the Maharashtra Gazetteer, says it is unknown. [n04] An earlier pass of this book’s research treated the figure of twelve thousand Maratha dead, often repeated online, as unsourced. But both da Cunha (1876) and the Bombay Gazetteer (1882) give it: the Marathas lost “about 12,000 in killed and wounded”. Both took it from the Bombay Quarterly Review. It is therefore a nineteenth-century figure, round and repeated, from writers relying on Portuguese testimony. It also counts the wounded as well as the dead. [n32; DC 148; BG 34; Summary of corrections] The honest statement is this: about eight hundred Portuguese died, and nineteenth-century writers put the Maratha killed and wounded at about twelve thousand, a figure no one has verified.

A few physical traces of the siege outlasted it. In the fort’s magazine, da Cunha saw stone cannonballs about six inches across. [n32; DC 148 n.] When Anquetil-Duperron visited in 1760, the Marathas had “condemned the south-west gate”, walling up one of the openings, while the south gate stood open. [n32; BG 34 n.2] The breach at São Sebastião became, in time, part of the ruin.

The churches of the countryside

The surrender protected five churches. What happened to the others is told mostly in parish histories, which are late and sometimes contradictory, but together they draw a map of the conquest’s path.

St Michael’s at Porim, the Jesuit church of 1606, was, its parish says, “the first to be demolished by the conquering Maratha army in 1739”. [n40; EI Manikpur] Mercês was destroyed and its Augustinian priest fled; afterwards the parish was looked after from Remedy. [n40; EI Merces] At Agashi, the parish church of St James was burned in 1739, according to the heritage survey, and the Franciscan church of Our Lady of Light, older still, was gone. [n40; CRIT Vol III asset 44; EI Agashi] At Pali the Franciscans “had to leave the church when this region was captured by the Marathas in 1739”. [n40; CRIT Vol III asset 107] Nirmal’s Holy Cross was destroyed, as told above.

Others survived. St Thomas at Sandor, the mother church of the south, “escaped destruction by the Marathas in 1739”, the parish says. [n40; EI Sandor] Our Lady of Grace at Papdi, too, “unlike other churches during the invasion, … was not destroyed”. [n40; EI Papdy; CRIT sheet 40] And the church of the Holy Spirit at Nandakhal, between Agashi and Nirmal, escaped, its Franciscans leaving but the building standing. Da Cunha, in 1876, called it “the oldest among the rural churches, and the only one standing in its primitive state”. [n40; CRIT Vol III asset 60b; DC p. 161] Nandakhal’s people tell a story about why: Maratha soldiers who stormed the church retreated when a swarm of bees came out of the surrounding woods and attacked them. [n40; EI Nandkhal] That is a legend, and it is told as one. But it says something true about how the Christian villages remembered 1739: as a storm that some of their churches came through.

Why were some churches destroyed and others spared? The sources do not say. The surrender treaty’s “one in the district” may account for one survivor, but the notes do not say which church it was. The pattern, if there is one, is not explained anywhere in the research. [n40, Gaps]

“As falls a stately tree”

In 1882, the British compilers of the Thana Gazetteer wrote the epitaph that has followed the siege ever since:

“Thus Bassein fell as falls a stately tree never to rise. No fight had been more glorious to the Portuguese; in none since the days of Albuquerque had they earned more unsullied fame.” [n15; Thana Gaz. XIV, p. 34]

It is worth noticing who wrote it and when. The writers were officials of a third empire, describing the fall of the second to the first, from a ruined town their own government had leased out for sugar cane. Their sympathy runs one way, toward the defenders. The Marathas also had a story to tell about May 1739, and they told it in stone, in temples, in bells hung far inland, and in a festival that Vasai still keeps.

The tree did not rise again. Portuguese power north of Goa was finished. [n15, World-history significance] But the land under the tree was not empty. The Christian villages of Nirmal, Papdi, Agashi and Nandakhal were still there, and so were their people: the Samvedi Christians of the northern gardens, the Christian Vadval carters and gardeners of the south, the Koli fishers of Mercês. The fidalgos of the Tombo, with their three-life leases and their toddy rents, went south by sea. The farmers who had paid those rents stayed on the land. The Hindu holy places the Portuguese had torn down waited to be rebuilt. And the conquering army brought a governor who would stay on and build.

When the dust settled over the breach at São Sebastião, the Court of the North had a new name, given in honour of a Peshwa, and a new master who would rebuild its gods.
