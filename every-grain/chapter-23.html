Period covered: from the coming of the railway to November 2026

In this chapter. From a railway halt to a suburb of a million: the station, the quiet decades, the opening of the green belt and the neighbourhoods of the town.

This chapter follows N1, which told the story of Sopara, the ancient town and its island, up to the railway age. It tells how a halt on the line beside the village of Nala gave its name to one of the densest suburbs of the Mumbai region. Chapters 14, 14 and 15 tell the modern history of the whole Vasai-Virar belt year by year; this chapter keeps to Nallasopara and points back to them where an event has already been told at length.

Tractors to the station, July 2026

In the second week of July 2026 the rain did not stop for three days. Across Vasai-Virar the low ground went under. Western Railway cancelled a hundred suburban trains on the Monday and 125 on Tuesday, 7 July. In parts of Nallasopara the water stood three or four feet deep outside people’s homes, and men with tractors charged a hundred to a hundred and fifty rupees a head to carry commuters through the flood to the station. [n48 §7; FPJ, 8 Jul 2026] On one day services between Vasai Road and Virar were suspended altogether when the tracks flooded near Nallasopara. [n29 §4; FPJ, Vasai station chaos]

The general manager of Western Railway, Ramashray Pandey, came to inspect the section. He ordered a survey into whether the tracks could be raised, and told his officers to check the planned height of the new fifth and sixth lines. The newspaper reports gave the reason in two sentences. “Palghar is located at a relatively higher elevation, while Virar, Nallasopara and Vasai lie on a lower gradient.” And, of Nallasopara: “the city and the railway station are at nearly the same level.” The municipal commissioner, Prithviraj B. P., promised action against “illegal structures obstructing natural drainage”. [n48 §7; FPJ, 8 Jul 2026]

The scene holds most of this chapter’s story in a single frame. The railway, which gave the town its name and its reason to exist. The low ground, which the Gazetteer of 1882 had called a salt waste “flooded and impassable” in the rains. The buildings that had gone up on that ground in the thirty years since the green belt was opened to housing. And the hundreds of thousands of people, most of them newcomers, who depend on the trains to reach work in Mumbai and who had paid tractor drivers to get to them. [n31 §2, §3 inferences; n12 §3]

How a village of plantain driers a mile north of Sopara became that city is the subject of what follows. Much of it can be told with dates and figures. Some of the most basic facts, including the year the station opened and the meaning of the word “Nala”, cannot.

The name

Two villages, one name

The name is made of two village names. Sopara is the ancient town whose story Chapter 22 tells: Shurparaka, Soupara, Subara, Supara. Nala, or Nila, was a village about a mile north of it. [n31 §1; n40 A15]

A popular account on the news site Lokshahi says that the name arose when two villages, Nala, east of the railway line, and Sopara, west of it, “came to be treated as one region”. It gives no dates and no source. [n31 §1; Lokshahi] The geography of the older records does not quite fit that account. The Gazetteer of 1882 placed Nila “about a mile north of Sopara”, not east across the line, and Wikipedia’s modern list of localities puts “Nale Gaon”, the old Nala village, in Nallasopara West, alongside Sopara Gaon, while “More Gaon” and “Achole Gaon” are in the East. [n31 §1; n40 A15; Gaz XIV p. 387] So Nala seems to have been a western village, a neighbour of Sopara on the same side of the line. The popular story of an east-west pair may have grown up after the station divided the town into East and West. That is an inference.

What Nala was before the railway

Nala appears in five documents before the railway age, all brought together in Chapter 22. A stone “brought from Nila about a mile north of Sopara” named the Yadava king Ramchandradev, of about 1280–1298, and named Shurparak twice. [n40 A15; Gaz XIV p. 387] The Portuguese register of leases of 1727–30, the Tombo de Baçaim, listed a pacaria, an estate, called “Nala Magir”, held by Miguel Francisco Mira de Vasconcelos. [n40 A15; Tombo fl. 43] The Gazetteer of 1882 named Nala as one of the nine Bassein villages where the famous dried plantains were made, as one of four Bassein villages where bills of exchange were cashed, and (in a reading of the OCR that is uncertain) as “Nela”, one of ten trade centres of the sub-division. And some of the Lad Vani merchant families who had come from Cambay in the 1760s lived at Nala. [n40 A15; n35 §8; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 63, 292, trade chapter]

So Nala was a real village, with a garden economy, a few Gujarati merchants and a small money market. But it had no entry of its own in the Gazetteer’s Places of Interest, and one research note, searching that volume for a place called “Nala” near Sopara, found none and concluded that the Gazetteer knew no such village. A later and fuller check found the stone and the trade tables. Both readings are in the record, and the second corrects the first. [n31 §1, §2; n40 A15]

What “Nala” means

Nobody knows. The research found no reliable source for the origin of “Nala” or “Nalla”. Informal explanations take it from nala, a stream or drain, or from the name of the separate village; neither is sourced. [n31 §1, Gaps; n12 §3 Gaps; n22] A Portuguese report of 1728 complained that “the Nil hill near Sopara” had been fortified badly, and an early timetable, discussed below, called the halt “Neela”. The research notes suggest that Nil, Nila, Neela and Nala might all be one name, perhaps of the hill, perhaps of the village; but no source makes that link, and it stays a possibility. [n31 §1 inferences, §2] There is not even a standard spelling in English. A Wikipedia talk page notes that Nala and Nalla are both used; the railway writes “Nalla Sopara” and gives the station the code NSP; newspapers write Nalasopara, Nallasopara and Nala Sopara, sometimes in the same article. [n22; n31 §1; Wikipedia: Nallasopara railway station] This book uses Nallasopara for the modern town and Nala for the old village. Wikipedia gives the city’s official name as “Shurparak Nagar”, without a source, and the research could not find who coined it or when. [n31 §1, Gaps]

The station

A boon to its people

The railway came to the island in the 1860s: the Bombay, Baroda and Central India Railway, running north from Bombay through Bassein Road, at Manikpur, and on to Virar. Chapter 12 and Chapter 28 tell the story. [n05; n46 §9] When the Gazetteer was compiled in 1882, there was no station near Sopara. Its list of stations for that year (Bandra, Andheri, Goregaon, Borivli, Bhayandar, Bassein Road, Virar, Saphala, Palghar and on north) has no Nallasopara and no Naigaon. [n28; Gaz XIII-1] The Sopara entry is explicit: the town was “not more than three-quarters of a mile from the line of railway”, and yet “somewhat badly off for communications”. “A station at Sopara or a made road to the town from the Agashi high road would be a boon to its people.” [n31 §1; Gaz XIV p. 316] The writer of 1882 knew of no station there and wished for one.

“Neela (Nalasopara)”

The only clue to the station’s first name comes from a newspaper article of 12 April 2022 in the Free Press Journal, marking 155 years since the first suburban train ran between Bombay and Virar on 12 April 1867. It says that an early Back Bay to Virar service halted at “Neela or (Nalasopara)”, then Bassein, Panjo, Berewla (Borivali) and so on. The article draws its history from a 1923 issue of the BB&CI company magazine, but it does not say in so many words that the list of halts comes from that magazine, or that the list describes the service of 1867 rather than a later one. [n31 §1; n46 §9; FPJ, 12 Apr 2022] Chapter 28 opens with the same train. If the list does describe 1867, then a halt called Neela existed fifteen years before the Gazetteer wished for a station at Sopara, and the Gazetteer’s author either ignored a minor halt or did not know of it. If the list describes a later timetable, then the halt came after 1882. The research could not settle which. [n31 §1 inferences]

The disputed year

Other sources give dates that do not agree. Wikipedia’s article on the station gives its opening as 1865, sourced only to a Western Railway book, Heritage, Traditions and Legends (2009), page 213. [n31 §1; Wikipedia: Nallasopara railway station] A tourism page on the Palghar district website mentions an 1869 start of regular trains from Bombay to Bassein, Nalasopara and Virar; the research did not verify it in full. [n11 Q2; palghar.gov.in] A local history site names the early stations of the line as Naigaon, Bassein, Nalasopara and Virar, and says the GIP and BB&CI were started in 1860. [n05; Vasaiker] Against all of these stands the Gazetteer’s station list of 1882, which has no station between Bassein Road and Virar. The chronicle of the taluka compiled for this book concludes that Nallasopara and Naigaon must date from between 1883 and the 1920s or 1930s, and that no source gives the year. [n28] This book does not state 1865 as fact. The opening year of Nallasopara station, and its original name, remain unresolved; the Western Railway working timetables and the 2009 heritage book are the places to look. [n31 §1, Gaps]

Wires, fast trains and the fifth line

Electric trains reached the line north of Bombay in the 1930s; one research note records a 1936 date from Wikipedia, unverified, and the electrification of Borivali–Virar is commonly put in that decade. [n28; n05] For most of the twentieth century Virar was the end of the suburban line, and Nallasopara the last station but one. The four-tracking of Borivali–Virar was finished only in 2007. [n37 §6; TIFR via search] On 16 April 2013 the railway minister flagged off the first suburban electric service from Churchgate to Dahanu Road, carrying the network beyond Virar for the first time. [n29 §3] India’s first air-conditioned local began on Christmas Day 2017 and ran regularly between Churchgate and Virar from 1 January 2018. [n29 §3; PIB] From 1 May 2026 fifteen-coach trains ran on Virar–Dahanu. [n06 §2] The fifth and sixth lines from Borivali to Virar, meant to separate the locals from the long-distance trains, were about thirty per cent built in August 2026, with completion targeted for 2029, at a cost of ₹2,184 crore over twenty-six kilometres. [n06 §2; Curly Tales; Rail Asia] Chapter 16 and Chapter 29 tell these changes from the corridor’s point of view.

Nallasopara is served by the locals and the MEMU trains; the long-distance expresses stop at Virar and Vasai Road, not here. [n06 §2] The research found no figure for the number of passengers who use Nallasopara station each day, and no figure for deaths on the tracks between Nallasopara and Virar, though a Marathi report of November 2026 counted five deaths in twenty-four hours on the Vasai–Vaitarna stretch. [n06 §2 Gaps; n29 §4; Aapla Mahanagar]

The quiet decades

What the record shows, 1882–1988

Between the Gazetteer of 1882 and the housing boom of the late 1980s, Nallasopara is almost invisible in the sources the research could reach. The notes found no census figure for Nala or Sopara villages between 1882 and 2001, no account of when the station area began to grow, no date for the foundation of the Nallasopara municipal council, and no description of the place in the first decades after independence. [n31 §2 Gaps; n28; n06 §1 Gaps] The chronicle of the taluka from 1800 to 1989 lists the founding years of the Vasai, Virar and Nallasopara councils among the things it could not find. [n28]

What the record does show is the frame. In 1981 the whole Vasai-Virar area had fewer than 250,000 people; Vasai town had 34,900 and Virar 23,303, and only Agashi among the villages passed 10,000. Many towns in the area lost people between 1971 and 1981. [n05; n06 §1; TIFR] The 1970s regional plan treated the area as a green zone apart from the land round the stations. [n37 §6; TIFR] A student project of the Tata Institute of Social Sciences, read only through a search summary, says that zoning for industry in the 1970s led entrepreneurs in Nallasopara to build chawls for workers. [n31 §3; TISS Make Break] Chawls are rows of small rented tenements; a later student study calls the older ones of Nallasopara East “low-rise baithi chawls”. [n31 §3; TU Delft repository] The same project describes a settlement at Makarandnagar where many residents had been displaced from the Sanjay Gandhi National Park, the forest at Mumbai’s northern edge, though it does not date their arrival. [n31 §3; TISS]

The economy round the station in these decades was still mostly the garden economy of the Gazetteer: plantains, betel, vegetables, flowers and rice, sent to Bombay now by train, and in the low ground to the south of the station, salt. The city plan of the 2000s still found “low-lying lands … marshy, khazan lands and some of them mainly to the south of Nallasopara Railway Station are still used for salt cultivation”. [n48 §2; CDP pp. 24–28] Farmers and vendors carried their produce on the local trains to the Bombay markets. [n05]

9 October 1989

The station enters the national record first through a killing. On 9 October 1989 a builder named Suresh Dube, aged thirty-one, was shot at point-blank range in daylight on the platform at Nallasopara. Prosecutors alleged that the killers were linked to the gang of Jayendra “Bhai” Thakur, brother of the man who would win the Vasai assembly seat the next year. Seventeen people were arrested in 1992. The case went through the TADA courts and up to the Supreme Court and back; on 24 May 2023 a special TADA court in Pune acquitted Bhai Thakur and others for lack of evidence, in what the Free Press Journal called the last TADA case in India, and Dube’s daughter said the family would appeal. [n29 §1, §4; n26 §2; FPJ, 24 May 2023; Lokmat Times] No one has been convicted of the murder in the sources read. Chapter 16 tells the story of the trial. That a builder should be killed on the platform of this station in 1989, a year after the green belt was opened to housing, is a fact the chronology places side by side; no source draws a causal line between them.

1988: the green belt opens

The decision

In August 1988 the state government of Maharashtra reclassified much of the Vasai-Virar green belt for residential use, without the infrastructure to support it, and against the advice of the Mumbai regional planning authority. [n29 §1; n06 §1; n05; Sabrang; TIFR] On 14 May 1990 CIDCO, the state’s town-planning company, was appointed planning authority for the Vasai-Virar sub-region. [n29 §1, §5; VVCMC DCR] Chapter 15 tells the story of the green-belt fight that followed: the Harit Vasai Saurakshan Samiti, formed in 1989 under Fr Francis D’Britto; the rally of 35,000 in October 1989; the rally of about 100,000 at the Vasai grounds on 26 January 1992; the ban on water tankers drawing from the green belt. [n29 §1; Sabrang]

For Nallasopara the important part of that story is a single sentence in the Sabrang account of the movement: in the early 1990s, housing colonies spread near the stations at Vasai, Naigaon, “Nala-Sopara” and Virar. [n29 §1; Sabrang] Builders drew groundwater for the new buildings, salinity rose, and farm produce suffered. On 20 May 1991 a Times of India report described violence against the area’s Christian residents, linked to the well-water and tanker agitation. [n29 §1; Sabrang]

How fast

The numbers came quickly. By 1991 the population of the whole Vasai-Virar area had risen about 150 per cent in a decade, “entirely due to residential usage by people commuting to work in Mumbai”, and the census showed growth of 290 per cent for Vasai, Virar, Sopara and Sandor together. [n05; n35 §9; TIFR] The census totals for the Vasai-Virar city area run 219,868 in 1981, 365,480 in 1991, 693,350 in 2001 and, depending on the boundary used, 1,222,390 or 1,343,402 in 2011. [n06 §1; Wikipedia: VVCMC]

A page written in 1996 by the Tata Institute of Fundamental Research’s “Bombay” project described what the new residents found: electricity two days a week, no piped water or central drainage, less than ten per cent of the area on motorable roads, and as little as ten litres of water per person per day. [n49; TIFR] That description is of the whole region, not Nallasopara alone, but there is no reason to think Nallasopara was better off.

The town counted

2001: 184,664

The first firm figure for Nallasopara itself is from the 2001 census: Nala Sopara, 184,664 people. [n31 §1, §3; n29 §2; Wikipedia: Nallasopara] Wikipedia’s account adds that minority languages spoken included Hindi, by 22.92 per cent, Gujarati, by 17.82 per cent, and Urdu, by 12 per cent. [n31 §3] Those three figures alone, if they are right, mean that more than half the town did not speak Marathi, the language of the state and of the old villages, as its first language. The same article lists Chakreshwar lake in Nallasopara West among its sights, and the Bhatela pond, with “remains of a Portuguese jetty and customs house”. [n31 §3]

2011: half a million

The 2011 census did not count Nallasopara separately. By then the town lay inside the Vasai-Virar City Municipal Corporation, which the census treated as a single urban unit of 1,222,390 people in 291,229 households. [n12 §1; n11 Q1] A student project at the Delft University of Technology, read only through a search summary, puts Nallasopara’s 2011 population at about 564,664, on forty-three square kilometres, against about 1.22 million for Vasai-Virar on 311 square kilometres. [n31 §3; TU Delft repository] If that figure is right, Nallasopara had about three times its 2001 population, and held nearly half the people of the corporation on about a seventh of its land. The figure comes from student work that the research did not open, and should be read with that caution. No later count exists: the census due in 2021 was not held. [n06 §1]

For the corporation as a whole, the 2011 census recorded religion as about 77 to 78 per cent Hindu, 8.5 to 9 per cent Muslim, 8.25 per cent Christian, 2.5 to 2.65 per cent Buddhist and 1.75 to 1.9 per cent Jain, depending on the unit; mother tongues as Marathi 46.5 per cent, Hindi 26.6, Gujarati 9.3, Bhojpuri 3.1 and Urdu 2.7. [n06 §1; n08; n49 §1h; n35 §9] The notes link the Hindi, Bhojpuri and Urdu shares to migration from Uttar Pradesh and Bihar, concentrated in Nallasopara and Virar, but no source separates migrants by state. [n08; n35 §9; n06 §1 Gaps] The corporation’s own website projected its population at 19.85 lakh, nearly two million, at the time of the research; that is a projection, not a count. [n40 §2; VVCMC]

The council and the corporation

At a date the research could not find, Nallasopara became a municipal council, a nagar parishad; neither the notification nor the names of its presidents turned up in any source. [n31 §1 Gaps; n28] On 3 July 2009 the Nallasopara council was merged with the councils of Vasai, Virar and Navghar-Manikpur and fifty-three villages to form the Vasai-Virar City Municipal Corporation. Some sources give 2010; this book follows the corporation’s own website. [n12 §1; n29 §2, §5; VVCMC] Chapter 16 tells how the corporation was formed and fought over: forty-nine of the villages opposed their inclusion, the state moved to exclude twenty-nine of them in 2011, and the matter went to the courts. [n29 §2, §3; n40 §2]

East and West

The line that divides the town

Every account of modern Nallasopara divides it in two: Nallasopara West and Nallasopara East, on either side of the railway. The postal service does the same. Nallasopara West has the PIN code 401203; Nallasopara East shares 401209 with Vasai Road East. [n12 §1; Wikipedia: Vasai-Virar]

The division is older than the town. The railway embankment of the 1860s ran along the eastern edge of the old garden island, on or near the line of the Sopara creek, so that the island’s villages lay to the west of it and the salt flats, rice land and hills to the east. [n31 §2; n12 §3 inferences] The research notes put the contrast plainly, as their own synthesis and not a sourced statement: the West is the old garden-and-Sopara side; the East, Achole, Tulinj, Santosh Bhuvan, was salt waste and rice flats below Tulinj hill, cheap land that later filled with dense, often unauthorised housing. [n12 §3 inferences]

The West holds what N1 described: Sopara gaon with its market and dargahs, the stupa at Mardes, Chakreshwar lake, the Kupari villages of Gass, Nirmal, Umrale and Wagholi, and Nale Gaon. [n31 §1; n40] The East holds Achole, Tulinj, More Gaon, Santosh Bhuvan, Shirdi Nagar, Pragati Nagar and Agarwal Nagari. [n31 §1; n12 §3] The planners’ figures for the whole sub-region underline the difference between old and new ground: the coastal villages, with 77,739 people in 2001, were “more or less stagnant”, while the station towns grew. [n48 §8; CDP p. 46]

The ground

The whole plain is low. The city development plan of the late 2000s gave the area’s average elevation as 1.5 to 2 metres above sea level, and the coastal strip as “hardly 3 to 4 ft. above sea level”. [n48 §2; VVSR CDP pp. 24–25] The Gazetteer of 1882 described the coast belt of rice-land as “completely … intersected by salt water channels”, with a large area “still flooded at spring tides”. [n48 §1; Gaz XIII-1] The railway’s inspectors in 2026 found Nallasopara station and the town round it “at nearly the same level”. [n48 §7] East of the line the ground rises to the hills: Tulinj, the Rakshi Dongar of the Gazetteer, with its basalt dyke; and further east and north, the hills the Gazetteer named “Baronda, Jivdhan and Nilemora”. [n12 §3; n48 §2; V1] The water from those hills runs west to the old creek in drains that the pollution board names the Pelhar river, the Sopara Nallah, the Tungar Nallah and the Waliv Nallah. [n48 §2; MPCB 2019]

The neighbourhoods

The research notes are frank that for most of Nallasopara’s neighbourhoods they found no sourced history at all: no founding date, no account of who settled them or why, no description beyond a name in a flood report or an address. [n31 §3 Gaps; n12 §3 Gaps] What follows is everything the sources say, neighbourhood by neighbourhood, and an honest statement of where they say nothing.

Achole

Achole has the longest record of any place in Nallasopara East. In 1882 it was “Achola, a mile and a half to the north of Manikpur”, where visitors were sent “to see its Portuguese fort”, and the road to Tungar passed “between the villages of Achola and Rajavli to Gokirva”. About half a mile south of Bab Naik’s house at Gass, on the way to Achole, ran the dagdi bandh, the old raised footway of basalt blocks across the mud. [n12 §3; n42 §1; Gaz XIV] A “stone dam” of basalt stood near it. [n31 §2] And Achole was one of the Bassein villages of Urap Agris, converts who went back to Hinduism in 1820 and 1828. [n40 §1; n49 §1e; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 116–117] So Achole has been a fort, a convert village and a reconverted one, a stop on the way to Tungar, and the far end of an old causeway.

Today the name covers a large part of Nallasopara East. The forty-one buildings of Agarwal Nagari stood on survey numbers 22 to 30 at Agarwal Nagar, Achole. [n06 §4; Land Conflict Watch] The Achole police arrested the builder of those buildings in 2022. [n06 §4] In July 2018 residents of Achole Road were stranded for five days, and an Achole gaon resident told the DNA newspaper that water had entered his hall, kitchen and bedroom. [n31 §3; NewsBytes; DNA] In March 2026 the corporation’s budget for 2026–27 included a 400-bed hospital at Achole. [n48 §8; FPJ, 11 Mar 2026] “Achole Gaon”, the old village, is listed separately among the localities of the East. [n31 §1]

Tulinj

Tulinj is named after the hill the Gazetteer called “Rakshi Dongar, or Tulinj hill”, about a mile north of Achole, with its basalt dyke of six-sided pillars “used when the railway was made”, and a circle of upright pillars across a ravine. [n31 §2; n12 §3] The hill gave stone to the line that made the town. The Tulinj police station went under about two feet of water in a flood, and Tulinj Road was submerged in July 2018 and again on 1 July 2026. [n31 §3; n48 §7; Lokmat Times; FPJ] On Tulinj Road stands the Shri Munisuvrat Swami Jain Derasar and Upashray. [n10 §3] Nothing else about the neighbourhood’s history was found.

Santosh Bhuvan, Shirdi Nagar and Pragati Nagar

These are among the best-known names of Nallasopara East, and the research found nothing sourced about any of them: not their origins, their dates, or the communities who live there. [n12 §3 Gaps; n31 §3 Gaps] None of the three names appears in the Gazetteer of 1882 or in the Portuguese register, so they belong to the modern town; who chose them, and when, no source says. They need the local Marathi and Hindi press, which the research could not reach in a useful way. [n12 §3 Gaps]

Nilemore

Nilemore carries an older name. The Gazetteer of 1882 described, north of the flat Tungar range, “a cluster of hills of which Baronda, Jivdhan and Nilemora are the most marked peaks”, and its Part II volume gave “Nilemora, Baronde and Jivdhan” heights of 1,500 to 2,000 feet, a round figure for the group. [n48 §2; V1; Gaz XIII-1; Gaz XIII-2 pp. 678–680] Whether the modern neighbourhood of Nilemore takes its name from that hill, the notes do not say; it is a natural guess, and only a guess. In 2026 a Hindi report described a ₹222-crore plan for flood-prevention water-retention ponds at Nilemore and at Gogte kharbhumi, salt land, with a tourism component; its status is unverified. [n42 §6; n48 §5; Navbharat Live] No history of Nilemore as a settlement was found. [n12 §3 Gaps]

Umrale

Umrale is an old Kupari village on the western side, the Umbrala of the Gazetteer, in the north of the “garden or agar of Sopara”, and one of the nine villages where the rajele plantains were dried. [n40 A9; n11 Q2; Gaz XIV p. 314; Gaz XIII-1 p. 292] Mass was said there from 1949 under the Nandakhal parish, and Umrale became an independent parish, St Joseph’s, in 1970. [n40 A9; Wikipedia: Holy Spirit Church Nandakhal] The heritage survey of 2008 lists the Kudhvat Talav and the Umbrale Talav, where Ganesh images are immersed at the festival, a Hindu use of a lake in a Christian village. [n40 A9; CRIT Vol I items 65–66] In June 2026 the Diocese of Vasai opened a pilgrim centre for the Year of St Francis at Umrale. [K7; n38] Whether Umrale counts as part of Nallasopara or of Virar depends on the map; the notes place it in the old Sopara garden, north of Gass.

Nirmal

Nirmal is the religious centre of the western villages for Hindus and Christians alike, and Chapter 22 and Chapter 36 describe it: the samadhi temple of a Shankaracharya on its wooded hill, with the November fair on Kartik Vadya 11 that in 1882 drew six or seven thousand pilgrims, “Hindus, Musalmans, Christians, and a few Parsis”; the eight temples Shankarji Keshav built about 1750; and Holy Cross church, mother parish of the Kupari villages round Sopara. [n11 Q2; n40 A1; Gaz XIV pp. 292–293] In 2008 the heritage survey listed a Nirmal Bazaar, dated 1957, in the Vagholi gram panchayat. [n40 A1; CRIT item 69] The temple hill is now reached from “Nirmal naka”. [n11 Q2] The research found no account of how far the suburb has reached into Nirmal by 2026. [n40 A1]

Gass

Gass, a mile south of Sopara, is the subject of Chapter 26. In Nallasopara’s modern story it appears as a largely Catholic Kupari garden village, nicknamed “Mini Goa” by the Hindustan Times “for the ancestral mansions that dot the surrounding farmland”, with “Gass Road” and “Mothi Sargodi Road” listed as localities of Nallasopara West, the church of St Gonsalo Garcia, attacked by vandals in August 2023, and a lake and farmland that its people defended against a dumping ground in 2025. [n42 §6; K7] That fight is told below.

Sopara gaon

Sopara village, the heart of the ancient town, is a locality of Nallasopara West, with its Burhanpur Chowk market, its dargahs, its Zilla Parishad school and its talavs along the Sopara–Gass road. Chapter 22 describes it. [n40 A15; n31 §1]

The flood-report neighbourhoods

Many of Nallasopara’s neighbourhoods appear in the sources only as places that went under water. In July 2018, Vishal Nagar, H and C Colony, Vidyamandir Road, Tulinj Road and Achole were submerged, and residents of Achole Road and Chheda Nagar were stranded for five days. [n31 §3; Lokmat Times; NewsBytes] On 1 July 2026 Tulinj, Vijay Nagar, Dhaniv Baug, Chulne, Bangli Road, Devtalav and Giriz were flooded across the Nallasopara and Vasai divisions. [n48 §7; FPJ] A 2025 report described Rahmat Nagar as dotted with substandard illegal buildings. [n31 §3; FPJ] The TISS project mentions Makarandnagar, whose residents included families displaced from the Sanjay Gandhi National Park, and Sai Society, where scrap dealers and recyclers all came from one village in eastern Uttar Pradesh. [n31 §3; n08; n35 §9; TISS] For none of these was any founding history found.

Agarwal Nagari

Agarwal Nagari, also called Lakshmi Nagar, in Achole, is the neighbourhood that the whole state came to know in 2024 and 2025, and it has a section of its own below.

Migrants and chawls

Who came

The people who filled Nallasopara came from everywhere. The notes can name some of the streams, but not measure them.

They came from Mumbai first. The TISS project says that after 2000 landowners in Nallasopara converted farmland into chawls for migrants, “many from Mumbai”, and the Tata Institute of Fundamental Research described the 1980s growth of the whole region as “entirely due to residential usage by people commuting to work in Mumbai”. [n31 §3; n06 §1; TISS; TIFR] Housing prices in Mumbai pushed lower- and middle-income families north to Virar, Vasai and Nallasopara. [n37 §6; Manickpur.com]

They came from Uttar Pradesh and Bihar: the Hindi, Bhojpuri and Urdu speakers whom the 2001 and 2011 censuses counted, and whom the notes describe as concentrated in Nallasopara and Virar. [n08; n31 §3; n35 §9] The scrap dealers of Sai Society, all from one village in eastern UP, are the only group the sources trace to a single origin. [n08; TISS] They came from Gujarat and Rajasthan: the Gujarati speakers, 17.82 per cent of Nallasopara in 2001, and the Marwari speakers counted in the taluka in 2011. [n31 §3; n35 §9] And some came from inside the Mumbai region, from the slums of the National Park at Makarandnagar. [n31 §3]

None of this is new in kind. The Gazetteer of 1882 listed the “recent settlers” of the Thana district: Upper India Pardeshis working as messengers and servants, market gardeners, Sindhi sweet-makers, Gujarat Brahmins and traders, Marwar Vanis. [n35 §9; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 64–65] The Lad Vanis had come by sea from Cambay to Sopara and Nala in the 1760s. [n35 §8] The research notes call the modern arrivals from Uttar Pradesh, Bihar and the south “the third wave”, after the sea-borne Gujarat wave and the Maratha-era wave of Deccan Brahmins. [n35 §9 inferences] What is new is the scale: a village of plantain driers became a town of hundreds of thousands in a generation.

The research found no census migration tables for Vasai-Virar, no study of where Nallasopara’s people came from by state or district, and no oral history of the first families to settle in the new colonies of the East. [n06 §1 Gaps; n35 §9 Gaps] Their story is told here in categories because the sources give nothing else.

How they were housed

The housing came in layers. First, in the 1970s, chawls for workers built by entrepreneurs after the industrial zoning. Then, from the late 1980s, housing colonies near the station. After 2000, landowners turning farmland into chawls. [n31 §3; n29 §1; TISS; Sabrang] By the 2010s, the TU Delft project describes the low-rise baithi chawls of Nallasopara East being replaced by buildings of ground plus four storeys. [n31 §3; TU Delft repository] And through all of it, buildings put up without permission, on land the plans had reserved for other uses, on ground that flooded. The TISS project notes that low-lying, flood-prone land overlapped the zones marked for building. [n31 §3; TISS]

Shared toilets were part of chawl life, and in 2020 they became a public-health crisis: the Deccan Herald called Nallasopara the worst-affected town in the belt during the first wave of COVID-19, with shared toilets worsening the spread. [n29 §4; Deccan Herald] Chapter 16 tells the pandemic in the corporation as a whole: the six-fold rise in cases in forty days, the 55 per cent positivity rate in April 2020, the seven ventilators and two ICU beds in the corporation’s hospitals on 6 May, the fourteen-day lockdown of eighteen areas in June. [n29 §4]

By 2026, listing sites gave asking prices for flats in Nallasopara East of about ₹10,103 a square foot, higher than the Virar average; the sites disagree with one another, and these are asking prices, not sales. [n48 §8; Squareyards] Who could afford them, and who still lived in the chawls, the notes do not say.

Agarwal Nagari

Forty-one buildings

On about thirty acres at Agarwal Nagar, Achole, survey numbers 22 to 30, private builders put up forty-one residential buildings. The land, the municipal plan said, was reserved for a sewage treatment plant and a dumping ground. [n06 §4; n12 §3; Land Conflict Watch; Lokmat Times] The sources differ on when the buildings went up: Land Conflict Watch says from 2006; ThePrint says between 2009 and 2012, and that about thirty of the sixty acres reserved were public, the rest private. [n31 §3; n29 §2; n48 §8; ThePrint] The buyers, ThePrint reported, were “daily wage earners, labourers, and lower-middle-class families”. [n48 §8]

The man named as their builder was Sitaram Gupta, a former corporator. In September 2022 the Achole police arrested him for land-grabbing and for building the forty-one. That was an arrest, not a conviction. [n06 §4; n26 §4; Land Conflict Watch] Gupta, for his part, disputed that the land had been reserved at all. [n12 §3; Lokmat Times]

The court and the bulldozers

The Bombay High Court declared the buildings “wholly illegal and unauthorised” and, according to ThePrint, ordered their demolition on 8 July 2024. [n06 §4; n48 §8] Residents received eviction notices in October 2024. The High Court refused them relief, and the Supreme Court dismissed their special leave petition. [n12 §3; n06 §4; Land Conflict Watch; PropNewsTime] Five days after the assembly election of 23 November 2024, on 28 November, demolition began with seven structures. The second phase, of thirty-four buildings, began on 23 January 2025; one report counted thirty-four brought down in a single day. By February 2025 all forty-one were gone, after an operation of twenty-two days. [n06 §4; n12 §3; Lokmat Times; ANI via LatestLY; PropNewsTime]

How many people lost their homes is not agreed. Land Conflict Watch’s headline said 8,000 residents faced eviction; its own text and other reports gave about 2,000; one report said “over 3,000 families”; ThePrint said about 2,100 families were affected. A Hindi report headlined demolition “without rehabilitation”, and ThePrint found no rehabilitation policy framed. [n06 §4; n12 §3; n48 §8; Land Conflict Watch; Ghar.tv; CSR Journal] The families had bought flats in buildings that the courts then found should never have stood. The sources describe no compensation paid to them.

Chapter 16 tells the demolitions in the corporation’s wider story. What makes them Nallasopara’s story is the ground they stood on: the old salt waste of the Gazetteer, east of the line, below Tulinj hill, where land was cheap, plans were weakly enforced and thousands of families needed somewhere to live within reach of the trains.

Not the only ones

Agarwal Nagari was the largest case, not the only one. A 2025 report described Rahmat Nagar in Nallasopara as dotted with substandard illegal buildings, and a tilted building in Nallasopara forced the evacuation of three structures, leaving hundreds homeless. [n31 §3; FPJ] In February 2026 a mob halted a demolition drive in Nallasopara, assaulted civic staff and blocked the bulldozers; more than thirty people were booked. [n02 §1 Gaps; Swarajya] And on 27 August 2025, in Virar East, the rear wing of Ramabai Apartment, an allegedly unauthorised building put up in 2011–12, collapsed at five past midnight, killing seventeen people, among them a one-year-old girl whose first birthday party was going on, and her parents. Chapter 29 tells that story; it belongs to Nallasopara’s too, as the cost of the same way of building. [n06 §4; V3]

The ED case

The allegations

In 2025 the Enforcement Directorate, the central agency that investigates money laundering, took up the Agarwal Nagari case. In May 2025 it raided officials of the municipal town-planning department and others, and said it had seized about ₹32 crore; it alleged a fraud on the order of ₹9,000 crore. On 3 July 2025 it issued a press release on searches in what it called the Jayesh Mehta case. [n48 §8; ThePrint; ED press release, 3 Jul 2025] On 13 August 2025 it arrested four men: Anil Pawar, who had been municipal commissioner from January 2022 to July 2025; Y. S. Reddy, the suspended deputy director of town planning; Sitaram Gupta; and Arun Gupta. [n06 §4; n29 §4; FPJ; News On AIR] Sources differ on the date of Pawar’s arrest (July or 13 August) and on one sum (₹169 crore in one report). [n29 §4]

The ED’s case, as reported, was that dummy builders had been used to put up the buildings; that approval documents had been fabricated; that commissions had been collected through a coded system in which “C” stood for the Commissioner; and, in its chargesheet, that Pawar had been the “chief controller” of illegal construction. [n06 §4; n26 §3; Deccan Herald; Tribune] These are the agency’s allegations. They are not findings of any court.

The court

In October 2025 a bench of the Bombay High Court led by the Chief Justice, with Justice Gautam Ankhad, held Pawar’s arrest illegal, called the ED’s stand “speculative and based on hazy facts”, ordered his release, and refused the ED’s request to stay the order. [n29 §4; UNI; FPJ; Deccan Chronicle; National Herald] The month comes from search summaries, not from the judgment. What happened next, whether the ED appealed, how its chargesheet stands, whether the others arrested were released, the research could not find. [n06 §7 Gaps; n29 §4] As of November 2026, therefore, no one has been convicted in the sources read of anything to do with the forty-one buildings, and the most senior official accused has had his arrest declared illegal. The families who lived in them have lost their homes.

The dumping ground that moved on paper

The land at Agarwal Nagari had been reserved for a dumping ground and a sewage plant. After the buildings came down, the reservation was to move. In 2025 the corporation proposed shifting the dumping-ground and sewage-plant reservation from “Agarwal Nagar (Vasai East)”, as one report put it, to a plot at Gass, the Kupari garden village a mile south of Sopara. [n42 §6; newsminimalist summary; HT via Magzter] The reports do not say in so many words that the reservation was the same one; the descriptions match, and Chapter 26 reads them as the same. [K7]

In March 2025 the people of Gass filed more than 2,500 objections. The plot was reported as “1,500 acres”, a figure that looks implausibly large and should be checked. The Hindustan Times report, read only through a reprint, carried the headline “No dumping ground in our midst”. In October 2025 the corporation withdrew its application; residents still wanted a formal commitment that the plan would not return, and the reports found do not say whether they got one. [n42 §6; K7] Chapter 26 tells the fight from inside the village.

Seen from the whole of Nallasopara, the episode has a shape. A reservation for the city’s waste lay on the cheap eastern land; it was built over by one set of people, who were then evicted; and it was moved on paper to the old western garden land, where another set of people, an old community with a church, a lake and ancestral houses, fought it off. Where the waste of a city that the corporation projects at nearly two million people will go is a question the corporation still has to answer. The notes record that in 2019 the city was dumping 630 tonnes of solid waste a day and treating only 30 of the 105 million litres of sewage it produced daily, at one plant at Bolinj, with seven more planned. [n48 §2; MPCB 2019] A landfill at Gokhivare or Kolhi was not researched. [n06 §4 Gaps]

Floods

The same ground

Nallasopara floods because of where it is. The 1882 Gazetteer described the way from Manikpur to Sopara across “a salt waste” that flooded and became impassable in the rains, and the coast belt as “intersected by salt water channels”, much of it “flooded at spring tides”. [n31 §1, §3 inferences; n48 §1] The research notes’ plain observation is that the same eastern low ground where flooding recurs is the ground the Gazetteer described; “the geography has not changed, only the buildings on it”. [n31 §3 inferences]

The causes are named in the sources. Western Railway blamed the flooding of the Vasai–Virar tracks on construction over salt pans, mangroves and waterholes in Nallasopara and Virar, on choked culverts, and on overflow from the Pelhar dam; it said recommendations made by NEERI and IIT Bombay after the 2018 floods had not been implemented. [n06 §2; n17; PropNewsTime] Environmentalists blamed the reclamation of wetlands. [n48 §7] The Harit Vasai movement says about half of Vasai’s land is low-lying or marshy. [n06 §4] The old creek that once drained the island was “dry” at Sopara by 1882, and the drains of 2026 are nallahs whose waters the pollution board measured, in 2019, at a biochemical oxygen demand of 76 milligrams a litre in the Sopara Nallah, against 98.6 in the Waliv drain and 26.6 in the Tungar drain. [n48 §2; MPCB 2019]

Year by year

The great Mumbai flood of 26 July 2005 is the one Vasai residents still call the benchmark, but the research found no Vasai or Nallasopara death toll or rainfall figure for it. [n29 §2]

In July 2018 the belt was, in the Deccan Herald’s account, the worst hit in the Mumbai region, with water in first-floor flats, the tracks at Nallasopara flooded and the Navy evacuating commuters; Palghar district had 240 millimetres in twenty-four hours against a high tide. Residents of Achole Road and Chheda Nagar in Nallasopara were stranded for five days. Vishal Nagar, H and C Colony, Vidyamandir Road, Tulinj Road and Achole went under. The Tulinj police station stood in about two feet of water, and the tracks at Nallasopara station were submerged. [n31 §3; n48 §7; NewsBytes; Lokmat Times; Deccan Herald; India TV]

In 2019 a Deccan Herald snippet reported more than 500 millimetres in thirty-six hours on the township and flash flooding on the Nallasopara tracks; in early August services between Vasai and Virar were suspended. [n48 §7] On 19 and 20 August 2025, under a red alert, waterlogging between Nallasopara and Vasai stations and point failures at Vasai junction disrupted the trains, and 497 people in the corporation were moved to safety. [n29 §4; n48 §7; Deccan Herald; FPJ]

On 1 July 2026 an average of 78.25 millimetres fell across the corporation’s divisions, and Tulinj, Vijay Nagar, Dhaniv Baug and other places flooded. [n48 §7; FPJ] The rains of 4 to 8 July were worse: ten rain-related deaths in Palghar district since 1 July, nine reported in Vasai-Virar by 13 July, more than 1,200 people in relief centres, NDRF and SDRF teams deployed, the tracks under water near Nallasopara, and the tractors to the station with which this chapter began. [n29 §4; n48 §7; FPJ; The Quint] On 13 September 2026, on the eve of Ganpati, overnight rain flooded Nallasopara East’s flower market and main market near the station, under an orange alert. [n29 §4; FPJ, 13 Sep 2026]

What is being done

The responses in the sources are plans. The railway ordered a survey into raising the tracks. [n48 §7] The commissioner promised action against structures blocking drainage. [n48 §7] A report described the ₹222-crore retention ponds at Nilemore and Gogte. [n42 §6] Whether any of it has been built, the notes do not say.

Water

For a town that floods every monsoon, Nallasopara has been short of water to drink for most of its modern life. In the 1990s, builders drew groundwater and tankers drew from the wells of the green belt until the Harit Vasai movement won a ban; in 1996 the TIFR page described as little as ten litres a person a day across the region. [n29 §1; n49; Sabrang; TIFR] Environmental papers of 2014 and 2017 for the Surya water project said most residents of the corporation relied on tanker water, and listed the existing supply: the old Pelhar scheme at 14 million litres a day, Usgaon at 24.5, and smaller sources. [n29 §3; n12 §7; Surya project summary]

The Surya Regional Bulk Water Supply Scheme of the Mumbai regional authority, with 185 million litres a day allotted to the corporation, was commissioned in June 2023, reached Vasai-Virar in November 2023 and was dedicated by the Prime Minister on 12 January 2024. [n06 §4; Indian Infrastructure] By the most recent figures in the notes the corporation received about 230 million litres a day against a need of about 372, by an MLA’s estimate; a dam at Kholsapada, more than eighty per cent built, was expected to add 22, and the Deharji project 190. [n29 §4; n12 §9; ThePrint; FPJ; PropNewsTime] On 15 November 2026 a Marathi report warned that reservoir levels were falling, scarcity was feared, and the region’s historic lakes were choked with garbage and water hyacinth. [n06 §4; eSakal] The notes found no figure for supply to Nallasopara alone, and no account of which of its neighbourhoods still depend on tankers. [n06 §4 Gaps]

Crime, policing and a name in the headlines

The 2018 case

In August 2018 the name Nallasopara reached the national press for a reason that had nothing to do with floods or buildings. What follows is a summary of a prosecution that had not ended when this book was written, and every statement about the accused is an allegation.

The Maharashtra Anti-Terrorism Squad raided the home and shop of Vaibhav Raut, a man of about forty described as a member, or coordinator, of the Hindu Govansh Raksha Samiti, a cow-protection group, in Nallasopara. It said it seized twenty crude bombs (reports split them as twelve at his home and eight at his shop), two gelatin sticks, four electronic and twenty-two non-electronic detonators and 150 grams of explosive powder; later reports spoke of about ten or eleven pistols. Raut and Sharad Kalaskar were arrested at Nallasopara; Sudhanva Gondhalekar of Pune and Shrikant Pangarkar were also held. Charges were brought under the anti-terror law (UAPA) and the Arms and Explosives Acts. The ATS alleged a plot against the Sunburn music festival in Pune, and reports linked the accused to the Sanatan Sanstha and the Shiv Pratishthan. A chargesheet of December 2018 named twelve accused, and the National Investigation Agency later took over. Raut’s associates said he was framed, and the Hindu Janajagruti Samiti and the Sanatan Sanstha alleged a frame-up; those are contested claims. Totals of weapons and accused conflict across reports. [n31 §3; n06 §4; n26 §4; DNA; The Wire; Madhyamam; Business Standard/PTI; Tribune] The Bombay High Court later granted Raut bail after more than five years in jail, noting that the trial was unlikely to end soon. [n06 §4; FPJ] No verdict was found in the sources. Chapter 16 tells the case in the same terms.

The research notes make a point worth keeping: the national press tied the name Nallasopara to this case, but the sources describe suspects arrested there, not a crime record of the town. [n31 §3 inferences] The town’s name in the headlines and the town’s own life are different things.

Policing

In October 2020 the state created the Mira-Bhayandar Vasai-Virar Police Commissionerate, with thirteen police stations, taking the area out of the rural district police. Reports give its detection rate as 75 per cent in 2021 and over 85 per cent in 2022. [n47; FPJ] The Achole police station, which arrested Sitaram Gupta in 2022, and the Tulinj police station, which flooded in 2018, are Nallasopara’s two that appear in the sources. [n06 §4; n31 §3] The research found no crime statistics for Nallasopara itself. [n31 §3 Gaps] On counting day in November 2024 the Free Press Journal reported BJP–BVA tension in Nallasopara, after the BVA had accused the BJP leader Vinod Tawde of handing out cash for votes; that was the BVA’s allegation, not a finding. [n26 §2; FPJ]

Markets, schools and hospitals

Markets

Sopara had a crowded weekly market in 1882, one of four in the Bassein sub-division, and a broad market place lined with carved wooden houses. [n46 §6; Gaz XIV pp. 314–315] The heritage survey of 2008 listed the Burhanpur Chowk market precinct at Sopara. [n40 A15] For the modern town the sources give one market by name: Nallasopara East’s flower market and main market near the station, which flooded on 13 September 2026, the night before Ganpati, when the flowers brought in for the festival stood in the water. [n29 §4; FPJ, 13 Sep 2026] The flower farms of the coast, at Arnala, Agashi and some twenty-five villages along an eighteen-kilometre stretch from Arnala to Vasai, peak at Ganesh Chaturthi, Diwali and Navratri, and their growers name price swings of up to twenty-fold as their great hardship. [n17; Vazhacharickal & Buerkert 2011; 101Reporters] The research found no other description of Nallasopara’s markets, shops or bazaars, and no account of its small industries, though the district says Vasai, Virar and Nallasopara have small and large industrial areas. [n47; Palghar district] The city’s own plan says about sixty per cent of its workers commute to Mumbai, and calls the area “a growing dormitory town to the Mumbai city”. [n48 §8; CDP p. 23]

Schools

The schools of Nallasopara that the sources name are the old ones of the western villages. Sopara has a Zilla Parishad school. [n40 A15; CRIT] Gass has the St Gonsalo Garcia Primary School, which Bishop Simon Almeida attended in the 1960s. [n42 §5, §6] Nirmal has Holy Cross High School, where he went on to study, and had a parish school in 1882 whose master played the violin in church. [n42 §6; n40 A1] Bolinj, just north of the old agar, has a Zilla Parishad school dated 1879. [n40 A11] For the schools and colleges of the modern city, the many private English- and Hindi-medium schools of the East, the research found nothing sourced. [n42 §6 Gaps]

Hospitals

The sources say almost nothing about hospitals in Nallasopara. In the first wave of COVID-19 the corporation’s hospitals had, on 6 May 2020, seven ventilators and two ICU beds. [n29 §4] The worst hospital disaster in the belt, the ICU fire at Vijay Vallabh Hospital on 23 April 2021 that killed thirteen to fifteen COVID patients, was in Virar, and Chapter 29 tells it. [n29 §4; n47] In March 2026 the corporation’s budget for 2026–27, of about ₹4,209 crore, included a 400-bed hospital at Achole. [n48 §8; FPJ, 11 Mar 2026] Whether it has been begun, the notes do not say.

Faith in the new town

Christians

The Christians of Nallasopara are of two kinds: the old Catholic communities of the western villages, overwhelmingly Kupari, and the newcomers. The 2011 census counted 8.25 to 8.28 per cent of the corporation’s people as Christian, a high share for the Mumbai region, which the notes attribute to the old East Indian and Vasai Christian communities. [n06 §1; n49 §1h]

The parishes of the western villages are old or grew out of old ones: Holy Cross at Nirmal, the mother parish; St Gonsalo Garcia at Gass, from 1942; the Holy Family at Bhuigaon, from 1946; St Joseph’s at Umrale, from 1970; the Most Holy Trinity at Gomes Ali, from 2004; and a parish for Mardes and Wagholi. [n40 A1–A9] In Nallasopara West the Diocese of Kalyan lists a parish of St George. [n31 §3; Kalyan Diocese] Since 1998 the Latin parishes belong to the Diocese of Vasai, created on 22 May 1998 with its cathedral at Papdi. [n42 §5; n11 Q2] The research found no history of the newer churches of Nallasopara East. [n09]

Twice the sources record violence against the area’s Christians. In May 1991 the Times of India described attacks on Christian residents linked to the water-tanker agitation. [n29 §1; Sabrang] On 11 August 2023 vandals broke into the church at Gass, defaced the altar and threw down the statue of Our Lady; no arrest or motive was found in the sources. [n42 §5; K7]

The Kuparis of Nallasopara

The Kuparis deserve a passage of their own here, because Nallasopara West contains a large part of their homeland, and because the city has come round them in a single lifetime.

Of the thirteen villages the community’s own writers list as Kupari, at least five lie in or beside Nallasopara West: Gass, Nirmal, Umrale, Mardes and Wagholi, with Bhuigaon just to the west. [n40 §1; Rajan D’Mello blog] A Marathi report counts sixteen Samvedi villages, Gass among them, with about 50,000 speakers of the dialect, Samvedi Brahmins, Pachkalshis and Samvedi Christians together; Ethnologue, via Wikipedia, gives about 60,000. [n20; n22; n34 §8] Community estimates of the number of Kuparis range from 40,000 to more than 60,000; no census counts them, and those figures are self-reported. [n34 §8] All Christians in Vasai taluka numbered about 110,800 in 2011. [n34 §8]

Their villages in 1882 were the “garden or agar of Sopara”, the best-tilled land in the district, and their fathers, in the Gazetteer’s words, “the most hardworking and skilful husbandmen”. [n11 Q1; n36 §5] Community writers say farming dominated until the late 1960s and early 1970s, and that people then moved into engineering, medicine, banking and information technology, and abroad to the Gulf, Europe, the United States and Australia. [n34 §8; Vasai Kupari blog; Post in Mumbai] Education and the Church were the ways out of farming. The parish schools of Nirmal and Gass, and the Jesuit schools that the Sahapedia account says the Samvedi Christians alone among the Bassein groups attended, were the ladders. [n34 §1, §8]

Two sons of these villages became bishops in the same see. Thomas Dabre, born at Bhuigaon on 23 October 1945, was the first Bishop of Vasai from 1998 and Bishop of Poona from 2009 to 2023. [n40 A4; n34 §8] Simon Almeida, born at Gass on 4 November 1960, schooled at the St Gonsalo Garcia Primary School and Holy Cross High School, Nirmal, was appointed Bishop of Poona by Pope Leo XIV on 1 November 2025 and installed on 17 January 2026. [n42 §6; K7] No source says either man is Kupari; that is an inference from their birthplaces. [n34 §8]

What the city has meant for the Kupari villages, the sources show only in fragments. The green-belt rezoning of 1988 and the housing boom fell hardest on the garden villages, and it was the Christian-led Harit Vasai movement, under a Franciscan from Jeladi near Nandakhal, Fr Francis D’Britto, that fought it; when he died on 25 July 2024, about three thousand people came to his funeral, and an advocate, Noel Dabre, said: “The greenery that you see now is because of the movement.” [n29 §1, §4; n40 A3; FPJ, 25 Jul 2024] In 2025 Gass fought off the dumping ground. In June 2026 a local website wrote that the Kupari red cap and the red lugda had “largely disappeared from everyday life”, kept now as marks of identity. [n34 §8; All About Vasai Virar, 29 Jun 2026] In October 2026 the Free Press Journal reported that East Indian Catholics were among thirty-three Maharashtra groups awaiting inclusion in the central list of Other Backward Classes; the reports name East Indians, not Kuparis, and a Kupari family obtains any such certificate as East Indian. [n34 §8; FPJ, Oct 2026] Their villages, a community source says, remain socially distinct, while the urban areas are mixed. [n34 §8]

The research found no study of how many Kupari families still farm in Nallasopara West, how much of their land has been sold, or how many of their young people have left. [n34 §8 Gaps; n42 §6 Gaps] That is the most important gap in the modern story of the community, and only the community can fill it.

Hindus

Most of Nallasopara’s people are Hindu, 77 to 78 per cent of the corporation in 2011. [n06 §1; n49 §1h] The old Hindu sacred places of the island are in or near the West: the Shankaracharya samadhi at Nirmal, with its November fair; the Chakreshwar temple and the Akkalkot Swami Math beside its lake; Ramkund, where pilgrims bathed in Shravan in 1882; and the Surleshwar temple by the Nirmal lake, known for funeral rites. [n10 §1.2, §1.3; n11 Q2] Jivdani, on its hill above Virar, and Tungareshwar, on Tungar, are the great pilgrimages of the wider belt. [n10] The Ganesh festival is the season of the flower market, and Ganesh images are immersed in the village lakes, even in the Christian village of Umrale. [n29 §4; n40 A9] The research found no history of the newer temples of Nallasopara East, of its Sai Baba temples, or of the gaondevis of its villages. [n08 Gaps]

Muslims

Nallasopara’s Muslims are both old and new. The Naitas of Sopara, descendants of Arab and Persian traders, were there in 1882 and long before; the mohallas of Nawayat, Kharkhandi and Bazar Peth in Sopara gaon, the Rehmatullah and Diwan Shah dargahs, and the Bandar Masjid near the old landing belong to them. [n11 Q2; n31 §1; n40 A15; n02 §6] Urdu speakers were twelve per cent of Nallasopara in 2001, and Muslims 8.5 to 9 per cent of the corporation in 2011. [n31 §3; n06 §1] The Hazrat Kutti Shah Baba dargah, the “Pahad wali dargah” on the hill near Nallasopara West station, with a mosque on the hill, appears only in a low-reliability listing. Wikipedia-derived text says Nallasopara has “many dargahs of Islamic Sufi masters of the medieval period”, and names none. [n10 §5] No reliable history, date or urs calendar was found for any of them, and no modern source on the town’s Muslim communities. [n10 §5; n08 Gaps] That is a large gap for a community of tens of thousands.

Jains

The Jains of modern Nallasopara are mostly Gujarati and Marwari families. [n08 §7] The Shri Munisuvrat Swami Jain Derasar and Upashray on Tulinj Road is the one modern derasar the sources name. [n10 §3] Behind them stands the old Jain tirtha of Soparaka, which Chapter 22 describes, and the temple at Agashi whose image, by tradition, rose from Chakreshwar lake. [n10 §3]

Buddhists

The 2011 census counted 2.5 to 2.65 per cent of the corporation’s people as Buddhist. [n06 §1; n49 §1h] The notes found no source on who they are, when they came, or where in the city they live, and none on Buddhist viharas in Nallasopara. [n49 §1h Gaps] What they record is the state’s interest in the stupa at Mardes: the 2012 plan for a meditation centre, museum and theatre; the 2014 decision to make the stupa the hub of a Maharashtra Buddhist circuit; the nine-foot Buddha donated by a Pune trust and set up about 200 metres away; and the judgement of 2014 onwards that the site “still lies largely neglected”. [n02 §1, §6] Chapter 22 tells the stupa’s story. Whether Nallasopara’s Buddhists gather there, the sources do not say.

Politics

The Thakurs’ seat

The Nallasopara assembly seat was created at the delimitation of 2008, and first contested in October 2009. Kshitij Thakur, elder son of Hitendra Thakur of the Bahujan Vikas Aaghadi, then about twenty-six, won with 89,284 votes, 52.84 per cent, beating Shirish Chavhan of the Shiv Sena by 40,782 on a turnout of 48 per cent; Wikipedia calls him the youngest MLA in the state at the time. In the same election his father lost the Vasai seat to Vivek Pandit. [n29 §2; n26 §1, §2; Wikipedia: Nallasopara AC] In October 2014 Kshitij Thakur won 113,566 votes, 50.72 per cent, beating Rajan Naik of the BJP by 54,499. In October 2019 he won 149,868, 56.37 per cent, beating Pradeep Sharma of the Shiv Sena by 43,729. [n29 §3; n26 §1] For fifteen years the seat was a Thakur seat.

The family’s wider story, Hitendra Thakur’s rise from 1990, the Bhai Thakur cases, the BVA’s hold on the corporation, is told in Chapters 15, 15 and V3. Its record in the sources is of allegations without convictions: Hitendra Thakur’s 2014 affidavit listed eight criminal cases, none ending in conviction, and Bhai Thakur was acquitted in the Dube case in 2023. [n26 §2; n29 §3]

2024 and 2026

On 23 November 2024 the seat fell. Rajan Balkrishna Naik of the BJP won 165,113 votes, 47.58 per cent, against Kshitij Thakur’s 128,238, a margin of 36,875. [n06 §3; n26 §1; Wikipedia: Nallasopara AC; ECI] In Vasai the BJP’s Sneha Dube Pandit beat Hitendra Thakur by 3,153. The Deccan Herald headline read “saffron wave hits bastion of Thakurs”. [n06 §3] The notes observe that the BJP’s gains coincided with a statewide wave, the weight of north-Indian voters and, soon after, the demolitions and the ED case; but no source links them causally. [n06 §3 inferences]

In the corporation elections of 15–16 January 2026, the first since 2015, the BVA held power with 71 of 115 seats, but the BJP rose from about one seat to 43; on 3 February 2026 Ajiv Patil of the BVA became mayor and Marshal Dominic Lopes deputy mayor. [n06 §3; n26 §3] Chapter 16 gives the details.

In the Palghar Lok Sabha constituency, which takes in Nallasopara, the BVA’s Baliram Jadhav won in 2009, the BJP’s Chintaman Vanaga in 2014, Rajendra Gavit in 2018 and 2019, and the BJP’s Hemant Savara in 2024. [n26 §1] Palghar district itself was carved from Thane on 1 August 2014, with Vasai as one of its eight talukas, so that Nallasopara, a Thane suburb for most of its life, became part of Palghar. [n29 §3]

People

The sources name few people from modern Nallasopara beyond politicians, officials and the accused in its cases. Two sportsmen stand out. The cricketer Ayush Mhatre is listed by Wikipedia as from Nallasopara; one news summary says Virar, and the notes treat him as “Nallasopara/Virar” until a primary profile is checked. [n27] Sagar Shelar of Nallasopara, described as an international athlete and Asian gold medallist, was appointed coach of the Indian mixed boxing team and welcomed across Vasai-Virar; the research read only the headline. [n27; FPJ] Suresh Dube, the builder shot on the platform in 1989, is the one victim the sources name. [n29 §1] Ramdas Kasidas Modi, “a native of Sopara” who helped the Gazetteer in 1882, is the first person of the place whom the record names as a historian’s helper. [n11 Q2] The rest of Nallasopara’s people, hundreds of thousands of them, are in the sources only as numbers.

Nallasopara in November 2026

What is Nallasopara like as this book is finished, in November 2026? The sources give a scatter of facts from the last months.

The city around it had a budget of about ₹4,209 crore for 2026–27, with property tax up fifteen per cent and water tax doubled. [n48 §8] The fifth and sixth railway lines were about thirty per cent built. [n06 §2] Fifteen-coach trains ran north of Virar. [n06 §2] The flood of July had killed nine people in Vasai-Virar, and the railway was studying whether to raise the tracks past Nallasopara. [n29 §4; n48 §7] The flower market had flooded on the eve of Ganpati. [n29 §4] In September there was a cross-party protest across Vasai-Virar against smart electricity meters and higher bills. [n06 §5] A Marathi report counted five deaths in twenty-four hours on the railway between Vasai and Vaitarna. [n29 §4] On 15 November a Marathi report warned of water scarcity and of historic lakes choked with garbage and water hyacinth. [n06 §4; eSakal] The ED case over Agarwal Nagari had no reported outcome. [n06 §7] The site where the forty-one buildings stood, and the families who lived in them, had no reported resolution. [n48 §8] In Gass, the dumping ground had been withdrawn but no formal commitment reported. [n42 §6] At Mardes, the stupa was protected and “largely neglected”. [n02 §1]

The research found no major-outlet story from October or November 2026 about Nallasopara itself beyond these. [n29 §4 Gaps; n06 §7 Gaps] It found no population estimate for 2026 that it could trust. [n06 §1] It found no survey of how many of the town’s people live in chawls, how many in unauthorised buildings, how many in the old villages; no history of its East-side neighbourhoods; no list of its schools or hospitals. [n31 §3 Gaps] The modern town, which has more people than all the other places in this book’s earlier chapters put together, is the least documented of them.

What is still unknown

  • The name. What “Nala” means; whether Nila, Nil hill, Neela and Nala are one name.
  • The station. Its opening year (1865, 1867, 1869, or after 1882) and its first name; when it was electrified; how many people use it today.
  • The council. When the Nallasopara municipal council was formed, and who led it.
  • The census. Population figures for Nala and Sopara between 1882 and 2001; an official figure for Nallasopara in 2011.
  • The neighbourhoods. The origins of Santosh Bhuvan, Shirdi Nagar, Pragati Nagar, Nilemore and the other colonies of the East.
  • The migrants. Where they came from, by state and district, and when.
  • Agarwal Nagari. The outcome of the ED case; what became of the families; what will happen to the land.
  • The 2018 case. The verdict.
  • Faith. The histories of the town’s mosques, dargahs, newer temples and churches; its Buddhist community.
  • The Kupari villages. How much land is still farmed; how many families remain.

The end of the halt

In 1882 the editor of the Gazetteer looked at Sopara, three-quarters of a mile from the railway, and thought a station would be “a boon to its people”. He was thinking of 1,700 people, a crowded weekly market, carts of molasses and betel leaf that could not cross the salt waste in the rains. When the station came, it brought a city. The salt waste is built over. The carts are gone. The people number in the hundreds of thousands, and in a bad July they pay tractor drivers to reach the platform. The ancient town that the halt was meant to serve is now a locality of the suburb that took its name, and the stupa that Indraji opened in 1882 stands among the flats, protected, and, as one report puts it, largely neglected.

Both chapters of this part of the book have ended on the same note: the record runs out. For Sopara the silence is at the beginning, in the centuries before Ashoka and in the dark centuries after the Satavahanas. For Nallasopara it is at the end, in the lives of the people who live there now. The first silence will be filled, if at all, by archaeologists. The second can be filled by the people of Nallasopara themselves.
