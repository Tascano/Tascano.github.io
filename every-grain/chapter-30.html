Period covered: the land as the 1882 Gazetteer found it, through the plantain and salt economy of the nineteenth century, to the flood, water and budget of 2026

In this chapter. Hills, plain, river and sea: the land as the Gazetteer found it, the plantain and salt economy, and the floods and water of the present.

From the flat top of Tungar hill, 2,800 feet above the sea, the compilers of the Gazetteer of the Bombay Presidency looked west on a clear day in the years before 1882 and saw what they called “a magnificent western view, with the Vaitarna to the north and the Bassein creek to the south”. What struck them was not the beauty of it but the water. Views from Tungar, they wrote, show “how large an area is still flooded at spring tides, and how completely the whole coast belt of rice-land is intersected by salt water channels”. [n48 §1–2; Gaz XIII-I, physical description] Below them, at the foot of the hills, lay a strip of low land a few miles wide, cut by creeks, edged with salt marsh, and in its middle a belt of gardens so rich that the people who lived there called it the agar, the garden: “richly wooded, well-watered and highly tilled”. [n48 §1; Gaz XIV p. 315]

A hundred and forty-four years later, in July 2026, another official looked at the same strip from the opposite end. Ramashray Pandey, General Manager of Western Railway, inspected the line between Vasai and Virar after the rains had stopped the trains for two days, and ordered a survey of whether the tracks could be raised. The reason, as the Free Press Journal reported it, was geography: “Palghar is located at a relatively higher elevation, while Virar, Nallasopara and Vasai lie on a lower gradient.” Near Nallasopara, “the city and the railway station are at nearly the same level”. [n48 §2; FPJ, 8 July 2026] The water that the Gazetteer’s compilers saw from Tungar was still there. It was now standing in the streets of a city.

This chapter tells the ground on which Virar stands, and what its people have made of it. It begins with the hills and the rock, the plain of silt, the river and the lost creek; it follows the climate, the floods and the long search for drinking water; it visits the forest on the ridge, the mangroves on the estuary and the birds on the shore; and then it tells the work, from the rice fields, plantain gardens and salt pans of the nineteenth century to the commuting, building and civic economy of 2026. The deep history of the rock, the Deccan lavas and the Ice Age seas, is told in Chapters 01 and 02; the story of the coast and its beaches in Chapter 32; the land and work of the whole taluka in the decades of the republic in Chapters 14 and 14. Here the lens is Virar and the northern villages: Agashi, Arnala, Bolinj, Vatar, Koprad, Umrale, Rajodi, Narangi and the hills behind them.

Much of the evidence comes from two documents a century and a quarter apart. The first is the Thana volumes of the Gazetteer of 1882, read directly from their scanned text; their prose is reliable, their tables often garbled by the scanner. [n48 §1] The second is the City Development Plan for the Vasai-Virar sub-region, prepared by the consultants Tandon & Associates for the corporation and CIDCO, whose file is dated September 2010 with some tables updated later. [n48 §3] Between them lie the census, the pollution board’s river plan, a few scientific papers, and the newspapers. Where they disagree, both are given.

The hills: Kamandurg, Tungar, Jivdhan

The skyline east of Virar is a ridge of broken hills that runs north and south a few miles inland. The Gazetteer named its peaks from south to north. “Further north, in Bassein, rises the lofty peak of Kamandurg (2160 ft)… Connected with Kamandurg, on the north, is the flat laterite-capped hill of Tungar (2800 ft), with well wooded sides and poorly clothed top… North of Tungar is a cluster of hills of which Baronda, Jivdhan and Nilemora are the most marked peaks.” [n48 §2; Gaz XIII-I, physical description] Jivdhan is the hill of the goddess Jivdani, about a mile east of Virar station; by the most reliable figure, from a pollution-board summary of March 2020, it rises 728 feet. [n45 §3; n49 §2c; MPCB executive summary 2020] Baronda has its own goddess, Baronda Devi, listed among Virar’s temples by Marathi Wikipedia. [n49 §1h; Marathi Wikipedia: Virar]

East of the ridge, the Gazetteer said, Bassein lies in the watershed of the Vaitarna and is “generally well wooded”; west of it, in the coast strip, the forests were “extensive”, chiefly on the slopes of Tungar and Kamandurg and in the villages of Pelhar, Kaman, Chichoti, Poman and Paya. [n48 §1; Gaz XIII-I, Forests] The forest on those slopes is now the Tungareshwar Wildlife Sanctuary, told below.

Basalt and laterite

The hills are made of two rocks, one laid on the other. The lower is basalt, the dark volcanic rock of the Deccan; the Gazetteer called trap “the lowest rock” of the district and noted a “vein of basalt” running from Bandra along the shore as a narrow dyke, and basaltic dykes through the northern Thane coast, which it thought might extend the “volcanic centre” of Rajpipla in Gujarat. [n48 §2; Gaz XIII-I, geology] The city plan of 2010 puts it more plainly: the area lies on the “Deccan Lava plateau”. [n48 §3; CDP p. 26] (The link between the Gazetteer’s “volcanic origin” and the Deccan Traps is the modern reader’s; Chapters 01 and 02 tell how those lavas were laid down some sixty-six million years ago.)

Over the basalt, on the highest flat tops, lies laterite: the red, iron-rich rock that forms when basalt weathers for ages under a hot, wet sky. The Gazetteer named Tungar among the laterite-capped hills of the district, with Matheran, Prabal and Mahuli, and described the rock as “red, of very coarse grain, and, though, when quarried soft and easy to work, hardens on exposure”. The basalt was “easily quarried without blasting, and has been largely used on the Bombay and Baroda railway”; there was “no difficulty in obtaining good road metal”. [n48 §2; Gaz XIII-I, Minerals] So the very line that made Virar was built, in part, of the hills behind it.

The railway also took something from the hills. Two miles east of Jivdhan, near Shirgaon, the Gazetteer recorded “Pandav Dungri, so called from a set of small caves which were cut away at the time of making the railway”. [n45 §1; Gaz XIV p. 383] The caves on Jivdhan itself, “very old-looking caves and cisterns said to be the work of the Pandavs”, survived and became the goddess’s shrine. [n45 §1; Gaz XIV]

The city plan adds one more mineral claim: “Traces of Bauxite have been found in the Tungar Hill ranges over an area of 80 sq.kms… mineral content of 30-35%”. [n48 §3, §7; CDP p. 26] Bauxite is the ore of aluminium, and it forms in laterite, so the claim is geologically plausible; but it is a planning document’s statement, and no mining of it has been reported. The plan also notes “few stone quarries in Rajawali area”, and quarrying “mainly to the east of railway line” in the Green Zone. [n48 §7; CDP pp. 26, 64] Those quarries are part of the story of livelihood, below.

What the notes do not hold is the geology of Jivdhan itself, quarry by quarry or layer by layer; no source beyond the Gazetteer’s list of hills was found. [n48 §2 Gaps] A CGWB brochure for Thane district, known only from a search-engine summary, says the basaltic rocks of parts of Dahanu and the Vasai-Virar talukas have poor recharge, which, if right, matters for the water story: rain that falls on the hills does not soak far into them. [n48 §2; CGWB Thane brochure (summary)]

The plain: kharpat, uthalpat, junglepat

Between the hills and the sea lies the plain on which Virar is built, and the people of the coast have long had names for its parts. The city plan of 2010 records three. The Kharpat, from khar, salt, is the coastal strip, running “from Chikhal-Dongri in the north to Naigaon in the south”, “hardly 3 to 4 ft. above sea level”. The Uthalpat is the fertile coastal belt with “fresh, sweet water sources”. The Junglepat is the east, the forested hills, “inhabited by tribals”, about two thirds of it forest. [n48 §3–4; CDP p. 27] The average elevation of the whole area above sea level, the plan says, is 1.5 to 2 metres. “The low-lying lands along the coast and along two creeks are marshy, khazan lands and some of them mainly to the south of Nallasopara Railway Station are still used for salt cultivation.” [n48 §3; CDP pp. 24–25, 28]

The Gazetteer of 1882 described the same ground in its own vocabulary. North of the Vaitarna “the shores are flat, with long sandy spits running into muddy shallows”, and divided “by wide wastes of salt marsh, tracts of slightly rising ground, covered by palmyra trees, stretch to the foot of the hills”. [n48 §1; Gaz XIII-I, general description] Round Bassein the villages were “thriving and populous”; their outskirts had “dreary salt marshes” and “wide treeless rice flats… bare and untilled during most of the year”, while nearer the houses were “palm gardens, sugarcane fields, and betel leaf or plantain orchards, sheltered by high hedges”, and ponds “gay with water lilies”. [n48 §1; Gaz XIII-I]

Its soils it divided into “sweet” and “salt”. Sweet land was the black shet of the rice fields and the red mal varkas of the hill slopes; salt land was the tidal ground. Rice land was either bandhni, banked and floodable, or malkhandi, open. “In the garden lands of Bassein and Mahim, the black soil is lighter and more sandy than in the interior.” Across the whole district (excluding Jawhar) the revenue survey counted 73,801 acres, 2.71 per cent of the land, as salt pans and marshes, and 37.84 per cent as forest. [n48 §1; Gaz XIII-I pp. 280–281]

The sunken garden

In the middle of the plain, between the Bassein creek and the Vaitarna, lay the garden strip, about three miles broad. The Gazetteer divided it into three agars, the agar of Agashi in the north, the agar of Sopara in the middle and the agar of Bassein in the south, and described the middle one, which includes Virar’s villages, in the most vivid passage it wrote about this coast. The agar of Sopara took in “Bolinj Koprad and Umbrala in the north, Nirmal and Vagholi in the west, Gas in the south, and Sopara and Mardesh in the centre”. [n48 §1; n45 §1; Gaz XIV p. 314] “Within this rich garden island, four or five feet below the level of the fields, shady lanes… wind among mango and plantain orchards, fields of rice and sugarcane.” “Till the month of March the island is full of water. Runnels from garden wells cross or flow alongside of the lanes… round ponds and long winding lakes, whose banks are fringed by high pile-supported Persian wheels.” [n48 §1; Gaz XIV p. 315]

Lanes sunk four or five feet below the fields: that is a landscape shaped by centuries of water, in which the paths were worn down by feet and carts and monsoon run-off while the fields were built up by silt and manure. It is also, read now, a warning. The plain was low, and its lowest parts were its roads.

The river and the sea

The Vaitarna

Virar’s northern boundary is a river. The Vaitarna, the Gazetteer said, is for its last twenty miles “a fine broad river”, whose mouth lies at Agashi; “north of the Vaitarna, whose broad waters open a scene of almost perfect loveliness”, the flat shores begin. [n48 §1–2; Gaz XIII-I, Rivers] Its tributary the Tansa flows nine miles through Bassein and joins the Vaitarna at Chimana, about eight miles from the sea; the Tansa is “tidal for many miles and navigable for eight”, and Usgaon, Khaniwade, Bhatane and Chimana were boat-loading stations with water for vessels of fifty khandis, about 124 tons. [n48 §1; Gaz XIII-I, Rivers] Usgaon, in 1882, was a river port. Its name returns later in this chapter as a dam.

The southern mouth of the Vaitarna was guarded by the island fort of Arnala, “commanding the southern and main entrance to the Vaitarna river”, whose story is told in Chapter 32. [n45 §1; Gaz XIV p. 10] The bay of Agashi, just south of it, was nearly closed: “Except one channel the mouth of the bay on which the town stands is closed by a reef, and the bay is full of shoals and navigable only by boats and small vessels.” [n36; n45 §1; Gaz XIV p. 1, citing Horsburgh]

The sea at the mouth of the Vaitarna has not stood still. The Gazetteer records that the sea had gained at “Chikhli, Gholvad… Dahanu”, and “at the mouth of the Vaitarna where, since 1724, four villages have been submerged”. [n48 §1–2; Gaz XIII-I p. 281] Which villages they were it does not say. Elsewhere the same page describes the opposite process, by which people reclaimed salt land into sweet rice land by embanking it, and warns that it takes “twenty or twenty-five years” for salted low land to sweeten. [n48 §1; Gaz XIII-I p. 281] The coast north of Virar was, and is, a line drawn and redrawn between people and the tide.

The lost creek

The most important waterway in the history of the Virar side no longer exists. In the nineteenth century the garden strip was called an island, the Gazetteer explained, “because it is cut from the mainland by a backwater which leaves the Bassein creek a little above the railway bridge and winds north to the Vaitarna. This backwater used to be called the Sopara creek. It is now locally known by three names, the Manikpur creek in the south, the Sopara creek in the centre, and the Bolinj creek in the north.” [n48 §1; Gaz XIV pp. 314–315] The northern reach, the Bolinj creek, ran past Virar.

It had been a real sea arm. “It was once a considerable arm of the sea overflowing a large breadth of land on both banks.” [n48 §1; Gaz XIV p. 315] A sea manual of seventeenth-century Kutch, by a modern study’s account, describes a regular cargo route through the Bolinj–Sopara creek. [n36; Shah et al. 2020] But it was silting. In 1728 a Portuguese writer complained that the creek could be forded at low water for about 4,000 feet between Sopara and Bolinj, and warned that unless it was dug “as deep as the height of a man” it would soon be filled “to a level with the fields, because of the great amount of silt that was brought by the river… from its two mouths”. [n36; Gaz XIV p. 322] Nobody dug it. By 1882, “with the mud-laden Vaitarna on the north and the Bassein creek or Ulhas river on the south, silting has been rapid, especially since the long railway embankment has dammed the flow of water from the south”. Boats of ten or twelve tons still came from the north as far as Bolinj, “about two miles from Dedar or the old Sopara landing”, and from the south as far as Gas; but “at Sopara the creek is dry”. [n48 §1; n36; Gaz XIV pp. 314–316] A modern study puts the silting into a long frame: about two thousand years of it, accelerated by deforestation inland and by a railway line on a high embankment in the second half of the nineteenth century. [n36; Arunachalam 2002, via Shah et al. 2020]

Here is the hinge of the whole history of this coast, told in mud. Sopara, the ancient port of Shurparaka, lost its water and became an inland village; Agashi, on the open bay, kept its boats into the nineteenth century; and Virar, on the railway that had helped to choke the creek, rose as the station through which the produce of the garden went out. The same embankment that made the creek “dry” at Sopara made the station at Virar the new landing. Chapter 12 and Chapter 39 tell the trade side of that change.

The creek’s line survives as a drain. In 2019 the Maharashtra Pollution Control Board described the Pelhar river, a non-perennial stream of nine or ten kilometres running from the Pelhar dam to Golani Naka, as flowing into “the Sopara creek”, fed by three nallahs: the Sopara Nallah, the Tungar Nallah at Sativali and the Waliv Nallah. [n48 §2; MPCB Pelhar action plan, 2019] The old arm of the sea had become the outfall of the city’s sewage. That is told below.

Mangroves and salt flats

Where the land meets the tide, on the creek banks and the estuary of the Vaitarna, the natural cover is mangrove, and the made cover is salt pans. Both are edges, and both are now contested.

The Gazetteer’s coast had “wide wastes of salt marsh” and salt wastes bordering the creek, “flooded and impassable in the rains” on the track from Manikpur. [n48 §1; Gaz XIV p. 316] Salt works were made from exactly that ground. They were embanked “from the muddy flats which are flooded at spring-tides”, and each had three parts: a khajina, a reservoir (the word means “treasury”); topavnis, brine pits; and kundis, evaporating pans. The season ran from January to May. [n48 §1, §5; Gaz XIII-I pp. 365–366] In 1880–81 the Bassein salt division had seventeen works in six groups, with 11,374 pans on 1,439 acres and thirty owners, about eighteen per cent of the district’s salt-pan area. [n21 §3; Gaz XIII-I p. 365] The salt itself is told below as livelihood.

In the sanctioned plan of 2007 the salt pans still covered 1,065 hectares of the urbanisable zone, 11.4 per cent of it. [n48 §4–5; CDP Table 9.2] “Umela is the only place where salt is produced on mass scale,” the plan said, at Naigaon west, and salt cultivation continued south of Nallasopara station. [n48 §5; CDP pp. 28, 47] Press estimates put Vasai’s salt pans at about 2,000 acres, with sources differing. [n17; Asian Age; Business Standard, 2007] The fate of those flats became, in the late 2010s, the largest single land-use fight on record here: the metropolitan authority’s draft Regional Plan for 2016–36 proposed turning 1,560 acres of former salt pans, previously a no-development zone, into a “growth centre” with housing, sports, education and commerce. Residents pointed out that the land floods every monsoon; The Quint reported at least 40,000 objections from Vasai–Virar, and the activist Samir Vartak put the number of objectors at about 63,000. [n17; The Quint via ICSF] The plan’s final status is not confirmed in the notes. [n17 Gaps]

The mangroves of the Vaitarna estuary are described below, with the birds of the shore. Their tenure is a story in itself. A Hindustan Times report of January 2022, seen only as a search snippet, said that Palghar topped Maharashtra’s list of “non-transferred” mangrove land, with 1,277.58 hectares, and that about 1,400 hectares in Vasai–Virar were expected to pass to the Forest Department. [n48 §6; HT, 2 Jan 2022 (snippet)] Mangrove land that is not yet under the forest department is mangrove land that can still be filled.

Climate and rain

Virar’s year has three seasons, and the city plan of 2010 sets them out. Winter runs from October to January, with mean maxima of about 28°C and minima of about 16°C. Summer runs from February to May, at about 34°C and 26°C. The south-west monsoon arrives in the first week of June and lasts until September, and in those four months falls “95% of the annual rainfall”. July is the wettest month, “averaging to 800 mm”. Humidity ranges from 49 to 85 per cent, highest in July; the mean wind speed is 16.52 kilometres an hour. [n48 §3; CDP pp. 26–27]

How much rain falls in a year is less settled than it sounds. The same city plan gives “annual average rainfall is about 2200 mm” in one place and “annual mean rainfall of 2200–2500 mm” in another. [n48 §3; CDP §3.5] A 2023 report by 101Reporters on the flower growers of the coast gives about 1,586 millimetres a year at Arnala and Vasai. [n17; n48 §2–3; 101Reporters, 7 Jan 2023] The two figures cannot both be the long-term average for the same place. The sensible reading, and the one this book follows, is that 1,586 millimetres is a local gauge reading, and 2,200 to 2,500 millimetres the planning figure used for the sub-region; no official India Meteorological Department series for Virar was found to settle it. [n48 §2–3 Gaps] Either way, a year’s rain is between one and a half and two and a half metres, nearly all of it in four months, on a plain whose average height above the sea is under two metres.

Storm totals are another matter and must not be mixed with the annual figures. On 9–10 July 2018, Palghar district had 240 millimetres in twenty-four hours, by a television report’s snippet. [n48 §2; India TV (snippet)] On 1 July 2026 the corporation’s divisions averaged 78.25 millimetres; the “approximately 690 mm” that headlines quoted that day was the sum of eight divisional readings, not a local measurement. [n48 §2; FPJ, 1 July 2026] On 7 July 2026 the Palghar collector reported an average of 203 millimetres in twenty-four hours. [n29 §4; FPJ, 7 July 2026]

The monsoon’s failures were once as dangerous as its floods. The Gazetteer lists them: in 1824–25 the rain failed in Dahanu, Bassein and Salsette, grain rose to famine prices, though no one died of starvation, and the government spent £1,550 clearing reservoirs; in 1837 the crops failed in Sanjan and Bassein for want of rain, and about Rs 45,000 in remissions were granted; in 1848 “most of the salt rice land crop failed”; in 1850 the coast tract “suffered severely from drought”; in 1854 “in Bassein the salt rice crops were partially injured by worms”. [n48 §1; n21; Gaz XIII-I] In the nineteenth century the farmers of the Virar side feared too little rain. In the twenty-first, the town fears too much.

The floods, 2005–2026

The great Mumbai deluge of 26 July 2005 is remembered in Vasai as the benchmark flood, but no Vasai-only death toll or rainfall figure for it was found. [n29 §2; Deccan Herald] The dated floods of Virar’s recent history begin in 2018.

July 2018. On 9 and 10 July the Vasai–Virar belt was the worst hit in the metropolitan region, by the Deccan Herald’s account: water reached first-floor flats, the tracks at Nallasopara flooded, and the Navy evacuated commuters. Heavy rain coincided with high tide. [n48 §2; Deccan Herald, 11 July 2018 (snippet); India TV] The environmentalists Debi Goenka of the Conservation Action Trust and Stalin Dayanand of Vanashakti blamed the reclamation of wetlands: Goenka said it had “shrunk the natural drainage”; Dayanand called Vasai–Virar a natural floodplain of wetlands and salt pans, unsuited to townships on raised fill. [n17; Asian Age via CAT, 13 July 2018] A report by NEERI and IIT Bombay followed the floods; its recommendations, the Deccan Herald said in 2025, were not carried out, and the report itself was not found. [n17; n06; Deccan Herald, 2025]

2019. A Free Press Journal page stamped 1 June 2019 (a date that looks wrong for a monsoon flood, and is probably a July event mis-stamped) reported that the Vasai fire brigade rescued twenty-one workers at a sand site at Chandip in Virar East, eighteen men and three women, and five people at Khaniwade, and that the villages of Chandip, Navsai, Khaniwade, Usgaon, Shiravali, Bhatane and Parol, “home to about 30,000 people, face flood threats every monsoon”. [n48 §2; FPJ, “Villages in Vasai-Virar marooned”] These are the villages of the Tansa side, and they show that Virar East floods from the river as well as from the sea. A Deccan Herald snippet of 4 September 2019 reported over 500 millimetres in thirty-six hours and flash flooding on the Nallasopara tracks; services between Vasai and Virar were suspended in early August that year. [n48 §2 (snippets)]

2024. No dated source for a Virar flood in 2024 was found. [n48 §2 Gaps]

August 2025. On 18 and 19 August the corporation rescued about 497 residents, opened thirty-four relief centres, distributed over 10,000 food packets and ran fifty-four suction pumps, with Commissioner Manojkumar Suryawanshi inspecting. [n48 §2; FPJ, 19 Aug 2025] Other reports of thirty to forty flooded buildings in Virar and 3,000 flooded homes come from pages that could not be opened and are not quoted as fact. [n48 §2] Western Railway blamed the waterlogging on its tracks that year on unplanned building on salt-pan land, mangroves and waterholes in Nallasopara and Virar, on choked culverts, and on overflow from the Pelhar dam. [n17; n06; PropNewsTime]

July 2026. On 1 July the Suncity Gas Road in Virar was submerged, with the Viva College area, Kumbharpada Road and Shriprastha Road and many places in Nallasopara; the Mumbai–Ahmedabad highway was waterlogged. [n48 §2; FPJ, 1 July 2026] From 6 to 8 July the rain stopped the trains: one hundred suburban services were cancelled on the Monday and 125 on Tuesday 7 July, water stood three or four feet deep outside some homes, and tractors charged Rs 100 to 150 a person to carry people to the station. [n48 §2; FPJ, 8 July 2026] Ten people died in rain-related incidents in Palghar district from 1 July; by 13 July nine deaths in Vasai–Virar were reported, and 389 families, 1,261 people, had been moved to relief centres in Palghar, Vasai and Wada talukas. [n29 §4; FPJ, 7 July 2026; The Quint, 13 July 2026] The General Manager ordered his survey of raising the tracks, and asked for a check of the elevation of the fifth and sixth lines under construction. The corporation’s commissioner, Prithviraj B.P., promised action against “illegal structures obstructing natural drainage”. [n48 §2; FPJ, 8 July 2026] A Hindi report described a Rs 222 crore flood-retention pond planned at Nilemore and Gogte; its status is unverified. [n48 §3; Navbharat Live (snippet)]

On 13 September 2026, one more night of rain flooded the flower market and main market near Nallasopara station just before Ganpati. [n29 §4; FPJ]

The pattern is the land’s. The plain is low; its old drains (the creek, the ponds, the salt marsh, the paddy fields that stood under water each monsoon) have been silted, filled or built on; and the railway embankment that dammed the Sopara creek in the nineteenth century still runs, now with more tracks, across the line of the water’s escape. The causal link between filled wetlands and floods is argued by activists and by the railway itself; no published study proving it was found. [n17 Inferences]

Groundwater and salt

Under the plain lies water, and on top of it, pressing in from the sea, salt.

The 1882 gardens drew their water from shallow wells. “Those in Bassein and Mahim, which are much the best in the district, are watered by Persian wheels from unbuilt wells”, the Gazetteer said: unlined pits, worked by bullocks walking round a wheel that lifted a chain of pots. [n48 §1; Gaz XIII-I p. 281] Beside the wells were the ponds. Each family group of the garden villages, of five to sixty people, owned a bavkhal, an irrigation pond fed by the unconfined aquifer and never used for drinking. A 2007 Minor Irrigation survey counted 479 of them in twenty-seven gram panchayats; a 2013 survey by the Yuva Vikas Sanstha found more than the records showed, 54 at Wagholi against sixteen on paper. [n17; Vikalp Sangam] Marcus Dabre, editor of the Vasai Times, explained that the bavkhals hold rainwater and feed the fields through underground connections. [n17; 101Reporters] Coastal water bodies are brackish and linked underground, said Macanzy Dabre of the Paryavaran Samvardhan Samiti. [n17; Vikalp Sangam]

That system survived until the boom. From the early 1990s builders and tanker operators drew groundwater from the green belt for the new colonies, and the farmers’ wells turned saline; Harit Vasai won a ban on tankers drawing water there. [n29 §1; Sabrang India] By 2012 about thirteen lakh people depended on tankers and bore wells, and tankers moved about fifteen million litres a day from bore wells off the Mumbai–Ahmedabad highway. [n17; Vikalp Sangam]

The city plan of 2010 counted the wells. “There are 34 tube wells at Vasai and 15 tube wells at Virar. In addition, there are 275 bore wells at Vasai… around 220 private tube wells at Virar [10–35 m deep]. The over drawal of water from underground is resulting into the ingress of salinity over the years. As a result potability of water is adversely affected.” [n48 §3; CDP p. 94] A Lok Sabha reply, known from a search summary, said that studies by the Central Ground Water Board in the Vasai–Virar area “have indicated possible sea-water ingress”, as a “localized phenomenon”. [n48 §3; Lok Sabha AU2540 (summary)] A conference paper by Chandrasekharam and colleagues, of about 2000, identified “two saline groundwater zones” in the Vasai–Palghar coastal belt by chemical mixing models and GIS, and attributed the contamination to salt-panning. [n48 §3; Taylor & Francis chapter (summary)] No village-by-village salinity map for Virar was found. [n48 §3 Gaps]

Restoration works. Where the Yuva Vikas Sanstha’s volunteers cleaned and recharged ponds, the total dissolved solids in nearby wells fell from 750 to 300 parts per million. [n17; n48 §3; Vikalp Sangam] But the ponds decayed after the mid-1990s, “as the paddy fields and the family orchards gave way to high-rise buildings”; many became garbage dumps that contaminate the groundwater beneath them. [n17; Vikalp Sangam] Water Crisis in Vasai: A Study counted 108 lakes within a sixty-kilometre radius, of which only twenty-eight survived. [n17] On 15 November 2026 a Marathi daily reported Vasai–Virar’s historic lakes choked with garbage and water hyacinth. [n06; eSakal]

Virar’s own ponds have their own history. In 1882 the town had “a large pond” with the Saturday market on its west bank under banyans and mangoes, and “Virar lake”, with a carved stone of female figures on its west bank. [n48 §3; n45 §1; Gaz XIV pp. 382–383] East of the town, at Phulpada, the Papadkhind (Papdkhind) lake is, by Marathi Wikipedia, “the city’s largest reservoir”, and the Papadkhandi dam at the foot of Jivdani was, by the district’s tourism page, “once a major source of fresh water”. [n49 §3c; Marathi Wikipedia: Virar; Palghar district] No inventory of Virar’s talavs, its tanks, was found. [n48 §3 Gaps]

Water from far away: Usgaon to Surya

Virar’s drinking water now comes from far beyond the plain, and the history of how it got there is a history of the town outgrowing every source it had.

The old schemes

The first piped schemes for the sub-region drew on small dams at the edge of the hills. By 2007, the table in the sanctioned plan shows, the Virar municipal area received 14 MLD (million litres a day) from the Usgaon scheme and 1.5 MLD from Papadkhind, with 30 MLD planned from the Surya. Across the sub-region the sources were Usgaon (24.5 MLD), Pelhar (14 MLD), Papadkhind (1.5 MLD) and wells and tankers (30 MLD), with Surya (100 MLD) as the future source; the rural Green Zone East got 7 MLD from Pelhar. [n48 §3; CDP Table 11.1, p. 95] Usgaon, the Tansa river port of 1882, had become a dam. [n48 §3]

The city plan traces the water’s route. In Virar the Usgaon water was treated at a 20 MLD plant at Parol, “existing from 13 years” at the plan’s writing, and the Surya water was to be treated at Dhuktan (100 MLD). It was carried to master balancing reservoirs at Kashidkopar (20 MLD) and Chandansar (2 MLD), and from them to elevated storage reservoirs at Virat Nagar, Bolinj, Ram Mandir, Phulpada and the Urdu School; the system covered 352,415 people. [n48 §3; CDP pp. 97–98] The Pelhar works were “existing from 29 years”. [n48 §3 Gaps; CDP] Capacities and construction dates of the Usgaon and Pelhar dams were not found. [n48 §3 Gaps; n06 Gaps]

The plan’s arithmetic was stark. At 150 litres per person per day, the 13.07 lakh people expected in 2011 needed 235 MLD; the sub-region was supplied 130; the gap was 105 MLD, rising to a projected demand of 750 MLD by 2041. It listed the schemes that might close it: Surya, Wandri, Ghateghar, Kaman, Kholsapada, Rajiwali-Sativali and Pinjal. [n48 §3; CDP Table 11.2]

Surya

The Surya Regional Bulk Water Supply Scheme of the MMRDA, built to bring water from the Surya river system in the north of the district, was designed for 403 MLD in all: 185 MLD for Vasai–Virar and 218 MLD for Mira-Bhayandar. [n48 §3; n06; FPJ, 3 Apr 2025; Indian Infrastructure] When it reached the city is disputed. One set of sources says Phase 1 was commissioned in June 2023 and water reached Vasai–Virar in November 2023, released on the chief minister’s order without a formal inauguration; a search summary gives January 2024; and the Free Press Journal, in March 2026, said Surya water reached Virar “last year”, meaning 2025. [n06; n48 §3, §10; Indian Infrastructure, 23 Nov 2023 and 17 Jan 2024; FPJ, 24 Mar 2026] Mumbai Live says the supply began at 8 MLD and rose to 78–85 MLD against a target of 185. [n48 §10; Mumbai Live] The likeliest reading is that water began to flow to parts of the city from late 2023 and reached Virar’s networks in quantity later; the sources do not allow more.

Even then the supply was fragile. In November 2023, by a search snippet, the city needed 372 MLD and got 230. [n48 §3] In March and April 2025 a transformer failure at Surya Nagar halted about 140 MLD; tanker prices rose to Rs 6,000–10,000 a load, against the usual Rs 2,000–3,000. [n48 §3; FPJ, 3 Apr 2025] By June 2026 the city’s sources totalled 400 MLD on paper, 230 from local dams (Surya-Dhamni and Kavdas, Usgaon and Pelhar) and 170 from the MMRDA scheme of a sanctioned 185, but the actual supply “falls well short” because of leaking old pipes; there were forty-seven water towers in the urban zones and fifty-two in the villages. The Kholsapada-2 dam (Rs 135 crore, approved in September 2018) was more than eighty per cent complete and would add 22 MLD; Kholsapada-1 (48 MLD) was pending. [n48 §3; FPJ, 14 June 2026] MMRDA’s Deharji project was planned for 190 MLD. [n29 §4; PropNewsTime]

What water meant in Virar is shown by the housing board’s flats at Bolinj, told in Chapter 29: 9,409 homes built in 2014, largely unsold for years because of water scarcity, and then sold at about twenty-five a day after Surya water arrived. [n48 §3, §8; FPJ, 24 Mar 2026] In 1882 the garden belt was irrigated by hand from shallow wells. In 2007 it needed 30 MLD from wells and tankers. By the 2020s it depended on a regional scheme drawing water from scores of kilometres away. [n48 §3 Inferences] “Rain 2,200 mm but thirsty”: the researchers’ phrase for it is exact. [n48 §3 Inferences]

What goes out: sewage and the Pelhar river

The other half of the water story is what leaves. The Maharashtra Pollution Control Board’s action plan of July 2019 for the Pelhar river, a “Priority IV” polluted stretch, gives the measures. Three drains feed the river: the Waliv Nallah, with a biochemical oxygen demand of 98.6 milligrams per litre; the Sopara Nallah, 76.0; and the Tungar Nallah, 26.6. (Clean river water has a BOD of a few milligrams.) Vasai–Virar generated 105 MLD of sewage, of which thirty were treated, at the one 30 MLD sewage treatment plant operating, at Bolinj; seven more plants, with 175 MLD capacity, were proposed. The city dumped 630 tonnes of solid waste a day. At the Pelhar dam end of the river, 150 tabelas, cattle sheds, held 25,000 to 30,000 animals. [n48 §2; MPCB Pelhar action plan, 2019, Tables 1–6] The plan also recorded that “no major polluting industry is located in the catchment area”: 144 effluent-generating units had their own treatment plants, with a total discharge of 0.334 MLD, and there was no common effluent plant. [n48 §2, §7; MPCB]

So the line of the old Sopara creek, the sea arm that once carried teak rafts and Kutchi cargoes, now carries the treated and untreated sewage of a city of more than a million, and the cattle dung of its milk sheds, to the sea.

Living things: the ridge, the estuary and the shore

Virar is a place where, in the researchers’ phrase, “ghats and mangroves share a municipal boundary”: a sanctuary ridge on one side, the creek and estuary on the other, and the built city between them, with landslide risk on one edge and flood risk on the other. [n48 §6 Inferences]

Tungareshwar

The forest on the slopes of Tungar and Kamandurg, which the Gazetteer called “extensive”, is now the Tungareshwar Wildlife Sanctuary, named for the temple of Tungareshwar on the ridge. Its area is about 85 square kilometres (Wikipedia gives 85.7); one Times of India report gives 95.24, and the sources conflict. [n17; Wikipedia; CAT/TOI] Its notification year was not found. [n17 Gaps] The first systematic study, by Nikit Surve of the Wildlife Conservation Society–India between February and July 2016, confirmed five leopards, against an estimated thirty-five in Sanjay Gandhi National Park to the south. [n17; DNA; CAT] About six hundred plant species, over 250 bird species, 150 butterfly species and thirty-six species of reptiles and amphibians have been recorded, with mammals including wild boar, barking deer, langur, bonnet and rhesus macaques and the black-naped hare. [n17; Nature in Focus; Vajiram & Ravi]

The birds of the ridge were noticed long before the sanctuary. The Gazetteer of 1882 records that the Nilgiri wood pigeon “has been found frequently on Tungar”, that the imperial pigeon was found there, and that a nest with eggs was found “below Tungar”. [n48 §6; Gaz XIII-I, natural history]

The sanctuary’s edge is a battleground. An eco-sensitive zone notified in 2018 put four villages, Paye, Payegaon, Brahmangaon and Khardi, inside the zone and banned mining, stone quarrying and crushing there. In 2020 the Paye-Brahmangaon Mining and Stone Crushers Owners Association asked for the four villages to be excluded, saying their quarries produce “Best Quality hard Black Rock (Basalt Stone)” for Mumbai, Thane and Kalyan, and that the villagers are Adivasis with no other income. [n48 §6; Business World (summary)] The National Green Tribunal issued an order on the zone on 29 January 2020. [n48 §6; India Environment Portal] The Supreme Court in 2022–23 clarified that Tungareshwar’s eco-sensitive zone is the one notified, not a blanket one-kilometre rule. [n48 §6; RP Realty Plus (summary)] The conservation group Vanashakti lists quarrying, road building, the building of religious structures, deforestation and hunting as violations of the buffer. [n48 §6; Vanashakti] And in April 2024, by a Hindustan Times report seen as a summary, “rampant, unauthorised construction is driving wild animals away”; after a landslide in Vasai killed two people, an investigation found construction had encroached on a mountain in the sanctuary area, and a tribunal case, Original Application 137 of 2024, followed. [n48 §6; HT, 28 Apr 2024 (summary)]

The basalt that the Gazetteer said was “easily quarried without blasting” and “largely used on the Bombay and Baroda railway” is still being quarried, for the city’s towers, from the edge of a forest where five leopards live. [n48 §2, §6]

The Vaitarna mangroves

On the estuary north of Virar, a survey by Salvi (Vanmali) and Jadhav, carried out in December 2016 and January 2017 and published in 2022, recorded the mangroves at three sites: the Dativare coast, the Vaitarna bridge and Wadhiv. They found Avicennia marina, the grey mangrove, most abundant at Dativare; Avicennia officinalis abundant at Wadhiv; Avicennia alba; the holly-leaved Acanthus ilicifolius; and Rhizophora mucronata, the stilt-rooted red mangrove, moderate at the Vaitarna bridge and Wadhiv, along with five associate species and one halophyte. The paper counts “4 true species” while naming five, an internal inconsistency, and states that before it “data on mangrove diversity from Palghar district is not available”. Its authors were from S.D.S.M. College, Palghar, and E.S.A. College, Vasai Road. [n48 §6; Salvi & Jadhav, Eco. Env. & Cons. 28 (2022)] A thin fringe of grey mangrove on a silted estuary is not a forest; but it is what holds the mud.

Satellite surveys give a mixed picture. A survey for 2005–19, reported by a search snippet, found that Palghar lost about nineteen square kilometres of sparse mangrove even as it gained eight of denser cover; the Forest Survey of India’s 2021–23 assessment says Palghar gained about 400 hectares. [n48 §6 (snippets)] Residents of the Vasai creek side said around 2018 that mangroves had been cleared from over a hundred acres for buildings “and the sea moves further onto land every day”. [n17; ICSF/Quint] The Vadhvan port to the north, in Dahanu taluka, was cleared with 98.3 acres of mangrove near its site and 10,179 trees to be cut. [n48 §5–6; Mongabay India, 23 Apr 2024] No figure for the mangrove cover of Vasai–Virar alone was found. [n48 §6 Gaps]

Arnala’s shorebirds

On the open shore, the beaches of Arnala and the coast south of it are a wintering ground for migratory shorebirds. Birding guides say Eurasian curlews and bar-tailed godwits use Arnala beach in winter, that the quieter shore toward Mamachi Wadi and Tuss Khadi is favoured, and that gatherings of Eurasian oystercatchers and an occasional crab plover are reported; the eBird hotspot at Bhuigaon beach, further south, lists 237 species. [n48 §6; Outlook Traveller; eBird L4543628; Nature In Focus] These are popular-press sources; no systematic bird list for the Virar–Agashi–Arnala coast from a primary survey was found, and no flamingo records on the Vaitarna (the flamingo grounds of the region are on Thane creek). [n48 §6 Gaps] On the same shore, belts of casuarina, the suru that gave Suruchi beach its name, were planted as coastal defence; Chapter 32 tells their damage in 2019. [n45 §5]

Rice

The work of the Virar side begins with rice, because everything else began after it. In the garden belt, the Gazetteer explained, rice was grown first, in the monsoon; after it the fields were ploughed from November and sugarcane was planted in May. [n48 §1; Gaz XIII-I p. 291] In 1880–81, of about 36,541 acres under tillage on government land in Bassein, grain covered 31,885 acres, and rice alone 29,687 of them; ragi took 1,846 acres. [n21 §2; Gaz XIII-II p. 680 (digits partly uncertain in the scan)] The salt rice lands of the kharpat, the bandhni fields behind their banks, were the ones that failed in bad years; “most of the salt rice land crop failed” in 1848, and “the salt rice crops were partially injured by worms” in 1854. [n48 §1; Gaz XIII-I] Husked rice was among the exports of the Bassein ports in 1881–82, and rice went by sea from the landings of Gas, Bolinj and Vagholi to Surat, Broach, Jambusar, Dholera, Bhavnagar and Gogha. [n21; n48 §1; Gaz XIII-I p. 358; Gaz XIV pp. 315–316]

“The most hardworking and skilful husbandmen are the Christians of Bassein,” the Gazetteer said, who grew sugarcane and plantains and “turned the light sandy country about Bassein into an evergreen garden”. [n21 §2; Gaz XIII-I p. 300] The people of the Sopara–Virar belt it listed as Christian husbandmen, many of them converted Samvedi Brahmins, Muslim Naitas, Hindu Samvedi Brahmins and Bhandaris; its traders were Muslim Naitas and Lad Vanis who had come from Cambay about 1760. [n48 §1; Gaz XIV pp. 315–316] Chapter 31 tells those peoples.

Rice was still grown in the 1980s. “Paddy was widely grown,” a 2019 account of the green belt remembered. [n17; Vikalp Sangam] Then the paddy fields went under buildings, and in the census of 2001 the whole sub-region had only 1,627 cultivators and agricultural labourers among its 157,304 main workers. [n48 §8; CDP Table 6.7–6.8] No acreage series for rice in Vasai taluka after 1881 was found. [n48 §4 Gaps]

The nine plantains

If rice fed the coast, plantains made its fortune. “Sugarcane… is grown all over the district, especially in Bassein where sugarcane and plantains are the chief watered crops,” the Gazetteer said. [n48 §1; Gaz XIII-I p. 291] And it named them: “Nine kinds of Plantains are grown in Bassein, basrai, mutheli, tambdi, rajeli, lokhandi, sonkeli, bankeli, karanjeli, and narsingi.” [n48 §1; n21 §2; Gaz XIII-I p. 291] The soil “must be light and sandy”. Fish manure “is cheaper, wants less water, and gives a better return than any other manure”, though it breeds worms. [n48 §1; Gaz XIII-I pp. 291–292] The fish of the Koli coast fed the gardens of the Kupari and Samvedi villages.

A footnote, from a Mr K. J. Ebden, gives the yearly sales of each variety, and is the best measure of the plantain economy of the 1880s, though the scanned digits are shaky and should be read as orders of magnitude. Green basrai sold about 220,000 bunches a year at Rs 24 the hundred; the red tambdi about 50,000 bunches at Rs 1.5 each; mutheli about 12,000 bunches at twelve annas; rajeli about 5,000 at eight annas (as the scan reads); lokhandi about 1,000; the bright yellow sonkeli about 200; the wild bankeli about 2,000; karanjeli and narsingi about 5,000 each, the latter at a rupee. [n48 §1; Gaz XIII-I p. 292 n.1] In all, about 300,000 bunches a year, mostly basrai. [n21 §2]

Nothing of the plant was wasted. The flower spike, kelphul, was sold as a vegetable at fourteen annas a dozen; the leaves were sold as plates at four to eight annas a hundred; and the dried, sun-powdered fruit of the wild bankeli was given as a nourishing food to the sick and to women after childbirth. [n48 §1; Gaz XIII-I p. 292] Banana flower, kelphul, is still listed among Vasai’s monsoon vegetables in a 2015 account. [n17; India Food Network]

Dried plantains: the Agashi trade

The most famous product of the Virar side was a plantain that had been cooked by the sun. “The well-known Bassein dried-plantains are the fruit of the rajeli variety. They are prepared only in the villages of Agashi, Vagholi, Vatar, Bolinj, Koprad, Nala, Umrale, Rajodi, and Murdes, all in Bassein.” [n48 §1; n21 §2; Gaz XIII-I p. 292] The list is very nearly the village belt round Virar.

The Gazetteer describes the process step by step. The ripe bunch was laid in a basket of rice straw for six or seven days, to produce heat; the fruit was peeled and spread “on a booth close to the sea shore”, and dried in the sun all day. In the evening it was heaped and covered with dry plantain leaves and a mat. Each time it was smeared with clarified butter, and the drying was repeated for seven days. [n48 §1; Gaz XIII-I p. 292] Booths by the shore, fruit under mats, ghee and sun for a week: it was a coastal craft, and the villages that practised it were those where the garden met the sea.

The scale was considerable. “At Agashi the yearly yield of dried plantains is estimated at 160 tons (3000 Bengal mans) worth about £2700 (Rs. 27,000).” [n48 §1; n21 §2; Gaz XIII-I p. 292] The crafts chapter of the same volume names the people who made them: about eighty-five families of Samvedi Brahmins, fifteen of Pachkalshis and about seventy-five of Native Christians, about 175 families in all, drying plantains at Agashi, Vatar and Koprad in a season that ran from October to January. They sold the dried fruit “to Thana, Bombay, Surat, Poona, and Sholapur” at about Rs 9 a Bengal maund of eighty pounds. They also cut unripe plantains into slices and sold them as fast-day food to high-caste Hindus. [n48 §7; Gaz XIII-I, Crafts, “Plantain-drying”] Brahmin, Pachkalshi and Christian families, side by side on the same shore, made the same product for the same markets: here, in a single paragraph of 1882, is the mixed society of the Virar villages at work.

The seventy-five Christian families in that sentence are the clearest picture the record gives of the Kuparis at work. The Gazetteer called them “Native Christians originally Samvedis and Pachkalshis”, and described the Christians among the driers as “gardeners who grow the fruit”; their villages, Agashi, Vatar and Koprad, and the other plantain villages of the list, Vagholi, Bolinj, Umrale, Rajodi and Murdes, are almost exactly the Kupari villages of today. Agashi in 1881 had 1,500 Christians among its 6,823 people, and the congregation of the Holy Spirit church at Koprad, which is very probably Nandakhal’s, was 2,415 in 1882. [n34; n40; n46; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 292, 395; Gaz XIV pp. 2, 210] The Gazetteer does not divide the seventy-five families between the three villages, and no family name among them is recorded. Chapter 31 (Section IV) and Part Seven, Chapters 35 and K4, tell the Kupari side of the trade.

The dried plantain, suki keli, outlived the Gazetteer’s trade. A 2015 food history calls it a signature Vasai snack, and Vasai sukeli is among the things sold at the stalls of the Nirmal yatra each November, from Nirmal Naka to Bhuigaon Phata. [n17; n20; India Food Network; Lokmat] When the Agashi industry of 175 families ended, and whether any family still dries rajeli by the shore, is not recorded. [n48 §4 Gaps]

Velchi and the later bananas

The plantain economy changed its varieties but not its logic. In the twentieth century the prized banana of the coast was the velchi, the small sweet fruit that “sold at a premium”, by a 2019 account; Vasai’s sweet yellow-skinned bananas were chosen for naivedya, temple offerings, and for shikaran, banana in cardamom milk. [n17; Vikalp Sangam; India Food Network] The flower growers of the 1980s, by 101Reporters and The Better India, had grown mogra, betel leaf or velchi bananas before they switched to flowers. [n48 §4; 101Reporters; The Better India] No banana acreage or output series for Vasai taluka after 1882 was found. [n17 Gaps; n48 §4 Gaps]

Agashi: the port that exported the garden

The plantains, the betel leaves and the salt went out through Agashi, and the Gazetteer’s entry on the town is a portrait of a working port in the last years before the railway took its trade. “In 1837 Agashi was the chief town on Bassein island with a large Christian and Hindu population, and a brisk trade to Gujarat and Bombay in grain, garden produce, and salt. Very good fishing boats and coasting craft were built.” In 1881 it had 6,823 people: 5,168 Hindus, 1,500 Christians and 155 Muslims. “Agashi drives a great trade with Bombay in plantains and betel leaves, its dried plantains being the best in the district.” [n48 §1; n21; Gaz XIV pp. 1–2]

The sea-trade returns for the five years to 1879 show an average of Rs 3,18,470 in exports and Rs 85,650 in imports a year; exports rose from Rs 2,46,430 in 1875 to Rs 4,81,810 in 1879. [n48 §1; n21; n36; Gaz XIV p. 2] Exports nearly four times imports: Agashi sold the garden’s surplus and bought little. Its molasses were prized in Gujarat for gudaku, the tobacco-and-molasses paste smoked in hookahs. [n36; Gaz XIII-I p. 338] The three ports of the Bassein division together, Agashi, Bassein and Navghar, had an estimated trade of £108,868 in 1881–82, exporting husked rice, molasses, coconuts and plantains to Bombay and Gujarat, and firewood and tiles to Bombay, and importing wheat, pulse, ghee, timber, piece-goods, oil and oil-cake and lime; their traders were “Musalmans, Marathas, local Vanis, and a few Brahmans”. [n21; n36; Gaz XIII-I p. 358] Their craft, mhangiris, machvas, paddvas and batelas of fifty to seventy tons, were “built locally by Marathas and Native Christians”. [n21; Gaz XIII-I pp. 358–359]

Agashi was also a small credit centre. The old system of bills of exchange, the hundi, survived in 1882 at Mahim and Bassein, where bills were cashed up to Rs 20,000, and in the Bassein villages of Nala, Agashi, Sopara and Navghar, up to Rs 10,000. [n48 §1; Gaz XIII-I] The port’s earlier history, its shipbuilding for Portuguese voyages to Europe in the sixteenth century and its customs revenues in the 1630s, is told in Chapters 07 and T1. [n36]

The railway changed the direction of the trade. Sopara’s molasses, rice and betel leaf went out by sea from the Gas, Bolinj and Vagholi landings to Gujarat, but by 1882 they also went “by rail chiefly from the Virar station” north to Ankleshwar, Nadiad, Viramgam and Patan, and “large quantities of plantains and betel-leaves are sent south to Bombay”. In return, oilcake from the Gujarat ports was “in great demand” as manure for the cane. [n48 §1; Gaz XIV pp. 315–316] A four-mile metalled feeder road from Virar to Agashi was one of the new roads built to bring produce to the Baroda line. [n48 §1; Gaz XIII-I] Virar station’s goods traffic rose from 1,730 tons in 1878 to 3,349 tons in 1880. [n48 §1; Gaz XIV p. 382] The researchers’ summary of what happened next is hard to improve: in 1881 Agashi alone exported Rs 3.2 lakh a year; “the exportable surplus has been replaced by exported people”. [n48 §8 Inferences]

Sugarcane and molasses

The second of the “chief watered crops” was sugarcane, and Virar’s own entry in the Gazetteer begins with it: “a rich village well-wooded and with many patches of sugarcane”. [n48 §1; Gaz XIV p. 382] After the rice harvest the fields were ploughed from November and the cane planted in May; about 750,000 canes were sent each year “chiefly to Bombay, Surat, and Broach” at Rs 14 the hundred. [n48 §1; Gaz XIII-I p. 291] The cane was manured with oil-cake, which the Christians of the garden called phod and others khap; the oil-cake came by sea from the ports of Gujarat. [n48 §1; Gaz XIII-I p. 291; Gaz XIV p. 316] In 1880–81 Bassein had 1,188 acres of sugarcane. [n21 §2]

The juice was boiled into molasses, gul, the chief product of Sopara and its neighbourhood with rice, plantains and betel leaf. [n48 §1; Gaz XIV p. 315] An attempt to industrialise it failed. The Bassein Sugar Company of the 1830s to 1860s did not prosper; in 1843 the firm of McGregor Brownrigg & Co. blamed “the poorness of the soil and the want of shelter”. [n48 §7; Gaz XIII-I, Sugar-making] The garden’s cane remained a household crop, crushed and boiled in the villages. Who boiled it the Gazetteer says plainly: sugar in Bassein was “chiefly made by Pachkalshis, Malis, Native Christians and Samvedi Brahmans”, the same Christian and Hindu garden families who dried the plantains; in the Virar-side villages the Native Christians were the Kuparis’ forebears (Chapter 31, Section IV; Chapter 35). [n34; n41; Gaz XIII-1 p. 395] In the 2020s the 101Reporters account lists sugarcane among the “former crops” of the area. [n17; 101Reporters]

Betel, areca and coconut

Three more garden crops complete the Gazetteer’s picture, and two of them are palms.

The betel vine, the pan vel whose leaf is chewed with areca nut and lime, was grown in the gardens alongside plantains, gourds and colocasia, and cut after two years. [n48 §1; Gaz XIII-I pp. 293–294] The Gazetteer calls it a chief watered crop of Mahim rather than Bassein, but Agashi’s “great trade with Bombay in plantains and betel leaves” and the rail exports of betel leaf from Virar show its importance here. [n21 §2; Gaz XIV pp. 2, 316] A twentieth-century memory adds a surprising destination: betel leaf from the green belt “was exported to Pakistan”, by a 2019 account. [n17; Vikalp Sangam] The flower growers of Arnala grew betel leaf before they turned to flowers. [n48 §4; 101Reporters] No acreage of betel in the area was found, and the Marathi terms nagvel and panmala were suggested as the way to look. [n17 Gaps]

The areca or betel-nut palm, the supari, was grown in gardens watered every second day for five years before the palms bore. [n48 §1; Gaz XIII-I pp. 297–298] The Gazetteer names Papdi Vanis as processors of the nut. [n21 §2] The growers, it said, were “partly Christians, Malis converted by the Portuguese, and partly Hindus of the Chavkalshi or Pachkalshi class”; on the Virar side those Christian gardeners would have been Kupari, though the Gazetteer names no village. [n41; Gaz XIII-1 ch. IV]

The coconut palm was “chiefly grown in Salsette, Bassein, Kelve-Mahim and Tarapur”; Bassein seedlings sold for three annas four pies to four annas, and some Bassein gardeners watered grown trees every seventh or eighth day. [n48 §1; Gaz XIII-I pp. 295–297] A footnote holds a small surprise for the later story: if sonchafa, the golden champak, Michelia champaca, is planted among coconut palms, “their strong perfume drives off the bees”. [n48 §1; Gaz XIII-I p. 297 n.] Sonchafa was already a garden tree here in 1882. The city plan of 2010 still described the coastal belt as “fringed with dense thickets of cocoanut groves with a near pastoral ambience… famous for Bananas & beetle leaves”. [n48 §4; CDP p. 27]

The palms were also tapped. A tapping cess was levied on brab, coconut and date palms; “in Bassein and Agashi the bud-dene cess was compounded with an excise cess” of about a rupee and two annas on each coconut and brab palm and about seven annas on each date palm. [n21; Gaz XIII-II ch. X (fractions uncertain in the scan)] And Virar had a craft that served the toddy trade: earthen pots called kondya, made at Virar, were sold for collecting palm juice. [n49 §3a; Gaz XIII-I] The Bhandaris, the tappers of the coast, are among the communities the Gazetteer names in the Sopara–Virar belt; no source on toddy tapping in Virar after 1882 was found. [n48 §1; n17 Gaps]

The Saturday market

All this produce had a local market as well as a distant one. In 1882, east of Virar station, “across the railway, is the market place, on the west bank of a large pond under big banyan and mango trees. A large market is held every Saturday, thronged by Kolis and Varlis, selling fish, garden stuff, and cloth.” [n48 §1; Gaz XIV p. 382] The trade chapter adds that Bassein had four weekly markets, at Agashi, Sopara, a place the scan reads as “Dhovh” (not identified) and Virar, held on Monday, Wednesday, Friday and Saturday and attended by 500 to 600 people, among fifty-three market towns in the district. [n48 §7; n49 §3a; Gaz XIII-I] Virar also had a post office. [n49 §3a]

The Saturday market of Virar was a meeting of the coast and the hills: the Kolis bringing fish from the creek and sea, the Warlis coming down from the forest villages of the east, and the garden villages selling their vegetables and fruit, with cloth for all. It was the commercial centre of a rural world, on the bank of a pond, under trees. A present-day description of Virar’s markets, their location and volume, was not found. [n48 §8 Gaps] Chapter 29 sets beside it the Virar Flea Market of October–November 2026.

The kawadis and the vegetables of the green belt

For most of the twentieth century the gardens’ surplus went to the city on the train, carried by the farmers themselves. Villages of the Vasai–Virar sub-region supplied vegetables to Mumbai’s suburbs “until the early 1980s”, by a 2019 account. [n17; Vikalp Sangam] The farmer-vendors carried their produce on a kawad, a shoulder pole with a loaded basket at each end, by the four o’clock train to Victoria Terminus, and sold at Bora Bazaar and near the Kabutarkhana at Dadar, where customers called them “Vasaiwale mama” and “Vasaiwale maushi”, uncle and auntie from Vasai. [n17; n48 §4; India Food Network, 6 Oct 2015]

What they sold followed the seasons. In the monsoon there were garlic chives, cilantro, kantol (teasel gourd) and banana flower; in winter, field beans, ghevda and toor; and through the year light-green okra, brinjal, colocasia leaves for alu wadi, and bottle gourd. [n17; India Food Network] Vasai’s white onion still sells in Mumbai at Rs 140–170 a bunch, by a Marathi report, and avocado is a new crop in Vasai. [n20; Lokmat; Pudhari]

The kawadis faded, the 2015 account says, as farm families moved to office jobs, as contractors tied farmers to supermarket chains, and as produce from Nashik and Pune took over the Mumbai market. [n17; India Food Network] Chapter 14 tells their last decades. The sources do not say which communities the Vasaiwale vendors came from. What the Kupari record adds is the same turn, told from inside the garden villages: community writers say the Kuparis depended mostly on farming until the late 1960s and early 1970s and then moved into engineering, medicine, banking and information technology, and abroad to the Gulf, Europe, North America and Australia; and the Nandakhal parish history remembers a 1920s parishioner’s verdict on its short-lived English school, “we get bread and butter from farming and not by studying”. Both are community testimony, without figures. [n07; n34; n40; Rajan D’Mello blog 2009] Part Seven, Chapter 37, tells the Kupari migration.

Flowers: from mogra to sonchafa

The garden belt’s last great cash crop is flowers, and they are grown above all on the Virar coast. A 2011 study of urban agriculture in the Mumbai region found that traditional farming with crop rotation and intercropping persisted in Arnala, Virar, Vasai and Nallasopara, and that Arnala and Agashi had “an extensive flower system” peaking at Ganesh Chaturthi, Diwali and Navratri; ninety per cent of the flower growers named price swings as their main constraint, with prices rising up to twenty-fold at festivals. [n17; n48 §4; Vazhacharickal & Buerkert, Leituras de Economia Política 19 (2011)]

By 2023, 101Reporters counted about five hundred floriculturist families in about twenty-five villages of Vasai–Virar, on plots from four gunthas to three acres, along an eighteen-kilometre stretch from the Arnala coast to Vasai. [n17; 101Reporters, Jan 2023] Their earlier favourite was mogra, the Arabian jasmine, Jasminum sambac, which fetched premium prices at Dadar flower market. Then came sonchafa. [n17]

The new sonchafa was a grafted cultivar, the “Velankar chafa”, developed by Uday Velankar of Vetal-Bambarde near Kudal in Sindhudurg, who in 2025 ran a twenty-acre nursery with three thousand mother plants producing about ten thousand grafts a year, sold at about Rs 150 a plant. It came to Arnala in the late 1980s through Moreshwar Bhoir, grandfather of Kiran Patil, the chairman of the Arnala Farmers’ Cooperative Society, which has 1,100 members. [n17; n48 §4; 101Reporters; The Better India, 18 Aug 2025] The Gazetteer’s footnote of 1882, about sonchafa trees among the coconut palms driving off bees, shows that the flower itself was old in these gardens; what was new in the 1980s was the commercial graft. [n48 §4 Inferences] The move from mogra to sonchafa followed the same logic as the move from dried plantains to vegetables to flowers: whatever perishable crop the city wanted, grown in the sweet-water gardens near the railway. [n48 §4 Inferences]

The economics are set out in the two reports, which do not quite agree. The Dadar market is about an hour and a half away. A box of a hundred flowers sells for about Rs 100 by The Better India’s account, about Rs 150 by 101Reporters’, rising to about Rs 700 at Ganesh Chaturthi. Each shrub gives 150 to 200 flowers a day. Subhash Bhatte of Satpala picked about 11,000 flowers a day from one acre, earning, by 101Reporters, about Rs 75,000 a month, and by The Better India up to Rs 9 lakh a year after expenses. [n17; n48 §4; 101Reporters; The Better India]

Then the weather turned against them. Unseasonal rain, the humidity and fungus that followed Cyclone Mandous in December 2022, and fewer hours of light roughly halved the yield; Bhatte’s output fell to a few thousand flowers a day. Flower growers get no crop insurance and no Kisan Credit Card loans; and waterlogging has worsened as open fields have vanished around them. [n17; 101Reporters] One plausible reading, offered in the notes but not stated by any source, is that sonchafa suited a coast whose ponds were failing because it needs little water. [n17 Inferences] Chapter 15 and Chapter 16 tell the flower growers’ story across the taluka.

Which communities grow the flowers the reports do not say. Agashi, one of the two flower centres of the 2011 study, is a Kupari village with Hindu Samvedi and Pachkalshi neighbours, and Arnala is a Koli village whose flower pioneer, Moreshwar Bhoir, is not identified by community; so Kupari flower growers are likely among the five hundred families, but no source names one. [n17; n39; n40] The one Kupari village where the notes do record flowers is on the Vasai side: at Bhuigaon, a 2011 account describes about forty large irrigation wells, kept by the women, watering bananas, vegetables and flowers behind a steel cross that the extended Marti family treats as its protector, “the local equivalent of a Gaondevi”. [n07; n11; Mumbai Paused 2011] Chapter 36 tells that village.

Fishing: the dol nets of Arnala

The sea that bounds Virar on the west has always been worked, and the fishing people of the Virar coast are the Kolis: Son Kolis, Mangelas and Vaitis, whose origins and villages Chapter 31 and Chapter 32 tell. Narangi, by the account already cited, is a Mangela village; Arnala, Navapur and Kalamb are named in an unverified list of Mangela Koli villages. [n49 §3c]

In 1851 a survey counted twenty-two salt-water and eighteen fresh-water fisheries in Bassein, out of 129 and 101 in the district. [n48 §1; n21; Gaz XIII-I fn.] Before 1844 Kolis, Mangelas and Vaitis paid a poll tax, the day-dene, of a rupee and a quarter for the privilege of fishing. [n36] In 1881 the taluka counted 2,375 Mangelas. [n36; Gaz XIII-II p. 680] By then the coastal fishermen as far as Bassein had taken to jolly-boats and outrigger canoes. [n48 §1; Gaz XIII-I] Local craft were hauled ashore from early June until Coconut Day, Narali Purnima, at the end of the monsoon. [n36; Gaz XIII-I p. 342]

The modern evidence centres on Arnala. “Arnala is one of the major dolnet landing centres in Thane District of Maharashtra,” a Central Marine Fisheries Research Institute note of 2010 by J. D. Sarang and Sujit Sundaram begins. “Dolnet is a gear exclusively used in Maharashtra and Gujarat… anchored to poles fixed to the sea bottom and generally operated from August to May. At Arnala, approximately 375 dolnets are operated and the operation is generally confined to a depth range of 18–22 m.” [n48 §5; CMFRI 2010, AGRIS record] A dol is a great bag net, held open by the tide; a 2004 CMFRI note names Bombay duck, bombil, as the main species at Arnala, and the 2010 note describes the oil sardine as an emerging alternative for the dolnetters. [n48 §5] The count of 375 dolnets is the only Arnala-specific gear count found; no later count exists in the notes. [n48 §5 Gaps]

The wider Vasai fleet tells of a shrinking sea. A 2016 Times of India piece, reprinted by the International Collective in Support of Fishworkers, called Vasai “a one-fish town”, dependent on pomfret: catches of catfish, shrimp and pomfret had fallen fifty to eighty per cent in gill and dol nets; the number of dol-net boats in Vasai had doubled in a decade to about a hundred, by the Koli boat owner Vishnu Ranghucha; trawlers and purse-seiners took about seventy-five per cent of the state’s catch; pollution came down the Vasai creek and plastics came up in the nets; Maharashtra’s catch fell twenty-three per cent in 2015. [n48 §5; n17; ICSF; Citizen Matters] A 2007 study, cited by a search summary, counted 5,007 active fishermen in Vasai taluka in 1997. [n36] The CMFRI’s Marine Fisheries Census of 2016 has village-level counts of fishers and boats for Palghar, but could not be opened. [n48 §5 Gaps]

At Arnala the fishers have organised: the Fishermen’s Sarvoday Sahakari Society and the Arnala Fishermen’s Association, which keeps a rescue boat named Namo Maria, appear in reports of rescues off the coast; their membership was not found. [n48 §5; FPJ] Down To Earth recounts a boat’s engine failing near Arnala in a piece on fisherfolk livelihoods. [n17]

Vadhvan

North of Arnala, in Dahanu taluka, lies the greatest threat the fishers of this coast have faced: the Vadhvan (Vadhavan) port. It is a Rs 76,220 crore project of the Jawaharlal Nehru Port and the Maharashtra Maritime Board on 17,471 hectares, cleared by the environment ministry in February 2024 and upheld by the Bombay High Court on 18 April 2024; about three thousand fishing vessels are registered between Palghar and Vasai; 51,991 objections were filed at the public hearing of 19 January 2024, and thirty-nine gram panchayats opposed it. [n48 §5; Mongabay India, 23 Apr 2024] The villages named in the clearance reporting are Vadhavan, Varor, Dhakti Dahanu, Dahanu, Chinchani, Ghivali, Gungwada and Dhumket; Arnala, Navapur and Kalamb are not named. [n48 §5] The Prime Minister laid its foundation stone on 30 August 2024 amid protests; a fishing leader said it would reclaim about 5,000 acres of sea and restrict about 30,000 acres more, and research cited by the fishers said it would directly hit sixteen fishing villages in Palghar. [n17; n29 §4; FPJ] In December 2025 the Palghar collector, Indu Rani Jakhar, called for a full social and livelihood survey of affected fishers; in November 2026 the port signed pacts worth Rs 5,700 crore and aimed at a first phase in 2029. [n17; n06] Chapter 16 tells the port’s story; for the dolnetters of Arnala, the question is what a container port at the next river mouth north will do to the fish that come down the coast.

Salt

The salt pans of the Virar side were part of a Bassein industry that the Gazetteer described in detail. Salt was among the exports in Agashi’s “brisk trade to Gujarat and Bombay in grain, garden produce, and salt” in 1837. [n48 §1; Gaz XIV p. 2] Salt-makers in the 1880s were paid by the piece, the day, the month or a share; “average earnings for the whole season (January–May) range from £2 to £5”, that is, Rs 20 to 50. [n48 §1, §5; Gaz XIII-I pp. 365–366] Bassein workmen were paid by a unit of eighty Bengal maunds, Rs 5 to 8. The salt tax was Rs 2 a Bengal maund of 82½ pounds, and the ground rent in Bassein Rs 1½ an acre. [n21 §3; Gaz XIII-I pp. 365–366]

The workers came from several communities. Agris worked the pans in Bassein, and Kharvas; and in the 1870s the salt proprietor Manekji Kharshedji brought three or four hundred Dublas and Dhodias, Adivasi labourers, from Surat “for the manufacture of salt at his pans in Bassein”, with much better salt than the district had made before. The Dublas, the Gazetteer said, “work only on the salt-works near Bassein Road station”. [n48 §1, §5; n21 §3; Gaz XIII-I pp. 365–366, 370] Since 1876, when the salt trade was opened to private enterprise, quality had improved, and Bassein salt competed in local markets and along the railways. [n21 §3; Gaz XIII-I p. 370]

By 2007 the salt pans, as noted above, covered 1,065 hectares of the urbanisable zone, with mass production only at Umela, south of the Virar side. [n48 §5; CDP] The researchers’ observation is that salt, fish and plantains were all “coast edge” trades in 1882; all three have shrunk, and the salt-pan edge has become the city’s floodplain and its political fight. [n48 §5 Inferences] No Portuguese or twentieth-century salt revenue figures for Bassein, and no count of Agri salt workers today, were found. [n17 Gaps; n21 §3 Gaps]

Sand

The creeks and the estuary hold another resource, and its extraction divides the people who live by the water. The city plan of 2010 records, approvingly, that sand is “extracted through the Vaitarna Creek as building material by dredging which helps the creek to remain desilted”. [n48 §3, §5; CDP p. 26] The fishers see it otherwise. Fishing groups say that sand mining in the Vasai creek destroys fishing; the Collector banned dredging between Bhayander and the villages south of Vasai in June 2006. [n48 §5; DNA (summary)] A public-interest petition in the Bombay High Court concerned illegal sand excavation on an island in the Vaitarna creek within the limits of the revenue village of Naringi, post Virar; in 2018 the state told the court that fifteen sand sites, eleven on the Tansa and four on the Vaitarna, had been destroyed. [n48 §5; HT, 10 Sept 2018 (summary)] In August 2024 the Watchdog Foundation wrote to the chief minister about mining along the Vasai creek. [n48 §7; FPJ, 7 Aug 2024]

Sand work has its own dangers. The twenty-one workers rescued by the fire brigade at Chandip in Virar East in a flood of 2019 were at a sand site. [n48 §2; FPJ] On the Arnala shore, locals blame the depressions left by sand mining for drownings; the Arnala sarpanch says picnickers ignore the warnings. [n45 §5; FPJ] Chapter 32 tells the drownings.

And sand has its politics. Naringi’s corporator, by Wikipedia’s unreferenced account, was the chairman of a sand-lifters’ cooperative. [n49 §3c] The Mangela fishing village at the end of the Naringi road had, in part, become a sand village.

Small industry: Waliv, Gokhivare, Sativali

Virar never became an industrial town, and the reason is a planning rule. “Due to the industrial location policy for the MMR, there are few small scale industries located in this sub-region mainly in Valiv-Gokhivare area,” the city plan says. “Vasai Municipal Council has also developed its own small-scale industrial estate… few small-scale industries have come up to the east of Vasai-Road Railway Station and on Shirsat Road in Virar Town. The MMRDA had prepared a plan for Waliv-Gokhivare-Sativali Industrial Complex.” [n48 §7; CDP §3.7, p. 27] Industry covered 155.4 hectares of the urbanisable zone, 1.7 per cent of it. [n48 §4, §7; CDP Table 9.2] “The industrial profile of VVSR is mainly service industry in small scale sector & not heavy manufacturing industry.” [n48 §7; CDP p. 49]

The estates lie mostly on the Vasai East side, south of Virar: Waliv, Gokhivare, Sativali, Navghar and Gauraipada. Gokhivare, a census town of 19,772 people in 2001, was in 1882 the home of Christian Vadval timber cartmen, “the best axemen in the district”. [n49 §3c; Wikipedia: Gokhivare; Gaz XIII-I pp. 97–98] A Deccan Herald flood report, probably of 2018, said that “more than 12,000 industrial units are spread across Navghar, Gokhivare, Waliv, Sativali and Gauraipada”; the page could not be opened and the number is unverified. [n48 §7 (snippet)] A DNA opinion piece mentions about five hundred hectares of industrial land in eastern Virar, also unverified; the Maharashtra Pollution Control Board lists a Gowaripada Industrial Estate in Vasai East. [n47; DNA; MPCB] The Palghar district’s own page says that Vasai, Virar and Nallasopara have small and large industrial areas, and that Virar workers commute north to the Tarapur industrial estate near Boisar. [n47; Palghar district] No history of an industrial estate in Virar itself, and no unit counts, employment or turnover for any estate from an official source, were found. [n47 Gaps; n48 §7 Gaps]

The pollution board’s 2019 plan for the Pelhar river counted 144 effluent-generating industries in its catchment, all with their own small treatment plants, and “no major polluting industry”. [n48 §7; MPCB] In June 2026 frequent unannounced power cuts were reported to be disrupting Vasai–Virar’s industrial units. [n48 §7; FPJ (snippet)]

The contrast with 1882 is complete. The crafts of the old Virar side, sugar, plantain-drying, boatbuilding and salt, all came from the sweet-water garden and the creek. The modern industry is of the opposite kind: stone, sand and small factories on the dry eastern side of the line. [n48 §7 Inferences]

Stone and brick

On the eastern edge, where the plain meets the hills, the two oldest building materials of the coast are still made. The quarries of the Rajawali area and east of the railway, in the Green Zone, cut the basalt; the quarry villages inside the Tungareshwar eco-sensitive zone, as their owners’ association said in 2020, supply “hard Black Rock” to Mumbai, Thane and Kalyan, and claim that their Adivasi workers have no other income. [n48 §6–7; CDP pp. 26, 64; Business World (summary)] No count of quarries round Tungareshwar was found. [n48 §6 Gaps]

The brick kilns are worked largely by migrant Adivasi labour. The People’s Archive of Rural India reported a group of eighteen Katkari Adivasis migrating to the kilns at Bhatane, in the Vasai block on the Tansa, during the COVID-19 period. [n48 §7; n17; PARI] Across Palghar district, kiln owners have been booked for forcing tribal families into bonded labour, and NGOs have fought the bayana, the advance payment whose debt binds a family to a kiln. [n17; Deccan Herald; ThePrint; NewsDrum] Anti-Slavery International estimates more than 100,000 kilns in India employing about 23 million workers, with debt-inflated advances as the mechanism. [n17] Vivek Pandit, Hitendra Thakur’s great rival, began his work in rural Vasai against bonded labour, founding the Shramajeevi Sanghatana in 1982. [n26 §2] No count of kilns in Vasai taluka was found; an inventory for Thane and Palghar exists in a National Green Tribunal report that was not opened. [n48 §7 Gaps]

A dormitory economy: commuting and work

The defining economic fact of modern Virar is written in the city plan in two sentences. “The people of VVSR have their work places located in Mumbai. About 60% of workers commute to Mumbai in the morning & return in the evening putting pressure on Public Transport system.” And: “VVSR presently serves as a growing dormitory town to the Mumbai city.” [n48 §8; CDP Executive Summary, p. 23] The plan adds a second dependency: “the industrialist and builders and developers are staying in Mumbai City and carrying their business in VVSR”. [n48 §8; CDP p. 23] It speaks of “unidirectional commuting during the peak hours”. [n48 §8; CDP p. 24]

The census of 2001, tabulated in the same plan, gives the shape of the workforce. The sub-region had 702,723 people, of whom 166,924 worked: 138,014 men and 28,910 women. There were 157,304 main workers and 9,620 marginal workers. Cultivators and agricultural labourers together numbered 1,627 (1,072 men, 555 women), and household-industry workers 4,005; the “other workers”, the clerks, shopkeepers, drivers, builders and every other kind of urban work, are printed as 161,292, a figure that exceeds the main-worker total, so the table has an arithmetic error in it somewhere. [n48 §8; CDP Tables 6.7–6.8, pp. 48–49] The plan concludes that a “negligible percentage (1.034%)” are in the primary sector and calls the city “tertiary”, with “98% of the working population”. [n48 §8; CDP] From the same table, about seventeen per cent of women and thirty-eight per cent of men were counted as working, a derived figure not stated in the source. [n48 §8]

The coastal villages, home to 77,739 people in 2001 and “mainly engaged in agriculture, horticulture and fishing”, were in the plan’s words “now more or less stagnant due to recent urban growth taking place near the railway stations”, while the station towns were “mainly engaged in tertiary sector”. [n48 §4, §8; CDP pp. 46–47] By the census of 2011 the corporation counted 490,018 workers among its 1,222,390 people, by one compilation; the census’s breakdown of those workers by type, and its journey-to-work tables, were not found. [n49 §1h; n48 §8 Gaps]

So Virar’s economy is, above all, the economy of its station. In 1880, the Gazetteer recorded 83,176 passengers at Virar in a year. In the 2020s, by an undated report, about 5.8 lakh a day. [n48 §8; Gaz XIV p. 382; FPJ (snippet)] The 1882 village exported dried plantains, molasses and betel leaf. The 2026 town exports people, every morning, and buys them back every night. [n48 §8 Inferences]

The garden villages joined that commute long before the boom. The 1882 Gazetteer already noted that English-speaking Christians of the Thana coast worked in Bombay as clerks, a remark written mainly of Salsette, and the Kupari families of Agashi, Nandakhal, Bolinj and Umrale, by their own account, left farming for city professions and emigration from the late 1960s. [n34; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 205–206; Rajan D’Mello blog 2009] No source counts Kupari commuters or emigrants. Part Seven, Chapter 37, follows them.

Land as livelihood: real estate

Since 1988 the most valuable crop of the Virar plain has been the plain itself. The sequence is told in Chapters 14, 14 and V3: the de-reservation of the green belt in 1988 against the advice of the metropolitan planning authority; the shift of about 8,500 hectares to urbanisable land in May 1990; and the building boom that followed, much of it unauthorised. [n17; n48 §8; TIFR] The 2007 sanctioned plan’s own land-use figures show how far the change had gone, and how far it had not. The non-urbanisable zone of 28,646.79 hectares was still 68.4 per cent agriculture, cattle and grazing land (19,603.55 hectares), 30.1 per cent forest (8,626.26 hectares) and 123.15 hectares of water. The urbanisable zone of 9,353.21 hectares was 58.1 per cent agriculture (5,437.06 hectares), 16.6 per cent residential (1,550.25 hectares), 11.4 per cent salt pans (1,065 hectares), 1.7 per cent industry (155.4 hectares) and 233.2 hectares of water. [n48 §4; CDP Tables 9.1–9.2] These figures describe the sub-region of about 380 square kilometres as the sanctioned plan found it, a baseline of 2007; much of the “agriculture” in the urbanisable zone has been built on since. [n48 §4]

What the land became is told in Chapter 29: Global City’s two hundred acres on the kharpat at Chikhal Dongari, MHADA’s 9,409 homes at Bolinj, and asking prices of Rs 6,000 to 10,000 a square foot in 2026. [n47; n48 §8] Its cost is told there too, and in Chapter 16: the forty-one illegal buildings of Agarwal Nagari in Nallasopara East, built in 2009–12 on land reserved for a dumping ground and a sewage plant, bought by “daily wage earners, labourers, and lower-middle-class families”, demolished by February 2025 on the High Court’s order, with no rehabilitation policy framed; and the Enforcement Directorate’s allegation of a Rs 9,000 crore fraud, with Rs 32 crore seized in raids in May 2025 from the corporation’s town-planning official Y. S. Reddy and others, which remained allegations. [n48 §8; ThePrint; ED press release, 3 July 2025] The land that had fed the coast for centuries now fed the corporation’s revenue and the builders’ profits, and, in the bad cases, the courts.

The civic economy: a budget of Rs 4,209 crore

The corporation that governs Virar is itself one of the largest economic actors in it. On 9–10 March 2026 Commissioner Manojkumar Suryawanshi presented the budget for 2026–27: Rs 4,208.99 crore, against a revised Rs 3,194.71 crore for 2025–26 (the Press Trust of India gave Rs 4,206.87 crore). Because collections from property tax, town-planning fees and market fees had fallen short, property tax was raised by fifteen per cent and the water tax doubled. Capital grants doubled, from Rs 320.78 crore to Rs 642.61 crore. Among its projects was a 400-bed hospital at Achole, and the budget was described as designed around “health, infrastructure, and tourism”. [n48 §8–9; FPJ, 11 Mar 2026]

The sources of the revenue say something about the economy beneath it. A corporation that lives on property tax and town-planning fees lives on building; when building slows, so does the budget. The doubling of the water tax, in a city whose water comes from scores of kilometres away through leaking pipes, is the price of the Surya. And the town-planning fees are the same fees whose irregular collection the Enforcement Directorate alleged in the Agarwal Nagari case. [n48 §8; n06]

Pilgrims and day-trippers

The last livelihood of the Virar side is visitors. They come for three things: the fort and beach at Arnala, the goddess at Jivdani, and the hills.

Arnala fort, “Jaldurg” or “Janjire Arnala” on the island at the mouth of the Vaitarna, is by the Palghar district’s tourism page about seventy-four kilometres from Mumbai and about ten from Virar station, reached by a short ferry from Arnala beach; there is no food on the fort, only a well. The beach is crowded with local tourists at weekends and swimming is not advised. [n48 §9; Palghar district (page last updated 26 Oct 2020)] Ferry timings and fares (Rs 10 to 50) vary across sources. [n45 §4] Maharashtra’s tourism department lists “Construction of jetty at Arnala fort, Tal Vasai” as a planned item, without a cost. [n48 §9] Around the beaches of Arnala and Navapur grew resorts and water parks; after a death at one in July 2024 the corporation razed unauthorised resort structures near Arnala beach, and a fifteen-year-old died after a slide fall at an Arnala resort in April 2026. [n45 §5; FPJ; Mid-day] The fort and the beach, their history and their drownings, are the subject of Chapter 32.

Jivdani draws pilgrims in their thousands on Sundays and festival days, by the district’s account; a 2012 web page claimed 25,000 to 30,000 a day, which is weak evidence. [n48 §9; Palghar district] The funicular and the planned glass skywalk, told in Chapter 29, are investments in that traffic. The oldest record of the pilgrimage is the Gazetteer’s: in 1882 “barren women from the surrounding villages” climbed to the caves to throw betel nuts into the niche. [n48 §9; Gaz XIV]

East of the highway, the city plan names “the Chinchoti fall, Tungareshwar Temple and thick forest” as offering “good tourism potential to this eastern part”, and notes holiday resorts along the national highway. [n48 §9; CDP pp. 25, 64]

No official visitor counts were found for Arnala or Jivdani, and no data on the income of the ferry boatmen. [n48 §9 Gaps] What links the three is the railway. In the researchers’ phrase, the fort, the beach and the shrine are a rail-fed day-trip economy that depends on Virar station: the station whose passengers rose from 48,294 to 83,176 a year between 1878 and 1880, and which now handles hundreds of thousands a day. [n48 §9 Inferences]

From the garden to the platform

Put the two documents side by side, the Gazetteer of 1882 and the city plan of 2010, and the change in how people have lived on this ground is plain.

In 1882 the plain of the Virar side was a garden of sweet water and sandy black soil, irrigated by Persian wheels from unlined wells, with ponds owned by family groups and lanes sunk below the fields. It grew rice in the monsoon and sugarcane after it; nine kinds of plantain, of which one, the rajeli, was dried by 175 families of three communities on booths by the sea and sold as far as Sholapur; betel vine, areca and coconut palms, with sonchafa among them to drive off the bees. Its port at Agashi sent out more than three lakh rupees’ worth of produce a year and built its own boats. Its salt pans on the tidal flats were worked by Agris, Kharvas and Dublas from Surat. Its Kolis fished the creek and the sea, and its Warlis came down from the hills to the Saturday market by the pond. Its railway station, sixteen years old, was beginning to carry the produce north and south.

In 2010 the same plain held a dormitory town of more than a million people, sixty per cent of whose workers went to Mumbai every day; one per cent of its workers were farmers. Its water came from a regional scheme scores of kilometres away; its groundwater was turning salt; its ponds were silted or full of garbage; its old creek carried sewage. Its gardens survived in the coastal villages, which the plan called “more or less stagnant”, and on them the last cash crop was a grafted flower for the Dadar market. Its fishers set 375 dol nets off Arnala and watched a mega-port rise to the north. Its quarries cut the basalt of the sanctuary’s edge, and its kilns burned the brick of the towers with Adivasi labour. And in July, the water that the Gazetteer had seen from Tungar, “flooded at spring tides”, came back to the low ground and stopped the trains.

The ground did not change. The rock is the same rock, the plain the same silt, the monsoon the same monsoon. What changed was what the people on it lived by, and who those people were. That is the subject of the next chapter, which tells the peoples of Virar, from the Kuparis, Samvedis and Vadvals of the garden villages and the Kolis and Agris of the shore to the Warlis of the hills and the migrants of the towers, and the villages in which each of them made a home.

What the record does not hold

  • Rainfall. No official IMD rainfall series for Virar; the 1,586 mm local figure and the 2,200–2,500 mm planning figure are unreconciled. [n48 §2–3]
  • Floods. No dated source for a Virar flood in 2024; no death tolls for 2005, 2018 or 2019; the NEERI–IIT Bombay report of 2018 was not found. [n48 §2; n29]
  • Geology. No quarry-level or layer-level geology of Jivdhan or Tungar beyond the Gazetteer’s hill list and the city plan’s bauxite note; no geomorphology or CRZ map of the Virar coast. [n48 §2]
  • Groundwater. No CGWB salinity map for Virar villages; the CGWB brochures and the Lok Sabha reply were known only from summaries. [n48 §3]
  • Water works. Capacities and dates of the Usgaon, Pelhar and Papadkhind dams; the date Surya water first reached Virar (2023, 2024 or 2025 by different sources). [n48 §3; n06]
  • Crops. No acreage or output series for rice, banana, betel or coconut in Vasai taluka after 1881; the end of the Agashi dried-plantain trade; the Persian-wheel wells after 1882. [n48 §4; n17]
  • Kupari livelihoods. No village-by-village split of the seventy-five Christian plantain-drier families of 1882; no named Kupari flower grower, vendor or emigrant; no figures for the Kuparis’ move from farming to professions and emigration. [n34; n40; n48]
  • Fishing. No catch series or cooperative accounts for Arnala, Navapur, Kalamb or Rajodi; the CMFRI 2016 census volume was not opened; the 2010 count of 375 dolnets is the only Arnala gear count. [n48 §5]
  • Biodiversity. No primary bird survey of the Virar–Agashi–Arnala coast; no mangrove-cover figure for Vasai–Virar; no count of quarries round Tungareshwar; no notification year for the sanctuary. [n48 §6; n17]
  • Industry. No official unit counts, employment or turnover for the Waliv, Gokhivare and Sativali estates or for Shirsat Road in Virar; no count of brick kilns in the taluka. [n48 §7]
  • Work. The 2011 census worker classification and journey-to-work tables for the corporation; household incomes; present-day market volumes in Virar and Agashi. [n48 §8]
  • Tourism. Visitor counts for Arnala and Jivdani; ferry incomes. [n48 §9]

The 1982 Thane District Gazetteer, the Census of India’s printed tables, the CMFRI census, the CGWB district reports, the Palghar agriculture office and the Marathi press are the places where these gaps would close.
