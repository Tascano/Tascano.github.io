Period covered: 1900 – 1947

In this chapter. From the census of 1901 to August 1947, this chapter follows the coast through the Gandhian years, the salt satyagraha and the Quit India movement to independence.

In 1905 the Government of India printed, in Calcutta, the seventh volume of a survey that set out to record every language and dialect spoken under its rule. The volume dealt with Marathi. Somewhere in its pages, among the specimens sent in from the districts, is a short text from the coast north of Bombay, set out line by line with an English gloss beneath each word. It is the Parable of the Prodigal Son in the dialect of the Vadvals, the garden people of Bassein, Mahim and Dahanu. Its first line reads Eka don por hote: “One man-to two sons were.” A few lines later the younger son has spent everything, kapnaylya-var, “after having spent”, and a ghan dukal, “a mighty famine”, falls on the land. [n22; Grierson, Linguistic Survey of India vol. VII, 1905, Specimen 36]

It is a borrowed story, chosen by the survey because it could be told in every tongue of the empire and compared word for word. But the words are local. They are the earliest connected sentences in a Vasai dialect that anyone has found in print, and they arrive at the opening of a century in which almost everything else about this coast falls silent. [n22; Inferences] Between 1900 and 1947 the island of Bassein, the old island of Sopara, was ruled from Bombay, counted by the census, crossed by an electrified railway and touched by plague, influenza and two world wars. It lay between the city where Gandhi launched the Quit India movement and the town up the line where police shot five men holding the national flag. For a long time the research behind this book found almost no event of the national movement that happened, on a known date, inside the taluka itself. [n28; 1900 to 1946 Takeaway; n16]

That silence turned out to be a problem of searching, not of history. When the Maharashtra government’s own volumes of Source Material for a History of the Freedom Movement in India, which reprint the weekly reports of the Bombay police, and the Indian Council of Historical Research’s Dictionary of Martyrs were read page by page, Bassein appeared in them again and again. [n43; Headline finding] A Caliphate meeting marched through Bassein with two black flags in December 1919. The first Maharashtra Provincial Conference met at Bassein in May 1921, and Gandhi came to speak to it. In 1930 three thousand people gathered at Virar to welcome a released Congress prisoner, and a prohibition drive was run into the villages from Sopara. In March 1942 five hundred volunteers enrolled in a People’s Volunteer Corps at Bassein. And on 9 September 1942, at Chowk Bazar in Vasai, a fifteen-year-old labourer named Bal Samant was shot by the police and died the same day. [n43; SM-X p. 24; SM-IX pp. 91–93; SM-XII pp. 245–253; SM-IV p. 23; DoM-3 p. 23] The same volumes reach back further, to an income-tax revolt at Bassein in December 1860, told in Chapter 11, and to the forest people who in 1897–98 “brought their loads to Bassein with riotous demonstrations” at the start of the Thana disturbances. Those were tax, forest and liquor grievances, not nationalist politics, but they were the coast’s first recorded defiance of the British state. [n43; SM-I pp. 22–25; SM-II pp. 636–37]

This chapter is built from two kinds of record. Where the record speaks, it is followed closely: the police reports and the martyrs’ dictionary for the freedom struggle, and for the life of the coast the census tables, the linguist’s survey, the caste compilations of 1920–22, the railway engineers’ files and the parish histories. Most of the freedom-struggle evidence is the government’s own, written by policemen and officials watching the movement, and it reads that way: it counts crowds, names speakers, and reports rumours alongside events. Where the record does not speak, the chapter says so, and then looks over the fence at the neighbours, at Bombay, Salsette, Palghar, Dahanu and Uttan, to show what was happening around Vasai and how we know it. Some silences remain. The weeks of the salt march in 1930, the rail lines in 1942 and the day of independence in 1947 are still blank for Bassein, and the records that might fill them sit in a volume whose text could not be searched, in a district gazetteer that could not be opened, in Marathi local histories and in parish registers that this book could not reach. [n43; Method; Gaps; n16; Gaps; n20]

The 1900s: a census, a survey and the words of the coast

The island at the turn of the century

The century opened with a count. The Census of India took the population of Bassein taluka in 1901, and the figure that survives, read back from the table of 1911, is about 80,251. [n21; Census 1911, Provincial Table I] It was a little more than the 68,967 counted in 1881, and it came at the end of a terrible decade. Plague had reached the Thana district in the 1890s; a search summary of the Bombay plague reports suggests that in four months of 1897 the town of Bassein, then about 11,000 strong, lost several hundred people. [n28; 1890s, provisional] Those figures could not be confirmed and should be treated as provisional. What the 1901 census report itself says is more reassuring: the population of Thana district fell by only about one per cent between the estimate and the result, the smallest gap of any district in the Presidency. [n21; Census 1901 Vol. IX Pt I] Whatever plague and famine had taken from Bassein, they had not emptied it.

The shape of the place in 1900 was the shape the Gazetteer had drawn in 1882, and nothing in the notes suggests it had changed. The island of Bassein was a strip of garden land about three miles broad, cut off from the mainland by a backwater that left the Bassein creek above the railway bridge and wound north to the Vaitarna. [n12; Gazetteer 1882] In the south the old Portuguese fort stood empty, a protected ruin waiting for a name. North of its walls lay the town in the village of Malonde, with its municipality founded in 1864, its Robertson market, its Balvantrav Hari Naik dispensary and its vernacular schools. [n21; Gaz XIV p. 36] Up the coast ran the garden villages: Papdi, Sandor, Pali, Manickpur, Gass, Sopara, Nirmal, Vagholi, Vatar, Bolinj, Koprad, Umrale, Agashi. Beyond the railway, on the east, were salt waste and rice land, and then the long forested back of Tungar hill, where the Varlis and Kolis lived. [n12; n11; Gazetteer 1882]

The people were the people of the 1881 census: about three in four Hindu, one in five Christian, a few in a hundred Muslim, and a handful of Parsis, Jews and Jains. [n21; Gaz XIII-II p. 680] Among the Hindus the largest groups were the Agris, the Bhandaris who tapped the palms, the Varlis, the Konkanis, the Kunbis and the Brahmans, among them the Samvedis of the garden villages. [n21; Gaz XIII-II p. 680] The Christians were the descendants of the Portuguese century’s converts, and the most skilful husbandmen of the coast, according to the Gazetteer, the people who had “turned the light sandy country about Bassein into an evergreen garden”. [n21; Gaz XIII-I p. 300]

The fort becomes a monument

The ruin that had been the Portuguese Court of the North was given a new legal status in this decade. On 26 May 1909 Bassein Fort was declared a protected monument, and the same date is given for the island fort of Arnala at the mouth of the Vaitarna. [n15; TIFR; n11; Wikipedia: Arnala Fort] From then on the walls that Chimaji Appa’s army had breached in 1739, and that Bishop Heber had found “perfectly uninhabited” in 1825, belonged to the archaeological state. [n28; Gaz XIV; n18] The notes do not say what protection meant on the ground in 1909: whether the fields inside the walls were cleared, whether the sugar factory in the old church of Nossa Senhora da Vida had left any machinery, whether the villagers who used the fort’s stones for their houses were stopped. Those details were not found.

The fort was already known far beyond the Konkan. On 1 February 1905 a New Zealand magazine, the New Zealand Illustrated, printed an account of Bassein that counted the Matriz, the Misericórdia and thirteen churches. [n09; Papers Past, 1 Feb 1905] It is a reminder that the ruins of Baçaim had become a sight for the tourists and readers of the empire, a romantic emblem of a fallen Catholic city, two generations before Bollywood found it. The number thirteen does not match the seven churches that later sources count inside the walls; the writer may have been counting chapels and churches outside the fort as well. [n09; Inferences]

A Catholic reference book of the same years gives the living Church a figure. The Catholic Encyclopedia, writing about 1907, counted in Bassein nine churches, twelve priests and 16,119 Catholics. [n04; Catholic Encyclopedia] The nine churches are almost certainly the nine Portuguese-era parishes outside the fort that are still in use today: Nirmal, Remedy, Sandor, Agashi, Nandakhal, Papdi, Pali, Manickpur and Merces. [n09; Vasaiker; Goanet 2020] They were not then part of the Archdiocese of Bombay. Under the arrangements between the Holy See and Portugal, the Bassein churches had passed in 1886 from the Padroado Archdiocese of Goa to the new Diocese of Damaun, and they would stay under Daman until 1928. [n09; Wikipedia: Sandor; n04] The Catholics of Bassein, in other words, still belonged in church law to a Portuguese see, ninety years after the last Portuguese soldier had left.

At Nandakhal, in the north of the island, the parishioners rebuilt their church between 1907 and 1910. The Holy Spirit Church had been built by the Franciscans in 1573 and had survived the Maratha attacks of 1737–39, so that it is called the oldest surviving church of north Vasai. The rebuilding used government timber and four metal pillars and added an upper floor; in 1908 a bell was given. [n09; Wikipedia: Holy Spirit Church Nandakhal] The bell is a small detail with a long echo. The bells of the fort’s churches had been carried off by the Marathas in 1739 as trophies and hung in temples across the Deccan. [n09; Bells] A new bell in a Kupari village church, 169 years later, marks how completely the Christian countryside had rebuilt itself in the quiet of British rule.

Grierson’s survey: what the coast spoke

The most important document of the decade for this coast is the one with which this chapter began. George Abraham Grierson’s Linguistic Survey of India was a colossal project, built from specimens and word lists sent in from every district. Its seventh volume, The Marathi Language, printed in 1905, treats Thana mostly under the heading of the “Konkan Standard” of Marathi, which Grierson reckoned was spoken from Daman to Rajapur by about two and a half million people. [n22; LSI vol. VII] But it also stops, briefly, at a cluster of small dialects in the Bassein country, and what it says about them is the first systematic description of how the people of Vasai spoke.

Grierson grouped three of them together as “broken dialects of Thana”: Vadval, Phudagi and Samvedi, which “still have preserved many of the characteristic features of Gujarati Bhili”. [n22; LSI vol. VII] Of the Samvedis he wrote: “The Samvedis live as husbandmen and gardeners in Bassein and Alibag. Their number is estimated at 2,700. They speak a mixed dialect, the chief components of which are the current Marathi of Thana and vulgar Gujarati.” [n22; LSI vol. VII] (The OCR of the place name is garbled and its reading as Alibag is uncertain.) He listed their sound changes: ch and chh becoming s and h, so that a son is hakra and a field hit; s becoming h; strong masculine nouns ending in -o, as in Gujarati; a dative in -do. His verdict was blunt: “a mechanical mixture of Konkan Marathi and Gujarati”. A Samvedi parable, Specimen 37, was printed in Devanagari, but the scanned text is unreadable in the copy the researchers used. [n22; LSI vol. VII]

The Vadvals, he wrote, were a sub-tribe of the Panchkalshi Kunbis, about 3,500 of them returned from Thana, living in the coastal talukas of Bassein, Mahim and Dahanu, and “supposed to have immigrated from Gujarat”. Their speech turned gh into s or h, and s and sh into h, “probably like the sound of ch in loch”; gold was hona, seven was hat, a servant sakar, the moon sand. Some words were Gujarati, not Marathi at all: mere, “near”; bhuke, “with hunger”. And “the cerebral l is used as in the Dekhan”, so the retroflex ळ, the sound that marks Marathi, Gujarati and Konkani alike, was alive in Vadval speech in 1905. [n22; LSI vol. VII] Phudagi, the speech of “a wandering tribe in Thana” with about 1,000 speakers, was “almost identical with Vadval”; only a word list had been received, and Grierson could not say whether the two were the same. [n22; LSI vol. VII]

The fishermen got their own paragraphs. The Mangelas were “a tribe of fishermen in Thana”, most numerous in Mahim, Bassein and Dahanu, 11,538 of them at the 1901 census; their speech was “probably nowhere different” from the district’s, but Grierson printed a “so-called Mangela dialect” parable and called it “a dialect of the same kind as Vadval, Samvedi, etc., with a strong admixture of Gujarati”. [n22; LSI vol. VII] The Kolis “everywhere speak the dialect of their neighbours”, he wrote, and local estimates put Koli speakers in Thana at 163,000. [n22; LSI vol. VII] The Varlis, about 89,000 in Thana and more than half the population of Dahanu, spoke what had once been a Bhil dialect, now Marathi-influenced “except in the extreme north”, where it was a form of Gujarati; they said nang-ne for “to see” and clipped their words. [n22; LSI vol. VII] Of the Konkani Muslims, chiefly of Salsette, Kalyan and Bhiwandi, he noted that their speech “contains a considerable amount of Hindostani words”. And in the north of Thana, he observed, Marathi “is gradually superseded by Gujarati”. [n22; LSI vol. VII]

Then there were the Christians. Grierson called them by the name their neighbours used, Kinstav; Europeans called them “Portuguese or Salsette Christians”. He counted about 26,600 of them in Thana. Their Marathi had taken in Portuguese words, pay for father, may for mother, mass, padron for godfather and madron for godmother, and even the English word estate. But his judgement was that it was “nothing else than the usual Marathi of the district”. [n22; LSI vol. VII] Specimen 14, a parable in the Christian dialect, was printed under the Konkan Standard to prove the point.

It is worth pausing on that verdict, because it settles a question that the earlier chapters of this book left open. Under the Portuguese the people of the Northern Province, from Daman to Chaul, had spoken a creole Portuguese that scholars call Norteiro. In 1906, the year after Grierson’s volume, the Goan scholar Sebastião Rodolfo Dalgado published the main description of Norteiro, and modern linguists still rely on it. [n22; Cardoso 2006] But Grierson’s evidence, gathered in the same years, shows that the Christians of Thana had by then shifted to Marathi, keeping Portuguese only as a layer of words for kin, church and godparenthood. [n22; Inferences] The Gazetteer had said the same in 1882: the Christians’ “home tongue is Marathi, very little different from the Marathi spoken by the Kunbis and Kolis”, though “a considerable number understand an ungrammatical Portuguese”. [n35; Gaz XIII-I p. 204] No source found by this book describes a Bassein-specific creole or the date it died. [n22; Gaps]

Where were the Kuparis in all this? The Samvedi Christians of north Vasai, whom the community calls Kupari from the Portuguese compadre, do not appear by name in Grierson’s text. The researchers’ reading is that his 2,700 Samvedis probably counted only the Samvedi Brahmans, while the Christian Samvedis were folded into the 26,600 Kinstav. [n22; Inferences] Grierson does not say so, and the reading cannot be proved. But it fits the evidence that later generations would give: the same sound changes Grierson recorded for Vadval and Samvedi, s becoming h, are the ones a 2024 machine-translation paper lists for Kadodi, the dialect of the Kuparis and Samvedi Brahmans, in which dried banana, sukeli, is hukeli. [n22; Kumar et al., WAT 2024; n20; mr.wikipedia: सामवेदी बोली] A sound change that Grierson heard in 1905 is still being spoken a hundred and twenty years later.

Grierson himself was impatient with the minor dialects of the coast. “It would be waste of time and paper,” he wrote, “to give specimens of all of them.” [n22; LSI vol. VII] That impatience is why we have only a few lines of each. But a few lines are more than any earlier century left.

The railway is strengthened

The railway that bound the island to Bombay was being made stronger as the century turned. Between 1896 and 1902 the BB&CI’s long bridge across Bassein creek, opened with the line in 1864 and divided by Panju island into a southern span of 69 girders and a northern span of 25, was given extra piles and new girders. Speed limits followed: fifteen miles an hour for local trains, eight for through trains. [n05, n28; Wikipedia: Vasai Creek Railway Bridge] A train crossing the creek at eight miles an hour would take several minutes to reach the island, a slow, rattling passage over the water that every traveller to Bassein knew. No other dated railway event of the 1900s was found for the taluka. [n28; 1900s]

Silences of the decade

Beyond these items, the 1900s left nothing that the researchers could find. There is no Bassein-specific record of the plague after 1897, of the famine years at the turn of the century, of the municipality’s finances after 1881, of schools founded, of temples or mosques built. [n28; Gaps; n21; Gaps] The Vasai Samachar, a Marathi weekly that had been published at Bassein every Sunday since about 1877, at two and a half rupees a year, is not heard of again in the notes. [n21; Gaz XIII-II c. p. 662] Whether it survived into the new century is unknown.

What was happening around Bassein can be told from the edges. In Bombay, the city’s land market was beginning the boom that would, within fifteen years, see the Panchkalshi landlords of Salsette sell villages they had held for generations. [n35; Enthoven III p. 163] The administration of the Bombay Presidency was still conducted partly in Modi, the cursive script of Peshwa government, which by one account remained in official use until 1952 even after Balbodh Devanagari had become the main script for printed Marathi. [n22; Wikipedia: Modi script] And on the island itself, if the 1882 Gazetteer is any guide, the Samvedi and Kupari gardeners of Agashi, Vatar and Koprad would have been drying their rajeli plantains between October and January, as eighty-five Samvedi Brahman families, fifteen Panchkalshi families and about seventy-five Christian families had done in 1881. [n21; Gaz XIII-I p. 395] No source dates the continuation of that trade into the twentieth century, but the dried banana of Vasai would still be famous a hundred years later. [n20; mr.wikipedia: वसई]

The 1910s: the count of 1911, a church without foreign aid, and the influenza

Seventy-nine thousand people

The census of 1911 is the first in this period whose Bassein figures can be read. Provincial Table I of the Census of India, “Area and Population of Talukas and Pethas”, gives Bassein taluka an area of about 225 square miles, two towns, ninety villages and 15,328 occupied houses. Its population was 79,978: 40,978 males and about 39,000 females, a density of 355 to the square mile. [n21; Census 1911, Provincial Table I, p. 552] The column alignment in the scanned table is garbled, and the area figure had to be inferred from the density, but the sum of males and females checks. [n21; OCR note]

Set beside the earlier counts, the number tells a story of stillness. Bassein had about 61,089 people in 1872, 68,967 in 1881, about 80,251 in 1901 and 79,978 in 1911. [n21; Gaz XIII-I p. 70; Gaz XIII-II p. 678; Census 1911] Between 1901 and 1911 the taluka did not grow at all; it lost a few hundred people. The researchers caution that the area of the taluka changed between censuses, from 221 square miles in 1881 to about 225 in 1911, 209 in 1931 and 203 in 1951, so the counts may not be strictly comparable. [n21; Inferences] But the broad picture holds. In the first decade of the century the island of Bassein stood still, while Bombay, two hours to the south by train, kept growing.

The two towns of the 1911 table are not named in the notes. One was certainly Bassein. The other may have been Agashi, which had 6,823 people in 1881 and was the largest settlement of the north of the island, but that is a guess. [n21; Gaz XIV p. 2; Inferences]

Virar is remodelled

The railway’s only dated work of the decade happened at the northern end of the island. In or after 1913, Virar station was remodelled, together with Borivali, with a down platform, an island platform and two signal cabins. [n28; Wikipedia: Virar railway station] Virar had been the end of the BB&CI’s suburban run since 12 April 1867, when the first regular service from Back Bay had reached it. [n28; Wikipedia: Western line] An island platform allows trains to be turned round and passengers to change between up and down services, and the remodelling suggests that by 1913 the railway expected more local traffic at its northern terminus. No passenger figures for Virar after 1880 were found; in that year it had handled about 83,176 passengers, up from 48,294 two years earlier. [n12; Gaz 1882, p. 382]

In 1916 the BB&CI fixed the loading standard to which the replacement creek bridges would be built. [n28; Wikipedia: Vasai Creek Railway Bridge] The old girders of 1864, strengthened at the turn of the century, were already marked for replacement.

Giriz builds its own church

In 1917 the Christians of Giriz, a village on the coast south-west of Vasai town, decided that they would no longer walk to church. Until then the people of Giriz had belonged to the parishes of Sandor and Merces, and the walk to those distant churches was hard, especially in the monsoon. On 14 February 1917 the foundation stone of the Church of St Francis Xavier was laid, and the villagers built it in about a year, finishing in 1918, “without foreign aid”. Fr Augusto D’Souza supervised the work and became the first parish priest. Its feast is kept on 3 December, the feast of St Francis Xavier, who had stopped at Bassein in 1548. [n09, n11; Wikipedia: St Francis Xavier’s Church, Giriz]

The phrase “without foreign aid” is the community’s own, and it carries a point. The Gazetteer had noted in 1882 that the Christians of the district had more than doubled in number in fifty years and that “some of their villages are as rich as any in the Thana district”. [n37; Gaz XIII-I pp. 204, 216] Giriz is the first of the twentieth-century daughter parishes of Vasai, carved out of the Portuguese mother churches by the villagers themselves. More would follow: Gass in 1942, Bhuigaon in 1946, Umrale in 1970, Nanbhat in 1975, Bolinj in 2001. [n09; Inferences] The family tree of Vasai’s parishes, rooted in the 1550s, put out new branches in the British century, and each new branch was a village that had grown large and confident enough to want its own altar.

A lawyer from Vasai in the Swadeshi years

The national movement’s first trace in a Vasai life comes from the edges of a revolutionary’s story. The ICHR Dictionary of Martyrs, in its entry on Vishnu Ganesh Pingle, one of the martyrs it lists for Maharashtra, records the circle he moved in at Bombay in the Swadeshi years around 1910 and 1911, when Govindrao Potdar ran an alkali works at Mahim. Among Pingle’s nationalist associates the entry names “Hari Laxman Patil, a lawyer from Vasai”. [n43; DoM-3, Pingle entry, c. pp. 293–94] That is all. The entry gives him no dates, no deed and no part in any event at Vasai, and a web search for him returned nothing. [n43; Gaps] He is a single line in a dictionary of other men’s deaths. But he shows that by the early 1910s a Vasai-born pleader could be found in the Swadeshi circles of Bombay, among men making Indian goods to break the hold of British ones, and the pleaders of Bassein would, within ten years, lead the town’s first political meetings.

Neighbours: the war of 1914–18

The First World War is a blank in the notes. No source found by this book describes recruitment from Bassein, war work, prices, shortages or any other effect of the war on the taluka. [n28; Gaps] It would be easy to fill the gap with what is generally known about India’s part in the war, but this book’s rule is to use only what the notes support, and for the war years they support nothing local. What they do show, a few years later, is the price of the war’s last months.

The influenza of 1918

Early in 1918 an influenza-like illness appeared in the Thana district. In September and October, according to a scholarly summary of the Bombay administration’s reports, mortality became “alarming”. [n28; Cambridge, “Death and the modern empire”, via search summary] One summary puts influenza deaths across the Bombay Presidency in October 1918 alone at 675,222; the researchers did not open the original and flag the figure for checking. [n28; 1918, provisional]

The firmest local number comes from the census of 1921. In a section on influenza, its report reconstructs what the district would have held without the epidemic. Thana, counted together with the Bombay Suburban district, recorded 912,756 people in 1921. The losses from influenza that the census added back were 36,642, making a reconstructed population of 949,398, against 882,309 in 1911. [n21; Census 1921 Vol. VIII Pt I, Sec. 9, p. 25, Subsidiary Table 15] The report says that Bombay City, Kolaba and Poona were struck first, and Thana after them. [n21; Census 1921]

The loss of 36,642 people is about four per cent of the district’s recorded 1921 population. [n21; Inferences, derived] It is not a Bassein figure, and no taluka-level death count was found. But if Bassein suffered in proportion, the influenza of 1918 would have killed something on the order of three thousand people on the island in a few months, more than the plague of 1897 is thought to have taken from the town. That is an arithmetic illustration, not a finding, and it should be read as such. The epidemic left no account from Bassein that the research could find: no parish register, no newspaper report, no memory recorded.

The view from 1920: Enthoven’s castes

At the end of the decade the Government of Bombay published the work that is, for this coast, the second great document of the period. R. E. Enthoven’s The Tribes and Castes of Bombay came out in three volumes between 1920 and 1922. It was compiled largely from caste informants; the Panchkalshi entry names its informants and a community body, the “S. K. Hitechhu Mandali”, so that what it records is often a community’s account of itself rather than independent evidence. [n35; Enthoven I–III, method note] Read that way, it is a portrait of how the communities of Bassein wanted to be seen around 1920.

The Panchkalshis gave Enthoven their history. They had come to the coast, they said, with Raja Bimba or Bhima about the end of the thirteenth century, from Paithan on the Godavari, “hence their Kshatriya origin”; another version made Bimba a prince of Anhilvada Patan in Gujarat who fled the Muslim conquests. [n35; Enthoven III pp. 161–162] Under the Portuguese, they said, “there was much persecution”, many fled to the southern Konkan, and “many were then converted to Roman Catholicism by force and their descendants may still be traced at Bassein and Salsette”. [n35; Enthoven III p. 162] For helping the Marathas in the siege of Bassein in 1739–40, the Naik family of Anjur had been given the title Sarpatil and inam lands by the Peshwa, and “the patelships of many families of the Vadval division” went back to the Bassein war. [n35; Enthoven III p. 162] The Vadvals, they said, had separated in the fourteenth century, reputedly over the admission of a Koli into the caste, a charge each side laid on the other; the Vadvals’ right to the Panchkalshi name rested on letters from the Shankaracharya of 1836 and 1852 and a resolution of the Palshikar Brahmans of Bassein taluka of 1855. [n35; Enthoven III p. 162] A community census of Vadvals in 1901 had returned 7,916. [n35; Enthoven III p. 160]

The occupations Enthoven recorded show the shape of the economy at the end of the 1910s. “Under the Portuguese many of them became skilled mechanics and carpenters (house and boat builders),” the Panchkalshi entry says, but “the majority of the Vadval and Ashtagarkar sections are still farmers”, working the land on the khand system, a form of sharecropping in which they supplied their own cattle. [n35; Enthoven III p. 163] By contrast, the Sashtikars, the Panchkalshis of Salsette, had been proprietors in most of the island’s villages until “land sales of the last fifteen years”, that is, the Bombay property boom of the 1900s and 1910s. [n35; Enthoven III p. 163] Salsette, across the creek, was already being bought up by the city. Bassein, across the water, was not yet.

The Kolis’ entry, written by C. Rothfeld of the Indian Civil Service, contains a sentence that might stand as an epigraph for the whole century of the coast’s languages. “The home tongue of Son Kolis,” it says, “is a patois of Marathi gradually obsolescent under the grinding of the Local Board school and the newspaper.” [n35; Enthoven II pp. 259–260] Its features were tavade for tikde, “there”, and avade for ikade, “here”. The school and the newspaper, the agents of standard Marathi, were already wearing down the speech of the fishing villages in 1920. A hundred years later the same pressure would be named as the threat to Kadodi and Vadvali. [n35; Inferences]

The Koli entry also records the fishermen’s religion as they told it: that they had been taught “the gods properly” by Kalu Bhagat, a Koli of Varsoli near Alibag, “in the times of the Peshwas some two centuries ago”, and directed to Khandoba of Jejuri; before that, they said, there had been only “ignorance”. Enthoven’s author read this as “the first Hinduising of the Koli tribe”. The women sold all the fish and “wield great power, manage household affairs”; they wore a silver bracelet on the right wrist and nothing else on that hand, a sign of the deity’s covenant protecting the fishers. [n35; Enthoven II p. 260]

Of the Varlis, whose Nihiri section lived in south Mahim and Bassein, Enthoven wrote that “regarding their origin nothing is known”; they were “an aboriginal tribe only slightly influenced by Brahmanism”, lived in huts of karvi reed on cow-dung floors, and wandered for labour in the dry season. “In Thana they always speak Marathi except those in the extreme north, who speak Gujarati.” [n35; Enthoven III pp. 445–447] The Katkaris, catechu-makers and charcoal-burners, spoke “a slightly disguised Marathi with many Gujarati and Hindustani words”; strangers from the Varli, Koli, Thakur and Kunbi castes could be admitted to them for five rupees. [n35; Enthoven II pp. 171–173] The Samvedis, by contrast, got a single line: “SAMAVEDIS are found only in the Thana district, where they chiefly grow garden crops.” [n35; Enthoven I p. 241]

Enthoven also kept the story of the reconversions of 1820 and 1828, when Christian Agris and Kolis of Bassein had been readmitted to Hinduism by Palshe Brahmans for a fee of 1,200 rupees, and were known afterwards as Uraps or “New Marathas”, still carrying the surnames Gomes, Soz, Frutad and Minez. [n35; Enthoven I pp. 9–14] In 1920 those families were a living reminder that the line between the coast’s faiths had been crossed in both directions.

December 1919: two black flags in Hati Mahal Street

The decade ended with the first political scene in Bassein town that the record describes, and it was set off by a celebration. In December 1919 the empire marked the peace that ended the war, and on 13 December the town held a Peace procession. The police listed those who took part: the Mamlatdar, Dr Abhyankar, V. M. Marathe, T. A. Dondi the District Pleader, P. S. Mahedakar, K. R. Galwankar and S. H. Golatkar. [n43; SM-X p. 24] It was the town’s official face: the revenue officer, a doctor and the pleaders of the courts.

Three days later the same streets saw something else. On 16 December 1919 the Sub-Inspector of Police at Bassein reported a meeting of the Caliphate Committee in Hati Mahal Street. About 250 people attended. He counted them by community, as the police of the time did: about thirty Hindus, six Bohras and the rest Muslims. P. N. Shinde, B.A., LL.B., took the chair. The meeting passed a resolution censuring the Bassein Municipality for “wasting money on the Peace Celebrations”, and then a procession went out carrying two black flags. [n43; SM-X p. 24] Black flags were the sign of mourning: for the Khilafat committees, the peace that others celebrated was a peace that threatened the Caliph.

The police noticed a detail that tells more than the numbers. Dr Abhyankar and Vishnupant M. Marathe, who had walked in the official Peace procession on the 13th, took part in the Caliphate meeting against the peace on the 16th. [n43; SM-X p. 24] A few of the town’s educated Hindus were standing on both platforms in the same week, loyal enough to march with the Mamlatdar and sympathetic enough to sit with the Khilafat Committee. The same report incidentally settles a small question that the earlier records left open: the Bassein Municipality founded in 1864 was still alive and spending money in 1919. [n43; SM-X p. 24]

The meeting was small, and the Muslim community of the town was small; the census of 1931 would count 1,148 Muslims in Bassein municipality. [n21; Census 1931] But it was led by the town’s lawyers, and it was mixed. Bassein’s entry into national politics came not through a Hindu caste body or a Congress committee but through a joint Muslim and Hindu protest, chaired by a graduate pleader, in Hati Mahal Street.

The 1920s: Gandhi at Bassein, a new bridge, a new name and the electric train

February 1920: the pleaders and the Caliphate fund

The Khilafat agitation did not end with the black flags of December. On 1 February 1920 a joint meeting of Muslims and Hindus was held at Bassein, reported to the government on 12 February. This time the chair was taken by D. K. Phadke, District Pleader. The meeting adopted the Khilafat resolutions passed at Amritsar, and a collection was made for the Caliphate fund: the Muslims gave Rs 153 and the Hindus Rs 15. [n43; SM-X, OCR line c. 4981; page not read]

The two sums tell their own story. The cause was a Muslim one and the Muslims of Bassein paid for it ten times over. But the Hindus gave something, and a Hindu pleader presided, as a Hindu graduate had presided in December. In the small town of Bassein the police reports show the Hindu-Muslim alliance of the Khilafat years in miniature: a mixed platform, run by the men of the courts. [n43; Inferences]

November 1920: non-co-operation comes to Bassein

By the autumn of 1920 the Congress had taken up non-co-operation. The police report for the Thana district for the week of 9 November 1920 records that “Meetings were held on non-co-operation at Umergaon, Moakhada, Wada, Kurla and Bassein. None were important except those of Kurla and Bassein.” [n43; SM-IX p. 82] Gandhi himself spoke at Kurla, the same report says. It does not say that he spoke at Bassein, and nothing in the record puts him there that month. [n43; SM-IX p. 82] But of five meetings across a large district, the police singled out two as important, and one of them was Bassein’s. The town that had marched with black flags in 1919 was, a year later, among the places in the Thana district where the movement drew attention.

No record has been found of what non-co-operation meant in practice on the island: whether any Bassein pleader gave up his practice, whether a national school was opened, whether khadi was spun or foreign cloth burned. The government volumes that cover those activities in detail deal with Bombay City and Sind, not with the Thana villages. [n43; Gaps]

May 1921: the first Maharashtra Provincial Conference

Then, in the spring of 1921, Bassein became for three days the political centre of Marathi-speaking India.

The Special Branch of the Bombay police reported it on 18 May: “The First Maharashtra Provincial Conference was opened at Bassein, district Thana, on the 6th May and lasted for three days. The audience numbered, according to report, from 1,000 to 1,700 and were for the most part Hindus. The president elected for the occasion was Dr. B. S. Moonje of Berar.” [n43; SM-IX p. 92; also SM-III/1] Why Bassein was chosen the reports do not say. The researchers’ reading is that a town with a municipality, a bench of pleaders and a Brahmin and Gujarati-Vani elite was a respectable, Congress-minded Marathi town, not an outlier, and a natural venue. [n43; Inferences] Which ground or hall held the pandal, who chaired the reception committee and how many delegates came from the taluka itself are not recorded in what was read. [n43; Gaps]

The conference opened in the shadow of a quarrel. The Congress in Maharashtra was divided between Gandhi’s followers and the group the police called the Poona Nationalists. A later police note records that V. J. Patel “denounced the Poona Nationalists for opposing his Malegaon resolution at the last Session of the Maharashtra Provincial Congress at Bassein”, and describes the Poona group in disarray: “Baptista has been lost”, N. C. Kelkar “lacks the spirit”. [n43; SM-III/1, page not checked] The Baptista of that note is Joseph “Kaka” Baptista of Bombay, the first president of the Indian Home Rule League, an East Indian Catholic whose father had come from Uttan, on the Salsette shore across the creek from Vasai. [n43; Wikipedia: Joseph Baptista] The Malegaon resolution concerned the riots at Malegaon, whose violence Gandhi would condemn from the same platform. So the arguments heard in the Bassein pandal were the arguments of the whole province: how far to follow Gandhi, and what to say when non-co-operation turned to bloodshed.

Gandhi himself had been at Surat. He left for Bassein on the night of 5 to 6 May. [n43; SM-IX pp. 91–92] On the 7th he and “some of his Moslem fellow workers” arrived at the pandal while C. V. Vaidya was moving the fifth resolution. [n43; SM-IX p. 92]

He began, according to the text printed from the Bombay Chronicle under the heading “Speech at Maharashtra Provincial Conference, Bassein, May 7, 1921”: “Your love has drawn me here, I am sorry I cannot stay in your midst for long.” [n43; SM-IX p. 93] The police summary records what followed. He spoke on the Mulshi Peta satyagraha. He condemned the violence of the Malegaon riots. And he told his Marathi audience, plainly, that Maharashtra was carrying on non-co-operation “in a half-hearted manner”. Then he asked them for money and for spinning wheels. [n43; SM-IX p. 92] It was a characteristic Gandhi speech: affection first, then reproach, then a practical demand. The audience answered the last part on the spot. About Rs 2,000 was collected there and then, and Rs 3,000 more was promised. [n43; SM-IX p. 92]

He did not stay. By about a quarter to six that evening, 7 May, he “arrived in Bombay from Bassein (after having attended the Maharashtra Provincial Conference there)”, and that night he left for Allahabad with Mrs Gandhi, Sarojini Naidu and others. [n43; SM-IX pp. 91–92] Gandhi’s stop at Bassein was a single day between Surat and Bombay. The reports say he came by train; they do not say whether he went anywhere on the island beyond the pandal. [n43; Gaps] But for a day the man who led the national movement had stood in Bassein and told the people of Maharashtra they could do better.

The speech text is printed in the Maharashtra government’s volume on Gandhi in Maharashtra, which cites the Bombay Chronicle and the Collected Works of Mahatma Gandhi; the exact volume of the Collected Works was not checked. [n43; SM-IX p. 93] The police report is the government’s own account, written by men whose job was to watch him.

July 1921: the Tilak Swaraj Fund

In the summer of 1921 the Congress was collecting for the Tilak Swaraj Fund, and the government tried to guess what each place would give. On 6 July 1921 the Collector of Thana sent a rough estimate by taluka: Palghar Rs 1,000; Bassein Rs 1,500; Umbergaon Rs 4,000; Bhiwandi Rs 4,000; Padgha Rs 2,000; Wada Rs 350; Rs 12,850 in all. He added that “the whole district would not subscribe more than Rs. 20,000.” [n43; SM-IX p. 106] Bassein’s share, by this estimate, was modest: well below Bhiwandi and Umbergaon, above Palghar and Wada. It was a guess, not a receipt. But it shows how an official in 1921 measured the town’s enthusiasm: real, but not the warmest in the district. [n43; Inferences]

After 1921 the record goes quiet again: for the rest of the decade the police volumes that were read have nothing more on Bassein. [n43; Gaps]

The creek bridge is rebuilt

The decade began with earth. In early 1920 work started on the approach banks for new railway bridges across Bassein creek. In 1923 the main work began, and on 11 September 1923 the engineers carried out pile-screwing trials at Panju island, the low island of salt pans and fishing hamlets in the middle of the creek. [n28, n05; Wikipedia: Vasai Creek Railway Bridge] The new bridges stood on piers 120 feet east of the old ones, with six piles to each pier, and by August 1925 they were well advanced. [n05; Wikipedia: Vasai Creek Railway Bridge] Another account dates the replacement to 1923–1926 or 1927. [n05; Wikipedia] The researchers caution that “1925” is a date of construction in progress, not a confirmed opening. [n28; 1920s, flag]

The bridges of the 1920s would carry the trains of Bassein for almost sixty years, until 1983, when the steel bridge was taken out of service for the first concrete one. [n05, n28; Wikipedia: Vasai Creek Railway Bridge] Every kawadi who carried bananas to Bombay in the twentieth century, every Kupari student going to college in the city, every worker of the 1980s boom, crossed the creek on them.

One weak source dates a road bridge over the creek, from Ghodbunder to Naigaon, to 1928. It is a single entry on an infrastructure website, it conflicts with better reports that put the first road bridge in 1968, and it should not be relied on. [n28; InfraLens, flag; n12] For the whole of this chapter’s period, as far as the evidence goes, the island of Bassein was reached from the south by train or by boat.

“Neela, or Nalasopara”

Somewhere in these years a name appears that the earlier chapters of this book have been waiting for. In 1882 the Gazetteer had called the old port town only “Sopara”, “the capital of the Konkan” in its own loose phrase, a rich country town of about 1,700 people with a crowded weekly market, three and a half miles from Bassein Road station and three and a half from Virar. It was “not more than three-quarters of a mile from the line of railway”, yet “somewhat badly off for communications”, and the Gazetteer’s author added: “A station at Sopara or a made road to the town from the Agashi high road would be a boon to its people.” [n31; Gaz XIV pp. 314–316] The railway’s station list of 1882 has Bandra, Andheri, Goregaon, Borivli, Bhayandar, Bassein Road, Virar and stations further north. It has no Nallasopara and no Naigaon. [n28; Gaz XIII-I] In 1882, then, there was no station at Sopara, and the author of the Gazetteer knew of none.

The first trace of one comes through the railway’s own memory. A 2022 newspaper article marking 155 years since the first local train ran between Back Bay and Virar lists the halts of an early service: “Neela or (Nalasopara)”, then Bassein, Panjo, Berewla (Borivali) and the rest. The article draws its history from the BB&CI’s company magazine of 1923, though it does not tie the list explicitly to that magazine. [n31; Free Press Journal, 12 Apr 2022] It is the only clue found to the station’s original name: Neela, or Nalasopara. Wikipedia’s article on the station gives its opening as 1865, citing a Western Railway heritage book of 2009, but that date cannot be squared with the Gazetteer’s complaint of 1882 unless the station was a minor halt the Gazetteer’s author ignored, or the date is wrong. The researchers did not find the true year, and this book does not state one. [n31; Gaps]

Where did “Nala” come from? The honest answer is that no reliable source says. A popular account, published without dates or sources, says the name arose when two villages, Nala to the east of the railway line and Sopara to the west, came to be treated as one region. [n31; Lokshahi] A Wikipedia list of localities, by contrast, puts “Nale Gaon” in Nallasopara West, alongside “Sopara Gaon”, which does not fit the east–west story neatly. [n31; Wikipedia mirror] The Gazetteer’s volume of places has no village entry for Nala. But its first volume, on the people and the economy, does mention the name twice: the Lad Vani traders of the coast, it says, lived at “Thana, Supara, Bassein, Agashi, Nala, Papdi and Dahanu”, and the dried plantains of Bassein were prepared only at Agashi, Vagholi, Vatar, Bolinj, Koprad, Nala, Umrale, Rajodi and Murdes. [n35; Gaz XIII-I pp. 112–113; n21; Gaz XIII-I p. 292] So there was a place called Nala in 1882, a place with Gujarati shopkeepers and banana-driers, close enough to Sopara to be named in the same breath. The Gazetteer also notes a “Nil hill” near Sopara, which a Portuguese writer of 1728 complained had been fortified without an engineer. [n31; Gazetteer footnote citing O Chronista de Tissuary] Whether “Nil”, “Neela” and “Nala” are one name is possible but not documented. [n31; Inferences]

What can be said is this. The railway, which had dammed the Sopara creek with its embankment and turned the old landings of Gass, Bolinj and Vagholi into silted fields, eventually gave the town a station of its own, east of the old village. [n31; Gaz XIV p. 315] The station took a double name, Nala and Sopara joined, perhaps because the halt stood nearer the smaller settlement than the old town. That is an inference, not a finding. [n31; Inferences] From then on the ancient Shurparaka of the Mahabharata, the Supparaka of the Pali texts and the Soupara of Ptolemy would be known to most of the people who used its name as a stop on the Virar line: Nalasopara, Nala Sopara, Nallasopara, spelled every way, with no standard English form to this day. [n22; Wikipedia Talk: Nallasopara; n31] The old village kept its own name, Sopara, west of the line. The modern city that would grow around the station took the railway’s.

The Church passes to Bombay

In 1928 the Catholic parishes of Bassein, which had belonged to the Diocese of Damaun since 1886, were transferred to the Archdiocese of Bombay. [n09; Wikipedia: Sandor; n04] For the first time since the Portuguese conquest of 1534, the churches of Vasai were governed from the city that the Portuguese had once given away as a wedding dowry rather than from a Portuguese see. The notes do not say how the transfer was received in the villages, or what changed for the parish priests. But it put the Kuparis, the Vadval Christians and the Christian Kolis of Bassein under the same bishop as the East Indians of Bombay and Salsette, the community whose name they had all taken in 1887. [n07; Catholic Connect]

The electric train approaches

On 5 January 1928 the first electric suburban trains ran on the BB&CI from Colaba to Borivali. [n28; Wikipedia: Western line] Virar was not yet included. A local history says the GIP, the other great railway of Bombay, had electrified its lines in 1927, and that the BB&CI’s electric commuter trains later ran to Virar. [n05; Vasaiker] In 1928 the electric line stopped about twenty miles short of the island, at Borivali, and the trains that crossed the new creek bridges to Bassein Road and Virar were still drawn by steam.

The Bimb chronicle in print

In 1924 the historian V. K. Rajwade published an edition of the Mahikavatichi Bakhar, the Marathi chronicle of King Bimb and the kingdom of Mahim, whose chapters go back to about 1448 and whose final text is a seventeenth-century recension. [n35; Wikipedia: Mahikavatichi Bakhar] It was the second modern edition, after R. P. Rane’s of 1877. Rajwade found its geography reliable, with 396 place names, 336 of them in the north Konkan; modern scholars point to historical inaccuracies and the lack of archaeological support for its early dynasties. [n35; Wikipedia] The researchers could not open Rajwade’s edition. [n35; Gaps] But its publication in the 1920s, in the same years that the Panchkalshis were telling Enthoven they had come with Bimba, shows the communities of the coast and the scholars of Maharashtra reaching back to the same medieval story, at a time when caste identity was being argued in print across the Presidency.

The 1930s: the salt year, the town in 1931, steam’s last years and the stupa reopened

The salt year

In 1930 Gandhi marched to the sea at Dandi and broke the salt law, and the Civil Disobedience movement made salt the symbol of the struggle. Bassein had salt in abundance. In 1880–81 its seventeen salt works, in six groups on the north bank of Bassein creek, held 11,374 pans on 1,439 acres, worked by Agris and, near Bassein Road station, by Dublas from Gujarat. [n21; Gaz XIII-I pp. 364–366] If anywhere on the north Konkan coast had the material for a salt satyagraha, it was here.

April 1930: Arnala, Virar and Vasai (unverified)

The strongest account of a salt satyagraha on this coast comes from a source this book could not read. The revised Thane District Gazetteer of 1982, in its chapter on the modern period, is reported by a search-engine summary to say the following. The Thane District Congress Committee opened its satyagraha centre at Arnala, the island fort and fishing village at the mouth of the Vaitarna, and there leaders sold salt they had made. At Virar a meeting on 15 April 1930, with D. Gokhale in the chair, called for the breaking of the salt laws, and seventy-five people took part. Officials posted at Arnala were boycotted. A procession broke the salt law at Kalyan creek, and “at various places, viz., Palghar, Vasai, Bhiwandi, etc. people broke the salt law in large numbers”. [n43; Thane Gazetteer 1982 ch. 2, secondary summary, unverified]

Every word of that paragraph should be read as unverified. The researchers could not open the gazetteer page, could not quote it and could not give a page number; the names of the leaders who made salt at Arnala were not in the summary; and the government volume that would hold the police reports for April to September 1930, Volume XI of the Source Material, has page images online but no searchable text, so it could not be checked. [n43; Method; Gaps] If the summary is right, Arnala, with its salt-makers and fishermen, was the coast’s salt-march site, and Virar its meeting ground. But no primary account of a Vasai salt-pan raid, of arrests at Arnala, or of the number of people who broke the law at Vasai has been read. [n43; Gaps]

Neighbours: Dharasana and the salt pans of Salsette

The salt year’s documented violence touched the district elsewhere. Kishore Yesav of Palghar, a Congress volunteer, was injured in the police lathi-charge on the salt satyagrahis at Dharasana in Gujarat, was held in Arthur Road jail in Bombay, and died at J.J. Hospital on 17 July 1930; the ICHR Dictionary of Martyrs lists him among the martyrs of Maharashtra. [n43; DoM-3, Kishore Yesav entry, c. p. 150] He was a Palghar man, not a Vasai one, but he came from the same district and the same railway line. In June 1930, far to the south in Panvel, sixty to seventy-five villagers cut 150 teak trees in the Karnala forest in a forest satyagraha, and Congress volunteers picketed liquor shops. [n43; SM-XII p. 305] And across the creek, the salt pans of the Bombay Suburban District, in South Salsette taluka of Thana, were focal points of Civil Disobedience: a “Satyagraha Chhavani”, a satyagrahis’ camp, was raided on 19 October 1930, along with camps at Chembur and Ghatkopar, and many were arrested. [n16; Scroll.in] South Salsette is the other side of the creek, the country of Bandra and Andheri. One later account says that Augustine Koli of Uttan, of whom more below, took part in the Salt Satyagraha, but he was born in 1921 and would have been nine years old in 1930, so the claim is doubtful. [n16; FPJ, flag]

September 1930: three thousand at Virar

From the autumn of 1930 the record becomes firm, because it comes from the weekly Abstracts of Intelligence of the Bombay police, reprinted in Volume XII of the Source Material. These abstracts were written for the government, district by district, week by week, and they count meetings and crowds with a policeman’s precision.

For the Thana district, in the week ending 20 September 1930, the abstract records: “There was a gathering of 3,000 persons at Virar to receive Jamnadas M. Mehta after his release from jail. Audiences at other meetings were below 500.” [n43; SM-XII p. 245] Jamnadas Mehta was a Bombay labour and Congress leader; the report does not say why he had been jailed. Three thousand was a large crowd for the district. In the same weeks the abstracts were describing Thana as one of the districts with “comparatively little agitation”, and by 1 November they would write that “Enthusiasm for the Congress appears to be definitely on the wane”. [n43; SM-XII pp. 241–251, approx.] Against that background, the Virar gathering stands out: at the northern end of the island, the largest crowd the Thana abstracts recorded that week came out to welcome a released prisoner.

October to November 1930: the prohibition drive from Sopara

The movement’s next form on the island was a campaign against drink. Picketing liquor shops was part of Civil Disobedience everywhere, and on this coast it had a long history behind it: the Koli rising of 1897–98 in the district had begun, in part, as a protest at the price of liquor. [n43; SM-II pp. 636–37] The abstract for the week ending 1 November 1930 records that “a Thana District Liquor Prohibition Mandal was formed on October 31 and has started work in Bassein taluka.” [n43; SM-XII p. 251] Two weeks later, for the week ending 15 November, it reports that “Prohibition is being [pushed] in the villages in Bassein taluka from Sopara as centre.” The verb is garbled in the scanned text, and “pushed” is the researcher’s reading. [n43; SM-XII p. 253]

Sopara as a centre: it is a striking phrase to find in a police file. The old port that had been the capital of Aparanta, that had lost its creek to silt and its trade to the railway, and that had only lately acquired a station of its own, was in November 1930 the base from which the Congress sent its workers into the villages of the taluka. [n43; Inferences] The abstract does not name them, nor say which villages they visited, nor whether toddy shops were picketed or merely preached against. On the same page the police note an attempted forest satyagraha at Kashi in the Thana Mahal, stopped when five leaders were arrested; that was in Thane taluka, not Bassein. [n43; SM-XII p. 253]

November 1930: a rumour at Juchandra

For the week ending 29 November 1930 the Thana abstract records six meetings and one alarming line: “There are rumours of a mass raid on salt works by the people of Juchandra in Bassein taluka.” [n43; SM-XII p. 255] The abstract places Juchandra in Bassein taluka; the researchers did not verify the village’s location independently. [n43; SM-XII p. 255, note] The line records a rumour, not a raid. No later abstract that was read says that the raid took place, and this book does not say that it did. [n43; Gaps] But the rumour itself is evidence of something: by late 1930 the police thought it believable that the villagers of Bassein taluka might march on the salt works.

December 1930: the land assessment

The year ended on a grievance closer to the gardens. For the week ending 27 December 1930 the abstract counts fourteen meetings in the Thana district, “six of which were in connection with request for remission of land revenue and a general revision of the recently increased land assessment. Bassein taluka is threatened with a week of prohibition propaganda.” [n43; SM-XII p. 259] The island’s land had been measured and rated by the revenue survey that came into force in 1862; the abstract’s “recently increased” assessment tied the island’s cultivators, the gardeners of plantains and betel and the rice farmers of the east, to the national campaign against revenue. [n43; Inferences] The secondary summary of the 1982 gazetteer adds that in a January, its year not given in the summary, “a meeting of the agriculturists in the Vasai taluka decided to pay only the first instalment of the revenue”. [n43; Thane Gazetteer 1982, secondary summary, unverified] If that January was January 1931, it would be the sequel to the December meetings, but the year is not known.

1931 and 1932

Taken together, the documented picture of the salt year on this coast is modest but real, and it is centred on the line from Virar through Sopara to Arnala: a large welcome at Virar in September, a prohibition drive from Sopara in October and November, a rumour of a salt raid at Juchandra, revenue meetings in December, and, if the unverified gazetteer summary is right, salt made and sold at Arnala and the salt law broken at Virar and Vasai in April. [n43; Inferences] For the second Civil Disobedience campaign of 1932 the search found nothing in Bassein taluka: the Thana entries of that year concern a meeting of 4,000 at Dhansoli in the Thana Mahal on 10 January 1932 and agitation against the census, and the forest satyagrahas of Kashi, Mire and Dhansoli were all in Thane taluka. [n43; SM-XII; Gaps] Whether anyone from Bassein was arrested in 1930 or 1932, and who the taluka’s Congress workers were, the volumes that were read do not say.

Bassein town in 1931

The census of 1931 gives the clearest picture of Bassein town in the whole period. Imperial Table V, which lists towns by religion, gives the Bassein municipality a population of 12,689: 6,640 males and 6,049 females. Of them, 7,392 were Hindus and 1,148 were Muslims; the Jain figure reads as about 3, though the OCR is uncertain. [n21; Census 1931 Vol. VIII Pt II, Imperial Table V] The table’s Christian figure was not recovered, but subtraction suggests that the rest of the town, a little over four thousand people, were mostly Christians and a small number of Parsis and others. That is a derived figure and should be treated as a rough guide. [n21; Inferences, derived]

The town had grown slowly. A separate 1872 census volume gives the Bassein municipality 9,356 inhabitants; the Gazetteer gives 10,356 for 1881; the 1931 figure is 12,689. [n21; Census 1872; Gaz XIV p. 28; Census 1931] In fifty years the town had gained about 2,300 people, less than a quarter. Thana district’s whole urban population in 1931 was 80,093. [n21; Census 1931] Bassein was a market town of some twelve thousand people, Hindu-majority, with a strong Christian minority and a Muslim community of about one in eleven.

The taluka table of 1931 is less kind. Provincial Table I gives Bassein an area of 209 square miles, but the OCR lost the population columns for its row. [n21; Census 1931, Provincial Table I] The literacy table by religion gives some numbers for the taluka: Muslims, 1,682 males and 1,269 females, of whom 644 males and 179 females were literate; Christians, about 11,238 males and 11,229 females, roughly 22,467 in all, though the Christian literacy columns are shifted and unusable. [n21; Census 1931, Provincial Table II, OCR flagged] If the Christian figure is right, the taluka’s Christians had grown from 14,070 in 1881 to about 22,500 in 1931, an increase of some sixty per cent in fifty years. The Muslims had grown from 2,292 to about 2,950. Both figures are flagged as OCR-uncertain, and the comparison should be treated as provisional. [n21; Gaz XIII-II p. 680; Census 1931]

The literacy figures for Muslims are the only ones that can be read, and they are telling: about two in five Muslim men could read in 1931, and about one in seven Muslim women. [n21; Census 1931, derived] Nothing comparable was recovered for the Hindu or Christian communities.

Steam ends on the island

On the railway the decade’s great change came in 1936. In that year all four lines from Bandra to Borivali, and the two lines from Borivali to Virar, were electrified at 1,500 volts direct current, ending steam working on that stretch. [n28; Wikipedia: Western line; IRFCA] The electric local train now ran through Bassein Road to Virar. [n28; Inferences]

The consequence would take half a century to show, but it began here. Virar, the end of the BB&CI’s local run since 1867, became in 1936 the end of the electric suburban network, the practical northern limit of Bombay commuting. [n28; Inferences] It would stay so until 2013. The researchers note that Wikipedia is the only dated source for the 1936 electrification, though the railway enthusiasts’ archive IRFCA lists the Western suburban electrification as Churchgate to Virar, which is consistent. [n28; flag]

What did the electric train mean for the gardens? The notes do not say directly. But they describe the economy that the railway sustained for most of the twentieth century: farmer-vendors from the Vasai villages, the kawadis, who carried their produce on a shoulder pole with a basket at each end and rode the afternoon train to Victoria Terminus, selling bananas, dried bananas and vegetables at Bora Bazaar in the Fort and near the Dadar Kabutarkhana. Their customers called them Vasaiwale mama and Vasaiwale maushi. [n05, n17; India Food Network, 2015] The source does not date the beginning of that trade, and this book does not claim that it began with electrification. It is enough to say that by the time anyone wrote about the kawadis, they had been riding the electric train for as long as anyone could remember.

A Marathi school at Nandakhal

In 1939 Fr Raymond Mendes started St Joseph’s School of Marathi at Nandakhal. The Carmelite Convent School in Bassein also dates its history from 1939. [n28; Carmelite School history; CCBI schools, via search summary, flag] Both dates come from search summaries of the schools’ own history pages, which the researchers did not open, and should be checked before they are repeated. But the Nandakhal school’s name says something. It was a school of Marathi, in a Kupari village, founded by a Catholic priest. It fits Grierson’s finding of 1905 that the Christians of Thana spoke the Marathi of the district, and it shows the Church in the Kupari heartland teaching children in the language of the land, not in Portuguese or English. [n22; Inferences]

The decade also brought a fiftieth anniversary. In 1937 the East Indian community, which had formally adopted that name by resolution on 26 May 1887 and founded the Bombay East Indian Association on the same day, published a souvenir for the association’s golden jubilee. It described the coincidence of the founding with Queen Victoria’s Golden Jubilee of 1887 as a “happy coincidence”. [n07; east-indians.com, via search summary] The name had been chosen, by one account, to mark the North Konkan Catholics as the Crown’s earliest Catholic subjects, and, by another, to distinguish the local Christians from Goan and Mangalorean migrants in Bombay. [n07; Catholic Connect, 25 May 2026] Whether the Bassein villages took part in the jubilee, and how far the Kuparis of Agashi and Nirmal thought of themselves as East Indians in the 1930s, the notes do not say. [n11; Gaps in ch. 11]

The stupa reopened

At old Sopara, archaeology returned for the first time since Bhagwanlal Indraji’s excavation of 1882. In 1939–40 M. M. Qureshi of the Archaeological Survey of India re-excavated the stupa mound, the Burud Rajacha Kot, and found stone lintels, two small stupas on its south side and glazed pottery of the Muslim period. [n02, n25; Shah 2020, citing Tripati and Gaur 1997] The relics that Indraji had taken from the stupa’s heart in 1882, the stone coffer with its nested caskets and eight bronze Buddhas, were by then in the collection of the Bombay Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society, now the Asiatic Society of Mumbai. [n18; Wikipedia: Sopara; Live History India] Qureshi’s season is the only fieldwork at Sopara recorded between 1882 and the 1950s, and it came just as the world went to war again.

The generation of 1914

One man of this generation would shape the next half-century of Vasai, and his early life is almost entirely hidden. Hari Govindrao Vartak, known as Bhausaheb, was born in February 1914; one source gives the date as 9 February, another as 14 February. [n26, n20; Wikipedia: Hari Govindrao Vartak] He was sixteen in the salt year of 1930 and twenty-eight in 1942. After independence he would be the Congress member for Bassein from 1962, a cabinet minister for public health, salt lands and fisheries, and later revenue, and a recipient of the Padma Shri. [n26; Wikipedia] He is listed in the Indian National Congress’s 1963 publication Year of Freedom, at page 339, which suggests that he had a record in the freedom movement. But the article gives no details, and the Marathi newspaper pieces that might supply them, a Maharashtra Times article of 14 February 2017 and a Lokmat article of 30 January 2019, were not read. Whether he was jailed in 1930 or 1942 is unverified. [n16, n20; Gaps] The government’s own record does not help: a full-text search of the Source Material volumes for 1919 to 1946 and of the ICHR Dictionary of Martyrs found no mention of him, not in the police abstracts of 1930, not in the Congress digests of 1942 to 1946. The name index of the unsearchable Volume XI, for April to September 1930, does list a “Vartak, Govind Dharmaji” at pages 813 and 852, but whether he was a Vartak of Vasai was not checked, and he is not Bhausaheb. [n43; Gaps; Where the rest probably is] Until a Marathi source is found, this book will not call him a freedom fighter in so many words. It is fair to say only that he belonged to the generation that came of age in the decades of the national movement, and that the institutions his family later built, Vartak College and the Vidyavardhini society, would educate the men who ran Vasai after him. [n16; Wikipedia: Hitendra Thakur]

The 1940s: a volunteer corps, the firing at Chowk Bazar, Sane Guruji, the Warli revolt and independence

A Koli from Uttan

January to March 1942: five hundred volunteers at Bassein

The decade’s first documented political scene at Bassein came in the anxious winter of 1942, when the Japanese advance was coming closer to India and the Congress was raising volunteers for civil defence. On 29 January 1942 the Congress held a meeting of 1,500 people at Bassein. The speaker, S. D. Deo, spoke against the British policy of “divide and rule” and called for a “united effort to gain national freedom”. [n43; SM-IV p. 9]

Two months later, in March 1942, the weekly digest of Congress activities reported that “the People’s Volunteer Corps at Bassein, Thana district, is gaining ground and some 500 volunteers have been enrolled.” [n43; SM-IV p. 23] The corps was part of the Bombay Provincial Congress Committee’s drive to raise a People’s Volunteer Brigade for civil defence during the Japanese advance. It was not a Quit India force, and it should not be read as one. [n43; SM-IV p. 23, note] But five hundred enrolled volunteers in one taluka town is a measure of how far the Congress had organised Bassein by the spring of 1942, five months before the movement that would ban it.

A Koli from Uttan

One freedom-struggle life that the research connects to the waters of Vasai belongs to a Koli fisherman’s son from Uttan, on the Salsette shore south of the creek. Augustine Koli was born at Uttan on 11 January 1921. In 1940, inspired by Gandhi, he left a railway job. He “worked undercover in Dahisar, Borivali and Panju Island in Naigaon near Vasai”. [n16; Free Press Journal, Augustine Koli obituary] Panju is the island in the middle of Bassein creek on which the railway bridge rests, a place of salt pans and a fishing hamlet that had 1,358 people at the 2011 census. [n12; census2011.co.in] For an underground worker of the early 1940s it had obvious advantages: it lay between the city and the green belt, reachable by boat, crossed by the railway, and populated by fishing families who were the kin and neighbours of the Kolis of Uttan.

He came into the open on 15 January 1941, when a speaker named Pandrang Nanaji Gawde addressed a meeting of more than three hundred people at Uttan. [n16, n26; FPJ] In August 1942 he was at Gowalia Tank in Bombay for the launch of Quit India. [n16; FPJ] After independence he went home and served as sarpanch of Uttan from 1954 for seventeen years, and he died in January 2022 at the age of 101. [n16, n26; FPJ] The obituary’s claim that he joined the Salt Satyagraha should be treated with caution, for the reason already given. [n16; flag]

Augustine Koli was a Christian Koli of the Salsette coast, not a man of Bassein taluka. His story was, before the government volumes were read, the closest that this book’s record came to a Vasai chapter of the freedom struggle. It is no longer the only one. But it still suggests something the police digests do not show: that the fishing villages and creek islands between Bombay and the green belt were used by underground workers in the years of Quit India, and that their people were part of the movement even where no event is recorded. [n16; Inferences] Augustine Koli is not in the government volumes that were searched. [n43; Christian participation] An earlier search suggested a local “Vasai mukti sangram”, a Vasai liberation struggle, about 1940–41, but no source supports it, and it is treated here as unverified, probably a confusion. [n16; Gaps]

Quit India

On 8 August 1942, at the session of the All India Congress Committee in Bombay, Gandhi launched the Quit India movement. The government answered with arrests of the entire Congress leadership, and the movement, largely leaderless, was carried on locally. Repression was severe: shootings, lathi charges, burnt villages and collective fines, and by December 1942 about 60,000 people had been jailed. [n05; Wikipedia: Quit India; Open University, Making Britain] In the Marathi country the movement is remembered above all for the parallel government that freedom fighters set up in Satara and Sangli, and for the martyrs of Gargoti; searches in Marathi for Quit India return those stories and nothing from Vasai, which is why Vasai’s own Quit India death, told below, had to be found in a government dictionary of martyrs rather than in the press. [n20; Lokmat; Pudhari; n43; Gaps]

The nearest documented event lies thirty kilometres up the railway line. On 14 August 1942, six days after the Bombay session, a crowd of eight to ten thousand people from the villages of Palghar taluka marched toward the railway station at Palghar. One group turned toward the tehsil office. The police blocked it and charged it with lathis, and when Govind Ganesh Thakur tried to break through the cordon they fired. The first shot was fired at 12:39 in the afternoon. [n16; Free Press Journal, Palghar martyrs] Five men died: Govind Ganesh Thakur, shot while holding the tricolour; Kashinath Hari Pagdhare; Ramchandra Mahadev Churi; Sukur Govind More; and Ramprasad Tiwari. Prasanna Naik saved the flag. [n16, n26; FPJ] A martyrs’ list places some of the dead in the villages of Satpati and Shirgaon, and records that one was a farmer, one died at a students’ protest and one died of his wounds early in 1943; the page did not render when the researchers tried to open it, so the matching of names to villages is unverified. [n16; Vikaspedia, via search extract]

Palghar built a memorial, Hutatma Chowk, Martyrs’ Square, in 1951, and every year on 14 August the town still stops at 12:39. [n16; FPJ] Palghar’s Wikipedia entry calls the town “one of the important points in the Chale Jao Movement”, Chale Jao being the Marathi name for Quit India. [n16; Wikipedia: Palghar] One newspaper account of the commemoration misdates the launch of Quit India to August 1941; the correct date is 8 August 1942. [n16; flag]

The ICHR Dictionary of Martyrs confirms the Palghar names, and places Kashinath Pagadhare, Ramchandra Churi and Ram Prasad Tiwari at Satpati and Sukur More at Shirgaon, both in Palghar taluka. It lists other Palghar-taluka participants too, Laxman Patil of Dhansar and Yashwant Govind Gavad, and men of Chinchani in Dahanu taluka. [n43; DoM-3, pp. 118–226, entries] All of them belong to Palghar or Dahanu. For a long time the research behind this book assumed that Palghar’s firing was the district’s only bloodshed of 1942, and that Vasai’s link to it was one of geography. The dictionary shows otherwise.

9 September 1942: Bal Samant at Chowk Bazar

A month after the Quit India resolution, the movement’s second wave reached Vasai town. The ICHR Dictionary of Martyrs, compiled from government files, has this entry:

“Born in 1927 at v. & taluka Vasai, distt. Thane, Maharashtra. A labourer, he took part in the anti-British ‘Quit India’ movement of 8 August 1942. He received severe bullet wounds in the police firing on 9 September 1942 at Chowk Bazar, Vasai, while participating in a rally that was fired upon by the British police and died on the same day.” [n43; DoM-3 p. 23]

The name at the head of the entry is Bal Samant. Born in 1927, he would have been fifteen years old. [n43; DoM-3 p. 23, derived] He was a labourer, not a pleader or a student. He went to a rally in the market square of his own town, the police fired on it, and he died of his wounds that day.

That is everything the record holds. The dictionary gives two sources for the entry: a file of the Home Department (Special) at the Maharashtra State Archives, F.No. 1110 (6) A (i) I, and the Who’s Who of Indian Martyrs, volume I, page 317. [n43; DoM-3 p. 23] Neither could be read. The Who’s Who page sits on a government digital library server that returned errors; the archive file has not been seen. Web searches in English and Marathi found nothing on the firing or on his name. [n43; Gaps] Bal Samant’s death therefore rests on a single government listing, and this book says so plainly. It does not say who called the rally, how many people were in Chowk Bazar that day, whether anyone else was hurt or arrested, or why the police opened fire, because no source read says any of these things. [n43; Gaps] Nor does it guess at his community: Samant is a common Marathi surname, and the entry does not give his caste or religion. [n43; Christian participation, Gaps]

The date fits what is known of the movement. After the arrests of 9 August 1942 the Congress was leaderless and banned, and the unrest of August and September was carried on by local crowds; a rally a month after the resolution belongs to that second wave. [n43; Inferences; n05] The weekly Congress digests that had reported the Bassein volunteers in March have no Bassein entry at all from August 1942 to the middle of 1944, which is what one would expect of a banned organisation whose work had gone underground. [n43; SM-IV, Gaps] Bal Samant’s death fell into that silence, and was recorded not by the digests but in the list of the dead.

The firing at Chowk Bazar changes the story of 1942 on this coast. The five deaths at Palghar on 14 August were the district’s great event, commemorated every year at 12:39. The death of a fifteen-year-old labourer in Vasai’s market a month later has no memorial that the research found, no anniversary and no newspaper account. Chowk Bazar, Vasai, is a place where the older residents of the town might still be asked what their parents remembered. [n43; Inferences]

What 1942 has not given up

Bassein was in the same Thana district as Palghar and on the same BB&CI line, and the movement of 1942 targeted exactly the kind of places that Bassein had: railway stations and tehsil offices. [n05; Inferences] Beyond the Chowk Bazar firing, no other Vasai-specific incident of 1942 has been found. There is no record of sabotage on the line at Bassein Road or Virar: a search for it found only station histories. There is no account of underground workers in the taluka other than Augustine Koli’s own obituary. There is no other death, and no list of arrests: the ICHR dictionary lists only martyrs, not those who went to jail, and the Maharashtra freedom-fighter pension rolls for Thane and Palghar were not found online. And there is no primary mention of Bhausaheb Vartak in any of the volumes read. [n43; Gaps; n16; Gaps] It is possible that more happened and was recorded in the 1942 volumes of the state’s series, which have not been digitised, in the Home Department file behind Bal Samant’s entry, or in Marathi memoirs. It is possible that the garden villages, many of them Christian, stayed largely apart from the movement. The evidence available does not allow either conclusion, and this book does not draw one.

A church for St Gonsalo Garcia

In the same year, 1942, in the Kupari village of Gass, the faithful built a church dedicated to St Gonsalo Garcia. It was carved out of the parish of Nirmal, whose Holy Cross church dated from 1557, and it would be renovated or expanded in 1957 and later become a major shrine of the saint, holding a week-long feast every February. [n07, n09; CatholicsIndia; Prayers & Petitions] Gonsalo Garcia, born in Bassein in 1556 or 1557 to a Portuguese soldier and a local mother, educated at the Jesuit college in the fort, crucified at Nagasaki on 5 February 1597 and canonised in 1862, was the first person born in India to be declared a saint. [n07; Wikipedia: Gonsalo Garcia] Lisbon records describe him as “natural de Agaçaim”, of Agashi. [n07; DNA, 2014]

The timing is striking. In 1942, while Bombay rose against the British and police fired at Palghar and in Vasai’s own Chowk Bazar, a Samvedi Christian village of the green belt chose to dedicate its new church to a son of Bassein who had died for his faith three and a half centuries earlier. The notes do not say why Gass chose him, or whether the choice had anything to do with the politics of the moment; to suggest that it did would be invention. What can be said is that in the 1940s the Kuparis were building a local Catholic identity around a local saint, as the East Indians of Bombay had built a communal one around their name. Gass is spelled with a double s in this book, as the village spells it; it lay, in 1882, at the southern landing of the old island of Sopara, where small boats came up the creek about a mile from the town, and its people said it was the site of old Sopara itself. [n37; Gaz XIV pp. 315, 322; n11; Gaz XIV p. 341]

In January 1943 a priest from Nandakhal, Fr Frank Lobo, was ordained; he is the only named native priest from a Vasai village before 1947 whom the researchers found, and he rests on a single parish source. [n25; parish source, flag] He belongs to a tradition that goes back to the Maratha conquest: after 1739, when the Portuguese nobles and the regular clergy left for Goa, the ordinary Christians stayed, “a native priest taking charge of their souls with the approbation of the Mahrattas”. [n37; Hull, citing du Perron] Two centuries later the villages of the green belt were still giving their sons to the priesthood.

1944 to 1946: Gadgil, Sane Guruji and the New English School

When the Congress digests take up Bassein again, the movement has changed its tone: no longer a crowd in a market, but speakers explaining the past and preparing the young.

On 25 August, very probably of 1944, N. V. Gadgil addressed about two hundred people at Bassein. The year is inferred from the surrounding entries, which concern C. Rajagopalachari’s formula on Pakistan and the Kasturba fund. Gadgil told his audience that the question of Pakistan “arises only after independence is achieved”. [n43; SM-IV p. 72] It is a small line, but it shows the arguments of 1944 about Pakistan being carried into the taluka towns. In the same years the digests name a Palghar Taluka Congress Committee, which met privately at V. V. Dandekar’s house on 27 January and resolved to carry out the constructive programme, and a Thana District Congress Committee under Appasaheb Kirtane. They name no Bassein taluka committee. [n43; SM-IV pp. 100–101, approx.] Whether one existed was not found.

Then came Sane Guruji. P. S. Sane, known across Maharashtra as Sane Guruji, spoke at Bassein on 10 June 1945 to a meeting of about 250. The police reporter wrote down two of his sentences. He said that “innocent persons were killed during the C. D. Movement of 1942”, and that “at least 10 to 20 lacs would be required to be sacrificed to achieve independence”. He urged his audience to join the Rashtra Seva Dal. [n43; SM-IV pp. 130–31] The next day he spoke to about 150 students; the record of what he told them runs on to a page the researchers did not read. [n43; SM-IV p. 131] The same entry names a school, the New English School, Bassein, where students were urged “to unite under the National Flag after completing their studies”. [n43; SM-IV, OCR line c. 6423] It is the only Bassein school named in the freedom-struggle record.

Sane Guruji’s words in June 1945 have a particular weight in this chapter. When he spoke of innocent persons killed in 1942, he was speaking in a town where, less than three years earlier, a fifteen-year-old labourer had died of police bullets in Chowk Bazar. The report does not say that he mentioned Bal Samant, and this book does not claim that he did. [n43; Inferences]

He came back. On a tour of the Thana district from 5 to 13 January 1946 he addressed eleven meetings. “At Bassein,” the police wrote, “he referred to the 1942 movement and justified the stand taken by the Congress in not withdrawing the August resolution. Referring to the Police firing in Thana District, he said that thousands of innocent people had been killed as a result of it, including V. L. Kotwal.” [n43; SM-IV p. 143] Kotwal, Veer Bhai Kotwal, was a martyr of the Bombay region, not of Vasai, and the “thousands” is an orator’s figure, not a count. [n43; SM-IV p. 143, note] But the speech shows what the movement in Bassein had become by 1946: a memory of 1942, defended in public, and a call to the town’s students to carry it forward.

Neighbours: the Warli revolt

North of Bassein, in the forested talukas of Talasari, Dahanu and Umbergaon, the most dramatic struggle of the 1940s in the Thana district was not against the British Raj directly but against the landlords and moneylenders who held the Adivasis in debt. Its leaders were Godavari Parulekar and her husband Shamrao, working through the Kisan Sabha. Godavari Parulekar had been jailed in 1932 and again from 1940 to 1942; her Marathi account of the revolt, Jevha Manus Jaga Hoto, “When Man Awakens”, would win the Sahitya Akademi Award, and she died on 8 October 1996. [n16; Wikipedia: Godavari Parulekar]

Organising began among the “aboriginal hill tribes of Talasari taluka” in December 1944. On 23 May 1945 a Warli conference met at the village of Zari under the Kisan Sabha, drawing more than five thousand Adivasis from Talasari and Dahanu, and the Parulekars called for an end to veth-begar, bonded and forced labour. [n16; Parulekar, Revolt of the Warlis; SabrangIndia; Drishti IAS] The Warlis of Dahanu declared the “liberation of debt slaves from November 7, 1945”. [n16; Parulekar]

The landlords struck back. On 10 and 11 October 1945, at Talwada in Umbergaon taluka, landlords’ agents spread a false report that Godavari Parulekar would speak there, while the police were told that an armed mob had gathered. Armed police fired from a moving van, on and off for about fifteen hours. Five Warlis were killed and many wounded, among them a twelve-year-old boy. Parulekar herself was ill at Kalyan. The seventeenth of October became a Martyrs’ Day. [n16; Parulekar] In late 1946 a general strike of “20,000 Warlis and Kolis” spread across forty villages of Palghar taluka. About three hundred Warlis were detained and four hundred arrested; the Kisan Sabha leaders, and even their lawyers, were externed from the district; the military was sent in and withdrawn within four days, and more than a thousand police were posted. At Namivali village police firing killed five Warlis and wounded thirteen. [n16; Parulekar]

These details come mostly from Godavari Parulekar’s own pamphlet and from other publications of the Communist Party of India (Marxist) and the All India Kisan Sabha, which are partisan sources; the government’s version of the firings was not found. [n16; Caution] And the pamphlet never mentions Bassein. Its list of forest Adivasi areas reads “Dahanu, Talasari, Jawhar, Wada, Mokhada, Palghar, etc.” [n16; Parulekar] Whether the Kisan Sabha organised in Vasai’s own forest east, on the slopes of Tungar and toward Kaman and Bhiwandi, is unknown. [n16; Gaps] The government volumes do not settle it either. The ICHR Dictionary of Martyrs lists Warli martyrs of Umbergaon taluka from the “tribal mobilisation” of 1945–46 and Quit India men of Chinchani in Dahanu, but no one from Bassein, and the Congress digests of 1942 to 1946 say nothing of the Kisan Sabha in the taluka. [n43; DoM-3 c. p. 118; SM-IV]

The revolt matters to this book because the Varlis were Bassein’s people too. In 1872 the taluka had 6,099 of them, and in 1881 the Hindu caste list counted 7,308 Varlis in the sub-division; the Nihiri section lived in south Mahim and Bassein. [n08; Gaz XIII-I pp. 182–183; n21; Gaz XIII-II p. 680] The Gazetteer of 1882 had already noted that Varlis who had pledged their land to moneylenders were employed in tillage in the rains, an early sign of the debt bondage that the Parulekars fought. [n08; Gaz XIII-I p. 183] Thirty-seven years after Namivali, a young couple from Bombay would found the Shramajeevi Sanghatana in a Vasai village to free bonded labourers, and in 1998 they would disprove the state’s claim that there had been no bonded labour in the area since 1992. [n16; India Environment Portal] The struggle that Godavari Parulekar led in the north reached Vasai, when it reached it, a generation late.

Neighbours: the Second World War

The Second World War, from 1939 to 1945, is as silent in the notes as the first. No source found describes any effect of the war on Bassein: no requisitions, no rationing, no air-raid precautions, no men in the army, no price figures. [n28; Gaps] The great Bombay docks explosion of 1944, the naval mutiny of 1946 in Bombay harbour and the shortages of the war years are part of the general history of the region; the researchers searched specifically for an echo of the 1946 mutiny in Vasai and found none. [n05; Gaps] What happened to the plantains, the salt and the fish of Bassein in the war years is not known.

A marriage at Bordi

In 1944 a young woman from Bordi, on the Dahanu coast, married into the Vartak family. Tarabai, born in 1926, became the wife of Narsinh Vartak. [n16, n26; Wikipedia: Tarabai Vartak] No role in the freedom struggle is recorded for her. In 1972 she would become president of the Thane Zilla Parishad, the first woman to head a Zilla Parishad anywhere in India, and later the member for Vasai and a minister of state, and she would found schools and colleges across Vasai and Virar. [n16, n26; Wikipedia] In 1944 she was eighteen, and her life’s work lay entirely on the far side of independence. She is mentioned here because her marriage is one of the very few dated personal events of the 1940s that the record keeps for a family of Vasai.

Bhuigaon’s church

In 1946, at Bhuigaon, on the coast west of Nirmal, the Holy Family Church was founded for Catholic families who had travelled far to the parish of Nirmal. [n09, n11; Prayers & Petitions; travelindiadestinations, single source] Bhuigaon was a Kupari village of wells and wadis; within living memory about forty large irrigation wells there, the bhowkals, maintained mostly by the women, watered bananas, vegetables and flowers. [n11; Mumbai Paused blog, 2011] Ten years after its church was founded, a fragment of Ashoka’s Rock Edict IX would be found in a Bhuigaon field. [n02; Sircar, Epigraphia Indica 32]

1947

India became independent on 15 August 1947. For Bassein taluka of Thana district, the research behind this book found no record of the day: no flag-hoisting, no procession, no speech, and no account of partition-era departures or arrivals. [n28; 1947; n05; Gaps] The gap is honest. Partition-era Muslim migration from the Konkan, for example, is listed among the things nobody found, not among the things that did not occur. [n05; Gaps] The second, closer search of the government’s volumes and of Marathi and English sources found nothing on the day either. [n43; Kisan Sabha and 15 August 1947]

What remains unfound

The freedom struggle on this coast now has a shape, but it has holes, and they should be named. Four stand out. [n43; What the film can say]

The first is the railway in 1942. Quit India crowds elsewhere attacked lines, stations and telegraph wires, and Bassein Road and Virar were obvious targets. No record of sabotage at either was found; a search turned up only station histories. [n43; Gaps]

The second is Bhausaheb Vartak. He was twenty-eight in 1942, and the Congress’s 1963 Year of Freedom lists him, but no primary source read for this book mentions him before 1947: not the police abstracts of 1930, not the Congress digests of 1942 to 1946, not the martyrs’ dictionary. His role, if he had one, needs a Marathi biography that has not been found. [n43; Gaps; n16]

The third is 15 August 1947, already described: no record of the day.

The fourth is the Christians. No primary source names a Christian freedom fighter from the Vasai villages. The nearest figures are men of the Salsette shore: Augustine Koli of Uttan, and Joseph “Kaka” Baptista, born at Mazagaon in Bombay in 1864 (another source says 1861), the first president of the Indian Home Rule League, whose father John Baptista “hailed from Uttan, near Bhayandar”; one mirror of the same account says “near Bassein”, but Uttan lies in the Mira-Bhayandar area, not in Vasai taluka. [n43; Wikipedia: Joseph Baptista] The only government mention of North Konkan Christians in unrest that the search found is older and hostile: in 1897–98 a Commissioner wrote that the “Native Christians who shared the Koli discontent at the price of liquor emulated them” in the Thana disturbances. [n43; SM-II p. 637] For the decades of the national movement, the Christian periodicals, Suvarta and the Catholic Examiner, and the histories of the Diocese of Vasai, which might say where the Kupari villages and their priests stood in 1942, were not accessible. [n43; Gaps] Whether Bal Samant himself was Hindu or Christian is not recorded. [n43; Gaps]

Beyond these, the names of the Arnala salt-makers of 1930 (if the gazetteer summary is right), the organisers and crowd of the Chowk Bazar rally, the reception committee of the 1921 conference, and any Vasai part in the Samyukta Maharashtra and Goa campaigns after independence remain to be found. The places to look are known: Volume XI of the Source Material, whose page images are online though its text is not; the 1982 Thane District Gazetteer’s chapter on the modern period; page 317 of the Who’s Who of Indian Martyrs; the Home Department file behind Bal Samant’s entry; and the Bombay Chronicle and Times of India of 1920 to 1947. [n43; Where the rest probably is]

The next census, in 1951, would give a measure of what the coast had become by the end of British rule. Bassein taluka then held 133,523 people on 203 square miles: 92,393 rural and 41,130 urban, the urban population living on fourteen square miles. [n21; Census 1951 Vol. IV Pt II-A] Between 1911 and 1951 the taluka’s population had grown by about two-thirds, after thirty years of near-stillness before. [n21; Inferences, derived; boundaries differ] The figures for 1921 and 1931 that would show when the growth began could not be recovered, so the timing of the turn is unknown. [n21; Gaps] But something had changed. The island that had stood still from 1901 to 1911 was moving.

The threads of the period

Land and sea

The land of this chapter is the land of the 1882 Gazetteer, slowly silting. The creek that had carried Sopara’s ships was by 1882 dry at Sopara itself; small boats reached Gass and Bolinj, and the Gazetteer blamed the speed of the silting “especially” on “the long railway embankment” that had dammed the flow of water from the south. [n31; Gaz XIV p. 315] Modern scholarship agrees that siltation over two thousand years was sped up in the second half of the nineteenth century by colonial deforestation and by a rail line on a high embankment that “dismembered the channel”. [n02; Shah 2020, citing Arunachalam 2002] No twentieth-century survey of the creeks before 1947 was found, and the notes record no flood, cyclone or storm in Bassein between 1900 and 1947. [n28; Gaps] The great hurricane of 1618 that had unroofed the fort’s churches and blown a boat into a house at Agashi has no recorded successor in this period. [n11; Gaz XIV p. 2]

The sea and trade

For the sea trade of the island in this period the record is empty. The three Bassein ports of 1881–82, Agashi, Bassein and Navghar, had an estimated trade of £108,868, shipping husked rice, molasses, coconuts and plantains to Bombay and Gujarat in mhangiris, machvas, paddvas and batelas of fifty to seventy tons built locally by Marathas and Native Christians. [n21; Gaz XIII-I pp. 358–359] After 1882, port trade figures were not found. [n21; Gaps] By the end of the nineteenth century the railway had already taken much of the perishable trade: in 1882 large quantities of plantains and betel leaves went south to Bombay by rail, and molasses, rice and betel leaves went north to Ankleshvar, Nadiad, Viramgam and Patan. [n12; Gaz 1882] It is a reasonable inference that the twentieth century continued that shift, but no source measures it.

The traders of the coast in this period were, as far as the notes show, the communities the Gazetteer had named: the Lad Vanis, who had come from Cambay about 1760 fleeing Momin Khan II and who spoke Gujarati among themselves and Marathi with others; the Marwari Vanis, who had arrived since the British conquest and “in great numbers” since the railway opened, lending money to husbandmen; and the Konkani and Naita Muslims of Sopara. [n35; Gaz XIII-I pp. 63–64, 112–114; n31; Gaz XIV p. 315] The Gazetteer’s own judgement on the Marwaris, “thrifty avaricious”, is a colonial author’s, and is given here as such. [n35; note] No twentieth-century source names a Bassein merchant house.

The sea’s other gifts continued, unrecorded. The fishermen of the coast, Son Kolis, Mangelas, Vaitis and Christian Kolis, would have fished through these decades, and their women would have sold the catch; Enthoven’s informants say so in general terms around 1920. [n35; Enthoven II p. 260] The salt works on the north bank of the creek, opened to private enterprise since 1876, would have kept working, but no figure for their output after 1881 was found. [n21; Gaz XIII-I p. 370; n17; Gaps]

Settlements

The settlements of the island changed little in shape, but they changed in name and in institutions. Sopara acquired a station and with it the double name that would become a city’s, and in November 1930 the old port was, for a few weeks, the centre from which a Congress prohibition drive went out into the villages. [n43; SM-XII p. 253] Bassein town was the taluka’s political stage: Khilafat meetings in Hati Mahal Street, a provincial conference in 1921, a volunteer corps in 1942 and a police firing at Chowk Bazar the same year. [n43] Virar’s station was remodelled for local traffic. The fort became a monument. New parishes were founded at Giriz, Gass and Bhuigaon. The taluka’s towns, two of them in 1911, grew slowly. [n21; Census 1911] Naigaon, south of Bassein on the creek, also acquired a railway station after 1882, but its opening year was not found. [n28; Gaps] No new village was founded, as far as the notes show.

Peoples and their languages

The peoples of the island in 1900–1947 were the peoples of 1882, described now by a linguist and a caste compiler. Their languages were the subject of Grierson’s verdicts: the “mixed” speech of Samvedis and Vadvals, half Marathi and half Gujarati; the Mangelas’ Gujarati-tinged fishermen’s talk; the Christians’ Marathi, flecked with padron and madron; the Varlis’ clipped forest speech; the Konkani Muslims’ Marathi with Hindustani words. [n22; LSI vol. VII] And they were already, in Enthoven’s phrase, under “the grinding of the Local Board school and the newspaper”. [n35; Enthoven II p. 259] The new arrivals of the period, if there were any, are not recorded; the great migrations from north India that would remake Nallasopara and Virar belong to the next half-century.

Religion and conversion

In religion the period’s story is consolidation, not conversion. No organised Hindu reconversion campaign, such as the Arya Samaj’s shuddhi movements elsewhere in western India in the 1920s, is documented for Vasai; the researchers found only work on Gujarat and the Malkana Rajputs, neither specific to this coast. [n37; Hardiman search result, flag] Under the British, the Gazetteer had written, “except a small mission of the Scotch free Church to Golwad near Dahanu, little effort has been made to spread Christianity”. [n37; Gaz XIII-I p. 66] The Catholic community grew by births, not by conversions, and built churches for itself. The Hindu communities, as Enthoven’s informants told it, were still settling their ranks: the Panchkalshis arguing their Kshatriya status, the Palshe priests defending theirs with Peshwa-era sanads, the Kolis recalling how Kalu Bhagat had taught them Khandoba. [n35; Enthoven I, II, III] The Uraps, reconverted in 1820 and 1828, still carried Portuguese surnames. [n35; Enthoven I] The coast’s faiths lived side by side; the Nirmal fair of 1882 had drawn “Hindus, Musalmans, Christians, and a few Parsis”, and there is no record that the twentieth century changed that. [n10; Gaz 1882, p. 292]

Work, food and festivals

The work of the coast in this period is known only at its edges. Enthoven found the Vadvals still farmers in about 1920, sharecropping on the khand system. [n35; Enthoven III p. 163] The Kupari community’s own history says that until the late 1960s and early 1970s the community depended mainly on farming. [n07; Rajan D’Mello blog, 2009] The plantains, betel, sugarcane and rice of the 1882 Gazetteer, the nine varieties of banana it named, basrai, mutheli, tambdi, rajeli, lokhandi, sonkeli, bankeli, karanjeli and narsingi, and the dried rajeli plantains of Agashi that were “the best in the district”, were presumably still grown, but no twentieth-century figure for any of them before 1947 was found. [n21; Gaz XIII-I pp. 291–292; n17; Gaps] The kawadis on the afternoon train are the period’s best human image of that economy, though they are undated. [n17; India Food Network] The festivals of the Kuparis, the candlelit Lenten processions called pirsao, the feasts of the saints, the songs of a bride leaving her father’s house and of a daughter returning after the rice planting, are recorded only in later community accounts and cannot be dated to this period. [n07; D’Mello blog; Vasaiker]

The Kuparis and the East Indians

For the Kuparis, the decades from 1900 to 1947 are the time of the Church’s quiet expansion. Their villages, Nirmal, Agashi, Nandakhal, Gass, Bhuigaon, Umrale, Bolinj, Mardes, Vagholi and Rajodi among them, built and rebuilt their churches: Nandakhal’s in 1907–10, Gass’s in 1942, Bhuigaon’s in 1946. [n07; D’Mello blog; n09] Their language was described, under another name, by Grierson, and their Portuguese words for godfather and godmother, padron and madron, were recorded in print for the first time; their own name for themselves, Kupari, comes from the same root, compadre. [n22; LSI vol. VII; n07; Wikipedia: Kupari] They passed with the rest of Bassein’s Catholics from the Diocese of Damaun to the Archdiocese of Bombay in 1928, and they were counted, with the other East Indians, as perhaps twenty-two thousand Christians in the taluka by 1931. [n09; n21; Census 1931, flagged] They were still, as far as the evidence shows, a farming people. Their dress, remembered later by the community, was the men’s dhoti, white shirt, black sleeveless jacket and red cap, and the women’s red blouse with a red checked sari; it is not dated, and one Hindu writer’s claim that the red cap and sari were markers imposed on converts by the Portuguese is unsourced polemic. [n07; D’Mello blog; Swarajya] The story of the Kuparis in this period is told more fully in Part Seven of this book.

Toward the republic

By 1947 the island of Bassein had a population of perhaps 130,000, a market town of some twelve or thirteen thousand, an electric railway that ended at Virar, a station at the ancient port that now bore the name Nallasopara, a ruined fort under the protection of the state, nine Portuguese mother churches and three new daughter parishes, a Shankaracharya’s samadhi at Nirmal, a goddess on Jivdhan hill and a forest on Tungar. [n21; Census 1951; n28; n09] Its people spoke a cluster of dialects that a linguist had dismissed as not worth the paper, and that would, in the next century, be studied as endangered languages. [n22; LSI vol. VII; Kumar et al., WAT 2024]

Its part in the freedom struggle is no longer an open question, though it is not yet a full answer. The government’s own record shows Bassein in the movement at every stage: pleaders chairing Khilafat meetings in 1919 and 1920; a non-co-operation meeting the police thought important in 1920; the first Maharashtra Provincial Conference, and Gandhi, in May 1921; three thousand at Virar and a prohibition drive from Sopara in 1930; five hundred volunteers in the spring of 1942; a fifteen-year-old labourer, Bal Samant, shot dead at Chowk Bazar on 9 September 1942; and Sane Guruji in the town in 1945 and 1946, speaking of the innocent dead. [n43] It was, by the police’s measure, a modest movement in a district of “comparatively little agitation”. [n43; SM-XII] But it was there. Around it lay the neighbours: Bombay, where Quit India was launched; Palghar, where five men died at 12:39 on 14 August 1942; Talasari and Dahanu, where the Warlis rose against debt slavery; Uttan, whose Koli son worked underground on Panju island in Bassein creek. [n16] And it held a generation of Vasai men and women, Bhausaheb Vartak born in 1914 and Tarabai Vartak who married into his family in 1944, who would govern the place after independence, and whose own part in the struggle is not yet documented. [n16, n26; n43] The rest of the record exists somewhere, in the Thane Gazetteer of 1982, in Volume XI of the Source Material, in the freedom-fighter pension rolls, in the Home Department files and in the Marathi books on the shelves of Vasai’s own history societies. [n16; n43; Gaps] When they are read, this chapter will grow again.

At four in the afternoon, in the years after independence, the kawadis would still board the train for Bombay with their baskets on their shoulders. The railway that carried them would soon lose its colonial name, and the quiet years of the republic would begin.
