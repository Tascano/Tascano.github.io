Period covered: 15 August 1947 to November 2026

In this chapter. The end of the line in 1947, the quiet decades, the opening of 1988, and Virar’s councils, elections and police.

On Tuesday, 5 May 2026, the morning crowd on the platforms of Virar station did something the railway had not planned for. Four days earlier, from 1 May, Western Railway had turned the 8.28 Virar–Churchgate local, an ordinary morning train, into an air-conditioned service, with the AC locals’ higher fares. On 5 May the commuters who had lost their train went down off the platform edge and onto the tracks. Trains stopped. The Free Press Journal called it a “massive uproar”; the form of protest has a name across India, rail roko, “stop the train”. [n47; FPJ, “Massive uproar at Virar railway station”, May 2026]

It is a fitting scene with which to begin the modern history of Virar, because almost everything in that history runs along the same pair of rails. The station had been the northern end of Bombay’s suburban service since the first train left “Viraur” for Back Bay at a quarter to seven on the morning of 12 April 1867, and the electric locals had reached it in 1936. [n45; n28; FPJ, 12 Apr 2022; Wikipedia: Western line] For most of the eighty years told in this chapter, Virar was simply the end of the line: the last stop of the commuter world and the first of the farming one. Then, from 1988, the commuter world came north to meet it. A village-town of a few thousand households at independence became, by 2001, a town of about 119,000 people, and by 2026 the northern third of a municipal corporation of well over a million. The people on the tracks in May 2026 were the town that the trains had made, protesting against the trains.

This chapter follows Virar from independence to November 2026: the quiet decades, the rezoning of 1988 and the shortages that followed, the town’s own municipal council and its merger into the Vasai-Virar City Municipal Corporation, the political family whose base it became, the trains, buses, flyover and corridors, the townships and the housing board’s unsold flats, the hospital fire of 2021 and the building collapse of 2025, the police, the churches and festivals, the cricketers of the Virar local and the town as it stood in the autumn of 2026. Chapter 27 has already told parts of this story in brief, and Chapters 14, 14 and 15 tell the history of the whole taluka in these decades; where they have told an episode at length, this chapter points to them and adds what is particular to Virar. Its companion, Chapter 30, tells the land, the water and the work beneath the town, and Chapter 31 tells its peoples and villages.

A warning first. For the four decades after 1947 the record for Virar is very thin. The research behind this book found no Virar-town census figure before 1981, no founding year for the Virar Municipal Council, no list of its presidents, no history of its schools before 1991, and no newspaper account in Marathi of the town’s life before the 1990s; every Marathi query sent in search of these returned only English mirrors of Wikipedia. [n47; n28] Where the record is silent this chapter says so, and says what was happening around Virar instead.

1947: the end of the line

What did Virar look like on the day India became independent? No document describes it. The nearest descriptions lie sixty-five years before and thirty-four years after.

The earlier one is the Gazetteer of the Bombay Presidency of 1882, whose compilers found Virar “a rich village well-wooded and with many patches of sugarcane”, about seven miles north of Bassein and about thirty-eight north of Bombay, with a railway station that had refreshment and waiting rooms. To the east of the station, across the line, was the market place, on the west bank of a large pond under big banyan and mango trees, where “a large market is held every Saturday, thronged by Kolis and Varlis, selling fish, garden stuff, and cloth”. A metalled road ran four miles west to Agashi and the sea. [n48 §1; n45 §1; Gaz XIV pp. 382–383] Station traffic had risen from 48,294 passengers in 1878 to 83,176 in 1880. [n45; n48; Gaz XIV p. 382]

The later description is the census of 1981, which counted 23,303 people in Virar, against 34,900 in Vasai town, and found that Agashi, with more than ten thousand, was the only village of the region over that mark. [n47; n17; TIFR, “The Vasai-Virar Region”] Somewhere between those two snapshots lies the Virar of 1947, and the only safe statement is that it was a small station town among gardens, larger than the village of 1882 and much smaller than the town of 1981.

A few things about it can be fixed. The station had been remodelled “after or in 1913” with a Down platform, an Island platform and two signal cabins, A and B, according to the railway company’s own magazine of 1923 as quoted by Wikipedia. [n45 §2; n28; Wikipedia: Virar railway station] The suburban electric trains, which had begun running from Colaba to Borivali on 5 January 1928, were extended in 1936, when the two lines from Borivali to Virar were electrified at 1,500 volts direct current and steam ended on the suburban section; Wikipedia is the only dated source for that year, though it is consistent with the electrification history kept by the Indian Railways Fan Club. [n28; Wikipedia: Western line; IRFCA] So by independence Virar had been, for eleven years, the northern terminus of electric commuter service out of Bombay, and it would stay the terminus for another sixty-six. On 5 November 1951 the old company name, the Bombay, Baroda and Central India Railway that had laid the line through Bassein in 1864, gave way to the Western Railway. [n28; Wikipedia: Western line]

The town’s people in these years are known only by their categories. The 1882 market was “thronged by Kolis and Varlis”: the fishing people of the coast and creek, and the Warli Adivasis of the hills to the east, coming down to sell. [n48 §1; Gaz XIV p. 382] Around the station lay the garden villages of Christian Samvedis, the Kuparis, and of Samvedi Brahmins, Vadvals, Pachkalshis and Bhandaris, whose histories are told in Part Seven and in Chapter 31. The mother church of the whole Virar belt was Holy Spirit, Nandakhal, in what is now Virar West, built in 1573 by Wikipedia’s account; the parish’s own page says it was “officially established in 1858” and became a parish in 1886, and the dates do not agree. [n49 §2b; Wikipedia: Holy Spirit Church Nandakhal; parish page] Every Catholic parish founded in Virar after independence would be cut out of Nandakhal.

For the whole taluka there is one census of the period whose figures survive in the notes. In 1951 Bassein taluka held 133,523 people, 41,130 of them in its urban areas, chiefly Bassein town itself. [n21; Census 1951 Vol IV Part II-A] Whether Virar counted as a town or as a village in that census is not recorded; the town tables of 1951, 1961 and 1971, which would answer the question, were identified by researchers in the Census of India’s own catalogue (a 1961 provisional talukawise table and a 1971 supplement on Standard Urban Areas giving 1951 and 1961 populations) but were never opened. [n47 Second Appendix; Census 1961 PRO; Census 1971 GPT] They are the first place a future historian of Virar should look.

Of independence itself, nothing local is recorded. Chapter 13 has told what the record holds of the freedom struggle on this coast: Gandhi’s visit to Bassein in 1921, the salt satyagraha of April 1930 at Arnala, Virar and Vasai, when a meeting at Virar on 15 April chaired by D. Gokhale saw seventy-five people break the salt laws, and the Quit India year of 1942. [n43; Thane Gazetteer 1982, secondary summary] For the day itself, 15 August 1947, there is no Virar-specific account at all.

The quiet decades, 1947–1981

The four decades after independence are the emptiest in the modern record of Virar, and for an honest reason: very little happened that anyone wrote down. The region, as the history of Bombay kept by the Tata Institute of Fundamental Research puts it, “remained mainly agricultural until the late 1980s”. [n47; TIFR] Its towns grew slowly; its farms sent vegetables, plantains and flowers to the city on the afternoon trains; its young people went to Bombay, the Gulf and further. The story of those years for the whole taluka is told in Chapter 14. Here are the few dated threads that run through Virar.

Politics at a distance

Virar had no assembly seat of its own. It voted in the Bassein constituency, later Vasai, whose first two members were socialists: Sadanand G. Warty of the Socialist Party in 1952 and, under the Praja Socialist Party label, in 1957. From 1962 the seat belonged for three terms to Hari Govindrao “Bhausaheb” Vartak of the Congress, a cabinet minister in Vasantrao Naik’s government who held public health, salt land and fishing and later revenue, and who received the Padma Shri in 1991. [n26 §1–2; Wikipedia: Bassein Assembly constituency; Wikipedia: Hari Govindrao Vartak] Tarabai Vartak, who married into the same family in 1944, became president of the Thane Zilla Parishad in 1972, the first woman to head a zilla parishad anywhere in India, and sat for Vasai from 1980 to 1985; she founded schools and colleges “in Vasai–Virar”, though the sources do not say which were in Virar. [n26; Wikipedia: Tarabai Vartak] After her came Pandharinath Choudhari of the Janata Party (1978), Tarabai Vartak herself (1980) and Dominic Gonsalves of the Janata Party (1985). [n26; Wikipedia: Vasai Assembly constituency]

The Vartaks’ most lasting work was a ladder: Vartak College in Vasai and the Vidyavardhini education society, linked to the family by Wikipedia’s accounts. [n26; Wikipedia: Hari Govindrao Vartak] Two boys who grew up in Virar in these decades climbed it. One was a future film star, Govinda, born in December 1963, whose family, by his own later accounts, moved from Bandra to a 300-square-foot chawl in Virar after financial setbacks; he went to school in Virar and took a commerce degree at Vartak College. [n27; WION; ETV Bharat; DNA India] His birthplace is disputed, and Chapter 27 sets out the dispute. The other boy was born in Virar on 3 October 1961, a son of the family that would later give its name to the Late Shri Vishnu Waman Thakur Charitable Trust. His name was Hitendra Thakur, and he too was a Vartak College man. [n26; n29; Wikipedia: Hitendra Thakur]

The goddess on the hill

On the hill a mile east of the station, the cave shrine that the 1882 Gazetteer had described, a niche in the rock where “barren women from the surrounding villages” threw betel nuts to an offended deity, was becoming a temple. When exactly is not known. One Marathi press account puts the prana-pratishtha, the ritual installation, of the goddess Jivdani’s image by devotees in 1956; another account calls the temple a seventeenth-century building; the Palghar district’s tourism page calls it about 150 years old; Wikipedia says that “the present idol is a very recent one” and that the original sanctum is “the hole in the niche of the cave”. [n20; n49 §2c; n47; Pudhari; Lokmat; eSakal; Palghar district; Wikipedia: Jivdani Mata] None of these claims can be reconciled with the others from the sources reached. The Gazetteer of 1882 mentions caves, cisterns and ruined fortifications on the hill, but no built temple, which is the one hard fact: whatever the age of the cult, the temple building that pilgrims climb to today postdates 1882. [n45 §1, §3; Gaz XIV] Chapter 27 tells the story of the goddess and the Mhar herdsman, and how the modern version of it has changed from the version printed in 1882.

The parishes divide

The Catholic villages of the Virar side leave the clearest dated marks of the period, because their parishes kept registers. In 1970 Umrale, one of the old Kupari garden villages north of Sopara, became an independent parish, the first to be cut out of Nandakhal. On 1 June 1975 Nanbhat followed, “under Monsignor Philip Tavares”. [n49 §2b; Wikipedia: Holy Spirit Church Nandakhal (flagged “needs more citations”; one source is a 2016 parish policy document)] At Shirlai, under the Agashi post office, a diocesan directory dates the parish of Our Lady of Lourdes to 1987, with its feast in February. [n49 §2b; UCAN directory (snippet)] These dates come from secondary listings and the parish’s own document, and they need checking against diocesan records, but their direction is clear: the old Christian belt round Virar was growing denser, and its single great parish could no longer hold it.

The people of these parishes were, by the community’s own list, Kuparis: Umrale, Nanbhat, Shirlaiwadi, Bolinj, Agashi, Nandakhal and Rajodi are all among the thirteen places it names. [n39; n40] The parish history fills in their decades in a few dated lines. Rev. Frank Lobo, Nandakhal’s first local priest, had been ordained on 5 January 1943 “after 370 years”; Mass began to be said in Umrale and Nanbhat themselves from 1949; the Queen of the Apostles sisters came to Nandakhal in 1952; and by 2016 the parish counted sixty-eight priests and many nuns given to the Church. [n40; Wikipedia: Holy Spirit Church Nandakhal] Agashi opened its St James Marathi School in 1968 and St James High School in 1977. [n40; EI Agashi] The same years were the ones in which, by community accounts, Kupari families left farming for the professions and for the Gulf and the West (below). Part Seven, Chapter 37, tells the Kuparis’ post-1947 history in full, and Chapter 31 (Section IV) tells it village by village.

Standing still

What happened to the population of these decades is the strangest fact of the period, and Chapter 14 discusses it in full. In 1971 the Vasai–Virar region held 194,262 people by one count. In 1981 the census found fewer than 250,000, and the TIFR account says that many towns and villages of the area had actually lost people in the decade before; it does not say whether Virar was among them. [n47 Second Appendix; n06; TIFR; Business Standard, 11 Apr 2014] The farm families’ sons and daughters were going to the city and abroad; the Kupari community histories speak of families moving into engineering, medicine and banking and to the Gulf, Europe, the United States and Australia from the late 1960s. [n34; Vasai Kupari blog] The kawadis, the farmer-vendors who carried their produce in baskets slung from a shoulder pole, still took the four o’clock train to Victoria Terminus and sold near Dadar Kabutarkhana and Bora Bazaar, but they were fading as families moved to office jobs. [n17; India Food Network, 6 Oct 2015] Chapter 30 follows their trade.

The railway, meanwhile, was getting ready. On 28 October 1986 twelve-coach trains began running between Dadar and Virar. [n28; Wikipedia: Western line] A twelve-car rake carries half as many people again as a nine-car one. The line had been stretched to carry a population that had not yet arrived.

1981: twenty-three thousand

The census of 1981 is the first in which Virar appears as a town with a number of its own: 23,303 people, by the TIFR account. [n47 Second Appendix; n17; TIFR] For the corporation area as later defined, the census series gives 219,868 for 1981. [n47; Wikipedia: Vasai-Virar] So in 1981 Virar held roughly one person in ten of the future city, and was smaller than Vasai town (34,900) and, very likely, than its own coastal neighbour Agashi, which had over ten thousand. [n17; TIFR]

It is worth pausing on that comparison. A century earlier, in 1881, the Gazetteer had counted 6,823 people in Agashi, the old port and boatbuilding town at the mouth of the Vaitarna, and had called it in 1837 “the chief town on Bassein island”. [n48 §1; Gaz XIV pp. 1–2] Virar in 1881 was a village whose population the Gazetteer did not even record. [n45 Gaps] By 1981 the station town and the port town were of roughly the same order. Within twenty years the station town would be more than ten times the size of the old port. The sea had made Agashi; the railway made Virar.

1988: the gates open

The decision that remade Virar was taken in Bombay, by the state government, in 1988. On 31 August of that year the Regional Plan for the Vasai-Virar sub-region was sanctioned, with a Green Zone among its zones. In the same year, as the TIFR history puts it, “the state government de-reserved this region from agricultural use, contrary to the recommendation of the MMRDA”, the metropolitan planning authority. Builders moved in at once. [n17; n47 Second Appendix; TIFR; VVCMC draft DCR 2013] Harit Vasai’s own chronology dates the conversion of the green belt to residential use to August 1988 and adds that it was done without any of the infrastructure that housing needs. [n29; Sabrang India, 30 Nov 2000]

Two years later the plan was widened. In May 1990 about 8,500 hectares were moved from the Green Zone to the urbanisable zone, for a planned city of twelve lakh people by 2011. [n17; Revised Land Use Plan document] The planning authority changed hands too. The sanctioned development control regulations cite a notification of 14 May 1990 appointing the City and Industrial Development Corporation, CIDCO, as planning authority for the sub-region; the TIFR history says CIDCO replaced MMRDA in 1991. [n29 §1, §5; n47 Second Appendix; VVCMC DCR; TIFR] The better-documented date is 14 May 1990; 1991 may be when CIDCO’s role first bit. A further modification of 1990 added about two thousand hectares more to the urbanisable zone; after objections, that land went back to the green zone “except land along a three-road stretch of Virar town”, by the summary of an academic case study that researchers could not open in full. [n29 §1; IDRC case study (search summary)] The detail is small and second-hand, but it is the only place in the planning record where Virar is singled out, and it singles it out as the place where the line between green and urban was drawn most generously.

The green-belt movement that rose against these decisions, the Harit Vasai Saurakshan Samiti led by Fr Francis D’Britto, was centred in the Christian villages south and west of Vasai, and its story is told in Chapter 15. Its great rallies were at Vasai: about 35,000 people on 1 October 1989, about 100,000 on 26 January 1992. [n29 §1; Sabrang India] Virar, at the end of the commuter line and with fewer of the old garden villages inside its limits, was the first and easiest place for the new housing to go. The movement’s leader, though, came from the Virar side: Fr D’Britto was a son of Jeladi, a Kupari hamlet of Vatar in Nandakhal parish, and when he died in July 2024 about 3,000 people came to his funeral at Nandakhal. [n38; n40; FPJ; Sabrang India] Chapter 31 tells his village and Chapter 37 his place in the Kuparis’ modern history.

What the new people found

The newcomers arrived in a place that was not ready for them. In 1991, at the moment CIDCO took over planning, power was available only two days a week; there was no piped water and no central drainage; under ten per cent of the area had motorable roads. By 1996 some residents could get as little as ten litres of water a day. [n47 Second Appendix; n17; TIFR] Ten litres is a single bucket. Many of the buildings that went up in these years were unauthorised and were later regularised. [n17; TIFR]

Water was the first battleground. In the early 1990s builders and tanker operators drew groundwater from the wells of the green belt to supply the new colonies; the farmers’ wells turned saline and their produce suffered. A Times of India report of 20 May 1991, quoted by Sabrang, described violence against the area’s Christian residents linked to the well-water agitation, and after ministerial-level talks in which Harit Vasai took part, tankers were banned from drawing water in the green belt. [n29 §1; Sabrang India] Housing colonies spread near the stations of Vasai, Naigaon, Nala-Sopara and Virar. [n29; Sabrang India] Chapter 30 follows the water story through the wells, the dams and the long pipelines.

Five times over

The numbers moved faster than the pipes. Between 1981 and 1991 the region’s population rose by about 150 per cent overall; the TIFR history says that Virar, Vasai, Sopara and Sandor together grew by about 290 per cent, against 150 per cent for the wider region, though the decade of that figure is not stated in the page itself and other notes place it in 1981–91. [n47 Second Appendix; n17; TIFR] The corporation-area totals ran 219,868 (1981), 365,480 (1991), 693,350 (2001). [n47; Wikipedia: Vasai-Virar]

For Virar itself there is one figure. An appraisal document of the Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs gives the 2001 population of Virar as 118,945, against 49,346 for Vasai. [n47; MoHUA appraisal] If the boundaries of 1981 and 2001 are the same, which is not verified, Virar grew about five-fold in twenty years: from 23,303 to 118,945. [n47 Second Appendix, Inferences] And by 2001 the station town, which had been smaller than Vasai in 1981, was more than twice as large. The 2011 figure often quoted for “Virar”, 1,222,390, is in fact the total for the whole corporation, not the town. [n47; Wikipedia: Virar]

Where did the newcomers come from? The sources do not say in numbers. Wikipedia, without a source, gives Virar’s languages as about half Marathi, 28.30 per cent Gujarati and 15.31 per cent Hindi, and attributes the town’s growth to migration from the Mumbai mainland because of property prices; it adds that about seventy per cent of residents are under thirty. [n49 §1h; n45 §6; Wikipedia: Virar] The census migration tables that would answer the question were not reached. [n06 Gaps] One family story is documented: the parents of the cricketer Prithvi Shaw, born in Virar in 1999, were economic migrants from Gaya in Bihar. [n27; Wikipedia: Prithvi Shaw]

The Virar Municipal Council

At some point in these decades Virar acquired its own local government: the Virar Municipal Council, a nagar parishad. It was one of four such councils in the taluka, with Vasai, Nallasopara and Navghar-Manikpur, that would later be merged into the Vasai-Virar City Municipal Corporation. [n47; Wikipedia: Vasai-Virar] That much is certain. Almost everything else about it is not.

When was the council founded? No source reached for this book gives the year. Searches in English and in Marathi (the researchers tried विरार नगरपरिषद स्थापना नगराध्यक्ष, “Virar municipal council founding, council president”) returned only Wikipedia mirrors and the corporation’s own pages; the TIFR history of the region does not mention a Virar council at all; the 1882 Gazetteer shows no municipality anywhere in Bassein sub-division. [n47 Second Appendix; n28; TIFR] The founding year of the Virar Municipal Council is unknown, and this book will not guess it.

Who led it? Two names survive, both from later sources. A page describing Rajeev Patil, known as “Nana”, calls him an ex-President of the Virar Municipal Council; the claim rests on a mirror of Wikipedia and should be checked. [n47; List of mayors of Vasai-Virar] And when Mukesh Save died in Dadar at the age of seventy-three, in late November 2025, the Marathi daily Sakal called him a former nagaradhyaksha, council president, of Virar. He was remembered less for his time at the head of the council than as the “architect” of the Vasai Taluka Kala-Krida Mahotsav, the arts and sports festival held each December, and “for the last thirty-seven years its main pillar and guide”; his show Mahi Vasai, on Vasai’s traditions and past, had been staged at the festival’s twenty-fifth edition. [n20; eSakal, 30 Nov 2025] His term as council president is not dated. Nor are the council’s elections, its wards, its budgets or its buildings. Between 1995 and 2009 the council must have held elections, and the researchers suggested the Maharashtra Government Gazette, Praja Foundation reports or the supplements to the Thane District Gazetteer as places to find them. [n47 Second Appendix, Gaps] They were not found.

So the council that governed Virar through its boom years, that approved or failed to stop the buildings of the 1990s, and that dealt with the water, the drains and the roads, exists in the record of this book only as a name, two presidents and an ending.

2009: the merger

The ending is dated, though not without dispute. By the corporation’s own official account, the Vasai-Virar City Municipal Corporation was established on 3 July 2009, from the four municipal councils and fifty-three villages. [n47; n29 §2, §5; VVCMC About] Wikipedia’s Virar page gives May 2009 as the date the Virar council was “upgraded”; its corporation infobox, and the environmental-clearance papers of 2014 and 2017 for the Surya water project, give 2010. [n45 §6; n49 §3a; n29 §5; Wikipedia; Surya EC summary] The best reading, offered in the notes as an inference and not as fact, is that 3 July 2009 is the notification and 2010 the first elected body. [n47; n29 §5]

The merger was resisted. Wikipedia’s Hitendra Thakur page says that on 3 July 2009 the state notified the inclusion of fifty-three villages and that forty-nine of them opposed it; the opposition was led by Vivek Pandit’s Gao Bachao Andolan Samiti, and the page alleges that Thakur “allegedly influenced police” to arrest and assault Pandit during the protest, which is an allegation with no finding. [n29 §2; n26; Wikipedia: Hitendra Thakur] A blog of September 2011 reported a state notification excluding twenty-nine of the fifty-three villages, followed by a court case. [n29 §3; vasairoad blog] Chapter 15 tells the protest and Chapter 27 the later litigation. For Virar town the merger meant the end of its council and its absorption, as the “northern third” of the city in Wikipedia’s phrase, into a corporation whose headquarters lay elsewhere. [n49 §3a; Wikipedia: Virar]

The mayors

The corporation’s mayors are listed in two tables that do not agree with each other, and both need to be set down.

By the list of mayors of Vasai-Virar (a mirror-based page), the first mayor was Rajeev Patil, the former council president, from 1 June 2010 to 27 December 2012; Narayan Mankar followed from 1 January 2013; and Pravin Shetty took office from 1 January 2021. [n47 Second Appendix; List of mayors of Vasai-Virar] That list, the researchers noted, “may be stale”.

By the corporation’s Wikipedia page, the order and dates are: Rajeev Patil, 28 March 2010 to 4 January 2012; Narayan Mankar, 5 January 2012 to 28 June 2015; Pravina Thakur, Hitendra Thakur’s wife, 29 June 2015 to 28 December 2017, called the first woman mayor; Rupesh Jadhav, 29 December 2017 to 25 July 2019; Narayan Mankar again, for twenty-eight days, 26 July to 22 August 2019; and Pravin Shetty, 23 August 2019 to 28 June 2020. [n26 §3; Wikipedia: VVCMC] Its first deputy mayor was Sagir Ahmed Dange. [n26 §3]

The two tables agree on the first names and on Rajeev Patil as the first mayor; they disagree on almost every date, and on Pravin Shetty’s tenure by more than a year. The second table has the support of fuller detail, but neither has been checked against the corporation’s records. Both leave a gap after mid-2020: civic elections across Maharashtra were delayed, and no mayor sat until 3 February 2026, when Ajiv Patil of the Bahujan Vikas Aghadi took office with Marshal Dominic Lopes as deputy mayor. [n26 §3; n06; Wikipedia: VVCMC; Wikipedia: 2025–26 Maharashtra local elections] Who ran the city in the five-and-a-half-year interval is not stated in the notes; an administrator is likely, but unsourced. [n26 Gaps] Whether the Rajeev Patil of 2010 and the Ajiv Patil of 2026 are related could not be confirmed. [n26 Gaps]

The Thakurs’ base

From the 1990s the political history of Virar is, to a degree that no other place on the coast can match, the history of one family. Chapter 15 tells how Hitendra Thakur rose; Chapter 27 summarises the family’s record. This section gathers what the notes say about Virar as the family’s base.

Thakur Niwas

Hitendra Vishnu Thakur was born in Virar on 3 October 1961, and Wikipedia gives his residence as “Thakur Niwas, Virar”. [n45 §6; n29 §1; Wikipedia: Hitendra Thakur] He was president of the Vasai Taluka Youth Congress from 1988 and in March 1990, aged twenty-nine, won the Vasai seat for the Congress, beating Dominik John Gonsalvis of the Janata Dal by 46,468 votes. [n29 §1; Wikipedia: Vasai Assembly constituency] He won again as an independent in 1995 (by 33,575 over Deepak Gawhankar of the Shiv Sena), 1999 (by 50,428 over Deepak Gavankar, the same opponent under a variant spelling) and 2004 (by 67,917 over Vivek Pandit, then standing for the Shiv Sena). [n29 §1–2; Wikipedia: Vasai Assembly constituency] Why he left the Congress between 1990 and 1995 is not explained in the sources. [n29 Gaps] His local organisation, the Vasai Vikas Mandal, became the Bahujan Vikas Aghadi (BVA), whose president he became in 2008 by Wikipedia’s infobox. [n29 §2; Wikipedia]

The seat he held, Vasai, did not include Virar after 2008. The delimitation of that year created a new Nallasopara constituency which, by the Deccan Herald’s description, covers Virar and parts of the corporation. [n47; n29 §2; Deccan Herald; Wikipedia: Nallasopara Assembly constituency] So from 2009 the member for Virar was the family’s next generation: Kshitij Thakur, Hitendra’s elder son, born on 10 July 1983 in the Virar area (another source gives Devgaon as his birthplace), called the youngest member of the state assembly when first elected. [n47; n26; n27; Wikipedia: Kshitij Thakur]

The Viva trust and its colleges

The family’s most visible mark on the town is not a building of government but a campus. The Late Shri Vishnu Waman Thakur Charitable Trust, named for Hitendra Thakur’s father, runs the Viva institutions on Viva College Road in Virar West. Their dates come only from aggregator websites, which conflict. One aggregator says the trust was formed in 1988 under Hitendra Thakur. Wikipedia mirrors say the trust’s Utkarsha Junior College was established in 1991, under the then MLA. The Viva College of Arts, Science and Commerce dates from 2000 or 2001, depending on the source. The Viva Institute of Technology, at Shirgaon in Virar East, dates from 2009. [n47; n49 §3c; VIVA College (Wikipedia); Admistay; Shaalaa] The college’s constituent wings carry family and associates’ names: the Bhaskar Waman Thakur College of Science, the Yashvant Keshav Patil College of Commerce and the Vidhya Dayanand Patil College of Arts. [n47; Shaalaa] The college’s own history page, which might settle the dates, was not read. [n47 Gaps]

If the 1988 date is right, the trust was founded in the same year that the green belt was opened and that Hitendra Thakur became youth Congress president; if 1991 is the first date of an actual college, it came in his first term as MLA. Either way the sequence matters. Before the trust, the ladder of higher education for Virar’s young people ran south to Vartak College in Vasai, the Vartak family’s institution, where both Govinda and Hitendra Thakur had studied. After it, the ladder had a Virar rung, and the rung bore the Thakur name. Wikipedia says the family controls the Viva Group and the Viva Trust, a statement flagged “citation needed”; Lokmat describes the Viva Group as owned by Bhai Thakur’s family. [n26 §2; Wikipedia: Hitendra Thakur; Lokmat Times]

In 2015, when the BVA won 106 of the corporation’s 115 seats, the Association for Democratic Reforms found that Hitendra Thakur had declared assets of Rs 158 crore, the highest of any candidate in those polls; his cousin Pankaj Thakur declared Rs 128 crore. [n26 §2; n06; ADR]

Bhai Thakur and the acquittal of 2023

The family’s history has a darker strand, and it must be told in the terms the sources allow: as allegations, with one court outcome.

On 9 October 1989 Suresh Dube, a builder aged thirty-one, was shot at point-blank range in daylight on the platform of Nallasopara station. [n26 §2; n29 §1; FPJ, 24 May 2023] Prosecutors alleged that the killers were linked to the gang of Jayendra “Bhai” Thakur, Hitendra’s elder brother, described by the Free Press Journal and Lokmat as the “strongman” of Vasai–Virar. [n47 Second Appendix; n26; FPJ; Lokmat Times] In 1992 seventeen accused were arrested, by a search summary of the Supreme Court’s judgment in S.N. Dube v. N.B. Bhoir; a Designated Court under the Terrorist and Disruptive Activities (Prevention) Act, TADA, at first acquitted all of them, finding the confessions inadmissible and the eyewitness evidence unreliable; Dube’s brother and the state appealed; and the Supreme Court later upheld the convictions of six. [n29 §1; n47; Supreme Court judgment (search summary); Lawtrend] The same summary says the judgment lists Hitendra Thakur among the accused and names his brothers among the gang’s members. His later political career shows that he was not convicted, but his final status in the case is not clear in the sources. [n29 §1] Two headlines cited by Wikipedia (“Administrative delay allows TADA detainee, Hitendra Thakur to get away”, India Today, 1994, and “TADA Acquits Thakur”, Times of India) point to a TADA matter against him whose charges and outcome were never read. [n26 §2; Wikipedia: Hitendra Thakur] His 2014 election affidavit listed eight criminal cases, with charges framed in seven and cognizance taken in one, and no convictions. [n26; n29 §3]

Bhai Thakur’s own trial in the Dube case lasted thirty-four years. On 24 May 2023 a special TADA court in Pune, under Judge S.R. Navander, acquitted him and others for lack of evidence. The reports differ on how many co-accused were acquitted with him (two or three; a search summary names Deepak Thakur and Gajanan Patil). The Free Press Journal described the verdict as ending the last case under TADA anywhere in India. Dube’s daughter Neha said the family would appeal to the Supreme Court. [n47 Second Appendix; n29 §4; n26; FPJ; Lokmat Times; ThePrint] Whether an appeal was filed by 2026 is not known. [n47 Gaps]

Beyond the courtroom, press summaries (not verified against full texts) say that Bhai Thakur was once linked to Varadarajan Mudaliar’s gold-smuggling network and later to the Dawood Ibrahim gang, and that he moved into real estate in Vasai–Virar and backed his brother’s election wins. These are press allegations, unproven in court so far as the research found. [n47 Second Appendix; Lokmat Times (search summary)] The Free Press Journal’s line that Bhai found it cost-effective to make his brother and nephew MLAs is the paper’s characterisation, not a legal finding. [n26 §2] And the green-belt movement’s charge, reported by UCA News in February 1995, that its main activity was opposition to “Thakur’s land acquisitions, unauthorized, ill-planned construction and illegal land transactions”, is the movement’s claim as reported. [n29 §1; UCA News, 1 Feb 1995]

The record in the sources, then, is this: electoral dominance for three decades; accusations, serious and persistent; and, in every case where a court outcome is stated, no conviction of a member of the family.

Elections, 2009–2024

From 2009 the family held both of the corporation’s assembly seats for most of fifteen years. In the October 2009 election Kshitij Thakur won Nallasopara with 89,284 votes (52.84 per cent), beating Shirish Chavhan of the Shiv Sena by 40,782 on a turnout of 48.06 per cent, while his father lost Vasai to Vivek Pandit, an independent with Shiv Sena support, by 16,798 votes, ending a run that had begun in March 1990. [n29 §2; n26; Wikipedia: Nallasopara AC; Wikipedia: Vasai AC]

In 2014 both Thakurs won: Kshitij took Nallasopara with 113,566 votes (50.72 per cent), beating Rajan Naik of the BJP by 54,499 on a turnout of 57 per cent, and Hitendra won back Vasai from Pandit by 31,896 (97,291 to 65,395). [n29 §3; Wikipedia] In the same year, on 1 August 2014, Virar changed districts without moving an inch: Palghar became Maharashtra’s thirty-sixth district, separated from Thane, with Vasai as one of its eight talukas. [n29 §3; Wikipedia: Palghar district; The News Minute] Chapter 16 tells that change. In 2019 Kshitij won Nallasopara again with 149,868 votes (56.37 per cent), beating Pradeep Sharma of the Shiv Sena by 43,729; Hitendra beat Vijay Patil of the Shiv Sena in Vasai by 25,995. [n29 §3; n47 Second Appendix; Wikipedia] At the parliamentary level the BVA had held the Palghar Lok Sabha seat once, through Baliram Jadhav in 2009. [n26 §1]

Then came 23 November 2024. In Nallasopara, the seat that covers Virar, Rajan Naik of the BJP, who had lost to Kshitij Thakur by 54,499 ten years earlier, won with 165,113 votes and a margin of 36,875. In Vasai, Sneha Dube Pandit of the BJP beat Hitendra Thakur by 3,153 votes on the Election Commission’s round-wise count; other outlets gave about 3,275 or “over 3,700”, and the ECI figure is the one to use. [n47; n26 §1; n29 §4; ECI; Lokmat Times] The Deccan Herald’s headline: “saffron wave hits bastion of Thakurs”. [n47; n06; Deccan Herald] On counting day the Free Press Journal reported BJP–BVA tension in Nallasopara, where the BVA had accused the BJP’s Vinod Tawde of cash-for-votes, an allegation and not a finding. [n26 §2; FPJ] No source explains the defeat; the notes observe that the BJP’s gains came alongside a statewide wave and the demolitions and investigations of 2024–25 told in Chapter 16, and say plainly that no causal link is established. [n06; n29 §4, Inferences]

January 2026: the whistle and the lotus

Fourteen months later the BVA came back, in part. In the corporation election of 15 January 2026, counted the next day, it won 71 of 115 seats against 43 for the BJP and one for the Shinde faction of the Shiv Sena, on a turnout of 57.12 per cent, one of the highest in the state; no other party won a seat. Wikipedia gives the ruling side as BVA 70 plus one Congress member. [n06; FPJ, 16 Jan 2026; India TV; Wikipedia] The BJP had risen from about one seat in the previous house to forty-three. The Deccan Herald put it in symbols: “whistle blows lotus off Vasai fort”, the whistle being the BVA’s and the lotus the BJP’s. [n06; Deccan Herald] Among the losses on the BVA side were Pankaj Thakur, beaten by the BJP’s Mehul Shah, and the former mayor Rupesh Jadhav, who lost Ward 21 to the BJP’s Vishal Jadhav. [n26 §2–3; n06] Ajiv Patil of the BVA became mayor on 3 February 2026. Ward-by-ward results for Virar itself were not found. [n47 Gaps]

The police: a commissionerate for the north

For most of its history Virar was policed from Thane, and then, after 2014, from the Palghar district police. In October 2020 the state created the Mira-Bhayandar Vasai-Virar Police Commissionerate, MBVV, with thirteen police stations, bringing the corporation area under a metropolitan-style police commissioner. [n47; FPJ, MBVV detection rate, 2022] By its own reported figures the detection rate of crime in the area rose from 67 per cent in 2017 to 75 per cent in 2021 and over 85 per cent in 2022. [n47; FPJ] Who the first commissioner was, and when the Virar police station itself was founded, are not in the notes. [n26 Gaps; n47 Gaps]

Two stations of the commissionerate cover Virar: Virar police station and, for the coastal west, Arnala police station. Both appear in the ordinary crime news of 2026. In January 2026 Virar police reported busting a burglary gang, solving five cases and recovering stolen property worth Rs 4.56 lakh. [n47; FPJ, Jan 2026] Arnala police station registered the case after the hospital fire of 2021 (below), and the Arnala Coastal Police imposed sea bans after drownings off the beach. [n47; n45 §5] And on 20 November 2026, the date at which this book’s research ends, a twenty-six-year-old woman, Mayuri Jadhav, was reported to have drunk phenyl inside Virar police station after saying the police had refused to register her complaint about irregularities at the regional transport office; she was treated at Chandansar Hospital in Virar East. The report came through the Times of India by way of a search summary and is unverified; it is an allegation by the woman, and the police’s response is not recorded. [n47 Latest news; FPJ]

The trains

The Virar local is more than a train. In the slang of Mumbai the phrase stands for the most crowded, most fiercely defended commuter service in the city, and for the people who ride it. Before the crowds, though, comes the timetable.

From terminus to through station

For sixty-six years after electrification, the suburban electric multiple units ended at Virar. Beyond it, to Palghar, Boisar and Dahanu, the passengers changed to mainline trains or slower shuttles. In 2002 Western Railway announced it would extend suburban services from Virar to Dahanu Road. [n29 §2; Wikipedia: Western line (search summary)] Wikipedia says, without a citation, that the quadrupling of the line between Borivali and Virar “just took place in 2007”, the work that gave the suburban trains tracks of their own as far as Virar. [n45 §2; Wikipedia: Virar] In March 2013, eighteen MEMU (mainline electric multiple unit) services were running daily between Virar and Dahanu. Then, on 16 April 2013, the Railway Minister, Pawan Kumar Bansal, flagged off the first suburban train from Churchgate to Dahanu Road; regular services began the next day, fifteen or twenty of them depending on the count. Before that day, as one account put it, the suburban trains ran “only to Virar (60 km)”. [n29 §3; Wikipedia: Western line; railenquiry.in forum (unofficial)] Virar had stopped being the end of the line. It remained the place where most of the trains turned back.

The station grew with the traffic. A fire in August 2016 disrupted services; after a remodelling in 2017 the platforms were renumbered; the Wikipedia infobox gives eight platforms and nine tracks. [n45 §2; Wikipedia: Virar railway station] It is also, as one Wikipedia page notes, the station at which every passenger train on the line halts. [n06; Wikipedia: Vasai-Virar] The Free Press Journal has reported an average of 5.8 lakh passengers a day from Virar and 2.6 lakh on the Dahanu route; the figure comes from a search snippet without a date and needs checking. [n48 §8; FPJ (snippet)] Set against the 83,176 passengers of the whole year 1880, it is a measure of what the line has become. [n48 §8; Gaz XIV p. 382]

Air-conditioning

Mumbai’s first air-conditioned local ran on Christmas Day 2017, between Churchgate and Borivali; regular Churchgate–Virar AC services began on 1 January 2018, with eight of twelve services running fast. [n29 §3; PIB; Tribune; Scroll] The AC train was a premium product, priced at a multiple of the first-class fare (sources disagree between 1.2 and 1.3 times), and it shared tracks and time slots with the ordinary trains. [n29 §3] Every new AC service therefore raised a question: which ordinary train did it replace?

At Virar the question was answered in the spring of 2026. From 1 May, the 8.28 Virar–Churchgate local became an AC train. On 5 May its former passengers went onto the tracks. [n47; FPJ] The Free Press Journal’s video report of the “massive uproar” does not, in the summary the researchers reached, record arrests, injuries or a railway response; what it records is the moment when a town built for the commute stopped the commute to make a point. The protest belongs with the AC-local disputes elsewhere on Mumbai’s network in these years, but the notes record no other in Virar. [n47]

Fifteen coaches

The same weeks brought the answer the commuters had wanted for years: longer trains. Under the Mumbai Railway Vikas Corporation’s quadrupling project, platforms 3A and 4A at Virar were widened from 6.5 to 10.5 metres and extended for fifteen-coach trains, against twelve before; the first photographs of fifteen-coach rakes arriving at the Virar car shed were published in April 2026. [n29 §4; n47; Lokmat Times, Apr 2026; FPJ] After what the Free Press Journal called a “six-year wait”, about seventeen services were upgraded from twelve to fifteen cars, eight of them on Virar–Dahanu and nine on Churchgate–Virar; other notes date the start of fifteen-coach services on Virar–Dahanu to 1 May 2026. [n47; n06; FPJ] A fifteen-coach train carries a quarter more people than a twelve.

It did not always stop where it should. On 8 April 2026 a fifteen-coach Virar–Dadar fast local ran through Bhayandar at peak hour without halting, and the railway ordered an inquiry. [n47; FPJ, Apr 2026]

Further south, the long-promised separation of suburban and mainline traffic was creeping north. The fifth and sixth lines between Kandivali and Borivali opened on 19 January 2026; the 26-kilometre Borivali–Virar fifth and sixth lines, costing Rs 2,184 crore under the third phase of the Mumbai Urban Transport Project, had reached about thirty per cent physical progress by August 2026, with completion targeted for 2029; and in July 2026 the corporation invited bids for the overhead electrification of the Borivali–Virar stretch, a thirty-six-month contract. [n06; Curly Tales, Jan and Aug 2026; Rail Asia; Metro Rail News] In September 2026 Western Railway announced two new platforms at Borivali, including a 330-metre platform 3A for Borivali–Virar locals, to add thirty to forty services towards Virar, with platforms also planned at Andheri and at Virar itself. [n06; FPJ, Sept 2026]

The cost of the line

The railway takes as well as gives. In November 2026 a Marathi report counted five deaths in twenty-four hours on the Vasai–Vaitarna stretch, four of them in accidents and one natural. [n29 §4; Aapla Mahanagar] Overcrowding statistics for the Virar line (passengers per rake, deaths on the tracks between Virar and Nallasopara) were not found. [n06 Gaps]

And the rains stop it. In July 2026 the floods brought the cancellation of one hundred suburban trains on a Monday and 125 on Tuesday 7 July; services between Vasai Road and Virar were suspended when water covered the tracks near Nallasopara; and the General Manager of Western Railway, Ramashray Pandey, ordered a survey of whether the tracks through Vasai, Nallasopara and Virar could be raised. [n48 §2; n29 §4; FPJ, 8 July 2026] Chapter 30 tells why the line floods.

The buses

For a town strung along a railway, the road network came late, and public buses later still. The Vasai-Virar Municipal Transport, VVMT, began on 3 October 2012, run under a Buy, Own, Operate and Maintain contract on a royalty model, with depots at Virar and at Sativali. [n47 Second Appendix; VVCMC transport page; FPJ] Its rules gave free travel to senior citizens, persons with disabilities, cancer and dialysis patients and some students. [n47; VVCMC] The longest route listed on the Moovit transit app is the 308, from Virar East to Ambadi in Bhiwandi taluka, across the hills. [n47; Moovit] The fleet size is not recorded. [n47 Gaps]

On 11 August 2026 the transport staff went on strike over pay, overtime, duty hours and working conditions, and the service came to a complete halt. The Free Press Journal put the number of daily riders affected at about 60,000 to 65,000. [n47; FPJ, Aug 2026] How the strike ended is not recorded. It was followed, in September, by a strike of sanitation workers over pending dues after a change of contractor, which stopped the city’s cleaning services (the date is approximate). [n47; FPJ, Sept 2026] Between them the two strikes showed how much of the corporation’s daily work had been handed to contractors, and how quickly it stopped when the contractors’ workers did.

The flyover at Naringi

For a century and a half the railway that made Virar also cut it in two. The market and the hill were east of the line, the garden villages and the sea west of it, and between them the town crossed at level crossings, where cars, buses, carts and people waited for the gates to rise between trains. Chapter 27 has noted that the village name Naringi, or Narangi, is the “Naringuy” of the Portuguese land registers. [P1; n49 §3c]

On Friday, 8 May 2026, the long-awaited Naringi flyover opened to traffic at Railway Crossing No. 41. It had been built by the state Public Works Department over six to seven years. The Guardian Minister of Palghar district, Ganesh Naik, inaugurated it, with Mayor Ajiv Patil in attendance; it was expected to save at least thirty minutes on the journey between Virar East and Virar West. [n47; FPJ; Mumbai Live] The reports do not print the year, but the minister’s title and the mayor’s presence (he took office in February 2026) fix it. [n47; n29 §4] Earlier, a 589-metre skywalk had been built over the station area on the west side for pedestrians. [n45 §6; Wikipedia: Virar] Naringi itself is, by Wikipedia’s unreferenced stub, a village of the Mangela Koli fishing community, whose corporator at the time of writing was Dr Vasant Mangela of the BVA, chairman of a sand-lifters’ cooperative. [n49 §3c; Wikipedia: Narangi] The flyover rose over a fishing village whose people now lifted sand from the creek.

The corridors

In the 2020s Virar found itself on the drawing boards of projects much larger than itself.

The bullet train

The Mumbai–Ahmedabad high-speed railway, the “bullet train”, has a station at Virar. Land for the corridor was resisted in Palghar district: farmers in Boisar staged a symbolic protest on the day the project was launched in September 2017; about a thousand tribal farmers protested at Azad Maidan in May 2018, arguing that the gram sabhas’ consent required by the Panchayats (Extension to Scheduled Areas) Act of 1996 had not been obtained; the state government paused acquisition for a time. [n29 §3; DT Next; Tribune; Mongabay India] Acquisition for the corridor was complete by March 2024. [n06; Wikipedia: Virar high-speed railway station]

In late May 2025 the National High Speed Rail Corporation began casting the first slab of the Virar station: a slab fifty metres by 35.32 metres, poured with 1,555 cubic metres of concrete, the first of nine for a station 425 metres long. [n47; Swarajya; NHSRCL press release] No count of slabs cast by 2026 was found. [n47 Gaps]

Virar to Alibaug

The Virar–Alibaug Multimodal Corridor is planned as a fourteen-lane road and transit corridor curving round the eastern edge of the metropolitan region from Virar to Alibaug on the Raigad coast. The state approved its first phase at Rs 31,793.47 crore; the whole is described as 126 kilometres long, passing through 104 villages with nine interchanges. [n47; FPJ] The cabinet approved it on a build-operate-transfer model; Phase 1 is 96.41 kilometres, from Navghar in Palghar district to Balavali near Pen, to be built by the Maharashtra State Road Development Corporation. [n06; FPJ; Lokmat Times] In late September 2026 the Free Press Journal reported that construction was “likely by March 2027”, pending the Centre’s approval of seventeen per cent viability-gap funding, and that the stretch overlapping the spur of the Mumbai–Vadodara Expressway at Mote Karanjade would be prioritised. [n47; FPJ, Sept 2026] An aggregator’s claim that preliminary site work on the Virar–Bhiwandi section began in February 2026 is weaker evidence. [n06] Where the corridor’s Virar interchange will sit is not in the notes. [n47 Gaps]

The sea link

The Uttan–Virar sea link, a planned 24.35-kilometre bridge over the sea from Uttan in the south to Virar, was at the planning and detailed-project-report stage in 2026. By an aggregator’s account, MMRDA began acquiring about 104 hectares at Uttan in June 2026, and in August the link received in-principle approval to connect with the Delhi–Mumbai Expressway near Virar. [n06; InfraCapitalist] Chapter 16 sets these projects beside the Vadhvan port to the north.

Earlier research for this book saw in these projects a possible future for Virar as a junction of freight, road and rail rather than a dormitory at the end of a commuter line, and rightly called it a projection. [n06 Inferences] In November 2026 the bullet-train station was the only one of the four under visible construction in Virar.

Building the town: townships and the housing board

The economy of land in modern Virar is told in Chapter 30. Here the concern is what was built, and for whom.

Global City

West of the station, on the road that leads from Naringi towards the northern coast, lies the largest single development in Virar’s history. Rustomjee Global City is a township of roughly two hundred acres at Chikhal Dongari, on the Narangi Bypass Road, Virar West. [n47; Rustomjee] Other pages name Rustomjee and Evershine together as its builders, over more than two hundred acres. [n45 §6; Housing.com] A Press Trust of India headline records the launch of one of its phases, “Avenue D1”, in October 2018. [n47; Business Standard/PTI] The sources for its prices are the developer’s own pages and property portals, which are promotional: they quote asking prices from about Rs 31 lakh to Rs 40 lakh for a one-bedroom flat, varying by listing. [n47; Rustomjee; Square Yards] When the township began, how many people live there, and what stood on its land before, are not recorded in the notes. The 2010 city plan called the coastal strip that runs “from Chikhal-Dongri in the north to Naigaon in the south” the Kharpat, low land barely three or four feet above sea level, which is a fact about the ground beneath it worth keeping in mind. [n48 §4; CDP p. 27]

Property portals in 2026 gave Virar an average asking price of about Rs 8,243 a square foot in March 2026 (down from Rs 8,403 in December 2025), Rs 8,024 for Virar East and Rs 9,797 for Bolinj; another portal gave a range of Rs 6,000 to 10,000, and a third much lower figures, Rs 5,300 to 6,300. [n48 §8; Squareyards; Real Estate Mumbai; Housing.com] The sites disagree, and all of them report asking prices, not sales.

The housing board’s flats at Bolinj

At Bolinj, the old Samvedi and Kupari village north of Sopara that the Gazetteer called “formerly a large town”, the state’s housing board built on a different scale. In 2014 the Konkan Board of the Maharashtra Housing and Area Development Authority (MHADA) completed 9,409 homes at Virar-Bolinj. [n48 §8; n49 §3c; FPJ, 24 Mar 2026] For years many of them stood empty. An undated Mumbai Live report says that more than 1,600 flats went unsold in one April–May lottery and that MHADA then held 2,200 unsold Virar-Bolinj homes; the cause, as both that report and the Free Press Journal tell it, was water. [n48 §8; Mumbai Live; FPJ]

Then the water came. When water from the Surya regional scheme reached Virar (in 2025, according to the Free Press Journal; other sources date Surya water in Vasai-Virar to November 2023, and the date is disputed), sales jumped. MHADA also offered discounts and a first-come policy, and pointed buyers to the planned sea links, coastal road, bullet train and Virar–Alibaug corridor. By 24 March 2026, 7,783 of the 9,409 flats had been sold, 3,750 of them in the previous 152 days, about twenty-five a day; 1,626 remained, and the board hoped to sell them within one or two months for about Rs 1,000 crore. [n48 §8; FPJ, 24 Mar 2026; n06] It is hard to find a cleaner demonstration of what decided the value of a flat in Virar: not the building, not the railway, but whether water would come out of the tap.

Building has its own toll. In September 2026 a construction worker fell to his death from the fifth floor of a site at Bolinj, reported as the fourth fatal construction accident in the area in about a month; the source is a small local site. [n29 §4; Urban Acres]

Health: the pandemic and the fire

The corporation area had few hospitals for its size, and the notes hold almost no history of those it had. When COVID-19 came in 2020, Nalasopara was called the worst-affected town in the belt; the corporation reported a positivity rate of fifty-five per cent in April; on 6 May its hospitals had, by one report, seven ventilators and two intensive-care beds; on 20 July 2020 it passed 10,000 cases (10,017 cases and 192 deaths), and by late August had 16,234 cases and 345 deaths. [n29 §4; Deccan Herald; FPJ; Mumbai Live] Chapter 16 tells the pandemic across the city. In Virar its worst night came in the second wave.

23 April 2021

At about 3.13 in the morning of 23 April 2021, fire broke out in the intensive care unit of Vijay Vallabh Hospital in Virar, where COVID-19 patients lay on oxygen. Early reports gave thirteen dead; by the Free Press Journal’s later count fifteen patients died. (Wikipedia and most wire reports give thirteen, The Hindu’s headline fifteen.) [n47; n29 §4; FPJ; Wikipedia: Virar hospital fire] The fire brigade suspected a short circuit in an air-conditioning unit. The hospital’s fire no-objection certificate had expired in March. [n29 §4; n47; FPJ] A case was registered at Arnala police station under sections 304 (culpable homicide not amounting to murder), 337 and 338 (causing hurt and grievous hurt by acts endangering life) of the Indian Penal Code. The hospital’s chief executive, Dr Dilip Shah, and its chief administrative officer, Dr Shailesh Pathak, were arrested, and directors and staff were booked; on 1 May 2021 the Vasai sessions court rejected the two officers’ bail. [n47; FPJ; Legiteye] These are charges. The outcome of the trial, and any compensation paid to the families, were not found. [n47 Gaps; n29 Gaps]

Five years later the corporation’s budget for 2026–27 included a 400-bed hospital at Achole, in Nallasopara East, as one of its main items, under a budget its commissioner described as designed around “health, infrastructure, and tourism”. [n48 §8–9; FPJ, 11 Mar 2026] On 20 November 2026, the woman who drank phenyl at Virar police station was taken to Chandansar Hospital in Virar East, one of the few Virar hospitals named in the record. [n47]

27 August 2025: Ramabai Apartment

The disaster that made Virar’s name a byword in 2025 was not a fire but a fall. Chapter 27 and Chapter 16 tell it in outline; this section gathers what the notes add.

At about five minutes past midnight on 27 August 2025, the rear part of Ramabai Apartment in Virar East collapsed. The building had been put up in 2011–12, with fifty flats; twelve of them were in the wing that fell. Seventeen people died. Among them were a one-year-old girl, whose first-birthday party was going on in the building that night, and her parents. [n06; n47; The Week/PTI, 28 Aug 2025; FPJ; Scroll; Tribune] The state gave Rs 5 lakh ex gratia to each family. [n06]

The building is described in every report as allegedly unauthorised. The builder, named in reports variously as Nitin, Nital or Nittal Sane, was arrested for culpable homicide; by later reports five people had been arrested, four of them since granted bail, and the city’s Crime Branch had taken over the investigation. [n47; n26 §3; FPJ; The Week/PTI] On 5 December 2025 the police arrested Gilson Gonsalves, assistant commissioner of the corporation’s Ward C, for failing to act against the building. [n47; FPJ, Dec 2025] All of these are allegations; no charge sheet and no verdict had been reported by November 2026. [n47 Gaps]

The collapse came two weeks after the Enforcement Directorate arrested the corporation’s former commissioner, Anil Pawar, and three others on 13 August 2025 in a money-laundering case over the forty-one illegal buildings of Agarwal Nagari in Nallasopara East, which had been demolished on court orders between November 2024 and February 2025; the Bombay High Court later held Pawar’s arrest illegal and ordered his release. [n06; n29 §4; FPJ; The Tribune] That case is Nallasopara’s and is told in Chapter 16. In Virar the long argument over unauthorised building ended, that August night, in a list of the dead.

Churches, temples and a mosque unrecorded

The religious map of Virar changed in these decades in two ways at once. The old faiths of the villages built new houses of worship for their growing congregations, and the newcomers brought faiths, rites and languages of their own.

The Nandakhal family of parishes

The Catholic parishes of the Virar side continued to divide from their mother church. After Umrale (1970) and Nanbhat (1975), a centre was founded at Bolinj on 10 May 1988, in the same year the green belt was opened; it became a parish on 10 June 2001, and a diocesan directory dates the church of Christ the King at Bolinj to 2000. The church at Rajodi, on the coast, was inaugurated on 1 May 1999. A prayer centre at Jeladi opened on 1 January 1990 and became a centre on 1 January 2006. Then, by the parish’s own account, the “Virar East, Virar West centers got separated from the parish” too. [n49 §2b; Wikipedia: Holy Spirit Church Nandakhal; UCAN directory] In December 2016, by a parish policy document cited on Wikipedia, Nandakhal itself still had 2,150 families and 7,339 members. [n49 §2b] Listings name a Holy Spirit Church and an Our Lady of Lourdes Church in Virar West, the latter said to have “strong Portuguese roots”, but give no founding years. [n47; n49 §2b; Prayers and Petitions]

These were Kupari parishes in origin, cut from the mother church of the Kupari villages; the Virar East and West centres served a town that by then also held migrant Catholics, among them the Kerala families described below, and no source says how many of their parishioners are Kuparis. [n39; n49] Two visits of these years are remembered in the parish history: Pope John Paul II’s stop at Vasai on 9 February 1986, and Mother Teresa’s visit to Nandakhal on 6 April 1986. [n40] In 2011 a statue of the pope was given to the Nandakhal church. [n40]

All of them belonged, from 22 May 1998, to the new Diocese of Vasai, carved out of the Archdiocese of Bombay, whose first bishop, Thomas Dabre, was born at Bhuigaon. By 2022 the diocese counted 117,026 Catholics in forty-nine parishes and four missions. [n49 §2b; n26 §4; GCatholic] Chapter 15 tells the diocese’s founding.

St Thomas, Yashwant Nagar

The newest Catholic congregation in Virar came not from the old villages but from Kerala. In 1989 Fr Vargehese Nadackal came from Vasai, with Joseph of Bolinj, to visit Syro-Malabar Catholic families who had settled in Virar. On 23 July 1989 the first Holy Qurbana, the Eucharist in the Syro-Malabar rite, was celebrated at the house of C. K. Paul, for twenty-six families. A hall was inaugurated on 17 March 1991 by Bishop Mar Paul Chittilapilly. Fr John Ponparackal began building a church, which was consecrated on 19 January 2013; it was erected as a parish on 13 April 2014. [n49 §2b; n47; Archeparchy of Kalyan] (One version of the parish page garbles the dates; the archeparchy’s page gives those above.) [n47] St Thomas Syro-Malabar Church stands in Yashwant Nagar, Virar West, and belongs not to the Diocese of Vasai but to the Syro-Malabar Archeparchy of Kalyan. Its first visit came in the year after the green belt was opened, and its first families were among the earliest of the newcomers: a Kerala Christian layer, distinct from the Kupari parishes, a few miles from churches that had served the Marathi-speaking garden villages for four centuries. That the parish began with a man from Bolinj is the thread that ties the two.

Other houses of worship

The Jain community, 1.90 per cent of the corporation’s population in 2011 by an aggregator’s compilation of the census, has a Shwetambar derasar of Vimalnath Swami at Doghar Pada, Vartak Ward, in Virar West, known only from a temple directory’s listing. [n49 §1h; census2011.co.in; jainmandir.org] The Muslims of Virar, part of the 9.03 per cent of the corporation in 2011, have left no dated trace in the notes at all: no history of any mosque in Virar town was found, and the Muslim presence on the Virar side is documented only by the 155 Muslims counted at Agashi in 1881 and the tomb of the saints Shah Ali and Hajji Ali inside Arnala fort. [n49 §1h, §2c; Gaz XIV pp. 2, 11] For the Buddhists (2.65 per cent in 2011) and the Sikhs (0.21 per cent) of the corporation, nothing Virar-specific was found. [n49 §1h]

Festivals

Jivdani

The great Hindu festival of Virar is the goddess’s. On the hill east of the station, by the Palghar district’s account, a temple about 150 years old draws thousands of devotees on Sundays and festival days; the steps to the top number about 1,375 by the district’s count, about 1,400 or 1,440 by travel guides and about 1,465 by Wikipedia. [n47; n45 §3; Palghar district; Wikipedia: Jivdani Mata] Navratri, the nine nights of the goddess in autumn, fills the hill, and a fair on Dussehra, the tenth day, is “attended by thousands”. [n45 §3; n20; Wikipedia; Marathi Wikipedia] The spring Navratri of Chaitra fell in 2026 from 19 to 27 March. [n47] A Marathi press account says the old hill fort has been transformed into a seven-storey temple. [n20; Pudhari; Lokmat]

The climb itself has changed. A ropeway or funicular was put through environmental clearance in 2019–20: the Shree Jivdani Devi Mandir Trust appears as a respondent in National Green Tribunal Original Application 100 of 2020, which matches a public hearing held by the Maharashtra Pollution Control Board in March 2020. [n45 §3; n49 §2c; NGT affidavit; MPCB executive summary] By April 2026, the Free Press Journal reported, “a funicular train was introduced in recent years”, and most visitors took it instead of the stairs; on the hilltop an Rs 84 lakh glass skywalk for up to two hundred visitors, described as “1,500-feet”, with glass credited to the Essar Group, was about to open. [n45 §3; FPJ, 11 Apr 2026] Its opening was not reported by November 2026. Chapter 27 discusses the dates, the legend and the question of whose goddess Jivdani is.

Ganesh

Ganeshotsav, the festival of Ganesh in August or September, is the great public festival of Maharashtra, and Virar keeps it. But the notes hold little that is Virar’s own. In the wider region, “Vasai cha Maharaja”, the image of the Anand Nagar Mitra Mandal, is called one of the oldest public Ganesh mandals of Vasai–Virar and won a “Lokmanya Mahotsav” vote; a page for a “Virar cha Maharaja” exists but is unreliable. [n47; Yahoo News] At Gaskopari, a village of the Virar side (not to be confused with Gass near Nallasopara), a Ganesh temple whose idol is “said to be centuries old” holds a large fair on Ganesh Jayanti in the month of Magh, and was renovated in a three-day programme in 2015; the same Marathi Wikipedia page says, as tradition, that the installation of household Ganpati images in the area began only thirty or forty years ago. [n49 §2c; Marathi Wikipedia: Virar] In September 2026, 1,709 Gauris, the goddess images installed beside Ganpati in many Marathi homes, were counted in Vasai–Virar city. [n20; eSakal, 17 Sep 2026] The rain did not spare the festival: on 13 September 2026, overnight rain flooded the flower market and main market near Nallasopara station just before Ganpati. [n29 §4; FPJ]

December

In December the whole taluka keeps two newer festivals. The Vasai Taluka Kala-Krida Mahotsav, the arts and sports festival whose architect was Virar’s former council president Mukesh Save, was being prepared for its thirty-seventh edition in October 2026, organised by the Vasai Taluka Kala-Krida Vikas Mandal; its first edition, by inference from that numbering, fell about 1988 to 1990. [n20; eSakal, 18 Oct 2026; n13] And the Vasai-Virar Mayor’s Marathon, begun in 2011 with about seven hundred runners, drew about 15,000 to its twelfth edition on 8 December 2024, flagged off by the wrestler Sakshi Malik; the thirteenth was scheduled for 13 December 2026, run by the corporation with the Kala-Krida Vikas Mandal. [n29 §3–4; Lokmat Times; FPJ] Chapter 16 tells both.

On the Catholic side of the calendar, the grounds of St James Church at Agashi hosted in 2026 the silver jubilee of Yuvajallosh, a youth festival organised by Yuvadarshan with the parishes of Shirlai, Agashi and Jyoti. [n49 §2b; Catholic Connect]

The Kupari parishes of the Virar side keep a calendar of their own, though the notes date little of it. Each village’s wayside cross has its annual feast and rosary, as at the Community Cross at Cross Naka in Agashi; the parish feasts fall through the year, Shirlai’s in February and, in 1760 at least, Agashi’s on 16 December; and community writers describe the candlelit Lenten procession through the villages, the Pirsao or miravnuk, and the forty-day Upas, and list Christmas (Natal), Easter and the fiestas of Pali, Bandra and San Jao among Kupari festivals. [n07; n09; n49; Rajan D’Mello blog 2009] On 7 June 2026 the diocese added a new pilgrimage: it made St Joseph’s Church at Umrale a pilgrim centre for the Year of St Francis, which runs to 10 January 2027, with a plenary indulgence. [n38; Catholic Connect] The old “Kupari songs” of Nandakhal, by one account, “are rarely heard as lifestyles have changed”. [n07; Vasaiker]

The Virar local: a culture on rails

By the 2020s “Virar” meant, to most of Mumbai, a train. The Free Press Journal’s feature “Wheels of affection” set out the lore. On the Virar line, groups of regular passengers block the doors; the joke goes that new films reach the Virar locals, on pirated phones, before they reach the cinemas; commuters ride about eighty kilometres. [n47; FPJ, “Wheels of affection”] The train has its own celebrations: viral videos showed a garba, the Gujarati circle dance of Navratri, danced inside a compartment, and passengers on a Virar–Andheri local holding a kelvan, the pre-wedding celebration, for a bride-to-be among them. [n47; FPJ, kelvan video] Celebrities have ridden the Virar–Churchgate train; the Free Press Journal names the actor Tusshar Kapoor. [n47] In April 2022 the paper marked 155 years since the first Back Bay–“Viraur” train. [n47; n45; FPJ, 12 Apr 2022] A song called “Virar ki local” was searched for and not confirmed. [n47]

The lore is press lore, half affectionate and half mocking, and it should be read that way. But it records something true. A town whose people, by its own city plan, mostly work in Mumbai, and whose station sends out passengers by the hundred thousand every morning, has made the compartment one of its public spaces: a place of festivals and feasts as well as of the daily crush. [n48 §8; CDP p. 23]

Two captains

The best-documented sons of modern Virar are two cricketers formed by that train. Chapter 27 gives their careers in full; here is the Virar part of their stories.

Prithvi Shaw grew up in Virar, the son of parents who had come from Gaya in Bihar. Sources disagree about where he was born, Thane or Virar; Wikipedia gives Virar, 9 November 1999. As a boy he trained at an academy in Virar under the coach Santosh Pingulkar, and his father took him to the six o’clock Virar local for cricket in the city, before the family moved to Bandra in 2006. [n47; n27; n26 §4; Sportskeeda; Business Standard/PTI; Wikipedia] He captained India to the Under-19 World Cup in 2018 and scored a century on his Test debut that October; in 2025 he moved from Mumbai to Maharashtra’s domestic team. [n27; n47]

Ayush Mhatre, born on 16 July 2007, is from Virar by ESPNcricinfo’s and Wisden’s accounts and from Nallasopara by Wikipedia’s; one news summary says Virar. [n47; n27] He commuted by train from Virar to Churchgate for cricket: about eighty kilometres one way by ESPNcricinfo’s account, about forty-six kilometres each way by Wikipedia’s (the figures conflict, and the railway distance lies between them). [n47; n27; ESPNcricinfo; Wikipedia] He captained India’s Under-19 team on a tour of England and Mumbai at the Buchi Babu tournament, captained India to the Under-19 World Cup in 2026, and was given a rally by Vasai–Virar on his return. [n47; n27; Tribune; FPJ] Two Under-19 World Cup winning captains, eight years apart, from the same commuter line.

Govinda, dancers and a dialect

The film star Govinda’s tie to Virar, discussed above and in Chapter 27, is that of a boyhood in a Virar chawl. [n27] Virar also has a place in the dance boom that made Vasai–Virar, in a Free Press Journal phrase, “India’s dance capital”: the Tuttix Crew, combining tutting and robotics, was founded by Shubham Verma and based in Virar. [n27; FPJ, 2019] The larger story of Suresh Mukund and Kings United, the Vasai crew that won NBC’s World of Dance in 2019, belongs to Vasai and is told in Chapter 16. [n27]

In scholarship, one Virar name stands out in the notes: Dr Naresh Naik, of Virar, wrote Samvedi Boli – Sanrachana ani Swarup (“The Samvedi dialect: structure and form”), which won the Krishna Mukund Ujalambkar Smriti award and a P. Na. Go. Kalelkar award in 2018. [n20] It is a book about the speech of the Samvedi Brahmins and the Kuparis, the garden people whose villages ring Virar, written from inside the town that grew over their fields. Chapter 36 and Chapter 31 tell that speech.

No film or television production was confirmed as shot in Virar itself. Generic location listings say Arnala beach is used for shoots, and the Vasai fort has appeared in a Coldplay video, but no named film for the Virar side was found. [n47; n45 §5; SceneLoc8]

October–November 2026: the flea market

The last cultural event of Virar recorded in the notes is a small one, and it is a good place to see the town as it had become. From 30 October to 1 November 2026, the Virar Flea Market was held, and among its stalls a handmade brand called Dhaagadhari won attention, by the account of a business-news site whose headline traced its journey “from needle and thread to Virar’s spotlight”. [n47; Bhaskar Live] Where the market was held, how many stalls it had and who organised it are not in the summary that reached the researchers. But the idea of it, a weekend market of small makers and sellers in a suburb of young commuters, is the latest form of something very old in Virar. In 1882 the Gazetteer had described another market on the east side of the station, on the bank of the pond under the banyans, every Saturday, where Kolis and Warlis sold fish, garden stuff and cloth. [n48 §1; Gaz XIV p. 382] A hundred and forty-four years later, the town still came together to buy and sell; the sellers had changed, and the goods.

Virar in November 2026

In the autumn of 2026 Virar was the northern third of a city whose population no one had counted since 2011. The census of that year had found 1,222,390 people in the corporation, 648,172 men and 574,218 women, in 291,229 households, with a literacy rate of 88.57 per cent by one compilation; the corporation’s own projections ran far higher, and no census was held in 2021. [n47 Second Appendix; n49 §1h; census2011.co.in; n06] Its civic government, after five and a half years without an elected house, was led again by the BVA under Mayor Ajiv Patil; its two assembly members were from the BJP. [n06; n26]

Water was the oldest complaint and the newest. In June 2026 the city’s sources totalled 400 million litres a day on paper (230 from local dams and 170 from the MMRDA’s Surya scheme) but supply “falls well short” because of old, leaking pipes; the Kholsapada-2 dam, approved in 2018 at Rs 135 crore, was more than eighty per cent complete and expected to add 22 MLD. [n48 §3; FPJ, 14 June 2026] On 15 November 2026 a Marathi daily warned that reservoir levels were falling and that the city’s historic lakes were choked with garbage and water hyacinth. [n06; eSakal, 15 Nov 2026] Chapter 30 follows the water.

The trains were longer, the AC protest unresolved in the record, the fifth and sixth lines from Borivali a third built. The buses had struck in August and the sanitation workers in September; a cross-party agitation had protested against smart electricity meters and higher bills that month. [n47; n06] The Naringi flyover carried traffic over the line that had divided the town for a century and a half. The bullet-train station rose; the Virar–Alibaug corridor waited for central funds; the Uttan–Virar sea link had an in-principle approval; to the north the Vadhvan port had signed pacts worth Rs 5,700 crore and aimed at a first phase in 2029. [n06; n47] The MHADA flats at Bolinj were almost all sold. The Ramabai families waited for a trial; the families of the hospital fire for a verdict. On the hill, most pilgrims rode the funicular to the goddess, and the glass skywalk had not been reported open. West of the line, the Kupari parishes of Nandakhal, Agashi, Bolinj, Umrale, Nanbhat and Rajodi went on as before, and Umrale’s church was in its year as a pilgrim centre. [n38; n40] In the police station a young woman said she had not been heard. [n47] And on 13 December the runners of the thirteenth Mayor’s Marathon would set out. [n29 §4]

Eighty years earlier, the last electric train from Bombay had stopped at Virar and gone no further. In November 2026 the trains ran on to Dahanu, the highways and corridors were being drawn round the town, and Virar was no longer the end of anything. Whether it had become a centre in its own right, or only a longer stretch of the metropolitan edge, was a question its history had not yet answered.

What the record does not hold

The gaps in Virar’s modern history are wide. Some can be filled from archives that exist but were not reached; others may need fieldwork.

  • Population before 1981. No Virar-town figure exists in the notes for 1951, 1961 or 1971, or for 1991 or 2011 (the “Virar” 2011 figure on many pages is the corporation’s). The Census of India’s 1961 provisional talukawise tables and its 1971 supplement on Standard Urban Areas, which give 1951 and 1961 town populations, were identified but not opened. [n47 Second Appendix]
  • The Virar Municipal Council. Its founding year, its list of presidents (beyond Rajeev Patil and Mukesh Save, both known only from later sources), its elections from 1995 to 2009 and its records. [n47; n20]
  • The mayors. Two tables of VVCMC mayors disagree on dates; neither has been checked against corporation records. Who ran the city from July 2020 to February 2026 is unrecorded. [n47; n26]
  • The Viva institutions. The trust’s founding (1988 or 1991) and the college’s (2000 or 2001), from the trust’s own records. [n47]
  • The Dube case. Whether the 2023 acquittal was appealed; the outcome of the TADA matter against Hitendra Thakur reported in 1994. [n47; n26]
  • The hospital fire and the collapse. The trial of the Vijay Vallabh accused, compensation to the families, and the charge sheet in the Ramabai case. [n47]
  • The police. The founding of Virar police station and the first commissioner of the MBVV commissionerate. [n47; n26]
  • The buses. VVMT’s fleet size and the outcome of the August 2026 strike. [n47]
  • Places of worship. Founding dates for the Virar East and West Catholic centres, for Global City’s churches, for any mosque in Virar and for derasars other than Agashi’s. [n47; n49]
  • The Kuparis since 1947. No count of Kuparis in Virar at any date; no record of how many of the town parishes’ members are Kupari; no figures for Kupari emigration or the move from farming; no Kupari organisation of the Virar side with a founding date. [n34; n49]
  • Jivdani. The temple’s founding (seventeenth century, about 150 years ago, or an image installed in 1956), the construction of the steps, the opening of the funicular and the skywalk. [n45; n20; n47]
  • Industry. No history of a Virar industrial estate was found beyond the city plan’s mention of small units on Shirsat Road; Chapter 30 sets out what is known. [n47; n48]
  • Culture. Films shot in Virar, the history of Ganeshotsav mandals in Virar, and the Marathi press for the whole period, which no search reached. [n47]

These are the places where the corporation’s archive, the parish and temple registers, the Maharashtra Government Gazette, the Census of India’s printed tables and the Marathi newspapers would add most.

From the station, the old metalled road of 1882 still runs west towards Agashi and the sea, and the hills still rise to the east towards Tungar. Between them lies a plain of silt, salt and sweet water that fed a garden for centuries, flooded every monsoon, and was sold, in the forty years told here, as building land. The next chapter tells that ground: its rock and its water, and how the people of Virar have made a living from it.
