Period covered: from the first peoples of the coast to the Census of 2011 and the dialect revivals of 2026

In this chapter. Thread two: how each community came, and how its language formed, from the Warli and Katkari to the newest tongues.

One parable, many tongues

Some time around 1900, government officers across the Bombay Presidency were handed the same short text and asked to have it put into the speech of the people around them. The text was the Parable of the Prodigal Son: a man had two sons, the younger took his share and squandered it, a famine came, he went home. The point was not religion. The point was comparison. If the same sentences were written down in every dialect of India, a linguist in Calcutta could lay them side by side and see how the languages differed. The results were edited by George Abraham Grierson and printed in 1905 as volume VII of the Linguistic Survey of India, the volume on Marathi. [n22; Grierson, LSI VII (1905)]

From the coast north of Bombay the parable came back in several forms. In the speech of the Vadvals of Bassein it began Eka don por hote, “One man-to two sons were”, and went on to say that the younger son began to squander all the money, mag tyan dhak’la dzak’la paisa udyaya lag’la, until a ghan dukal, a mighty famine, fell on the land. Another version came from the Samvedis, the Brahmin gardeners of the same coast; it was printed in Devanagari as Specimen No. 37. A third came from the Mangela fishermen, a fourth from the native Roman Catholics, whom their neighbours called Kinstav. Grierson studied them, described them in a few short paragraphs, and decided that he would not go further. Of the many minor caste dialects of the Konkan, he wrote, “it would be waste of time and paper to give specimens of all of them.” [n41; LSI VII pp. 145-147] [n22; LSI VII]

That sentence is a fair warning for this chapter. The people of the Sopara and Vasai coast were never asked, for most of their history, where they came from or how they spoke. When someone finally did ask, in the 1880s and the 1900s, it was a colonial officer with a printed form, a district gazetteer with a fixed list of headings, or a caste committee anxious to prove its rank. Yet the answers they gathered are precious. They are the earliest written record of how a dozen peoples came to share one strip of creek, garden and shore, and of how each of them, in coming, made a language out of what it brought and what it found.

This thread of the book follows them in rough order of arrival. It starts with the forest peoples whose ancestry reaches back to the first hunter-gatherers of India, and the fishing peoples of the creeks. It goes on to the Brahmin gardeners named in a copper plate of 1026, the warrior-gardeners who say they came with a king named Bimba, the salt-makers and toddy-tappers, the Arab and Persian traders, the Parsis, the Jains, the new Christians of the Portuguese century, the Brahmin officials of the Peshwas, the merchants of Cambay and Marwar, and finally the millions who came on the suburban trains after 1980. For each, it asks the same questions. What is the earliest evidence? What does the community say about itself, and how does that compare with the evidence? Why did they come, and how did they settle? What work did they do, and how did their religion change, and why? And how did their speech take shape?

How to read this chapter

Three kinds of statement run through what follows, and the chapter keeps them apart.

The first is evidence: a dated inscription, a census figure, a sentence in a traveller’s account, a word list taken down by a known person at a known time. The 1882 Gazetteer of the Bombay Presidency for Thana, edited by James M. Campbell, is the backbone. Its population chapter was compiled from district officers’ reports and the census of 1872, and it records, caste by caste, numbers, dress, food, speech, gods and customs. [n08; Gazetteer XIII-1 (1882)] Grierson’s survey of 1905 is the backbone for speech. Census reports from 1901 to 2011 supply numbers.

The second is scholarship: the reasoning of a scholar, colonial or modern, about what the evidence means. The Gazetteer’s editors were scholars as well as compilers, and they often argued with the stories they recorded. So did Gerson da Cunha in 1876 and 1900, S. M. Edwardes in 1902, and, in the twenty-first century, geneticists, linguists and historians such as Mendiratta. [n41; Edwardes 1902; da Cunha 1900] [n34; Mendiratta 2017]

The third is tradition: what a community says about itself. R. E. Enthoven’s Tribes and Castes of Bombay (1920-22) is the richest single collection of these, but its entries were often written from caste informants. The Panchkalshi entry names the men who supplied it, including the secretary of the S. K. Hitechhu Mandali, a community society. Enthoven’s volumes therefore tell us, very reliably, what each group said about its origins around 1910. They do not prove those origins true. [n35; Enthoven I-III, method note] Community websites, blogs and Wikipedia pages are traditions of the same kind, recorded later and with less care.

There is a fourth thing to watch for: silence. No genetic study has been made of the Son Kolis, Mangelas, Agris, Vadvals, Samvedis or Bhandaris. No linguist has written a historical study of how the Kupari dialect formed. No census since 1931 has counted castes. Where the notes are silent, this chapter says so. [n01; Debortoli et al. 2020, gaps] [n34; dialect gaps] [n41; Q13]

The first peoples: the Warli

The oldest ancestry on this coast belongs, as far as anyone can now measure, to the Warli.

In 2020 a team led by Debortoli published a genome-wide study of six populations of western Maharashtra in Scientific Reports. Two were caste groups, Deshastha Brahmins and Kunbi Marathas. Four were tribes: the Bhil, the Pawara, the Kokana and the Warli. The Warli and Kokana samples were collected at Jawhar in Palghar district, the hill country behind the Vasai coast, where the paper notes that the two tribes “live in the same geographic region”. [n01; Debortoli et al. 2020, PMC7308293] [n14]

Geneticists who study South Asia describe its people as mixtures of a few ancient sources. One of them is called AASI, for Ancient Ancestral South Indian: the ancestry of the hunter-gatherers who reached the subcontinent tens of thousands of years ago, along the southern coasts of Asia after the out-of-Africa dispersal, probably between about 65,000 and 50,000 years ago. Later came ancestry related to early farmers and hunter-gatherers of Iran, and, in the first half of the second millennium BCE, ancestry from the steppe. [n14; Narasimhan et al. 2019] The Debortoli team found that the Warli carry “the highest AASI-related contribution” of all the groups they tested: about 54 per cent by one method (qpAdm) and about 91 per cent of the AASI-like component in another (ADMIXTURE at K=3). An earlier study by Narasimhan and colleagues, with only three Warli samples, had found the same thing. The tribal groups as a whole had much more AASI ancestry than the caste groups. [n01; Debortoli et al. 2020] [n14]

The same study found two further things worth knowing. The Warli share a high amount of their genome within the group (33.4 centimorgans per pair on average), a sign of long endogamy or of a small founding population. And the Warli are the only tribe in the study whose effective population did not shrink in recent times: it grew over the last twenty or so generations. The paper gives no dates for when the mixing happened, and it does not discuss any link with the Austroasiatic or Munda languages. [n01; Debortoli et al. 2020]

What does this mean for the story? The research notes draw a careful inference, and it is the right one: high AASI ancestry combined with an Indo-Aryan language suggests that the Warli descend largely from the region’s ancient indigenous population, which took up an Indo-Aryan language later. It is consistent with calling them a “first people”. It does not prove that their ancestors lived at Vasai itself, or that they made the small stone tools found on the hills of Manori and Marve, a few kilometres to the south. [n01; inference] [n14; Manori, Marve]

The written record of the Warli begins much later. The 1882 Gazetteer explains their name as “probably originally Varalis or uplanders”, and connects it with Varalat, the sixth of the “seven Konkans” of tradition. In 1872 there were 70,015 Varlis in the Thana district: 45,330 in Dahanu, 6,804 in Mahim, 6,099 in Bassein, 4,277 in Shahapur and 2,142 in Vada. The Gazetteer divides them into three sections, the Murdes, the Davars and the Nihiris. The Nihiris belonged to South Mahim, Bassein, Jawhar and Vada, and did not marry with the other two. Their clans included Bhavar, Sankar, Pileyana, Ravatia, Bantria, Bhangara, Meria, Vangad, Thakaria, Jadav, Karbat, Bhendar and Kondaria. [n08; Gazetteer XIII-1 pp. 182-183] So the Warli of Vasai taluka, in the oldest record, were mainly Nihiris, a branch living on the southern edge of the Warli country.

Enthoven, forty years later, counted 151,693 Varlis in 1901, with their headquarters in Dahanu, and wrote plainly: “Regarding their origin nothing is known.” He gave the missionary scholar John Wilson’s derivation of the name from varal, a small cultivated patch in the uplands, and described the Varlis as “an aboriginal tribe only slightly influenced by Brahmanism”. He listed four divisions: Shuddha, Murde, Davar and Nihir. [n35; Enthoven III, “Varli”, pp. 445-447]

The Gazetteer wrote one sentence about them that has a ring of truth to it: “Their love for the forests is so great that… they cannot be induced to go so far from their woods.” It also noted that Varlis who had pledged their land to moneylenders were employed in tillage during the rains, an early glimpse of the debt bondage that would mark Adivasi life here for the next century and a half. [n08; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 183] Modern summaries add that before the 1870s the Warli were the main landowners in the Umbergaon, Dahanu and Palghar talukas, and that they were traditionally semi-nomadic, living in small groups under a headman, before they settled into growing rice and wheat. [n01; Wikipedia, citing Chattopadhyay 1996] Enthoven describes their huts, walls of karvi reeds on cowdung floors, and their habit of wandering for labour in the dry season. [n35; Enthoven III pp. 445-447]

Their religion is animist with Hindu borrowings. At its centre, in the ritual square (chauk) of the famous paintings, sits the mother goddess Palaghata, linked to fertility; male gods are unusual. The paintings themselves, white rice paste on red ochre or cow-dung walls, made with circles, triangles and squares, were made by women until the late 1970s, when Jivya Soma Mashe turned them into a studio art. How old the tradition is cannot be said. One art historian dated it to 2500-3000 BCE; others place it around the tenth century CE; neither date has documentary support. [n01; Dalmia 1988; Tribhuwan & Finkenauer 2003]

How Warli speech took shape

The Warli language is the clearest case on this coast of a people keeping its ancestry while changing its tongue. Grierson in 1905 counted about 89,000 Varli speakers in Thana, “more than half” the population of Dahanu, and called the speech “originally a Bhil dialect”, by then strongly influenced by Marathi “except in the extreme north”, where it had become a form of Gujarati. Enthoven said the same: “In Thana they always speak Marathi except those in the extreme north, who speak Gujarati.” [n22; LSI VII] [n35; Enthoven III p. 446]

What survived of the older speech were particular words and habits. Both the Gazetteer and Grierson noticed nāngne, “to see”. Enthoven added tal, “here”, davar, “old man”, jhinguor, “blanket”, and yayki or tup, “ghee”. All three noticed a way of clipping words: kot jās or kotja for the Marathi kothe jātos, “where are you going?”, and vichai for vichaila, “he asked”. [n08; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 183] [n35; Enthoven III p. 446] [n22; LSI VII] The Gazetteer judged that their speech differed little from the Marathi of the Kunbi cultivators.

So the formation looks like this, as far as the colonial word lists allow it to be seen: an older speech related to the Bhil dialects of the north, overlaid by Marathi in the Konkan and by Gujarati in the far north, with a residue of words like nāngne that are neither. Linguists today class Warli variously as Marathi, Konkani or Bhil. Current figures put its speakers at about 390,000 in the 2011 census; UNESCO’s atlas lists it as potentially vulnerable. A teacher in Palghar has been profiled for teaching children in Warli. [n22; Wikipedia, Varli language; PARI] There is no linguistic fieldwork on the Warli of Vasai taluka specifically. [n35; gaps]

It is worth saying what the notes did not find. Claims that the Warli once spoke an Austroasiatic (Munda) language, or that a Dravidian layer underlies the place names of the Konkan, are sometimes made. No source the research found supports either. [n01; gaps] [n22; gaps]

The Kokna

The Kokna (Kokana) are the Warli’s neighbours in the Jawhar hills, and genetically their kin. The 2020 study found a “mutual affinity” between the two tribes, with between-group sharing of 10.17 centimorgans per pair, and the Kokana, like the Warli, carry much more AASI ancestry than the caste groups. Within-group sharing among the Kokana (13.84 cM) is lower than among the Warli, and in a principal-component plot the two separate along the second axis. [n01; Debortoli et al. 2020]

Beyond that, the written record on the Kokna of the Vasai area is almost empty. The researcher who read Enthoven’s volumes did not find a Kokna entry in the parts scanned, and Grierson’s Kokna material was not found either. [n35; gaps] [n22; gaps] One faint trace may exist. The Gazetteer’s list of the district’s hill tribes in 1872 includes “Konkanis 4,500”, alongside Varlis, Thakurs and Kathkaris; and the 1881 caste table for the Bassein sub-division, in a fuzzy scan, lists “Konkanis 7,048” among Hindus, next to Varlis (7,308) and Kathkaris (1,600). [n08; Gazetteer XIII-1, Population intro] [n21; Gazetteer XIII-II p. 680] It is tempting to read these “Konkanis” as the Kokna tribe, since they appear among the forest peoples; but the scan is unreliable and the Gazetteer uses “Konkani” for other people too, the Konkani Muslims among them. The identification is an inference of this chapter, not a finding of the sources.

The Katkari

The Katkaris take their name, according to both Enthoven and Grierson, from kath, catechu, the dark extract boiled from the heartwood of the khair tree, and karne, to make. Catechu and charcoal were their trades. [n35; Enthoven II, “Katkari”, pp. 171-173] [n22; LSI VII]

Enthoven’s informants and authorities give two kinds of origin. One is scholarly: the Katkaris are “probably of Bhil origin… believed to have come from the north, and to have been originally settled in the Gujarat Athavisi, the present district of Surat. They have a division named Athavar to this day.” The other is their own: they are descended from the monkeys of Rama’s army, blessed by him and turned into men. [n35; Enthoven II pp. 171-173]

Their society, as Enthoven recorded it, shows how this coast’s peoples absorbed newcomers and outcasts. There were five endogamous divisions: the Athavar; the Dhed or Dhor; the Sidhi, whose African origin Enthoven called “doubtless”; the Son or Maratha; and the Varap, who were reverts from Christianity or Islam. Strangers from higher castes, Varlis, Kolis, Thakurs or Kunbis, could be admitted to the Katkari community for a payment of five rupees. In 1901 there were 75,710 Katkaris in the Presidency. [n35; Enthoven II pp. 171-173] The Gazetteer of 1882 noted the African (Sidi) strain among some Katkaris and Thakurs, as among Thana Christians and Konkani Muslims, and that the Kunbis feared the Katkaris for their reputed powers over spirits. [n35; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 64] [n08; Gazetteer, Kathkari section] An earlier survey in the Indian Antiquary divided them into a Northern or Dhor and a Southern or Maratha branch, most numerous in Thana and Kolaba. [n01; Indian Antiquary vol. 3]

There were about 34,000 Katkaris in the whole Thana district in 1872 and about 1,600 in the Bassein sub-division in 1881. [n08; Gazetteer, hill tribes] [n21; Gazetteer XIII-II p. 680]

Their speech was, in Enthoven’s words, “a slightly disguised Marathi with many Gujarati and Hindustani words”. It dropped the personal endings of verbs, so that kusi gela, “where did he go”, stood for Marathi kothe gela, and it kept words of its own: suna for dog, him for snake, narak for bear, akii for fire, vadis for wife. Grierson called it “originally a Bhil dialect closely related to Khandeshi”, now heavily Marathi. [n35; Enthoven II pp. 171-173] [n22; LSI VII] So the Katkari pattern matches the Warli one: a Bhil-related older speech, a Marathi surface, and a sprinkling of Gujarati and Hindustani that fits the tradition of a northern origin.

The Katkaris’ later history is the hardest on this coast. In 2026 the Shramjeevi Sanghatana, the Adivasi movement founded in 1982 by Vivek Pandit after he and his wife Vidyullata began work in Dahisar village in Vasai, staged silent fasts for the Katkari tribe, who still face bonded labour, child trafficking and the collapse of schooling as families migrate to brick kilns each season. Katkari families at a Palghar brick kiln refused the bayana, the advance that binds a worker to an owner, and the owner signed an affidavit that it was illegal. [n08; FPJ 2026; Newsdrum; Wikipedia, Vivek Pandit]

The Thakur

The word Thakur means a lord, and it is common among Rajput chiefs. The Thakur tribe of the Thana hills, 122,019 strong in 1901 across Thana, Kolaba and Nasik, explained its name with a story of refuge. Enthoven recorded that “the Rajput element in the tribe is said to be due to certain fugitives from Gujarat having taken refuge among them during the reign of Mahmud Begada (1459-1511).” John Wilson put it more strongly: the Thakurs “are the descendants of the Thakurs of Gujarat, who fled to the hills in the time of Mahmud Begada’s persecutions”, and in his day they could hardly be told apart from the Warlis. [n35; Enthoven III, “Thakur”, pp. 376-377; Wilson via Enthoven II p. 245]

That is a tradition. The only object cited in its support is a copper plate found in the possession of a Thakur in Nasik district, which shows the word Thakur in use since about AD 650. It shows that the title is old, not that the tribe came from Gujarat. [n35; Enthoven III pp. 376-377]

In 1872 the district had about 55,500 Thakurs; the Bassein sub-division had only 698 in 1881. [n08; Gazetteer, hill tribes] [n21; Gazetteer XIII-II p. 680] Their home tongue was Marathi, “spoken with a long drawl”. Grierson located the Thakar dialect in the hill country of south and south-east Thana, called it “of the kind usual between the Konkan and the Dekhan” with a Gujarati admixture, and noticed its agent case in -han: mahan, “by me”, tuhan, “by thee”. [n35; Enthoven III pp. 376-377] [n22; LSI VII]

The hill Kolis: Malhar and Mahadev

“Koli” is the most slippery name on this coast. Enthoven called it “a term of vague meaning covering a number of tribes of inferior status”, and judged that “probably the term Koli has been used for centuries to describe the greater part of the original inhabitants of the Presidency, so long as they adhered to a tribal organization”. John Wilson glossed the word as “clansman” and called the Kolis the aborigines of the plains, as the Bhils were of the hills; they “do not differ in race from the Kulambis or Kunbis”. There were 1,714,107 Kolis in the Presidency in 1901. [n35; Enthoven II, “Koli”, pp. 243-245] In the Konkan the four main groups were the Dhor, Mahadev, Malhar and Son Kolis. The Gazetteer counted twelve Koli tribes besides the Son Kolis, among them the Dhor or Tokre, the Dongari, the Mahadev, the Malhari (also called Chumli, Kunam or Panbhari), the Marvi and the Raj. [n35; Enthoven II pp. 245-246] [n08; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 148]

The Malhar Kolis were found “in almost every village in the Deccan, in Bombay City and along the sea coast in the Thana district”. Their surnames, as Enthoven recorded them, were Bhoir, Jadhav, Kerav, Langa, Povar, Sharanpad, Shelar, Sojval and Vekhande. Under the Marathas they were the hereditary guardians of hill forts such as Purandhar, Sinhgad, Torna and Rajgad. In the Thana district, A. M. T. Jackson observed, they “frequently become Maratha Kunbis”, which is a precious early record of a people changing caste by changing work. Their dress, customs and speech did “not differ from the Mahadev Kolis”. The Mahadev Kolis were the high-status “Raj” Kolis; the chief of Jawhar, the hill state behind Vasai, was himself a Mahadev Koli. [n35; Enthoven II pp. 246, 257-258]

Some of the forest fishing peoples of the north, the Mitne Machhis of Mahim and Dahanu, worshipped only Chaitya and Hirva, forest gods, and used no Brahmin priests. [n08; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 148]

How much any of this applies to Vasai taluka today is unknown. The research found no source documenting present-day Malhar or Mahadev Koli communities in Vasai, and no reliable origin study of them. [n08; gaps] [n01; gaps] What can be said is that their speech, in Grierson’s words about Kolis in general, followed a rule: the Kolis “everywhere speak the dialect of their neighbours”. [n22; LSI VII]

The fishing peoples of the creeks

Behind the forest belt lay the creeks, and on the creeks lived the people of the sea. In the 1882 Gazetteer the sea-fishing mass of the coast are the Son Kolis, with two separate fishing castes, the Mangelas and the Vaitis, beside them, and with Christian Kolis in Bassein. [n08; Gazetteer XIII-1 pp. 147-149, 182]

The Son Kolis

The Gazetteer estimated about 8,000 Son Kolis in the district in 1872, “chiefly along the coast south of the Vaitarna”, and described them as “probably a tribe of Kolis who have mixed with foreign settlers from beyond the sea”. It offered three meanings of the name: “perhaps the younger or later-come Kolis”, or from son, red, or from sonay, stranger. [n08; Gazetteer XIII-1 pp. 148-149] A century later the Maharashtra State Gazetteer of 1982 suggested another, from Sonag, a southern Indian word for Yavana or foreigner, and wondered whether the coastal Kolis “might have foreign blood”; Enthoven had already quoted Caldwell’s Dravidian sonag. [n01; 1982 Thana Gazetteer via thesis chapter] [n35; Enthoven II] None of these etymologies is proven, and the research notes are right to leave the question undecided. No genetic study of the Son Kolis exists. [n01; gaps]

The Kolis’ own account of where they came from is short and uncertain. C. Rothfeld, the civil servant who wrote Enthoven’s Son Koli entry around 1920, found the Son Kolis “along the North Konkan coast from Bassein to Ratnagiri”, and wrote: “History is not clear as to their arrival on the coast, but a probable inference from tradition is that a majority of the present Kolis is descended from immigrants from the hills who arrived some four hundred years ago.” He then doubted his own inference: “doubt is thrown upon the tradition by the difficulty of understanding the sudden conversion of hillmen into fishers”. [n35; Enthoven II p. 259] John Wilson, in the nineteenth century, counted the Son Koli as “the original inhabitant of Bombay Island”. [n35; Enthoven II pp. 245-246] The Gazetteer recorded that the Thana Son Kolis said they had originally come from Alibag in Kolaba, the seat of their tribal head, the Vagh Patil, whose disciples (shishyas) the village headmen were. [n08; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 149]

They were short and sturdy, the Gazetteer said, with powerful shoulders; the men wore a top-knot and moustache, a loincloth, a waistcloth, a woollen jacket and a red broadcloth cap. The women wore a loose long-sleeved bodice and a tightly wound robe ending above the knee, with glass bangles on the left arm only. “At their weddings the bangles intended for the right hand are consecrated and thrown into the sea, the ocean being invoked to take care of the husband.” They wore silver bangles on the right instead. Enthoven, forty years on, recorded the same silver bracelet on the right hand and nothing else there, as a sign of the deity’s covenant protecting fishers. [n08; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 149] [n35; Enthoven II p. 259] Girls married after eighteen and boys after twenty-two; the Kolis burned their dead, allowed widows to remarry and practised polygamy. They ate rice, nachni bread, pulse and fish, though few would eat porpoise, crocodile or whale. Women sold all the fish, and in Enthoven’s account they “wield great power, manage household affairs”. “Most of them have a good market for their fish,” the Gazetteer said, “and on the whole are well-to-do.” [n08; Gazetteer XIII-1 pp. 148-149] [n35; Enthoven II p. 260]

Their religion changed in a way that the Kolis themselves remembered. Their family gods in 1882 were Khanderao, Bhavani, Bhairav, Babdev, Vir, Kalkai, Cheda and Marubai, kept in the houses of the older men, where the rest came daily to pray and take turmeric (bhandar) or sacred ash. They went on pilgrimage to Benares, Nasik, Pandharpur and Jejuri. [n08; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 149] Around 1920 the Kolis told Enthoven’s author how this had come about. They had been taught “the gods properly” by Kalu Bhagat, a Koli of Varsoli near Alibag, “in the times of the Peshwas some two centuries ago”, who directed them to Khandoba of Jejuri. Before that, they said, there had been “ignorance (adnyani)”. The author read this as “the first Hinduising of the Koli tribe”. [n35; Enthoven II p. 260] It is a rare thing: a community’s own memory of a religious change, dated by itself to the early eighteenth century, and explained not by force or by a king but by a teacher from within the caste.

The sea itself was worshipped. On Narali Purnima, Coconut Day, the full moon of Shravan, the fishing communities of Mumbai, Thane and Palghar offer coconuts to the sea to mark the end of the monsoon closure and the start of the new season. In June and July, when the boats could not go out, the 1882 fishermen made new ropes, nets and sails. Koli Holi, Shimga, can last fifteen days; in Vasai, as in Worli and Colaba, the Holi pyre is made of areca-nut branches. [n08; Jio Institute; News on AIR; Gazetteer fisheries section; FPJ] Cheda, a local spirit, appears in Son Koli houses as in Agri, Bhandari and Pachkalshi ones, a god shared across the castes of the coast. [n08; inference from Gazetteer]

Koli speech

The Son Koli dialect impressed every outsider who heard it. The Gazetteer called it Marathi with “so curious an accent” that it was hard to follow, with the r, l and ṇ sounds shifting. Enthoven’s author gave examples: syncopated verb inflections, different adverbs of place, tavade for tikde, “there”, and avade for ikade, “here”. [n08; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 148] [n35; Enthoven II pp. 259-260]

The oldest description of coastal speech near Bombay is older still. A paper read to the Bombay Geographical Society in the 1830s, comparing the “oldest races” of the island, said that five of them, the Kolis, the Bhandaris, the Palshe Brahmins, the Pathare Prabhus and the “Panchkulseas, Wadavuls or Carpenters”, shared a “Colee bhasha”, a Koli speech strongest among the Kolis themselves, and that it was fading under Brahminical schooling. [n41; Trans. Bombay Geog. Soc. I pp. 128-131] Edwardes in 1902 said the same in another way: the home tongue of Prabhus, Panchkalshis and Bhandaris “contains a large percentage of words borrowed from the aboriginal settlers, the Koli and the Agri”. [n41; Edwardes p. 35]

So the Koli speech of the coast was not a curiosity of one caste. In the earliest accounts it was the common ground under the speech of several communities. Grierson’s rule that Kolis “everywhere speak the dialect of their neighbours” is the other half of the same truth: speech flowed both ways. He estimated about 163,000 Koli speakers in Thana. He also recorded the old opinion that the Kolis had “often been connected” with the Munda tribes, the Kols, but only as an opinion; nothing supports it. [n22; LSI VII] The Christian Kolis, too, read in their own speech: the Gazetteer noted that the Christa Purana, a life of Christ in Marathi verse, was “still well known and much read by the people… in the home dialect of the Thana Son or Sea Kolis”. [n34; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 207 n.1]

Enthoven’s author wrote one more sentence about Koli speech, around 1920, that reads now like a prophecy. “The home tongue of Son Kolis is a patois of Marathi gradually obsolescent under the grinding of the Local Board school and the newspaper.” [n35; Enthoven II p. 259] That is the first written observation on this coast of the force that would, over the next century, wear down every small dialect described in this chapter: standard Marathi in the schoolroom and the press.

The Mangelas

The Mangelas were a separate fishing caste, 10,718 strong in 1872, living in Mahim, Dahanu and Salsette; in the Bassein sub-division the 1881 table counts 2,375. Grierson in 1901 put them at 11,538, most of them in Mahim, Bassein and Dahanu. In Dahanu they were also called Divars. A modern list includes Pachu Bandar, Vasai and Arnala among Mangela Koli villages. [n08; Gazetteer XIII-1 pp. 147-148; Wikipedia, Mangela Kolis] [n21; Gazetteer XIII-II p. 680] [n22; LSI VII]

The Gazetteer gives no origin story for them, but it places the Mangelas, with the Machhis, Mitne Machhis, Kharvis and Kharpatils, among people of Gujarat stock who “dress in Gujarat fashion and speak Gujarati at home”. [n35; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 64] Their surnames in 1882 were Nijak, Dhanu, Kinhi, Mare, Somte, Pagdhar, Naik and Chodhre. They did not shave the crown of the head. They were fishermen, coasting traders and labourers; the men wore a red broadcloth cap, and the women, whose robes were wound less tightly than Koli women’s, sold the fish. They married at sunset with a Palshe Brahmin as priest, kept no images at home, and had a patil to settle their disputes. [n08; Gazetteer XIII-1 pp. 147-148]

Their speech is the clearest example on the coast of a fishing dialect with a Gujarati layer. The Gazetteer called it “indistinct… with many Gujarati words” and gave examples: kain gelā hotā? for Marathi kothe gelā hotā?, “where had you gone?”; āilo for ālo, “came”; shāḍi for shāḷā; kyāla for sakāḷī, “in the morning”; mana bhuk nāngli for malā bhuk lāgli, “I am hungry”. It also recorded an ugly local saying: “The child is a Mangela” was a common phrase for an abusive boy. [n08; Gazetteer XIII-1 pp. 147-148] Grierson printed a parable in the “so-called Mangela dialect” and called it “a dialect of the same kind as Vadval, Samvedi, etc., with a strong admixture of Gujarati”, though he thought it “probably nowhere different from the district” dialect in general. [n22; LSI VII]

Beside the Mangelas were smaller groups whose speech shows the same northern pull. The Machhis of Bassein spoke Gujarati at home and Marathi outside, fished, hired out boats and worshipped Maruti. The Sorathias, 686 of them in Mahim and Dahanu in 1872, were Kathiawar immigrants speaking a mixture of Gujarati and Marathi. [n08; Gazetteer XIII-1 pp. 130, 147]

The Vaitis

The Vaitis numbered 4,596 in 1872, in north Bassein and south Mahim; the 1881 Bassein table counts 967. The Gazetteer ranked them below the Son Kolis but thought them “apparently the same tribe”. They named their founder as “one Valhya Koli”, an echo of Valya Koli, the robber who became the sage Valmiki in the legend of the Ramayana. Their headman lived at Chaul in Kolaba. They had no surnames. They were cultivators, fishers, sailors and day labourers, and a few dealt in timber and hay. They worshipped chiefly Ram and the sea, and kept only a coconut at home as an object of worship. Widows could remarry. [n08; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 182] [n21; Gazetteer XIII-II p. 680] The research found no scholarly source on Vaiti origins beyond these lines. [n35; gaps]

Before Act XIX of 1844 the Kolis, Mangelas and Vaitis all paid a fishing poll-tax, the dāy-dene, of one rupee four annas. [n08; Gazetteer fisheries footnote]

The Samvedi Brahmins: a copper plate of 1026

The first community on this coast that can be connected, however loosely, to a dated document is the Samvedi Brahmins.

In 1026, in the reign of the Shilahara king Chhittaraja, a copper plate was issued that was later found at Bhandup in Salsette. It granted land to a Brahmin named Amadevaiya, son of Vipranodamaiya, of the Chhandoga shakha, the branch of the Sama Veda, living at Nour, which is Nahur, in Shatshashthi, the “sixty-six villages” that became Salsette. [n03; Gazetteer XIII-II p. 425 n.] [n35] The name of the grantee is Kannada in form, ending in -aiya, like those of many Shilahara ministers and grantees; the Gazetteer noted that names of this kind ran through the Shilahara court, and that its grants were written in Sanskrit while the people spoke “a corrupt Prakrit, the mother of the modern Marathi”. [n03; Gazetteer XIII-II p. 428]

The plate proves that a Brahmin of the Sama Veda received land on this coast in 1026. It does not prove that the Samvedi Brahmins of Vasai descend from him or his kin. The research notes say so plainly: the grant “does not prove the modern caste’s continuity”. [n03; inference] What can be said is that it is the only documentary anchor the community has.

The next trace is a stone. At Koprad, near Bolinj and Sopara, about ten miles north of Bassein, there is a Devanagari inscription dated Shaka 1386 and Hijri 868, that is, 1464 CE. It names Mahim, Thana, Koprad and “Bimbstan”, carries Muslim names, and in 1882 it lay near the house of a Samvedi Brahmin who was the village headman. [n03; Gazetteer XIV, Koprad and Sopara entries] By 1882, too, the Gazetteer recorded Samvedi Brahmin headmen at Koprad and at Gass, the southern landing of the Sopara island. [n03; Gazetteer XIV]

The Samvedis in 1882

The Gazetteer’s account of the Samvedis is short but full of life. In 1872 there were 2,563 of them, 1,323 men and 1,240 women, “found only in Bassein and Mahim”. “They speak incorrect Marathi,” it said, and were “strong, tall, and fair, with regular features… clean, neat, sober, thrifty and orderly. Husbandmen and gardeners by craft, they live in one-storied houses with walls of wood planking and tiled roofs. Their daily food is rice pulse and vegetables.” [n08; Gazetteer XIII-1 pp. 81-82] Enthoven’s entry, forty years later, is a single line: “Samavedis are found only in the Thana district, where they chiefly grow garden crops.” [n35; Enthoven I pp. 241-242, 249]

So in the oldest detailed record the Samvedis were not priests. They were landholding farmers and gardeners who kept a Brahmin identity, a Vedic name and Brahmin rites. The men wore short waistcloths, a shoulder cloth wrapped round the body, and red broadcloth caps “like Telugu Brahmans”; on high days, white turbans, coats and silk-bordered waistcloths. The women wore the Maratha bodice and robe and a cascade of gold: mudaga and khudi in the hair; lavanga, bali, mugdya and gāthe in the ears; putlyāchi māl, vajratik and necklaces of gold, coral and flint beads; tode, vāle and phule on the feet. [n08; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 82]

Their customs, as the Gazetteer recorded them, included things that would later be seen among their Christian cousins too. On the fifth day after a birth the spirit Sati was worshipped; the child was named on the twelfth with a caste dinner. At the thread ceremony, before the age of ten, the boy stood on a raised earthen seat with a cloth held between him and his father; when the chanting ended the cloth was pulled away and the drums beat. Then came the mock pilgrimage to Benares: the boy walked about fifty paces as if setting out for Kashi, his maternal uncle called him back and promised him a daughter in marriage, and he returned and begged alms from the guests. Girls married before ten. A bride-price of 200 to 1,000 rupees went to the girl’s father. The day before a wedding twenty or thirty earthen pots were brought from the potter and worshipped, rice-flour lamps were lit in a bamboo basket and held over the elders’ heads, and the priest called “Vidāvidā āla ho”, “time for sandal and betel”. The groom rode to the bride’s house on horseback; bride and groom stood on stools with a cloth between them and garlanded each other when it was drawn aside. Widows could not remarry. The dead were burned, except children under three; on the third day the ashes were gathered and food on a leaf plate was given to the village Mhar. The Samvedis had no headman; they settled disputes by majority vote and spent the fines on feeding Brahmins. “They send their boys to school and are on the whole prosperous.” [n08; Gazetteer XIII-1 pp. 82-83]

Three theories of origin

Where did the Samvedis come from? There are three answers, of very different weight.

The first is the community’s own modern account, found on Wikipedia and in a community blog of 2008. It says that they came to Shurparaka from Utkala or Kalinga, that is Odisha, as expert temple and court singers; that their Vedic practice lapsed under Buddhism and was restored by Adi Shankaracharya at Nirmal, whom they regard as their chief preacher; and that their deity is Nirmaleshwar on the Nirmal hillock. It explains the local name “Shamedi” from musicians at a samadhi shrine. It goes on to say that under Raja Pratap Bimba their work shifted to growing sugarcane and nagvel, betel vine; that paddy was added in the late seventeenth century; and that flower gardens and wadis came in the eighteenth century as Bombay grew. Musicians of the community still play the sanai, choughada, mridanga, bheri and veena at the Shankaracharya Samadhi Mandir at Nirmal, the Sureshwar Mandir and the Bhavani Shankar temple at Agashi. [n08; Wikipedia, Samvedi; nirajpatilsblog 2008] This account is tradition, and weakly sourced tradition at that. The Wikipedia article has a single reference and is tagged for original research, and its dates contradict each other: it places the arrival around “1500 BC” and Shankara in “497 BC”. [n08] A Marathi newspaper feature of 2026 adds that the Samvedi dialect has been spoken in north Vasai “since about the sixth century”, which is equally unsupported. [n20; Saamana, 22 Mar 2026] No scholarly source supports the Odisha origin. [n35]

The second answer ties the Samvedis to Raja Bimba and the settlement of the north Konkan coast. It exists only in modern community tellings. In the two oldest written strata, the 1882 Gazetteer and the Bimbakhyan as reported in it, it is the Palshe Brahmins, not the Samvedis, who come with Bimb. The Bimbakhyan’s list of Bimb’s followers, as Edwardes summarised it, names nine families of Yajurvedi Brahmins and no Samavedis. [n08; inference] [n41; Edwardes pp. 22-23] There is no scholarly source for the Samvedis’ arrival in the Bimba story. [n03; gaps]

The third answer is not a story but an inference from speech, and it is the researcher’s own, flagged as such in the notes: Grierson’s description of Samvedi as a “mechanical mixture of Konkan Marathi and Gujarati” points to a western contact zone linked with Gujarat, not to Odisha. [n35; inference] It sits beside the Gazetteer’s view that the whole coast was “a favourite resort for refugees and settlers from Kathiawar”. [n41; Gazetteer XIII-1 pp. 61-62] It is a hint, not a proof. A people can acquire a neighbour’s words without having come from the neighbour’s country.

Honesty requires a fourth sentence: nobody knows. The 1026 plate shows Sama Veda Brahmins on the coast in the eleventh century. The 1464 stone and the 1882 Gazetteer show Samvedi headmen in the Sopara villages. Between them lie four centuries without a single document naming the community. [n35; gaps]

How Samvedi speech took shape

The Samvedi dialect, today usually called Kadodi, is the best-described small language of this coast, and it carries the clearest marks of mixture.

The 1882 Gazetteer, in a footnote, gave three markers of “incorrect” Samvedi Marathi: āde for kothe, “where”; hād for kā, “why”; and geltu for gelā hotā, “had gone”. [n08; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 82 n.] Grierson, twenty years later, wrote the first proper description. “The Samvedis live as husbandmen and gardeners in Bassein and Alabim” (the OCR of his text is damaged here; the research notes read the place either as Alibag or, more probably given the Gazetteer’s “Bassein and Mahim”, as Mahim). “Their number is estimated at 2,700. They speak a mixed dialect, the chief components of which are the current Marathi of Thana and vulgar Gujarati.” [n22; LSI VII] [n34; LSI VII; reading of “Alabim”]

He listed its features. The sounds ch and chh become s and then h, so that hakra means “son” and hit “field”. S itself becomes h. The genitive suffix becomes -ho, -ha or -a. Strong masculine nouns end in -o, “as in Gujarati and Konkani”. The dative ends in -do, as in mansa-do, or in the Marathi -la or -ti. The verbs follow Thana Marathi, but the past tense of the third person masculine ends in -o. His conclusion was blunt: “a mechanical mixture of Konkan Marathi and Gujarati”. He grouped it with Vadval and Phudgi as “three small dialects in Thana, which still have preserved many of the characteristic features of Gujarati Bhili”. [n22; LSI VII] [n34; LSI VII]

The modern record bears him out. The Marathi Wikipedia article on the dialect gives examples of the same s to h shift: sukeḷī, the sun-dried banana for which Agashi is famous, becomes hukeḷī; sheṇī, cow-dung cakes, becomes henī. Standard ālo, “I came”, becomes ālô, and jevlo, “I ate”, becomes jivlô. Asked jevlāt kā?, “have you eaten?”, a speaker answers jivyāsā hāy. [n20; mr.wikipedia, सामवेदी बोली] A 2024 paper at the Workshop on Asian Translation, describing “Suman”, a Kadodi-Marathi-English dictionary of 949 entries and 942 sentence triples made by native speakers, sets out the sound system: Kadodi lacks the vowels ऐ and औ and the consonants च, छ, ण and ष in its own inventory; श and स are commonly replaced by ह; and it prefers plain dentals to aspirated ones. Cow dung is haen where Marathi has shen. “He makes a living by working as a labourer” is tou majuri karon pout bhartae (Marathi to majuri karun pot bharto); “he is sleeping” is tou nejlay. Kadodi often leaves out the present-tense “is”. [n22; Kumar et al., WAT 2024]

The same s to h change that Grierson heard in 1905 is still there in the dictionary of 2024: a feature continuous for at least 120 years. [n22; inference]

How many speak it? Grierson’s estimate of 2,700 probably counted only the Hindu Samvedi Brahmins, since he listed the Christians separately. Modern figures range from about 50,000 (the Saamana feature of 2026, which counts Samvedi Brahmins, Pachkalshis and Samvedi Christians together) to about 60,000 (Ethnologue, via Wikipedia, 2016). The two sets of figures measure different things, and the notes rightly warn against reading them as growth. [n22; inference] [n20; Saamana] [n13; Wikipedia, Kadodi]

The Saamana feature describes Kadodi as a mixture of Marathi, Konkani and Portuguese, with some Gujarati, spoken with a sing-song intonation by a “singing community” rich in folk songs, proverbs and stories, in twelve coastal villages “from Agashi to Bhuigaon”. The Marathi Wikipedia names sixteen villages: Bhuigaon, Wagholi, Kofrad, Gas (Gass), Umrale, Bolinj, Gavdhani, Vatar, Valunje, Vanda (Mardes), Padhai, Karmale, Navale, Somadi, Bankar and Mandpe. Two scholars stand behind the modern study of it. Paul Rumav, a teacher from Nandakhal, edited Samvedi Lokgeete (Samvedi folk songs), Madhachya Ghagri (Pots of Honey), Samvedi Khristi Samaj (The Samvedi Christian Community) and a book of proverbs of the Vasai area. Dr Naresh Naik of Virar wrote Samvedi Boli: Sanrachana ani Swaroop (The Samvedi Dialect: Structure and Form), which won the Krishna Mukund Ujalambkar Smriti award and a P. Na. Go. Kalelkar award in 2018. [n20; Saamana; mr.wikipedia]

The Portuguese strand in Kadodi belongs to a later part of the story, the conversion of many Samvedi families in the sixteenth century, and is told below. Here it is enough to say that before the Portuguese came, the Samvedis already spoke a Marathi coloured with Gujarati, and that what the Christians among them would later add was, in the main, the vocabulary of the church and of the family.

The king who brought a people: Bimba and the coastal castes

Several of the communities of the north Konkan coast tell the same founding story. A prince named Bimba, or Bimb, or Bhima, or Bhimdev, came to the coast with his followers, made his capital at Mahim, called Mahikavati, and parcelled out the land among them. The Palshe Brahmins say they came with him as his priests. The Pathare Prabhus say they came as his officers. The Panchkalshis, also called Pachkalshis, Somvanshi Kshatriya Pathares, Vadvals, Sutars, Malis and Chaukalshis, say they came as his warriors and gardeners. Some Agris and Bhandaris say the same. The 1882 Gazetteer summed it up: “one large settlement seems to have supplied the foreign element in the Palshe Brahmans, Patane Prabhus, Pachkalshis, Chavkalshis, Somvanshi Kshatris, Sutars, Malis, and according to their own statement in some of the Agris and Bhandaris”. [n41; Gazetteer XIII-1 pp. 61-62]

The story matters to this book for two reasons. It is how the Vadvals, one of the two great garden peoples of Vasai, explain their own presence here. And it is a case study in how a founding legend can be argued over for two hundred years, because so much depended on it: caste rank, land rights, the claim to be Kshatriyas.

The sources of the legend

The legend is written in Marathi chronicles collectively called the Mahikavatichi Bakhar, also known as the Shri Bimbakhyana. The oldest part, chapters two and three, was written by Keshavacharya about 1448 for Nayako-rao, the Desala or revenue officer of Malad, to justify hereditary revenue rights. It was expanded and finalised in the seventeenth century. It has been edited by R. P. Rane (1877), V. K. Rajwade (1924), R. C. Dhere (1973) and Dahisarkar (2024). [n03; Wikipedia, Mahikavatichi Bakhar] Neither the research for this book nor its sources could read the text of these editions directly; what follows rests on the summaries of the Gazetteer (1882), da Cunha (1900), Edwardes (1902), Enthoven (1922), a society paper of the 1830s that compared five manuscripts, and the modern Wikipedia article. [n41; gaps] [n35; gaps]

Three versions of Bimba

The first version: a Yadava prince from Paithan, about 1294-97. This is the version most Panchkalshis gave Enthoven’s survey: they “claim to have come to these parts of Bombay, Salsette, Kelva-Mahim, the Ashtagar sea-coast villages of Alibag taluka and the adjoining districts, with Raja Bimba or Bhima, about the end of the thirteenth century, from Paithan on the Godavari south of Devgiri. Hence their Kshatriya origin.” [n41; Enthoven III pp. 160-161] The Palshes were “generally believed to have come in 1297 from Mungi Paithan… with Bimb”. [n08; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 81] A bakhar written by Janardan Ganesh of Mahim shortly after the Maratha capture of Bassein in 1739, one of the five manuscripts compared in the 1830s, starts with the defeat of Ramdev, the Yadava king of Devgiri, by Ala-ud-din Khalji around 1294, makes Bimb Raja his second son, and has him leave in 1295 “with twelve other Solar princes, 64 Lunar Kshatriya families” and take Chikhne, Tarapur, Asheri, Kelve-Mahim, Thal, Salsette and Mahim: fifteen mahals and 444 villages. A Persian paper of the Sirdesai family of Malad, dated 1495, says that Bimbshah fled after his father Ramadev’s defeat with his royal guru Purushottam Pant and took Parnera, Bardi, Sanjan, Daman, Sigaon and Mahim, dividing the land into twelve (elsewhere fifteen) parts. [n41; Trans. Bombay Geog. Soc. I pp. 128-137; Edwardes p. 25] Edwardes, writing The Rise of Bombay in 1902, accepted this version: Bhimdev was “the son of Ramdev, Raja of Devgiri”, and the settlers came “from Paithan” at the time of Ala-ud-din’s siege. [n41; Edwardes pp. 23-25, 30-31]

The second version: a prince of Anhilvada Patan in Gujarat. Enthoven’s Panchkalshi informants gave this one too, as an alternative: “king Bimba, though descended from the royal family of Devgiri, had a principality at Anhilvada Patan in Gujarat proper, whence he migrated on the overthrow of the Hindu principalities by the Muhammadans… during the regime of the Khiljis.” [n41; Enthoven III pp. 160-161] The Bimbakhyan, as Edwardes summarised it, has Bimbadev come to the Konkan “by way of Anahilvada” in Shaka 1216 (1294 CE) and halt on Mahim island. With him, it says, came nine families of Yajurvedi (Madhyandin) Brahmins and sixty-six other families: twenty-seven Somavanshi kulas, twelve Suryavanshi, nine Sheshvanshi, five Panchal, seven Kunbi or Agri, and Lad, Moda and other families. [n41; Edwardes pp. 22-23] Gerson da Cunha went further. Bhima, he wrote, was “evidently… the Solanki ruler of Anahilavada in Gujarat, and not the Yadav Bhima of Devagiri”. [n41; da Cunha, Origin of Bombay, pp. 42-43] The Gazetteer leaned the same way. It cited the Arab historian Ibn al-Athir, who names a Bimb, nephew of the king of Anhilvada, who came to relieve Somnath when Mahmud of Ghazni attacked it in 1025, when many of Somnath’s people escaped by sea. It cited a Prabhu account that made Bimb “Bhimdev II of Anhilvada or Patan, who fled… on the approach of Kutub-ud-din in 1194” (from the Ras Mala). And it cited a grant to a Palshe priest, whose privileges the family still enjoyed, which said the priest had come “from Pattan” and that Bimb was “of the Anhilvada family”. [n41; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 62] [n35; Gazetteer XIII-1 pp. 61-62]

The third version: Pratap Bimb, about 1138. The bakhar itself, in the modern summary, tells a longer and earlier story. Pratap Bimb, brother of Govardhan Bimb of Champaner, arrives about 1138. He holds the coast from Chikhli to Tarapur and makes his capital at Mahikavati, which is Mahim near Kelve in Palghar. His general Balkrishnarao Somvanshi takes Thane from the Shilahara “Yashwantrao”. Later he founds a second Mahikavati at present-day Mahim in Bombay. Only after him come Nagar-shah of Gandevi, the Yadava prince Bimbdev, son of Ramdevrao, the Tughluq conquest, local chiefs, the Gujarat sultans from 1429 to 1534, and the Portuguese. [n03; Wikipedia, Mahikavatichi Bakhar] This is the version the community’s modern Vadval Samaj website echoes, when it says that Pratapbimb conquered Mumbai, Thane and Palghar and settled sixty-six kuls, and that the Vadvals have been in the north Konkan “since about 1060 CE”, having lived before that “in the Palanpur princely state”. That website gives no source for either claim. [n41; vadvalsamaj.com]

Beside these three sits a fourth voice, hostile to all of them. Among the Bassein papers compared in the 1830s was one that made Bimb a Shudra chief, whose followers split into Palshe priests, Prabhu ministers and “humbler followers… settled in the gardens as cultivators and carpenters, the forefathers of the present Panchkulseas”. The writer of the paper noted that Brahmins “treat the pretensions to caste of all these three classes with contempt, maintaining that they are all sprung from one Shoodra origin”. [n41; Trans. Bombay Geog. Soc. I pp. 128-137]

What the evidence can and cannot bear

Set side by side, the dates cannot be reconciled: 1025 (Ibn al-Athir’s Bimb at Somnath), about 1138 (Pratap Bimb in the bakhar), 1194 (Bhimdev II in the Ras Mala account), about 1294-97 (Bimbadev in the Bimbakhyan and the community’s Paithan story), and 1495 for the Persian paper that tells it. Edwardes warned that the dates are inaccurate and the statements “so very contradictory” that they cannot be accepted without independent evidence. [n41; Edwardes pp. 22-23] [n35; inference]

There is some independent evidence that a Bimb polity, or at least a place-name, was real. The Salsette Explorations Project in 2016 found an inscription of 1368, of a king Hambirarao, at Deonar in Bombay, mentioning “Konkan-Bimbasthana”; five such inscriptions were known by 2023. The Koprad stone of 1464, near the Samvedi headman’s house, names “Bimbstan”. An inscribed stone brought from Nila, about a mile north of Sopara, names the Yadava king Ramchandradev, whom the Gazetteer dated to 1280-1298, and names Shurparaka twice: so the Devgiri Yadavas were overlords of this coast in exactly the decades when the Paithan version sets Bimba’s arrival. The Gazetteer noted that copies of local grants referring to Bimb belong to about 1280, when the Yadavas were overlords, and that “Bimbsthan” seems to have been an old name of Bhiwandi. [n03; Wikipedia, Mahikavatichi Bakhar; Dalal et al. 2023; Gazetteer XIII-II p. 437 n.; Gazetteer XIV] [n40; Gazetteer XIV p. 387] Rajwade, who edited the bakhar, judged its geography reliable: of 396 place-names it gives, 336 are in the north Konkan. [n35; Wikipedia]

The fair reading, then, is the one the notes reach: treat the story that Raja Bimba brought the Panchkalshis, Prabhus and Palshes to Mahim, Salsette and Vasai as a founding myth written down in the fifteenth to seventeenth centuries, whose kernel, a Bimbasthan polity of the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, perhaps linked to the Yadavas, is supported by inscriptions. [n03; inference]

Why did it matter so much which version was true? Because each version carries a rank. A Yadava prince from Devgiri, or a Solanki prince from Patan, makes his followers Kshatriyas. A Shudra adventurer makes them Shudras. The 1830s paper shows the argument already raging in Bombay. And the Maratha conquest of 1739-40, which brought Deccan Brahmins to power on this coast, made it bitter for the Palshes in particular. The notes’ summary line is a good one: three origin stories for the Palshes, Paithan, Anhilvada Pattan, Gujarat; and the 1740 conquest is why the argument mattered. [n41; inference] [n35; inference]

The Gazetteer’s case for Gujarat

The Gazetteer did not simply prefer one legend. It argued from evidence, and its arguments are worth setting out, because they are the only attempt in the sources to test the story.

First, it found no record that Mungi Paithan was sacked by the Muslims before 1318, so a flight from Paithan in 1297 had no known cause. Second, fifty years seemed too short for all that Salsette was said to have developed under the settlers before Muhammad Tughluq overthrew Bimb’s chiefship (the Gazetteer gave 1347). Third, both the Prabhu and the Palshe records agree that their first settlements were on the coast, at Kelve-Mahim, Bassein and Salsette, not inland. Fourth, the Prabhus’ home speech was “incorrect Marathi” with Gujarati words for household things: dadar for a ladder, tākābāri for a wall bookcase, ovara for a room, ota for a veranda, phanas, lodhi. The Gazetteer itself cautioned that two hundred years of Prabhu service in Gujarat complicated this point. Fifth, the Palshes followed the White Yajurveda, the Yajurveda of Gujarat, and, as in Gujarat, forbade marriage between people whose mothers’ fathers shared a family stock. Sixth, the grant to the Palshe priest from “Pattan”. [n35; Gazetteer XIII-1 pp. 61-62 and nn. 1-4] [n41; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 62] The Gazetteer’s conclusion was that “the correctness of this story is doubtful”, and that the coast had been “a favourite resort for refugees and settlers from Kathiawar”. [n41; Gazetteer XIII-1 pp. 61-62] In another volume the same Gazetteer called even the Gujarat view “doubtful”. [n03; Gazetteer XIII-II p. 437 n.] It was a question it never settled.

Grierson’s evidence, collected independently twenty years later, points the same way as the Gazetteer: the Vadvals were “supposed to have immigrated from Gujarat”, and their speech had Gujarati forms and sound changes like those of the Bhil dialects of Chhota Udaipur. [n41; LSI VII pp. 145-146] The notes draw the sensible conclusion that language is the best independent evidence, and that it favours a Gujarat or north-coast background. They add an important qualification: the bakhar has Bimb passing through both Paithan and Anhilvada, so the two versions may each be true of different layers of settlers. [n41; inference]

The research found no primary or scholarly source linking the Vadvals to Magadha or to Odisha, though some modern web pages say so. [n41; Q1] [n01; mirror of Somvanshi Kshatriya page]

The Palshe Brahmins: priests of the coast

The Palshe Brahmins called themselves Vajasaneyi Brahmans. They followed the Madhyandin branch of the White Yajurveda and traced their line to the sage Yajnavalkya. Their name, according to the Bimbakhyan as the Gazetteer reports it, came from Palsavli, a village in Kalyan, which Bimb gave to his family priest. [n08; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 81] [n35; Enthoven I pp. 248-249]

The 1882 Gazetteer counted 9,311 of them in the Thana district in 1872, in Panvel, Bassein, Mahim, Dahanu, Bhiwandi, Salsette and Kalyan; another table in the same volume, as read in a different note, gives 2,311 Palshes. The difference is probably a matter of reading the scan, and the larger figure is the one given in the Palshe paragraph itself. Enthoven’s census of 1901 returned only 478 “Palshikars”, which the notes explain as a narrow count. [n08; Gazetteer XIII-1 pp. 79-81] [n35; Enthoven I] Their surnames were Kavle, Joshi, Phatak, Pandit, Chhatre, Mogre, Kirtane, Purandare, Devdhar, Paranjpe, Upadhye, Kshirsagar, Javaje, Parashare, Trivedi and Shesne. They worked as priests, physicians and astrologers. [n08; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 81]

Their place in the society of the coast was central. They were the priests of the Pathare Prabhus and of the Panchkalshis; they married the Mangela fishermen at sunset; they would later readmit Christian Agris to Hinduism. In Salsette, Bassein, Mahim and Dahanu they held “the privilege of fixing the auspicious time for marriage and thread ceremonies for all classes”. [n35; cross-cutting findings] [n08; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 81] Enthoven treated them as Madhyandin Yajurvedi Brahmins and recorded that they “are generally believed to have come in 1297 A.D. from Mungi Paithan on the Godavari with Bimba. But it seems that they formed part of the large settlement which from Gujarat supplied the foreign element to the Thana district.” [n35; Enthoven I pp. 248-249]

Their speech had its own small marks: mad for madhe, “in”, and kai for kothe, “where”. [n08; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 81]

Their history after 1739 belongs to a later section of this chapter, but one detail belongs here, because it shows how a rival’s story could be written into a name. The Deccan and Chitpavan Brahmins who arrived with Maratha rule mocked the Palshes with a false etymology of their name: palā-āshi, “flesh-eater”. The Gazetteer recognised it for what it was: “probably the work of their rivals the Deccan and Chitpávan Bráhmans at whose hands the Palshes have suffered much since the Maratha conquest of western Thana (1740).” [n08; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 81 n.]

The Pachkalshis, Vadvals and Somvanshi Kshatriyas

Many names for one people

No community on this coast has more names than the one the 1882 Gazetteer called the Pachkalshis. “They are known as Sutars, Malis, Vadvals, Chaukalshis, Somvanshi Kshatris, and Pathares, all of whom except the Chaukalshis eat together and intermarry.” In 1872 there were 15,367 of them, strongest along the coast. [n41; Gazetteer XIII-1 pp. 129-130] Enthoven’s entry, written from the community’s own informants, distinguished the names more carefully. “Somvanshi Kshatriya Pathare” was “the racial or tribal name”; Sutar and Vadval were sub-division names by occupation; “Panchkalshi may be considered generic”. The three main endogamous divisions were the Ashtagarkars of the Alibag coast (about 2,000), the Sashtikars of Salsette (about 6,000), and the Vadvals, of whom a community census of 1901 returned 7,916 and who numbered “about 8,000” around 1920. [n41; Enthoven III pp. 160-161]

Each name has its own explanation.

Panchkalshi means “five pots”. The Gazetteer said the name came from “their using in their marriage ceremonies five earthen pots, kalas”. Enthoven’s informants gave two versions: the bridegroom sat on a chair of state, a simhasan, decked with five kalashas or little cupolas; or five earthen water-pots stood on either side of the main entrance from the veranda at weddings. [n41; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 129; Enthoven III p. 160] A Marathi writer of 1935, Gajanan Govind Naik of Anjur, argued that “Pachkalshi” in the bakhar was not a caste name at all but a badge of Kshatriya status, “five kalashas used on the throne or at a wedding as a mark of kshatriyahood”. Kshatriya groups who came with Bimb used it; those who lost the right to one kalash became “Chaukalshi”, four-pot people; only later did it harden into a caste label. In his own day, he noted, “other castes and even some Christians now call themselves Panchkalshi Christians”. [n41; Naik 1935 p. 15 ff.] The Chaukalshis were the four-pot group of Vasai and Palghar. In 1882 they were still a separate group with different rules: they wore the sacred thread only at marriage, did not shave a widow’s head, and allowed widows to remarry, while some Pachkalshis always wore the thread and forbade widow marriage. [n41; Gazetteer XIII-1 pp. 129-130]

Vadval is a place-and-work name. Enthoven: “The name Vadval is the title of the sub-division particularly found in the Bassein, Mahim and Dahanu talukas of the Thana district formerly known as the Feringana and literally means the managers of wadis or orchards, viz., of cocoa-nut and palm groves.” “Feringana”, the land of the Franks, is the old name for the territory of the Portuguese. The Bombay City Gazetteer of 1909 explained Vadvals as people “whose chief occupation was the tending of orchards and gardens, the weaving and preparation of palm-leaves for thatching, and the manufacture of coir-rope and brooms”. The paper of the 1830s said they “received the name of Wadavul, or gardener, merely from having given up war, and taken to the cultivation of the cocoanut gardens”. [n41; Enthoven III p. 160; Bombay City Gazetteer I p. 238; Trans. Bombay Geog. Soc. I pp. 130, 136] Marathi Wikipedia adds a folk etymology from vadvadil, ancestors, tagged “citation needed”. [n41; mr.wikipedia]

Panmali, “betel-gardener”, is a label found only in Wikipedia-derived text. The researcher searched the Gazetteers and Enthoven for it and found nothing. Betel vines were certainly a garden crop of the Kelve-Mahim gardens in 1882, grown under thatched booths for twelve to twenty-four months before harvest and “far more largely sent to Gujarat than to Bombay”; but the Gazetteer does not say which caste grew them, and the name Panmali must be treated as a modern label. [n41; Q2]

Somvanshi Kshatriya Pathare, “Kshatriyas of the lunar line”, is the name the community has used for itself since the nineteenth century. It is a claim to the warrior order, and the 1882 Gazetteer and Grierson’s survey did not accept it: both classed the Pachkalshis as Kunbis, cultivators “of part Gujarat descent”. Grierson called the Vadvals “one of the sub-tribes of the Pānchkalsī Kunbīs”. Enthoven, in 1922, classed them as Kshatriyas. The British censuses had at one point recorded them together with the Pathare Prabhus, which caused further confusion. By 1931 the Census listed “Panchkalshi, Somvanshi Kshatriya” among the “Advanced” Hindu castes of Thana, Kolaba and Bombay City. [n41; Gazetteer XIII-1; LSI VII p. 145; Census 1931] [n08; Wikiwand]

The fourteenth-century split

Enthoven’s informants told of a division inside the caste that they dated to the fourteenth century. “The Vadvals are said to have separated some time in the fourteenth century. There is a disposition to challenge their claim to be Panchkalshis; but a careful examination of various documents including letters from the Shankaracharya in 1836 and 1852 AD and a resolution of the Palshikar Brahmans of the Bassein taluka in 1855 seems to leave little doubt that the Vadvals are entitled to style themselves Panchkalshis. The split between Vadvals and the rest of the caste is reputed to be due to the admission of a Koli into the caste. Both the Vadvals and the Sashtikars allege that the Koli was admitted to the sub-division to which they do not belong. The fact remains that for some such reason Vadvals became a separate endogamous sub-division several centuries ago.” [n41; Enthoven III pp. 161-162]

The “Koli admitted” story has three forms in the sources. In the verse Bimbakhyan compared in the 1830s, the Panchkalshis are “Lunar Kshatriyas who were degraded and forced to become carpenters for having admitted to their caste and eaten with a Koli who had assisted in seizing their enemy in the water”. In Enthoven, it is the cause of the split between Vadvals and Sashtikars, each blaming the other. In the Brahmin polemic, it is part of the case that they were never Kshatriyas at all. [n41; Q4] The notes draw a careful inference: since the Vadvals’ home speech is so close to the Kolis’, the story may preserve a real memory of social contact and mixing. [n41; inference]

The dispute was still alive when Enthoven’s survey was being made. In his introduction he noted that circulating his draft monographs had provoked claims, “such… as those of Panchals to be classed as Vishva Brahmans, of Sonars to be Daivadnya Brahmans, and of Vadvals to be admitted to rank as Panchkalshis, or Somvanshi Kshatriyas. Interesting documents have been produced bearing on these disputed points.” [n41; Enthoven I Introduction p. xix] The Shankaracharya’s letters of 1836 and 1852 and the Palshikar resolution of 1855 themselves have not been found; only Enthoven’s summary survives in the notes. [n41; gaps]

There were other splits. A caste meeting in 1779 passed a resolution against marriage between Sashtikars and Ashtagarkars. The Janjira Panchkalshis neither married nor dined with the rest. The Malis of Chaul broke away around 1820 because their women sold flowers and fruit in the open market. [n41; Enthoven III p. 161] And the same meeting of 1779 passed “stringent resolutions on the prevention of costly litigation, the re-marriage of widows, the sale of girls, and temperance”, the earliest recorded act of social reform by a caste of this coast. [n41; Bombay City Gazetteer I pp. 238-239]

Under the Portuguese

The community remembered the Portuguese century as a time of persecution. Enthoven’s informants said: “Under the Portuguese, who succeeded the Muhammadans, there was much persecution, and this is said to have forced many members of the caste to fly to the outlying parts of the Southern Konkan and join those who were already settled in the Ashtagar. Many were then converted to Roman Catholicism by force and their descendants may still be traced at Bassein and Salsette.” The Bombay City Gazetteer of 1909 added that others “were forcibly converted to Christianity and became the ancestors of some native Christian families now resident in Bombay, Bandora and Marole”. [n41; Enthoven III p. 161; Bombay City Gazetteer I p. 238] These are the community’s and the colonial writers’ accounts; the research found no Portuguese document naming Vadvals. [n41; inference]

The Portuguese century also changed their work. “In consequence of the political revolutions,” Enthoven’s informants said, “they seem to have taken to agriculture and day labour. Under the Portuguese many of them became skilled mechanics and carpenters (house and boat builders).” [n35; Enthoven III p. 163] The 1880s Gazetteer lists Pachkalshis among the builders of the coast’s lateen-rigged boats, alongside Marathas, Christians, Gujarat carpenters, Cutch Muslims and Kolis. [n36; Gazetteer XIII-1 pp. 342-343] The Christian Vadvals who descend from the converts are told in the section on the Portuguese below.

The reward of 1739

When the Marathas besieged Bassein in 1737-39, the Pachkalshis took their side, and were rewarded. Three government texts agree on this. Enthoven: “They rendered material help to the Marathas during the siege of Bassein and the acquisition of adjoining country in 1739-40. In consequence of this help the Naik family of Anjore, among others, obtained the title of Sarpatil and received inam lands from the Peshwa’s Court. To the siege of Bassein and other events of the great Maratha-Portuguese struggle are traced back the patelships of many families of the Vadval division.” The Bombay City Gazetteer names the men: “As late as 1739 AD two Panchkalshis, named Bugaji and Gangaji Naik, were granted the village of Anjur in inam with the title of Naik Sarpatel by the Peshwa in return for help accorded to the Marathas against the Portuguese.” The Thana Gazetteer’s footnote, credited to W. B. Mulock, says that “the disturbances of the eighteenth century aroused the old warlike spirit of the Pachkalshis”, that the Peshwa made one family chief Patils of Salsette, another proprietors of Angaon in Bhiwandi and a third proprietors of Anjar in Bhiwandi. [n41; Enthoven III p. 161; Bombay City Gazetteer I p. 238; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 130 n.]

That footnote dates the siege “1743”, which is almost certainly a slip for 1739; the siege of Bassein ended in May 1739. [n41; inference] [n35] Anjur itself is in Bhiwandi taluka, not in Vasai. [n40; B12]

In 1935 Gajanan Govind Naik, a Pathare Kshatriya writer of Anjur, published a full study of Gangaji Naik’s part in the campaign, Sashtichi Bakhar urf Vasaicha Durdhar Dharmasangram. It quotes a source it calls “Hemadri p. 151”: “In 1739, in the conquest of Vasai province from the Portuguese, Gangaji Naik and Chuba Naik of Anjur and some Somvanshi Kshatriya gentlemen of heroic character gave the Peshwa the greatest help; if they had not, the Maratha army would have been badly ruined.” It lists the helpers by caste, Pathare Kshatriyas, Brahmans, Pathare Prabhus, Somvanshi Kshatriyas (the Chaudharis of Umele, Ambemoda), Bhandaris, Kolis, Christians and Agris, and names Raut helpers of Vasai: Dhaga Raut, Gan Raut, Laxman Raut and Mayaji Raut. [n41; Naik 1935 pp. 15, 320 ff.] The reward was patilships and inam land, not a caste title, and it underlies the landed position of the Vadval patil families since. [n41; inference]

Work, gods and dress

“Pachkalshi āni kon mhanel ālshi?” ran the proverb: “Who can call a Pachkalshi idle?” [n41; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 130 n.; Edwardes pp. 29-30] They were husbandmen, gardeners and carpenters, and a few were writers and day labourers. “The wives of husbandmen and gardeners help their husbands by selling vegetables, butter, and milk.” [n41; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 130] The majority of Vadvals in Enthoven’s day were farmers holding land directly from the state; others cultivated land belonging to other members of the community on the khand system, for a fixed share of the produce, supplying their own cattle. [n41; Enthoven III p. 166] In Bassein the Christian cartmen of Chulna, Manikpur, Gokhivra, Sandor and Barhanpur “are known as Vadvals or gardeners”; they bought fallen timber, they were “the best axemen in the district”, and “their carts are larger and their cattle stronger than those found in other sub-divisions”; gangs of five to thirty carts travelled by night. [n41; Gazetteer XIII-1 pp. 97-98] Betel-nut growers were “partly Christians, Malis converted by the Portuguese, and partly Hindus of the Chavkalshi or Pachkalshi class”, and sugar in Bassein was “chiefly made by Pachkalshis, Malis, Native Christians and Samvedi Brahmans”. [n41; Gazetteer XIII-1 ch. IV]

In their houses, the Gazetteer noted in 1882, “images of Cheda and other demon-gods” sat beside brass and stone images of Ganpati, Shiv and Krishna. Enthoven, from the community’s own account, described them as both Shaivas and Vaishnavas, worshipping Dattatraya, Ganpati, Vithoba and a little Khandoba, with household goddesses Mahalakshmi, Ekvira, Vajreshwari and Jogeshwari, and Shitala for smallpox; he did not mention Cheda, and wrote that they “believe in astrology and witchcraft, but cannot be described as demon-worshippers”. [n41; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 130; Enthoven III pp. 164-165] The difference may be one of class or of self-presentation: the community’s own account, prepared for a government survey, left out the local spirit that the district officer had seen.

Their dress varied by place and wealth. “Near Bombay the men dress like Brahmans, in Bombay like Prabhus, and in outlying parts they call themselves Marathas.” In 1909 a Vadval man wore a short waistcloth, a bara bandi waistcoat folded double with twelve fastenings, six on each side, and “either the Koli cap or a large red turban”; the women’s cloth reached the knee, tucked up and fastened behind like a Koli woman’s, with a silver belt and necklace. [n41; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 130; Bombay City Gazetteer I pp. 240-241] The red head-cloth and the Koli cap belong to a shared coastal dress, worn by Hindus before any conversion-era identities were fixed.

How Vadvali formed

The Vadval dialect, called Vadvali and, in one of its forms, Phudagi, is the second great mixed speech of the Vasai coast, and Grierson’s account of it is the most detailed he gave of any.

“The Vadvals are one of the sub-tribes of the Pānchkalsī Kunbīs. They are returned to the number of 3,500 from Thana, where they are found in the coast talukas of Bassein, Mahim and Dahanu. Like the rest… they are supposed to have immigrated from Gujarat.” [n41; LSI VII p. 145] The dialect, he wrote, “is most intimately connected with the usual Marathi of the Central and Northern Konkan”. It used the cerebral ḷ as in the Deccan: doḷā, eye. The ṇ was often written but “seems to be pronounced as a dental”, as in pāni, water. Then came the changes that marked it as something more than Konkan Marathi. Ch became s or h: sākar, servant, for Marathi chākar; sānd, moon, for chānd; sār, four, for chār. S and sh became h, “probably like the sound of ch in loch”: hona, gold; hambhar, a hundred. The genitive -sā became -hā or dropped away: bābāhā, bābāgā, bābāā, “of the father”. The verb “is” was hāy or āy. And “some forms and words are Gujarati and not Marathi”, such as mere, near; potali, one’s own woman, for wife; bhuke, with hunger; lyāt-nā, from among them. He compared the sound changes with those of “the Bhil dialects of Chhota Udaipur”. [n41; LSI VII pp. 145-146] (An earlier note for this book misread one of Grierson’s rules as gh to s; the corrected reading is ch to s or h. [n41; correction to n22])

The grammar showed both strands: an agent case in -n (bābān, by the father), genitives mād, mādzā, tād, tudzā, a past tense geltāv and a perfect ālāy, “he has come”. His specimen, the Prodigal Son, gave the lines quoted at the start of this chapter, and words such as kapnaylya-var, “after having spent”. [n41; LSI VII pp. 145-147] [n22]

Phudgi, the dialect of “a wandering tribe in Thana”, returned by a thousand speakers, was “almost identical with Vadval” in the only material Grierson received, a standard word list: ghoḍā horse, pāni water, sākar servant, sār four, hona gold, sāt seven, visā twenty. He kept them apart only because the evidence was thin. [n41; LSI VII p. 147]

Two other old descriptions fill out the picture. The 1882 Gazetteer said the Pachkalshis spoke “incorrect Marathi using l for ḷ and n for ṇ”, which conflicts in detail with Grierson’s report of a Deccan ḷ. [n41; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 130] And the Bombay City Gazetteer of 1909 drew a line by class: “The true language of the Panchkalshis is closely akin to that of the Prabhus and resembles the Marathi found in old works like the Dnyaneshwari. It contains an admixture of Gujarati words… the language of the lower classes of Panchkalshis, particularly those engaged in agricultural pursuits outside the island of Bombay, is closely akin to that of the Kolis and Agris, and the home-dialect of a Panchkalshi woman of this class is hardly distinguishable from the dialect of a Koli fish-wife, who uses kain for where, mijim for by me, and gehlum for I had gone.” [n41; Bombay City Gazetteer I p. 239]

A modern study, Smita Patil’s chapter “Wadavali” in the People’s Linguistic Survey of India (2017), read only through a relay site, gives further examples of the same shifts: sākhar, sugar, becomes hākar; sānj, evening, hānj; shimpi, tailor, himpi; chamchā, spoon, samchā; chinch, tamarind, shis; bhint, wall, bhitya; pahāt, dawn, pāt; aishi, eighty, arshi. It reports that the community is recording and printing its oral folk songs because it fears the language will vanish. [n41; indiandistricts.in relaying PLSI 17.2]

Put together, the formation looks like this. A Gujarat-linked people brought sound changes and words of the Gujarati and Bhil-Gujarati north. Settled for centuries among Kolis and Agris, they took on the coast’s common Koli speech, most of all in the homes of farming families, until a Vadval farmer’s wife sounded like a Koli fishwife. Their upper families, in Bombay, kept a more literary Marathi close to the Prabhus’. The notes’ summary is exact: Bhil-like shifts and Gujarati forms suit a Gujarat-border origin; Koli-like women’s speech and Koli and Agri vocabulary show a long local overlay; and the 1830s “Colee bhasha” is the earliest statement of that common substrate. [n41; inference]

The dialect’s present state is fragile. The 1931 Census still listed “Vadval” as a name under Marathi. Ethnologue’s figure, from a count of 2000, is about 2,500 speakers around Dahanu, Naigaon and Thane; Glottolog classes Phudagi (code phud1238, ISO phd) as “threatened”. Whether Hindu and Christian Vadvals speak the same variety has never been studied. [n41; Census 1931; Glottolog; Wikipedia, Phudagi] A Vasai community site says that South Vasai (Vadval) speech and dress differ slightly from those of North Vasai (Kupari), and that the songs show it. [n13; vasaiker.com]

Salt, toddy and rice: the Agris, the Bhandaris and the Kunbis

The Agris

The Agris were the salt-makers of the coast. Their name is generally connected with the agar, the salt pan or the low coastal farmland, and Marathi Wikipedia’s article on Agri speech treats the whole coastal “agar” belt as their country, counting Kuparis and Vadvals among its “bhumiputra” farming castes alongside Agris, Gavlis, Kunbis and others. [n22; note 20 relay] [n20; mr.wikipedia, आगरी बोलीभाषा]

The Bimbakhyan’s list of Bimb’s followers includes “seven Kunbi or Agri” families, and the Gazetteer recorded that some Agris, by their own statement, shared in the Bimb settlement. [n41; Edwardes pp. 22-23; Gazetteer XIII-1 pp. 61-62] It also recorded a different memory among some Agris and Bhandaris: “the remembrance of a hurried flight from the south and some traces of Lingayat customs”. [n35; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 63] Neither story has independent support; the notes found no modern anthropological study of the Vasai Agris. [n35; gaps]

The 1882 Gazetteer divided them into three groups who neither ate together nor intermarried. The Sudagris had three sub-divisions named by work: Mithagris, salt-makers; Jasagris, toddy-drawers; and Dholagris, drummers. Their surnames were Bhoir, Chaudhari, Chavan, Gharat, Gulvi, Jadhav, Kini, Mali, Mandre, Mhatre, Mukul, Navraye, Naik, Pawar, Shelar, Shelke, Vaze and Yadav, and their families bore “badges” of trees and fruits: pineapple, hirda, mango, fig. The Dasagris lived along the Tansa and Vaitarna rivers, and in Bassein at Kofar, Chandve, Navsai and Khanivde; their badges included naddi, river, marichimbori, crab, and mordi, peacock. Their own story of their origin was a story of excommunication: an Agri’s mistress, vowed to the god of the Mhars, carried a cow’s bone, and the group around her was put out of caste. The third group, the Urap Agris, were Christians who had returned to Hinduism, and their story belongs with the Christians below. [n08; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 116 and n.]

Almost all Agris were husbandmen, salt-makers and labourers. Their staple food was nagli or coarse rice with fish. They favoured “Cheda and other local spirits or devs”, and the Gazetteer, in the condescending tone of its time, called them “excessively fond of liquor”. [n08; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 116] In 1901 Enthoven counted 211,176 Agris, 88,733 of them in Thana. The 1881 table for the Bassein sub-division, in a fuzzy scan, gives 8,461 (perhaps 3,461). [n35; Enthoven I pp. 9-14] [n21; Gazetteer XIII-II p. 680] Salt has outlasted much else: Vasai still has about 2,000 acres of salt pans, and monsoon floods strand saltpan workers at Vasai’s Mithagar. [n08; Outlook; Deccan Herald]

Their speech the Gazetteer called “rough” Marathi. Grierson recorded an Agri dialect only in Kolaba, at Pen, Alibag, Panvel and Uran, with 22,826 speakers, and gave no Thana count. Marathi Wikipedia describes the Vasai-Virar speech of the agar belt as somewhat nasal. [n08; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 116] [n22; LSI VII] [n20; mr.wikipedia] The research found no word list for the Agri speech of Vasai specifically. [n13; gaps] What the older sources do say is that Agri and Koli words ran through the home speech of the Prabhus, Panchkalshis and Bhandaris, and that a farming Vadval woman sounded like a Koli or Agri. [n41; Edwardes p. 35; Bombay City Gazetteer I p. 239] The Agris were part of the common ground.

The Bhandaris

The Bhandaris were the palm-tappers. Their name, the Gazetteer said, came from Sanskrit mandhārak, a distiller. In 1872 there were 13,088 of them in the district. They said they had come with Bimb; others said the Portuguese had brought them from Goa, which the Gazetteer thought unlikely. Some kept, like some Agris, the memory of a hurried flight from the south and traces of Lingayat customs. [n08; Gazetteer XIII-1 pp. 149-150] [n35; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 63]

They were divided by the trees they climbed and by their religious history. The Kirtes, the highest, tapped coconut palms; the Gavads tapped brab palms; there were Sindes; and the lowest were the Kirpals, who were “once Christians”. The Gazetteer guessed that their name came from kriyāpāl, people “allowed Hindu rites” again. Their surnames included Surve, Jadhav and Kadam. [n08; Gazetteer XIII-1 pp. 149-150]

The Gazetteer’s description of them at work is one of its most vivid passages. They were “often seen with a hollow gourd full of palm-juice on their head”, they “always carry on their left thigh a heavy broad-bladed tapping knife”, and “they sing while they tap the trees”. At work they wore a scarlet waistcoat and skull-cap; their women decked their hair with flowers. They worshipped Cheda with goats and fowl and fasted on Ganesh Chaturthi; their headman was the mukadam. Then came a change imposed from outside: “Since (1877) the recent rise in the palm tree cess, many have become husbandmen and labourers.” [n08; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 150] At Sopara in 1882 the Hindus were “chiefly Samvedi Brahmans and Bhandaris”. [n34; Gazetteer XIV p. 315] The goddess Jivdani, on the hill above Virar, is named as the kuldevi of the Mangela, Koli and Bhandari communities. [n08; apnisanskriti]

The Kunbis

The Kunbis were the great cultivating caste of the district: 183,144 in Thana, the largest Hindu group. But they were a central and eastern people, not a Vasai coast people. In 1872 there were only about 1,050 Talheri Kunbis in Bassein against 33,000 in Shahapur; the 1881 Bassein table, which may count Kunbis more broadly, gives 5,978. [n08; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. ~127] [n35; Gazetteer XIII-1 pp. 124-125] [n21]

The Gazetteer’s analysis of the Kunbis is a small essay on how this district’s population formed. The Talheri Kunbis, it said, were “composed of two main elements, a local apparently little different from the Son Koli, and a foreign. The early or local element is much stronger than either in Gujarat or in Deccan Kunbis.” The foreign element “belongs to two periods, before and after the times of the Musalmans and Portuguese”. The early layer showed in Rajput-like surnames: More (perhaps Maurya), Salunkhe, Jadhav, Yadav, Povar, Chohan, Shelar. The later layer showed in Maratha surnames: Bhosle, Kadam, Shirke, Samble. “Talheri seems to mean a lowlander, perhaps as opposed to Varli an uplander and Malhari a highlander.” And the Maratha Kunbis “represent the conquerors, who, during the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, passing north from Satara and Ratnagiri, settled across the whole south of the district”. [n35; Gazetteer XIII-1 pp. 124-125]

The caste was not closed. A Koli who settled to farming in the Deccan “frequently becomes a Kunbi”, Enthoven wrote, and in Thana the Malhar Kolis “frequently become Maratha Kunbis”. The Gazetteer noted that the Talheri caste recruited from the children of Pardeshi (north Indian) and Marwari men by local women. [n35; Enthoven II pp. 243-245, 257; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 125] For the cultivators of the Vasai coast the Gazetteer used a third frame: they belonged to a group “probably of part Gujarat descent”, including “Malis, Chavkalsis, and Somvanshi Kshatris”, which “may be roughly brought under the general term Pachkalshis”. [n41; Gazetteer XIII-1, Kunbis] Grierson put Kunbi speakers in Thana at about 350,000. [n22; LSI VII]

One more group of this period should be named. The Sonars, goldsmiths, who claimed to be Daivadnya Brahmins, said they had settled in Thana when the Portuguese conquered Goa in 1510: a migration caused by one Portuguese conquest, and a century before Bassein’s. [n35; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 63]

Arabs, Persians and the Konkani Muslims

The earliest Muslims of the coast

Islam came to this coast by sea, and earlier than any other religion now practised here except Hinduism, Jainism and Buddhism. The first contact was not friendly. In 636 or 637 an expedition sent by the governor of Bahrain and Oman plundered the coast near Thana, and the place was sacked again about 660; the Caliph Umar disapproved and forbade further attempts. [n03; Gazetteer XIII-II p. 421]

Settlement followed. The Konkani Muslims of Bombay, Enthoven recorded, were “generally known among Musalmans by the term Kufis”, and “seem, as the name shows, to have come to India from the Euphrates valley… The tradition… agree[s] that the bulk of them fled to India from the Euphrates valley about the year 700 (82 A.H.) to escape massacre at the hands of the fierce governor Hajjaj bin Yusuf.” He placed them in the same wave of Arab settlers as the Navayats of the Kanara coast. [n35; Enthoven III, “Musalmans”, Konkan section] That is tradition. The name “Kufi” is a place-based self-description, from Kufa on the Euphrates, not a fact about language. [n35; inference]

The documents begin in the tenth century, and they show a coast that welcomed traders. Al-Masudi, who visited around 915-16, named Subara (Sopara), Thana and Saymur (Chaul) as coast towns where the “Lariya” language was spoken, under the Balhara, the Rashtrakuta king, “the most friendly to Musalmans of all Indian kings”. At Chaul he found 10,000 Persian and Arab settlers. [n03; Gazetteer XIV p. 321; Gazetteer XIII-II pp. 432, 434] A copper plate of 926, of the Rashtrakuta king Indra III, found at Chinchani up the coast, names a Tajika, an Arab, called “Madhumati Sugatipa”, that is Muhammad, as governor of Sanjan. The Shilahara kings allowed Arab merchants to have mosques and a headman of their own to settle disputes. Sanjan, the Arab geographers wrote, had a Jama mosque. Reinaud’s Abulfeda places Arabs at Sopara (“Sofala”) in very early times. [n03; Dalal 2019; Gazetteer XIII-II p. 432; Gazetteer XIV, Sanjan] [n08; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 61 n.]

The 1882 Gazetteer summed up their origin: the Konkani Muslims “owe their origin to the Arab and Persian refugees, merchants, and adventurers, who settled along the coast in the eighth, ninth, and tenth centuries”. [n35; Gazetteer XIII-1 pp. 64, 216] Enthoven described later waves: settlers of the Rajput period at Anhilvada; and, “during the fourteenth, fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, when much of the Konkan was in the possession of the Bahmani and Bijapur kings, a fresh impulse… from the demand for Arab and Persian soldiers”. “As late as the Marathas the services of Arab seamen were still in demand.” [n35; Enthoven III]

The Naitas of Sopara and Bassein

One group among them can be traced to this very coast. Garcia d’Orta, the Portuguese physician and botanist of the sixteenth century, mentions the “Naitas of Bassein”, who had married local women and were rich traders when the Portuguese came. In 1882 the Muslims of Sopara were “many of them Naitas, descendants of Arab and Persian refugees”. [n37; Gazetteer XIII-1 pp. 216-217; Gazetteer XIV p. 315 n.] [n34; Gazetteer XIV p. 315] The Naitas are the same name as the Navayats, the Arab-descended Muslims of the coast further south. [n35]

The Koprad stone of 1464, beside the Samvedi headman’s house, already carried Muslim names and a Hijri date alongside its Shaka one: a single stone on which Hindu and Muslim neighbours of the Sopara villages appear together. [n03; Gazetteer XIV] At Sopara today the heritage survey of 2008 lists the Rehmatullah Alleya dargah, dated 1555 and rebuilt, and the Hazrat Diwan Shah dargah on the Sopara-Gass road; at Arnala fort there is a tomb of two Muslim saints, Shahali and Hajiali. [n40; CRIT Vol I-II] [n37; Gazetteer XIV pp. 10-11]

Why people became Muslims

The 1882 Gazetteer made a strong claim: “Though most of Thana was for over 400 years (1300-1720) nominally under Musalman rulers, their power was never thoroughly established, and, unlike Gujarat and the Deccan, Thana seems never to have been the scene of any forcible conversion of Hindus to Islam.” Muslim presence, it said, came from “traders, refugees and adventurers from Africa, Arabia and Persia” from 640 onward. [n37; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 216] Yet on the same pages it also said that the Konkani Muslims included “most of the local Hindus who were converted to Islam either by the preaching of missionaries or by the compulsion of Ahmednagar or Moghal rulers”, and named Bawa Malang of Malangad as a missionary with “considerable success”. The two statements are not reconciled. [n37; Gazetteer XIII-1 pp. 66, 217] The fairest reading is the one the notes propose: on this coast Islam spread through traders, marriage and saints; local compulsion is claimed for Ahmednagar and Mughal rule, not for the Gujarat sultans who held Bassein. [n37; inference] One Gazetteer passage claims that many of Sopara’s temples “are said to have been destroyed by the Musalmans in the beginning of the fourteenth century”; but its only footnote does not obviously support it, and the real stripping of Sopara’s temples came later, when their stones were carried off to build Portuguese churches and the walls of Bassein. [n37; Gazetteer XIV p. 323]

Under the Portuguese the position reversed. The treaty of 1534 that gave Bassein to Portugal kept 5,000 larins of Bassein’s revenue going to the mosques. Within about twelve years the Crown ordered that “3,000 pardaos formerly employed in the maintenance of the Mahomedan mosques” be given to priests “who give a helping hand towards the conversion of the infidels”. The Gazetteer noted that the Portuguese treatment “of their chief rivals, the Musalmans, was specially severe”. [n37; Danvers I p. 416; da Cunha p. 215 n.; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 201 n. 2] A traveller of about 1630 recorded that persecution had made “many Hindus, Mussulmans and Parsees abandon their homes and live in the dominion of Shah Jehan”. [n37; Gazetteer XIII-II pp. 463-464]

By 1872 the Bassein sub-division had 2,025 Muslims, 1,866 Sunni and 159 Shia; by 1881, 2,292, about 3 per cent of the population. Bassein town had 885. [n08; Gazetteer XIII-1 pp. 216-217] [n21; Gazetteer XIII-II p. 680; Gazetteer XIV p. 28]

Konkani Muslim life and speech

The Konkani Muslims were, in 1882, “the largest and most prosperous class of Thana Musalmans”. Their organisation was complete under a headman, the mukadam. The well-to-do owned two- and three-storeyed brick houses that they let to artisans. They were landholders, some holding inam lands as qazis; traders in rice, timber and salt; and sailors. They belonged to the Shafi’i school of law, unlike the Hanafi majority of the Deccan. At weddings they carried a dough lamp to a well or other water source, a custom the notes identify as Hindu in origin. The Gazetteer noticed traces of African blood among some of them, as among some Christians and Katkaris. [n35; Enthoven III; Gazetteer XIII-1 pp. 64, 216-224] [n08; Gazetteer XIII-1 pp. 216-217]

Their speech shows exactly how an immigrant community settles into a language. Enthoven: “Home speech is Konkani Marathi with a slightly peculiar pronunciation and a mixture of Urdu, Persian and Arab words.” The Gazetteer: “a dialect of Marathi”. Grierson, describing the Konkani Muslims “chiefly of Salsette, Kalyan and Bhiwandi”, said their dialect “contains a considerable amount of Hindostani words”, among them asman, heaven. [n35; Enthoven III] [n08; Gazetteer] [n22; LSI VII] Arabic remained the language of prayer; Persian and later Urdu supplied the vocabulary of religion, law and refinement; but the household spoke the Marathi of the coast.

Bohras, Khojas and Memons

Three other Muslim communities reached the district much later, as merchants from Gujarat, and their speech stayed Gujarati.

The Bohras, Enthoven wrote, were all “converts made in the eleventh century by Shiah missionaries of the Ismaili sect”, early missionaries from about 1067 having been treated kindly by the kings of Anhilvada. Their name probably comes from Gujarati vohoru, to trade, and Sanskrit vyavahāra. Their headquarters was Surat, and there were 118,307 in the Presidency in 1901. One twist of naming links them to this coast: “the class of Konkan Musalmans who take service with Europeans are known in Gujarat as Konkani Bohoras”. [n35; Enthoven I, “Bohora”, pp. 197-198]

The Khojas were Nizari Ismailis, converted from the Lohana trading caste by missionaries, by tradition Pir Sadruddin around 1430. They were “still addressed by the Lohana title of Thakkar” and used hymns in a language close to Sindhi and Kathiawar Lohana speech. There were 50,837 in 1901, some in Thana. [n35; Enthoven II, “Khojah”, pp. 217-220]

The Memons, by their own tradition, were Lohanas converted in 1421 under Sayad Yusuf-ud-din Kadiri in Sind. They moved to Cutch under Rao Khengar (1548-84), then through Kathiawar to Gujarat and Surat (1580-1680), and then to Bombay, with large flows after the famine of 1813. They were Sunni Hanafis, and the Cutchi Memons kept the Lohana custom of excluding daughters from inheritance. [n35; Enthoven III, “Meman”, pp. 51-53]

The Gazetteer of 1882 called the Bohoras and Memons “recent arrivals” who came “from Gujarat through Bombay”, with Momin weavers from Upper India; the Bohoras, Memons and Khojas preferred Gujarati shoes and dress. [n35; Gazetteer XIII-1 pp. 64, 216-224] Khojas appear among the boat-owners of the coast in the 1880s, and Muslim and Khoja merchants dealt in hides and bones. [n36; Gazetteer XIII-I pp. 301, 342] When Bohras and Khojas settled in Vasai-Virar itself is not documented; probably mostly in the twentieth century, with Bombay business. [n35; gaps]

In 2011 Muslims made up about 8.45 per cent of Vasai taluka and about 9.03 per cent of the Vasai-Virar city area (two different units, not to be mixed); Urdu was the first language of 2.72 per cent of the city. [n35; Wikipedia; census2011.co.in] [n22]

The Parsis: “adopt the language of the land”

The landing at Sanjan

No migration story on this coast is better known than the Parsis’, and none states more clearly the bargain an immigrant people struck over language.

It is told in the Kisseh-i-Sanjan, a Persian poem of the Parsi community. Fleeing Persia after the Arab conquest, the Zoroastrians went first to Ormuz, then spent nineteen years at Diu, and then landed at Sanjan, on the coast north of Vasai, ruled by a king the poem calls Jadi Rana. The Gazetteer took him for “apparently a Yadav chief of Sanjan”. [n35; Enthoven III, “Parsi”, p. 193] [n03; Gazetteer XIII-II p. 421] The Rana set conditions. The refugees must explain their religion. They must “give up the language of their mother-land of Persia and… adopt… that of the new country”. Their women must adopt Indian dress. They must disband and give up their arms. They must hold their marriages in the evening. The Dastur, their priest, agreed, and answered: “We shall be the friends of the whole of India.” [n35; Enthoven III p. 193, entry by S. J. J. Modi]

This is tradition, recorded centuries later, and the research could not open Sanjana’s Ancient Persia and the Parsis to test it further. [n35; gaps] But its language clause is borne out by everything that followed: the Parsis’ home tongue became Gujarati, the language of Sanjan’s hinterland, while Avestan and Pahlavi stayed in the fire temple.

The evidence

The evidence for Parsis on this coast is old and good. The Chinchani plates, dated between 926 and 1053, mention Parsis as subjects and refer in all five grants to the hamyamana, the anjuman, the Parsi community assembly. [n03; Dalal 2019] By the beginning of the tenth century, the Gazetteer judged, “the Parsis seem to have risen to wealth in Sanjan”, and they built fire temples at Chaul. [n36; Gazetteer XIII-II pp. 431-432] [n03] At Kanheri, in Salsette, there are Pahlavi inscriptions of 999 and 1021 by Parsis “from their names fresh arrivals from Persia”. Friar Jordanus in 1322 and Friar Odoric in 1323 found Parsis at Thana, worshipping fire and exposing their dead. [n35; Enthoven III pp. 192-195] [n37; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 66]

Sanjan was sacked. The Gazetteer believed the attacker was Alp Khan, Ala-ud-din Khalji’s governor of Gujarat, who came south between 1312 and 1318 and defeated Sanjan’s chief and “his warlike subjects the Parsis”. The Parsi tradition blames a general of Mahmud Begada, about 150 years later. The two cannot both be right, and the conflict should be shown, not resolved. Survivors went to Nargol and the hills. [n03; Gazetteer XIII-II p. 438 and n. 4; Gazetteer XIV, Sanjan]

The Portuguese century drove the Parsis away from the Thana coast. Enthoven says they were “very numerous until about 1533… when the Portuguese conquered Salsette”, and gives a tradition from B. B. Patel: the Parsis of Thana feasted the Portuguese officers, made them drunk, and slipped away to Kalyan, where a tower of silence was built in 1533. They came back under British rule, when Cowasji Rustomji Patel became Patel about 1774. [n35; Enthoven III pp. 192-195] The traveller of about 1630 who saw “Parsees” leaving for the Mughal lands confirms the general picture. [n37; Gazetteer XIII-II pp. 463-464]

In the 1880s the Parsis of the district were “Gujarat Parsi liquor-contractors and Government servants, found all over the district”. The Bassein sub-division had 27 Parsis in 1881, and Bassein town 14. [n35; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 65] [n21; Gazetteer XIII-II p. 680; Gazetteer XIV p. 28] The research found no Vasai-specific Parsi figure after that. [n35; gaps]

The Bene Israel, briefly

The Bene Israel, the Jews of the Konkan, are not a Vasai community, but they belong to the same linguistic pattern and are worth a paragraph. Their tradition says they “came to India about fourteen hundred years ago from the north” and were wrecked off Navgaon in Kolaba, where “only fourteen, seven men and seven women, were saved”; Enthoven placed this probably in the sixth century, and the Rev. J. H. Lord argued in 1907 that they may have come by trade to Chaul. Their home tongue was Marathi, with r for l and “several Arab words”; their prayers were in Hebrew. They worked as husbandmen, carpenters, soldiers and, most famously, oil-pressers, the Shanwar Teli, “Saturday oilmen”, because they kept the Sabbath. [n35; Enthoven I, “Bene-Israel”, pp. 69-71] Jewish merchants, the Radhanites, were among the great carriers to India around 900. In 1881 Bassein town had five Jews; the Bene Israel were then found in the district only at Thana and Panvel. No medieval source places them near Vasai or Sopara. [n03; Gazetteer XIII-II p. 432; Gazetteer XIV]

A pattern: the sacred tongue and the household tongue

Set the Parsis beside the Bene Israel and the Konkani Muslims and a single pattern appears, one the notes name precisely. Each immigrant group kept its liturgy in a sacred language, Avestan and Pahlavi, Hebrew, Arabic, and shifted its household speech to the local Indo-Aryan language, Gujarati for the Parsis of Sanjan, Marathi for the Bene Israel and the Konkani Muslims, leaving only a residue of loanwords and a few sound habits: r for l among the Bene Israel and in Parsi Gujarati; Urdu, Persian and Arabic words among the Konkani Muslims. The Christians of the Portuguese century, as will be seen, did exactly the same, with Latin in church and Marathi at home. [n35; inference]

The Jains: a holy place and a merchant people

Sopara in Jain memory

For the Jains, Sopara was holy long before it was a trading town in their records. Jain tradition calls it the earliest taleti, the foothill shrine, of the sacred hill of Shatrunjaya, founded by Bharata Chakravarti, eldest son of the first Tirthankara Rishabhanatha; the taleti later moved to Valabhi and then to Palitana. The Paumacariyam, the Jain Ramayana, has Lava and Kusha conquer Sopara. In the Shripal legend the king of Sopara is Mahasena. Jinaprabhasuri’s Vividha Tirtha Kalpa of the fourteenth century lists Sopara among the eighty-four most important Jain tirthas. Ashoka’s grandson Samprati is said to have protected Jain monks in the region. [n01; Shah et al. 2020] [n08; Indianetzone; Comprehensive History of Jainism] The Gazetteer recorded the tradition of an image of Rishabhadev at Sopara, and that one of the eighty-four Jain gachchhas, monastic lineages, is named after Sopara. [n37; Gazetteer XIV p. 319]

All of these are texts written long after the events they describe. One literary detail is revealing: a Jain text, as reported by Shah and colleagues, says that the liquor-making Kalals of Sopara were not excluded and could eat with others, which suggests a mixed, cosmopolitan town. [n01; Shah et al. 2020]

The evidence

Physical traces are fewer but real. Near Sopara’s Chakreshwar lake the Gazetteer recorded, among old carved stones, a Parshvanatha image, of the twenty-third Tirthankara, “probably of the tenth century or earlier”. In 2019 a research team of Siddharth Kale, Monish Shah and two Jain monks found inscriptions of the thirteenth to sixteenth centuries showing a Jain clan of the Modha nyati at Sopara. [n03; Gazetteer XIV pp. 336-337; Dalal 2019] The Bimbakhyan’s list of Bimb’s followers includes “Lad, Moda and other families”, which may be read beside this, though the notes do not connect them. [n41; Edwardes pp. 22-23] One scholar’s gloss derives the name Vasai from vas or vasti, a settlement, of Jain monks; another view links it to Jain migrants of the eleventh to fifteenth centuries. Both are speculative; the plain derivation from Sanskrit vas, to dwell, is the most defensible. [n01; Shah et al. 2020; Sandesara 1945] [n22; inference]

The living Jain centre of the area is the Munisuvrat Swami tirth at Chalpeth, Agashi, in Virar West. Its image is said to have “emerged from a pond at Chakreshwar” about two hundred years ago; a travel blog attributes the temple to a maritime merchant named Motisha Seth. These are temple and blog claims; no scholarly date for the temple was found. Newer temples include the Munisuvrat Swami Jain Derasar and Upashray on Tulinj Road, Nalasopara East. [n08; jainmandir.org; Inditales]

Who the Jains were

The Jains of this coast in historical times were traders, and their numbers were small. In 1872 the whole Thana district counted 1,823 Shravaks, Jain laity; the 1881 Bassein table counts 80 Jains. [n08; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. ~60] [n21] The Gazetteer noted that Marwari Jains grew “considerably” under the English, through traders arriving, not local converts. [n37; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 66]

Enthoven’s survey of the Vania castes explains who they were. The Oswals are “wholly Jains”. The Porvads and Shrimalis are predominantly Jain, with Hindu sections called Meshri and Jain sections called Shravak, and with Visa, Dasa and Pancha divisions. The name Shrimali belongs both to a Vani and to a Brahmin sub-caste. [n35; Enthoven III, “Vania”, pp. 422-430] The research found no evidence of when Oswal or Shrimali Jain families first reached Vasai. [n35; gaps] In 2011 Jains made up about 1.75 per cent of Vasai taluka and 1.90 per cent of the city; Marwari was the first language of about 1.6 to 1.8 per cent. [n35; Wikipedia; census mirror] [n22]

Their language followed the merchant pattern: Gujarati or Marwari at home, Marathi in the bazaar, and Sanskrit and Prakrit in the temple.

Christians before the Portuguese

The Christians of Vasai are usually said to begin in 1534. They do not quite. The Greek traveller Kosmas, about 535, describes a bishop appointed from Persia at Kalyan, and the Gazetteer thought the Christian settlements at Kalyan and Sopara were probably strengthened by Nestorian refugees from Persia and Mesopotamia in the fifth century, a hedged inference. In 1321-22 Friar Jordanus stayed fifteen days at “Supera”, found “many Nestorian Christians and a church dedicated to St Thomas”, buried there four Franciscan companions executed at Thana, baptised people and gave communion to about ninety Christians. [n03; Gazetteer XIII-II p. 420 n. 4; Gazetteer XIV pp. 321-322] [n37; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 200]

Those Christians left no descendants that anyone can trace. “No trace seems to be left either of the Nestorian or of the Latin converts,” the Gazetteer wrote. It added a tantalising footnote: “One recent report mentions a Christian village in Bassein that claims to be older than the Portuguese. No confirmation of this statement has been received.” [n34; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 200 n. 7]

The Portuguese and the making of new communities

Who came, and what they did

The Portuguese took Bassein from the Sultan of Gujarat by the treaty of December 1534. The conquerors themselves were few. In 1634, a century later, Bassein held about 400 Portuguese families, 200 Indian Christian families and 1,800 slaves. [n37; section 3, citing n04] They were soldiers, officials, landholders and priests; their slaves, many from Africa, were “made Christians and sometimes raised to be priests”. [n37; Gazetteer XIII-II p. 460 n. 3] They were not a settler people in the way the Parsis or Vadvals had been: when Bassein fell in 1739, “all the Portuguese ‘reinóis’ and ‘descendentes’ fled or were expelled… leaving only the Indian converted population behind”. [n34; Mendiratta pp. 216-218]

What they made was not a colony of Portuguese but a set of new Indian communities. The story of how and why the conversions happened is told at length in chapters K2 and T4 of this book; here only its outline is needed, as the cause of the communities and their speech.

The Franciscans came first. Fr António do Porto built an orphanage for forty boys at Agashi in 1535, “the first place of this district visited by Franciscan missionaries”; between 1534 and 1552 he is said to have destroyed two hundred temples, made over ten thousand converts and built twelve churches. The Jesuits followed, with a college at Bassein from 1548. Between about 1570 and 1620 they and the Franciscans converted “a majority of the population” of Salsette and of the Bassein and Agashi subdivisions; “whole villages were converted, normally following the conversion of the respective ‘matará’ or headman”. In 1588, 9,400 people were baptised in the church of Bassein alone. [n34; da Cunha pp. 159-160, 234; Mendiratta p. 211] [n37; Gazetteer XIII-II p. 461]

The means were mixed, and the sources disagree about the mixture. The Jesuit Gonçalo Rodrigues wrote from Bassein in 1558, with unusual frankness, that “those who come to conversion are commonly the poorest and most needy, and are converted more drawn and moved by hope of some interest and remedy of life than by preaching”, and described the “snares of human things” the Jesuits set: houses, cheap land, clothes, rice until the first harvest, seed and plough oxen. Leading men were baptised in grand processions with the governor and nobility, followed by banquets. Portuguese landlords at first resisted conversion of their tenants and later competed for it; the Census of 1931 explained the Portuguese surnames of the coast by saying that in many villages “every tenant in the village was baptized and given the surname of the owner of the village”. Laws restricted Hindu worship and, after 1580, ended its toleration. The Goa Inquisition had a commissary at Bassein. The 1882 Gazetteer’s own verdict was that “force may have been used in some cases, but the Portuguese accounts seem to show that persuasion and interest were the chief means of conversion”. Community traditions, recorded in 1859 and 1882, speak of flesh thrown into wells and biscuit and pork forced into mouths. Both sides are told in full in chapter K2. [n34; Documenta Indica IV; Census 1931 pp. 363-364; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 201 n. 2] [n37; sections 2-4]

What matters for this chapter is the result. Converts kept their castes. “In matters of marriage the lower classes keep to their old caste distinctions,” the Gazetteer recorded. The Christians of Thana in 1882 were drawn from “Brahmans, Prabhus, Pachkalshis, Charkalshis [Chavkalshis], Sonars, Khatris, Bhandaris, Kharpatils, Kunbis, Kumbhars, Nhavis, Dhobis, Kolis, Bhois, Mhars, Chambhars”; “the bulk of them are Bhandaris, Kolis and Kunbis”. [n41; Gazetteer XIII-1 pp. 205-206] [n34; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 200] So the Portuguese century did not make one Christian community. It made a Christian branch of almost every community already on the coast, each keeping its village, its work and, as will be seen, its speech.

The Kuparis: Christian Samvedis

The best period evidence for the Kuparis’ origin is a pair of sentences in the 1882 Gazetteer. At Sopara, “the husbandmen… are Christians, many of them converted Samvedi Brahmans”. At Agashi, Vatar and Koprad, “about eighty-five families of Samvedi Brahmans, fifteen families of Pachkalshis, and about seventy-five families of Native Christians originally Samvedis and Pachkalshis” dried ripe plantains for market. [n34; Gazetteer XIV p. 315; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 395] The same Gazetteer names the “garden or agar of Sopara” as Bolinj, Koprad and Umbrala in the north, Nirmal and Vagholi in the west, Gass in the south and Sopara and Mardesh in the centre, and says that the dried-plantain trade was done “only in the villages of Agashi, Vagholi, Vatar, Bolinj, Koprad, Nala, Umrale, Rajodi, and Murdes”, nine of the thirteen villages the Kupari community today names as its own. [n40; Gazetteer XIV pp. 314-315; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 292]

A modern sociologist’s summary says that the first converts were Samvedi Brahmins settled near the Vaitarna between Agashi and Nalasopara, and that they were “the only Bassein group to receive Jesuit education”. [n34; Rodricks, Sahapedia] The historian Mendiratta adds that “rural” Bassein and Salsette Catholics in general came to be labelled “samvedi” East Indians, which suggests that the word broadened beyond Brahmin descent. [n34; Mendiratta p. 218]

There is an opposing voice. Gerson da Cunha, a Goan Brahmin scholar in Bombay, wrote in 1876 that the Catholics of Bassein were “pretty numerous clans of different types of the Kolis and Bhandaris”, with a European-descended mixed element, and that “there is no descendant of any high-class native amongst them”. Mendiratta reads his tone as contempt rooted in the Goan-versus-local rivalry of his day. Modern historians agree with him on one point: most converts were farmers, fishers and toddy-tappers. The Census of 1931 judged that at least 90 per cent of the Presidency’s Indian Christians descended from the “Sudra section of Hindus”. [n34; da Cunha 1876 pp. 250-251; Mendiratta p. 234; Census 1931] So “converted Samvedi Brahmins” has real support from the 1880s for the northern villages, but it describes part of the Christian community, not all of it. [n34; inference]

Both groups were farmers. The Samvedis were already gardeners, not priests, in 1882, so even a Brahmin origin for the Kuparis means a landed farming community. The Hindu and Christian Samvedi households of the 1880s lived in matching tile-roofed, wood-walled houses, wore ornaments with the same names (mugdya, gāthe), wore red broadcloth caps, and guarded new mothers against the same spirit, Sati or Sathi, on the fifth or sixth night after a birth. [n34; Gazetteer XIII-1 pp. 82, 204-205, 210-211] Conversion divided the community by religion, but not by village, work or household custom.

The name “Kupari”

The name itself is a puzzle. A search of the full text of fifteen primary and early sources, the 1882 Gazetteers, Enthoven, Grierson, the censuses of 1901, 1911 and 1931, da Cunha’s books and four volumes of Jesuit letters, found no “Kupari”, “Kumpari” or “Cupari” at all. The Christians of Bassein appear in them as “Native Christians”, “Portuguese Christians”, “Firgi” and “Kinstav”. The first attestations found are a community blog of 2007 and Wikipedia. [n34; headline finding]

The derivation most often given is from the Portuguese compadre, the godfather of one’s child or co-parent, with feminine comadre, “Kumari”. No period or scholarly source states it, but it fits the evidence: the Christians’ own vocabulary used Portuguese godparent words. At baptism, the Gazetteer recorded in 1882, the child was taken to church by its godfather, padrinho, and godmother, madrinha; in 1905 Grierson heard padron, godfather, and madron, godmother, in the Christians’ Marathi. [n34; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 210; LSI VII] A second folk derivation, from kūp, a well, “those who drank from the well”, circulates and is offensive to many; it echoes the tradition that converts were made by polluting wells, but there is no evidence for it as the origin of the word. [n34; Talk:Kupari; inference]

The Vadval Christians

The second great Christian group of Vasai descends from the converted Pachkalshis and Chaukalshis, and from Bhandaris, Kharpatils and Kolis who were counted with them. The Gazetteer of 1882 names the Christian Vadval cart-villages, Chulna, Manikpur, Gokhivra, Sandor and Barhanpur, and calls some of the lower-class Christians, “as the Vadvals”, “among the most skilful cultivators in the district”. The Bassein Christians, “originally Brahmans, Bhandaris, Chavkalshis, Pachkalshis, Kharpatils and Kolis”, were “the most hardworking and skilful husbandmen… grow sugarcane and plantains”, and had “turned the light sandy country about Bassein into an evergreen garden”. [n41; Gazetteer XIII-1 pp. 97-98, 205-206, ch. IV]

So the word Vadval, in the 1880s, already meant Christians as well as Hindus. [n41; inference] Their villages lie in a belt south of the Kupari belt, around the old fort: Sandor, Papdi, Remedy, Merces, Pali, Manickpur, Chulne, Gokhivare. Sandor’s people are described as “predominantly of the Vadval sub-ethnic group” speaking the Vadvali dialect. [n40; Part B; Wikipedia, Sandor] A modern summary of East Indian sources says that Christian Vadval women wore red saris and the men red caps, and that a village council fined those who shirked their duties at deaths and weddings. [n41; Sahapedia] The 1935 Marathi editor noted that “some Christians even now call themselves Panchkalshi Christians”. [n41; Naik 1935]

Koli Christians, Agri Christians, and the way back

On the shore, conversion made Christian Kolis. The Gazetteer recorded in 1882 that “the Uraps and other Christian Kolis in Bassein… are professional fishermen, but the mass of the sea-fishing population are Son Kolis”. The fishermen among the Christians wore red broadcloth caps, like their Hindu Koli neighbours. [n08; Gazetteer fisheries section] [n34; Gazetteer XIII-1 pp. 204-205] Christian Kolis are found today in Mumbai, Bandra, Gorai, Vasai and Agashi; they share the shakarpuda engagement and other customs with Hindu Kolis, and some revere the goddess Ekvira. [n08; Wikipedia, Koli Christians]

The line between Christian and Hindu was crossed in both directions, and this coast is unusual in how well the return journeys are documented.

After the Maratha conquest of 1739, the Gazetteer recorded as “local story” that “numbers of the Christians were… purified by Brahmans and admitted into Hinduism”, among them probably the Bhandaris known as Kirpals. The Census of 1911 said the Kirpal Bhandaris “were forcibly converted to Christianity by the Portuguese, but soon after the conquest of Bassein by the Marathas (about 1739) they were given the choice of reverting to Hinduism, which they accepted”. Gerson da Cunha says that the Peshwa Madhavrao (who ruled 1761-72) instituted a tax to support Brahmins who would purify the native Christians before receiving them back into their castes. [n34; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 202; Census 1911 para 136; da Cunha 1876 p. 149] [n37; Gazetteer XIV p. 34]

Then came the cholera years. In 1818-20, and again in 1828, “some of the Christian Kolis, finding that they suffered as much as their Hindu neighbours, took to propitiating the goddess of cholera, and either left or were driven from the Church. Some of these people with the help of a Palshi Brahman became Hindus, and are known as Uraps or Varaps.” Another source of 1838 gave a different cause, “an attempt to extend the priestly prerogative”. [n37; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 203 and n. 3] The details are precise. In 1820 the Christian Agris of Bassein were readmitted to Hinduism by Ramchandra Baba Joshi, a Palshe Brahmin, for a fee of 1,200 rupees; his caste put him out for a time, and his successor Bhai Makund Joshi was also outcasted. On 12 November 1828 another group was readmitted by Vithal Hari Naik Vaidya, a Palshe Brahmin of Bassein. [n08; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 117] [n37; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 117] They were called Urap or Varap Agris, also “Nave Marathas”, New Marathas. The Gazetteer thought “Urap” might come from Marathi orapne, to sear with a hot iron, for a purifying ordeal; Enthoven added Persian urf, alias, and “Europe” as other guesses. The only sign of their Christian past by the 1880s was their surnames: Gomes, Soz (Souza), Fernan, Frutad (Furtado) and Minez (Menezes). They ranked below the other Agris, who would neither marry nor eat with them. [n08; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 117] [n35; Enthoven I pp. 9-14] The villages where they lived, Navapur, Umela, Juchandra, Achole, Virar and Kolwad, are Agri villages, not Kupari ones. [n40; section 1] The Katkaris’ Varap division and the Kirpal Bhandaris carry the same history in their names. [n35; Enthoven II] [n08]

One more number tells the scale. In the cholera of 1820-21, “about 3000 Christians rejoined the Hindu fold”, which a British report blamed on the lack of “respectable ministers”. [n34; Mendiratta p. 220] Those who stayed, however, were the majority, and they survived the century without Portuguese rule because the Portuguese had trained a native priesthood. Under a Vicar General at Kurla, “the native or… Kanarin vicars managed the churches and kept the bulk of the people from forsaking Christianity”. [n34; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 203] [n37; inference]

Names for the Christians, and the East Indians of 1887

The Christians had many names, and each was a statement. The upper classes called themselves “Firgi”, from Firangi, Frank. The lower classes “call themselves by their caste name”. Hindus called them “Firangis and more often Kiristans”. Grierson heard their speech called “Kinstav”. The word Kanarin for native clergy was “considered a term of reproach”. Europeans called them “Portuguese” or “Salsette Christians”. [n34; Gazetteer XIII-1 pp. 199-200, 203 n. 1; LSI VII]

On 26 May 1887 about thirty leaders of the community met in Bombay to change their designation from “Bombay Portuguese” to “Bombay East-Indian”, and founded the Bombay East Indian Association with a weekly paper. The new name recalled a century and a half as subjects of the East India Company and a claim to be “sons of the soil”, and set them apart from the Goan Catholics who were arriving in Bombay. The Christians of Bassein and Salsette were included under it as “rural” or “samvedi” East Indians. [n34; Mendiratta pp. 234-236] Elsie Baptista’s book of 1967, which could not be read for this chapter, is cited for the five East Indian groups: Kulbis, Samavedi Christians “commonly called Kuparis”, Koli Christians, Wadvals and Salsette Christians. [n34; Catholic Connect, citing Baptista] So the Kuparis and Christian Vadvals are not an 1887 creation; they are older village communities that the 1887 umbrella and later writing fixed in print. [n34; inference]

How the Christians’ speech formed

What did the converts speak? Not Portuguese, for the most part.

The 1882 Gazetteer is clear: “Their home tongue is Marathi, very little different from the Marathi spoken by the Kunbis and Kolis of the district. A few know and a considerable number understand an ungrammatical Portuguese, and among some of the higher families and in the Khatri ward of Thana town Portuguese is the home speech. Latin is the language of the Church.” Sermons were in Portuguese or Marathi, and prayer books were printed in both. [n34; Gazetteer XIII-1 pp. 204, 207] Grierson’s verdict on the specimen sent to him, in 1905, was even blunter. The dialect of Thana’s native Roman Catholics, about 26,600 people, “usually called Kinstav by the Hindus and Musalmans”, had “adopted some Portuguese words, such as pay, father, may, mother, mass, … padron, god-father, madron, god-mother”, and the English word estate; but it was “nothing else than the usual Marathi of the district”. [n34; LSI VII]

There was also a creole. The Portuguese of the Northern Province, from Daman to Chaul and Korlai, spoke Indo-Portuguese varieties that scholars call Norteiro, described by Dalgado in 1906; Wikipedia says the people of Bassein had a distinct Norteiro creole. Mendiratta writes that in the 1880s Portuguese proper was a home language only for well-to-do families in Bandra, Thane, Bassein and Kurla, but that “Indo-Portuguese creoles were more widespread in Salsette and Bassein… and survived until later”, so that “the majority of non-educated East-Indians from Salsette and Bassein would communicate either in Marathi or in Indo-Portuguese creole”. Portuguese schools tried by the bishops of Daman between 1887 and 1925 “don’t appear to have been long-lasting”. [n22; Cardoso 2006; Wikipedia, Norteiro] [n34; Mendiratta p. 241] No source describes the structure of a Bassein creole or when it died. Its relatives survive elsewhere: Korlai, near Chaul, had about 760 speakers in 2010, Daman about 4,000, Diu about 180. [n22; Open Magazine] The 2024 dictionary paper calls Kadodi’s Indo-Portuguese ingredient “(now extinct)”. [n22; Kumar et al. 2024]

So the Christian speech of the coast formed in layers. Each convert group kept the Marathi of its own caste and village: the Kuparis the Gujarati-coloured Marathi of the Samvedis, the Christian Vadvals the Vadvali of the Pachkalshis, the Christian Kolis Koli speech. On top of that each added a Portuguese layer, and the notes make a sharp observation about which words it contained: father, mother, Mass, godfather, godmother. “The Portuguese strand entered through the parish, not through settlement or trade.” [n34; inference] Later lists add household things. The East Indian or “Vasai Marathi” dialect, as a 2024 newspaper report gives it, says mai and pai for mother and father where Marathi says āī and bābā; kadair for chair, from Portuguese cadeira; zanel for window, from janela; sukhala for a toast; and Ramacha Pass for the Palm Sunday procession, from ramo, branches, and passo, steps. [n13; Free Press Journal, 14 Mar 2024] The Kuparis’ Kadodi, as it stands today, is best described as a Samvedi base with Portuguese vocabulary and later Marathi levelling. [n34; Takeaway, §4]

Grierson’s separate entries make one more point. In 1905 he listed Samvedi (Hindu, 2,700) and Kinstav (Christian, 26,600) as different things. The Kupari dialect did not exist as a named category in his survey; the Christian Samvedis were counted with the Kinstav. Whether they kept the Samvedi s to h shift while the Kinstav specimen did not is a question that only fieldwork could answer. [n34; inference] [n22; inference]

The Christians also read their own literature in the coast’s speech. The Christa Purana, a Marathi verse life of Christ that the Gazetteer dated to 1659, was “still well known and much read by the people” in 1882, “in the home dialect of the Thana Son or Sea Kolis”; and Grierson printed a third Koli specimen from “the old Puran by Vas de Guimaraens”, another Christian Marathi text. [n34; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 207 n. 1] [n22; LSI VII] Music was part of the church’s language too: each church in 1882 paid one or two music masters who played the violin; at Nirmal the parish schoolteacher played the violin in church; the Agashi parish school taught Portuguese, arithmetic, singing, doctrine and music. [n34; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 207; Gazetteer XIV pp. 1-2, 293]

Today these dialects are in danger. The newspaper report of 2024 calls the East Indian dialect “declining, if not in danger of extinction”, spoken mostly by older people; an East Indian dictionary is being compiled under the Mobai Gaothan Panchayat, edited by Gleason Barretto and Amarylisa Gonsalves, and singers write songs in it. [n13; Free Press Journal, 2024] The Kadodi dictionary of 2024 claims to be the first machine-translation work involving the dialect. [n22; Kumar et al. 2024]

After 1739: the Peshwas’ Brahmins

A new ruling class

When Chimaji Appa’s army took Bassein in May 1739, the coast changed masters, and a new community came with the new masters. The Gazetteer is direct about it: after the Maratha conquest, priestly Chitpavan and Deshastha Brahmins “settled as priests, and to a great extent ousted the Palshis and other older classes of priests”. Writing of Bassein itself, it said: “Of Maratha power the chief relics are priestly Brahmans of the Konkanasth and Deshasth classes.” [n35; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 63]

It gives one family as an example. Two Kudale Brahmins migrated to the north Konkan about 1728, a decade before the conquest. One settled at Thana and was made a Sardar. The other, Yeshwantrao, settled in Bassein, and his son became Atrekar, a karkun or clerk, of Bassein fort. [n35; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 63 n. 2] The Gazetteer describes the “Pandharpeshas”, a privileged class of land-holding Brahmins and Prabhus, as relics of Maratha rule, along with Maratha fort guards such as the Raos of Murbad and the Karhada Kadams of Panvel. [n35; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 63]

The new rulers also set out to restore a Hindu population. “Under the Marathas Bassein, with the name of Bajipur… To restore a Hindu population grants of rent-free land were offered, and a tax was levied for the support of Brahmans who were brought to purify the Christians and make them fit to take their place in their old castes.” Da Cunha added that these measures “brought down a crowd of Hindus from Maharashtra and Gujarat, especially Prabhus”. [n37; Gazetteer XIV p. 34] [n34; da Cunha 1876 p. 149] The Sarsubhedar of Bassein, Shankarji Keshav Phadke, built eight temples at Nirmal around 1750, where the Portuguese had destroyed the temple and its linga, and the site was purified and given the footprints of Dattatreya; he is also credited with the Vajreshwari temple, built to commemorate the victory over the Portuguese. “On the decline of Portuguese power (1740) Brahmanism revived, and except those that are more modern, most of the present Hindu temples date from the eighteenth century.” [n37; Gazetteer XIV pp. 292-293; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 66] [n03; Gazetteer XIV, Vajrabai]

The Marathas, all the same, left the Christians their faith. “The Marathas allowed them the freest exercise of their religion, their processions and festivals were respected.” The French traveller Anquetil du Perron found Agashi in 1760 with “lanes full of people going to church as freely as in a Christian country”. [n34; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 203; Gazetteer XIV pp. 1-2] The religious map that resulted, with Brahmin officials, rebuilt temples and Christian parishes side by side in villages like Nirmal and Agashi, is the one the 1882 Gazetteer recorded. [n34; inference]

Who the newcomers were

Enthoven’s survey gives the self-accounts. The Chitpavans told of fourteen shipwrecked foreigners burned on a pyre (chita) by Parashuram and restored to life, hence “pure from the pyre”. Scholars derived the name from Chiplun, in Ratnagiri. They began to call themselves “Konkanasths” about 1715, as the Peshwa Balaji Vishwanath, himself a Chitpavan, rose to power; there were 113,605 of them in 1901. The Deshasths, Enthoven wrote, “appear to be the earliest Brahman settlers who migrated to the south of the Vindhyas”, with Yajurvedi, Rigvedi and Atharvan divisions. A Deshasth would not invite a Chitpavan to a shraddha ceremony; a Chitpavan had no such objection. [n35; Enthoven I pp. 243-245] The 1872 census counted 585 Karhada Brahmins in Thana district, another Deccan-Konkan group. [n35; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 63] [n08; Gazetteer pp. 79-80]

How many such families stayed on the Vasai coast, and where, the notes cannot say; the research found no specific information on Chitpavan or Deshastha inamdar or temple-priest families in Vasai. The Samvedi Wikipedia page assumes that some Chitpavan, Karhade, Devrukhe and Gujarati Shukla Yajurvedi surnames in Vasai arrived under Peshwa rule, but it is an assumption. [n08; gaps; Wikipedia, Samvedi] One caution about surnames: Vartak, a common Pachkalshi surname of Virar and Agashi, is shared with a Chitpavan Brahmin clan from Kelshi, so a surname alone does not show community. [n41; inference]

The cost to the old priests

For the Palshes, the arrival was a fall. They had fixed the auspicious hours for every caste of the coast; now Chitpavans refused to dine with them, and their rivals spread the palā-āshi, “flesh-eater”, slur. The Palshes defended their rank with Peshwa-era sanads, and in Enthoven’s day the Smarta Shankaracharya had “recently recognised them”. Their own self-account came to stress Paithan and Yajnavalkya, the Deccan and the orthodox sage, against the Gazetteer’s case for a Gujarat origin. [n35; Enthoven I pp. 248-249; inference] [n08; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 81 n.] The Palshes’ later role as the priests who readmitted Christian Agris to Hinduism, in 1820 and 1828, at the cost of being outcasted by their own caste, shows them still serving the coast’s lower castes when the new elite would not. [n35]

Language

The new Brahmins brought the Marathi of the Deccan and of Ratnagiri, and with it the machinery of Maratha government, written in the Modi script, the cursive in which the Peshwa records, some four crore pages of them, were kept from the seventeenth to the mid-nineteenth century. [n22; Wikipedia, Modi script; FPJ] For the small dialects of the coast, the Peshwa century was the first time that the language of power was a standard Marathi rather than Sanskrit, Persian or Portuguese. Grierson’s “Konkan Standard”, the Marathi of about 2.5 million people from Daman to Rajapur, was by his time the norm against which the Samvedi, Vadval and Mangela dialects were judged “incorrect”. [n22; LSI VII]

The Lad Vanis from Cambay, 1760s

The next newcomers came by sea, and in a hurry. “About the middle of the eighteenth century (1760-1766) a considerable number of Cambay Vanis, chiefly of the Lad sub-division, and with them several Gujarat Brahmans settled in Supara, Bassein, and other coast towns to avoid the exactions of Momin Khan II (1748-1783).” [n35; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 63] Momin Khan II was the Nawab of Cambay, the old port at the head of the Gulf of Khambhat; his exactions drove its traders down the coast to the Maratha-held towns that had, only twenty years earlier, been Portuguese.

The Gazetteer recorded them in detail. “Of about 120 families of Lad Vanis about forty are in Thana, thirty-five in Supara, and the rest in Bassein, Agashi, Nala, Papdi, and Dahanu.” They were shopkeepers, moneylenders, superior landholders and merchants. [n35; Gazetteer XIII-1 pp. 112-113] Sopara’s traders in the 1880s were Gujarat Vanis of the Lad subdivision who came from Cambay about 1760. [n37; Gazetteer XIV p. 315] The Vanis of Papdi, two miles from Bassein, bought the areca nuts of the Bassein gardens wholesale and sorted them into six classes, phulbardi, tambdi, chikni, lavangchuri, pandhri and dagdi or kapkadi; “Bassein is famous for its phulbardi betelnuts”. At Nala, Agashi, Sopara and Navghar bills of exchange (hundis) could be cashed up to a thousand pounds. [n21; Gazetteer XIII-I pp. 298-300] [n40; Gazetteer XIII-1, trade chapter]

Were they the first Gujarati traders on this coast? Almost certainly not, and the Gazetteer said so itself. “Masudi’s statement that when he wrote (915) the Lar language was spoken in the coast towns as far south as Chaul makes it probable that from very early times Lad Vanis had settled along the Thana coast for purposes of trade. But it would seem that most of the present families are late settlers.” [n35; Gazetteer XIII-1 pp. 112-113] The Lads take their name from Lat-desh, the old name of south Gujarat, the Lar country of the Arab geographers. In another volume the Gazetteer went further: “the trade tongue of the Thana ports was Gujarati, and the leading traders were probably Gujarat Vanis”. [n35; Enthoven III p. 428] [n36; Gazetteer XIII-II pp. 431-432] That is the Gazetteer’s inference from Masudi’s “Lariya”, not a document; but it suggests that Gujarati had been heard in Sopara’s harbour for a thousand years before the families of 1760 arrived. A Kutchi sea manual of the seventeenth century describes a regular cargo route through the Bolinj-Sopara creek. [n36; Shah et al. 2020]

The Lad Vanis kept their own ways and adapted others. They had Visa and Dasa divisions; their family goddess was Ashapuri of Ashmai near Petlad; their priests were Khedawal Brahmins; their women were “noted for their taste in dress”. They were Vaishnavas of the Vallabhacharya sect, yet they “observe Shaiv fasts and feasts”, which the Gazetteer explained by their “close connection with Maratha Brahmans”. They had stopped giving daughters to the Thana Lad Vanis, and took brides from Cambay, Jambusar and Bombay. [n35; Enthoven III p. 428; Gazetteer XIII-1 pp. 112-113]

And their language followed the merchant pattern exactly. “They speak Gujarati among themselves and Marathi with others.” [n35; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 113] The notes rightly see them as the best documentary precedent for the later Gujarati and Jain presence in Nallasopara and Virar: they came by the Cambay-Bassein sea route 120 years before the railway. [n35; inference]

The Marwaris, after 1818

The British took the Bassein country in 1818, and with British rule came a new kind of trader. The Marwari Vanis, the Gazetteer said, “are believed to have come to Thana from Rajputana or Marwar, almost entirely since the British conquest. Their usual route has been through Gujarat and Bombay, and since the railway has been opened, they have come in great numbers.” [n35; Gazetteer XIII-1 pp. 64, 113-114]

The Gazetteer gave a precise economic cause for their first movement. It “followed the very liberal and general reduction of rent… between 1835 and 1838. The reductions left a large margin of profit to the landholder and the Marvadis came, advanced money at from 100 to 200 per cent to the husbandmen, and sold them up.” In 1846 the Collector, Mr Law, noticed that “the thrifty avaricious Marvadi had begun to settle even in the remotest villages”. They were Jains; they kept weekly fasts in Bhadrapad; and, the Gazetteer complained, they “carried their gains to their own country the Konkan benefiting nothing”. [n35; Gazetteer XIII-1 pp. 113-114] Those judgements, “avaricious” among them, are the colonial author’s, and are quoted here as his. The Gazetteer’s picture of Varlis who had pledged their land and worked it for their creditors in the rains belongs to the same decades. [n08; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 183]

Beside the Marwaris came other merchant groups from the north-west. The Lohanas and Bhatias, from Cutch and north Gujarat, were “grain and cloth merchants in most of the leading towns”. The owners of the coast’s boats in the 1880s were “Gujarat and Marwar Vanis, Brahmans, Agris, Bhatias, Lingayats, Bhandaris, Machhis, Kolis, Khojas”. [n35; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 64] [n36; Gazetteer XIII-1 pp. 342-343] The Gazetteer’s list of “recent settlers” in 1882 reads like a forecast: Upper India Pardeshis working as messengers and servants, Kachis as market gardeners, halwais from Sindh, Gujarat Brahmins and traders, Marwar Vanis and Lingayat Vanis. [n35; Gazetteer XIII-1 pp. 64-65]

The Marwaris, like the Lad Vanis, kept their home speech, Marwari, among themselves, and used Marathi with their customers. In 2011 about 1.6 per cent of Vasai taluka, and 1.8 per cent of the city, gave Marwari as their first language. [n35; Wikipedia] [n22]

Gujarati on the northern coast

By 1900 Gujarati was not only a merchants’ tongue on this coast. Grierson observed that in the north of Thana district Marathi was “gradually superseded by Gujarati”. [n22; LSI VII] The Warli of the extreme north already spoke a form of Gujarati; the Mangelas, Machhis and Sorathias spoke Gujarati at home or mixed it heavily into their Marathi; and the Samvedi and Vadval dialects carried Gujarati forms. [n35; synthesis] The Vasai coast sat on the boundary between two great languages, and its small dialects are the record of centuries spent on that boundary.

The great migration: 1980 to 2011

From villages to a city

For most of the twentieth century the Vasai coast grew slowly. The Bassein taluka had 61,089 people in 1872, 68,967 in 1881, 79,978 in 1911 and 133,523 in 1951. [n21] Then Bombay’s growth reached it. The suburban railway, the price of housing in the city, and the planners’ decisions turned the garden villages into dormitory towns. The population of the Vasai-Virar area went from 219,868 in 1981 to 365,480 in 1991, 693,350 in 2001 and 1,221,233 in 2011 inside the municipal area, or 1,343,402 for the whole taluka. [n08; Wikipedia, Vasai-Virar] [n35; Wikipedia, Vasai taluka] The 1991 census showed a 150 per cent increase for the whole Vasai-Virar area, and 290 per cent for Vasai, Virar, Sopara and Sandor, which a TIFR study attributed to people commuting to work in Mumbai. [n35; TIFR] The 1970s regional plan had treated the area as a green zone apart from the station areas; in 1991 CIDCO replaced the MMRDA as planning authority; Mumbai’s housing prices pushed lower- and middle-income families north to Virar, Vasai and Nalasopara; and the quadrupling of the Borivali-Virar line was finished in 2007. [n37; TIFR; manickpur.com; Wikipedia] In 2009 the Vasai-Virar City Municipal Corporation was formed from four municipal councils and fifty-three villages. [n40; VVCMC]

Who came

The newcomers came from everywhere, but the census of 2011 shows their weight. In the Vasai-Virar city area, the first languages of the population were: Marathi 46.49 per cent, Hindi 26.58 per cent, Gujarati 9.26 per cent, Bhojpuri 3.10 per cent, Urdu 2.72 per cent and Marwari 1.78 per cent. In the larger unit of Vasai taluka they were Marathi 49.97 per cent, Hindi 24.78, Gujarati 8.60, Bhojpuri 2.92, Urdu 2.52 and Marwari 1.64. [n22; Wikipedia, Vasai-Virar; Wikipedia, Vasai taluka] [n35] These figures come through Wikipedia and census mirror sites; the primary Census tables (C-16 for mother tongue, D-series for migration) were not opened, and the notes warn against mixing the two units. A Wikipedia page for Virar gives Gujarati at 28.30 per cent and Hindi at 15.31, which contradicts the city-wide figures and probably refers to a different area or tabulation. [n22; gaps and conflicts]

By religion, Vasai taluka in 2011 was 78.05 per cent Hindu, 8.45 per cent Muslim, 8.25 per cent Christian, 2.53 per cent Buddhist and 1.75 per cent Jain; the city area, 77.16 per cent Hindu, 9.03 per cent Muslim, 8.28 per cent Christian and 1.90 per cent Jain. [n35]

Where in India the Hindi and Bhojpuri speakers came from, and when, cannot be stated from the notes. No primary study separating migrants by state was found, and no dated study of South Indian migration into Vasai-Virar. [n35; gaps] [n06; gaps] Hindi and Bhojpuri together point to Uttar Pradesh and Bihar, which is how the city’s growth is usually described; Gujarati and Marwari to Gujarat and Rajasthan; but these are inferences from language, not migration records. One small piece of fieldwork shows how such migration works on the ground. A study by the Tata Institute of Social Sciences found, in Sai Society in Nallasopara, a settlement of scrap dealers and recyclers who all came from one village in eastern Uttar Pradesh. [n08; TISS, “Unsettling the periphery”] [n35] That is chain migration, village to village, the same pattern by which Lad Vani families and Marwari moneylenders had come to this coast a century and more before.

The notes make a historical observation worth keeping. The Gazetteer’s 1882 list of “recent settlers”, Pardeshi servants from Upper India, Gujarati traders and Marwari moneylenders, is the same cast of migrants, 140 years earlier. The modern UP and Bihar and South Indian arrivals are a third great wave, after the sea-borne Gujarat wave of merchants and the Maratha-era wave of Konkanasth and Deshastha Brahmins. [n35; inference]

What became of the old dialects

In 1905 Grierson could treat the Konkan Standard Marathi of about 2.5 million speakers as the language of this coast, with a fringe of small, mixed dialects and a Gujarati edge in the north. By 2011, Marathi of any kind was the first language of fewer than half the people of the Vasai-Virar city. [n22; inference] The units are different, and the two figures should not be put on one graph; but the direction is plain.

For the small dialects, the pressure came from two sides at once. From above, the standard Marathi of school, newspaper, office and film, the “grinding of the Local Board school and the newspaper” that Enthoven’s author had noticed in 1920. From around, Hindi, the language of the trains, the markets and the new housing societies. The notes record what remains. Kadodi has perhaps 50,000 to 60,000 speakers and a dictionary; Vadvali about 2,500 by an old count and a “threatened” rating; the East Indian dialect is “declining, if not in danger of extinction”; the Son Koli patois was already “obsolescent” a century ago. [n22; n41; n13; n35]

The revival has been led by the communities themselves. The Vadval Samaj records and prints its oral songs. Paul Rumav collected Samvedi folk songs and proverbs; Naresh Naik wrote the grammar. The Mobai Gaothan Panchayat is compiling the East Indian dictionary. A Kadodi magazine has a website. Speakers of Kadodi built a dictionary that taught a machine to translate it. [n41; indiandistricts] [n20; mr.wikipedia] [n13; FPJ] [n22; Kumar et al. 2024] A community site for the Chaukalshi Vadvals and the Somvanshi Kshatriya Samaj Sangh, whose sports festival has reached its forty-second edition, keep an identity alive even as the spoken form recedes. [n41; vadvalsamaj.com]

How the languages of the coast were made

Step back from the communities one by one, and the colonial word lists, for all their faults, show four patterns by which the speech of this coast formed. The patterns are a synthesis made for this book’s research notes from the Gazetteer, Enthoven and Grierson; they are an argument from evidence, not a published finding. [n35; section 10]

First: a Bhil-related older speech under a Marathi surface. The Katkari, Varli and Thakar dialects were, in Grierson’s words, Bhil dialects that had turned Marathi. What survives of the older layer is a handful of words (nāngne, to see; suna, dog; him, snake), a habit of clipping (kotja for kothe jātos), and, in the far north, a Gujarati instead of a Marathi surface. The genetics of the Warli, with the highest AASI ancestry measured in the region, suggest that this pattern is very old: an ancient people taking up a new language while keeping its ancestry. [n22; LSI VII] [n35; Enthoven II-III] [n01; Debortoli et al. 2020]

Second: a Gujarat-linked, Gujarati-mixed Marathi. The Vadval, Phudgi, Samvedi and Mangela dialects, the household words of the Prabhus and Panchkalshis, and the speech of the northern Varli all carry Gujarati forms. Grierson thought the first three kept “many of the characteristic features of Gujarati Bhili”. Their markers are sound shifts: ch to s or h, s and sh to h (hona for gold, hukeḷī for sukeḷī), masculine nouns in -o. These dialects fit, without proving, the traditions and the Gazetteer’s view that much of the coast’s settled population came from Gujarat and Kathiawar. [n22; LSI VII] [n41; LSI VII pp. 145-147] [n35; Gazetteer XIII-1 p. 62]

Third: a Marathi base with a loanword layer from a sacred or ruling language. The Konkani Muslims kept Arabic for prayer and added Urdu, Persian and Arabic words to their Marathi; the Bene Israel kept Hebrew and added Arabic words; the Christians kept Latin and added Portuguese words for father, mother, Mass and godparents, and later for chair and window; the Pathare Prabhus took Hindustani, Portuguese and English words; the Katkaris took Gujarati and Hindustani ones. The Parsis did the same thing with Gujarati instead of Marathi, under the condition the Kisseh-i-Sanjan says they accepted: give up the language of the old country and adopt the language of the new. [n35; inference] [n34; LSI VII] [n13; FPJ 2024]

Fourth: a common Koli ground. Beneath all of these, the oldest description of the coast’s speech, the “Colee bhasha” of the 1830s paper, and the later observations of Edwardes and the Bombay City Gazetteer show Koli and Agri words in the home speech of Prabhus, Panchkalshis and Bhandaris, and a farming Vadval woman speaking like a Koli fishwife. Grierson’s rule that the Kolis “everywhere speak the dialect of their neighbours” is the same truth from the other side. [n41; Trans. Bombay Geog. Soc. I; Edwardes p. 35; Bombay City Gazetteer I p. 239] [n22; LSI VII]

Some sounds cross the patterns. The use of plain l and n for the retroflex ḷ and ṇ was noticed among Panchkalshis and Son Kolis; the Bene Israel and Parsi Gujarati used r for l. The retroflex ḷ itself, a feature of Marathi, Gujarati and Konkani, is often called a Dravidian borrowing, but it may equally descend from Vedic Sanskrit, and the book does not treat its origin as settled. [n35; section 10] [n22; section 5]

The mixing went with mixing of people. The Katkaris admitted Varlis, Kolis, Thakurs and Kunbis for five rupees; Malhar Kolis became Maratha Kunbis; the Talheri Kunbis recruited the children of Pardeshi and Marwari fathers; the Vadval split was blamed on the admission of a Koli; Urap Agris became “New Marathas”. The Palshe priests served almost every Hindu caste of the coast, and Cheda was worshipped in Agri, Bhandari, Pachkalshi and Koli houses alike. [n35; section 10] [n08; Gazetteer] The speech of the coast was made the way its people were made: by arrival, settlement, marriage, conversion and return.

All of these comparisons rest on colonial word lists and compilations. The Devanagari specimens Grierson printed, including the Samvedi Prodigal Son, are unreadable in the scanned text and must be seen on the page; no modern fieldwork tests the old descriptions against today’s speech, except the Kadodi dictionary of 2024. [n35; gaps] [n22; gaps]

Coda: a coast of arrivals

Every people in this chapter came from somewhere. The Warli’s ancestors came with the first humans along the southern coast of Asia, tens of thousands of years ago, if the genetics are read rightly. The Kolis, by their own tradition, came down from the hills and then from Alibag. The Samvedis are first glimpsed in a grant of 1026 and explain themselves with a journey from Odisha that no evidence supports. The Panchkalshis and Palshes came, they say, with a king whose dates no one can fix. The Arabs and Persians came by sea from the eighth century, the Parsis by way of Diu and Sanjan, the Jains with the merchants, the Portuguese with guns and priests, the Chitpavans with the Peshwa’s army, the Lad Vanis fleeing a Nawab, the Marwaris following a rent reduction, and the families of Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Gujarat, Rajasthan and the south following the suburban trains. Each brought a language and each, within a few generations, spoke the language of the coast, coloured by what it had brought.

What none of them did was arrive in an empty place. They arrived in villages: on the garden island of Sopara, on the creek landings at Gass and Bolinj, around the fort of Bassein, in the forest hamlets under Tungar and Jivdhan. The next thread of this book follows those places: how each settlement, from Sopara’s port to the newest housing colony of Nallasopara East, was born, and why it stands where it does.
