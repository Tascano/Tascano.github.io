Period covered: from the basalt of about 62.5 million years ago to November 2026, and what remains to be gathered

A pot on the hearth

In the revenue year 1880–81, the British officers who compiled the Gazetteer of the Bombay Presidency counted what the island of Bassein grew. About 36,541 acres of government land were under tillage. Grain covered 31,885 of them, and rice alone took 29,687. The plantain and betel gardens that made the island’s name, and that sent their fruit south to Bombay by every train, covered only a few thousand acres near the wells. By acreage, the garden of the north Konkan was a rice country. [n21; Gaz XIII-II p. 680] The same volumes describe what its families ate. The Christians of the Thana villages had two meals a day, “of rice, nagli and vari”, with pulse and vegetables, and on good days mutton, pork, fowl or fish; “the whole family eats together, men, women and children”. [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 204] The Hindu Samvedis ate “rice pulse and vegetables”. [n08; Gazetteer 1882, p. 82] The Pachkalshis ate coarse and fine rice, and rice bread. [n41; Gazetteer XIII-1, p. 130] In the Christian villages the first fruits of the rice harvest were carried into the church to be blessed. [n34; Gazetteer XIII pp. 207–210] And one of the Kupari songs that a community website says “are rarely heard as lifestyles have changed” told of a married daughter coming home after the monsoon rice planting. [n07; Vasaiker, “Music of Vasai”]

The image that gives this epilogue its title is the author’s own: a history is like a rice pot, and an honest one keeps an account of every grain: where it was grown, who carried it, who cooked it, and which grains were lost on the way. This book has tried to keep that account for one stretch of coast, from the Vaitarna to Vasai Creek, from the birth of its rock to November 2026. This last chapter lifts the lid and looks at what is in the pot, and at what is missing.

It begins, fittingly, with a missing grain. For all the rice in the later record, the evidence for rice on this coast before the historic period is nil. The first securely reported charred rice in the wider region comes from Ter, on the Deccan plateau, in levels that begin about the fourth century BCE; for the Konkan itself no crop of any date before the Early Historic period has been found. [n24; IAR 1957-58, pp. 21–23; Gaps] Nobody can yet say when rice was first sown on the island of Sopara, or by whom. The pot that seems the most ordinary object in this history has no recorded beginning. That is the right note on which to start a reckoning: every grain counted, and the empty places named.

The grains, in order

Fire, and a land that leans toward the sea

The first grain is the ground. About 62.5 million years ago, as a block of continent carrying the Seychelles and the Laxmi Ridge tore away from India’s west side, lava poured across what would become Mumbai, and the edge of India sagged toward the opening ocean along the great bend that geologists call the Panvel flexure. [n14; Pande et al. 2017; Sheth & Pande 2014] The rhyolites of Uttan and Dongri, just across Vasai Creek, are dated to between about 62.5 and 61 million years ago; they are the nearest dated rocks to Vasai. [n14; n23; IAVCEI-SA2025 abstract] Nobody has published a date for the basalt inside Vasai taluka itself. That the ground under the gardens is the same Bombay-type sequence, tilted seaward, is an inference from its neighbours, not a measurement. [n14; Inferences; Gaps] Everything that followed, the quarried black stone of temples and fort walls, the laterite of the hills, the shallow wells of the gardens, rests on that inference.

The drowned shore

The second grain is water. At the height of the last ice age, about 20,500 years ago, global sea level stood some 125 to 130 metres below today’s. [n23; Yokoyama et al. 2018; PMC4234583] A core taken off Bombay by the National Institute of Oceanography shows the sea there about 101.5 metres below the present level some 14,500 years ago, and then rising, with pauses, until most of the rise was done before 10,000 years ago. [n24; Nigam et al. 1992] In that colder world there was no Vasai Creek and no tidal Vaitarna estuary; the shore probably lay on the order of a hundred kilometres to the west, out on what is now the continental shelf. That figure is reasoning from the shelf’s shape, not a measured shoreline. [n14; n23; Inferences] Any camp the first coastal travellers made on that plain is now under the Arabian Sea. The only radiocarbon date near Sopara that the research found is a shell from a cemented dune at Karal, about 3,250 years old, give or take a century; it speaks of the sea, not of people. [n24; Rajaguru & Ghate]

Shurparaka

The third grain is the first town. Two fragments of the Emperor Ashoka’s words have come out of the ground at Sopara: part of Rock Edict VIII, found by Bhagwanlal Indraji near the Bhatela pond in 1882, and part of Rock Edict IX, found at Bhuigaon on 1 January 1956. [n02; Sircar, EI 32; Shah 2020] In April 1882 Indraji opened the stupa mound, the Burud Rajacha Kot, and took out nested caskets of copper, silver, stone, crystal and gold, now in the Asiatic Society of Mumbai. [n02; Asiatic Society of Mumbai] The Buddhist tale of Purna, son of a Sopara merchant by a slave girl, who went to sea, defied the merchants’ guild and invited the Buddha to a sandalwood hall near the town, was painted on the wall of Cave 2 at Ajanta; historians judge the Buddha’s visit legend, not history. [n02; LiveHistoryIndia; n36; Brancaccio 2023] A first-century Greek sailing guide, the Periplus of the Erythraean Sea, lists “Souppara” among the local marts after Barygaza. [n15; Hobson-Jobson, “Supara”] For a few centuries this was a port of the western sea.

Copper plates and quiet centuries

Then the record thins. A survey relayed by Shah and colleagues found Sopara active from a pre-Ashokan phase to the third century CE, and again from about the ninth to the thirteenth, with “no cultural remains from the 4th to 9th centuries”. [n02; n36; Tripati & Gaur via Shah 2020] When the villages step back into the light, they do so on copper. In 1026 the Shilahara king Chhittaraja granted land to Amadevaiya, a Brahmin of the Sama Veda branch living at Nahur in Salsette; on 20 February 1049 King Mummuniraja granted villages to fourteen Brahmins, some from Karhad, in the Shurparaka-shatshashti vishaya, “the territory round Shurparaka”. [n03; CII-VI pp. 115–118; n25] The notes are firm that the 1026 plate shows Samaveda Brahmins on this coast, not the unbroken descent of the later Samvedi caste. [n03; §6; n35; §1] About 1160 a Shilahara stone at Bassein recorded the gift of a field for restoring a temple, the first royal record found at the place that would become Vasai, though no inscription securely names Vasai or Basai before the fifteenth century. [n03; TG-II pp. 426, 436–437; §1 Gaps]

Sultans, friars and a city of churches

The next grains are foreign hands. In 1321 or 1322 the Dominican friar Jordanus carried the bodies of four companions, killed at Thana, north to Sopara and buried them there. [n38; Gazetteer XIV pp. 356–357] In 1514 the Portuguese writer Duarte Barbosa described “Baxay”, a good seaport of the king of Gujarat. [n15; Thana Gaz. XIV] On 23 December 1534, on board a galleon off Gujarat, Sultan Bahadur Shah signed Bassein over to Nuno da Cunha; the Gazetteer dates the agreement to 1533, and the dates remain in dispute. [n04; Wikipedia: Treaty of Bassein; n03; TG-II p. 451; G 1.1] In 1535 the Franciscan António do Porto founded an orphanage for forty boys at Agashi. [n34; da Cunha 1876, pp. 159–160] The Gazetteer credits him with destroying two hundred temples and making over ten thousand converts; it also records that in famine “the lower Hindus sold their children to Musalmans and Christians”. [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 461 and notes] Baçaim, walled and filled with the churches of four orders, became the “Court of the North”. [n04; Vasai Fort, Wikipedia] A boy of Bassein, Gonsalo Garcia, schooled at its Jesuit college, was crucified at Nagasaki on 5 February 1597 and canonised in 1862. [n38 §4; Catholic Encyclopedia] The conversion of the garden villages, why it happened and how far it was forced, is told on both sides in Chapter 34; Mendiratta’s judgement that the harshest Goan provisions were “never enforced” in the subdivisions closest to the mainland is one side of that ledger, and the Franciscan’s two hundred temples the other. [n34; Mendiratta pp. 210–211]

1739

On 17 February 1739 Chimaji Appa’s army began the siege of Baçaim. Mines brought down the walls, and on 16 May, the best attested date, the city surrendered. [n04; n32; BG 34; DC 147–148] The Portuguese administration left on 23 May. [n04; n18] The city became Bajipur, after the Peshwa, and its first Maratha governor, Shankarji Keshav, left his name on more of Vasai’s sacred landscape than any other person, the temples of Nirmal among them; his own dates are not documented. [n32; BG 34; n10; Gazetteer 1882, p. 367] The villages did not move. Their cultivators paid their dues to new officers, and the native clergy whom the orphanages had trained kept the faith of the Christian villages alive under a Hindu state. [n41; Enthoven III, p. 161; n37]

Plantains on the train

Then came the railway. By 1882 the Gazetteer could write that “large quantities of plantains and betel-leaves are sent south to Bombay”, chiefly from Virar station. [n12; Gazetteer XIV; n05] Nine varieties of plantain were grown, basrai above all; the dried plantains of the rajeli variety, made by Samvedi Brahman, Pachkalshi and Christian families “originally Samvedis and Pachkalshis” at Agashi, Vatar and Koprad, were “the best in the district”. [n21; Gaz XIII-I pp. 292, 395] “The most hardworking and skilful husbandmen,” the same volume says, “are the Christians of Bassein”, who had “turned the light sandy country about Bassein into an evergreen garden”. [n21; Gaz XIII-I p. 300] For most of the next century the farmer-vendors, the kawadis, carried bananas and vegetables on shoulder poles by train to Bora Bazaar and Dadar, and their customers called them Vasaiwale mama and Vasaiwale maushi. [n05; n17; India Food Network, 2015]

Gandhi at Bassein, a boy at Chowk Bazar

The coast’s part in the freedom struggle, long thought silent, was found in the police reports reprinted by the Maharashtra government. On 7 May 1921 Gandhi came to the first Maharashtra Provincial Conference at Bassein. “Your love has drawn me here,” he began, and then he told his audience that Maharashtra was carrying on non-co-operation “in a half-hearted manner”, and asked for money and spinning wheels; about Rs 2,000 was collected on the spot. [n43; SM-IX pp. 91–93] On 9 September 1942, at Chowk Bazar in Vasai, a fifteen-year-old labourer named Bal Samant was shot by the police and died the same day. His death rests on a single entry in the government’s Dictionary of Martyrs; who called the rally, and why the police fired, no source read says. [n43; DoM-3 p. 23; Gaps]

The green belt and the city

In 1988 the state de-reserved the region from agricultural use, against the regional planner’s advice; in May 1990 about 8,500 hectares of green zone were made urbanisable. [n17; TIFR; Revised Land Use Plan] A Franciscan, Fr Francis D’Britto, convened the Harit Vasai Saurakshan Samiti, and on Republic Day 1992 a crowd put at about a hundred thousand filled the Vasai grounds. [n29; Sabrang India, 2000] The western island stayed green. East of the railway, on the salt waste the Gazetteer had called impassable in the rains, a city rose: 219,868 people in 1981, 1,221,233 inside the municipal area by 2011. [n08; n35; Wikipedia: Vasai-Virar] At D’Britto’s funeral in July 2024 a colleague said, “The greenery that you see now is because of the movement.” [n29; FPJ, 25 Jul 2024]

November 2026

The last grain is the present. In the first week of July 2026 the worst flood in the sources fell on the coast; by 13 July nine rain-related deaths had been reported in Vasai-Virar alone, and Tulinj, Vijay Nagar and Giriz in Nallasopara stood under knee-deep water. [n29; The Quint, 13 Jul 2026; n31; FPJ] In January the Catholics had kept the feast of St Gonsalo Garcia in the roofless Jesuit church inside the fort; in June the diocese made Umralla a pilgrim centre for the Year of St Francis. [n38; FPJ, 11 Jan 2026; Catholic Connect] In November 2026 five people died in twenty-four hours on the railway between Vasai and Vaitarna. [n29; Aapla Mahanagar] No census has been taken since 2011; the city probably holds well above two million people, but no source counts them. [n06, §1 Inferences]

The people at the table

Who eats from this pot now? Every community in this book is still here, though not one of them is counted as itself.

The Kuparis, the Samvedi Christians of the garden villages, entered 2026 as they entered the century: uncounted by any census, estimated by themselves at tens of thousands, living in Nirmal, Agashi, Nandakhal, Gass, Bhuigaon, Umrale, Bolinj, Mardes, Wagholi, Rajodi and their neighbours, and in Bombay, the Gulf, Europe, North America and Australia. [n34; n42 §3; n07 §4] Their red caps and red lugda are, a June 2026 article said, “largely absent from daily life but remain part of community identity”. [n34; All About Vasai Virar, 29 Jun 2026] Their origin is still disputed. The 1882 Gazetteer’s statement that the Christian husbandmen of Sopara were “many of them converted Samvedi Brahmans” is the best period evidence for part of their ancestry; the family story of an origin among entertainers from Orissa has no source behind it. [n34; Gaz XIV p. 315; G 1.34] In 2025 a son of Gass, Simon Almeida, was named Bishop of Poona. [n42 §6; Fides]

The Vadvals, the gardeners of the wadis, stand on both sides of the religious line: Hindu Somvanshi Kshatriya Pathare and Christian Vadvals within the East Indian community. [n41] Enthoven put the Vadval division at about 8,000 around 1920; no one has counted them since. [n41, §13] Their associations hold sports festivals and keep directories; their dialect is listed as threatened. [n41; vadvalsamaj.com; Glottolog]

The Samvedi Brahmins of Agashi, Koprad and the northern villages still share a dialect with the Kuparis, and in the November fair at Nirmal sukeli made in Christian and Hindu gardens is sold side by side. [n20; Saamana, 22 Mar 2026; Lokmat]

The Kolis of Arnala, Pachubandar and Naigaon still go to sea for pomfret, against trawlers, purse-seiners and LED lights, and they have shut their harbours in protest against the Vadhvan port rising fifty kilometres to the north. [n17; n20; n06] The Agris, the salt-makers whose seventeen salt works on the north bank of Bassein creek held 11,374 pans in 1880–81, have watched those flats turn into a city on fill. [n21; Gaz XIII-I pp. 364–366; n06] The Warli of the eastern hills carry, by a 2020 genome study, the highest share of the region’s most ancient ancestry of any group tested, an inheritance older than every name in this book; about 390,000 people spoke Warli at the 2011 census. [n01; Debortoli et al. 2020; n22]

The Muslims of the coast trace themselves to Arab and Persian settlers of the eighth to tenth centuries; in 1882 the Muslims of Sopara were “many of them Naitas, descendants of Arab and Persian refugees”, and the old village still keeps its Nawayat mohalla. [n35; Gazetteer XIII-1 pp. 64, 216; n34; Gaz XIV p. 315; n31] The Jains, for whom Sopara was one of the eighty-four great tirthas in a fourteenth-century list, left inscriptions of a Modha clan of the thirteenth to sixteenth centuries; in 2011 they were 1.75 per cent of the taluka. [n01; Shah et al. 2020; n03; Dalal 2019; n35]

And the new migrants, from Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Gujarat, Rajasthan and the south, who made the city. In 2011 Hindi was the first language of 26.58 per cent of the city, Gujarati of 9.26 and Bhojpuri of 3.10; where exactly their speakers came from, and when, no migration study in the notes says. [n22; n35; gaps] The notes observe that the Gazetteer’s 1882 list of “recent settlers”, servants from Upper India, Gujarati traders and Marwari moneylenders, names the same cast 140 years earlier. [n35; inference] The Virar fast that pulls out full every morning is the plantain train of the twenty-first century. The coast still sends its produce south. Only now the produce is people.

The words in the steam

Languages leave a pot first, like steam. The earliest connected sentences in a Vasai dialect that anyone has found in print are the Prodigal Son parables of Grierson’s Linguistic Survey of India in 1905. [n22; LSI VII] A century later the old speech of the gardens is spoken mostly by the old.

Kadodi, or Samvedi, the speech of the Samvedi Brahmins, the Pachkalshis and the Samvedi Christians, was put by Saamana in March 2026 at about 50,000 speakers in sixteen villages from Agashi to Bhuigaon, and by Ethnologue at about 60,000. Saamana called its people “a singing community”. [n20; n22; Saamana, 22 Mar 2026] Dr Naresh Naik of Virar wrote its grammar; Paul Rumav of Nandakhal collected its songs and proverbs; and in 2024 native speakers built a dataset of 949 dictionary entries and 942 sentences that taught a machine to translate it. Its sample sentence was tou majuri karon pout bhartae, “he makes a living by working as a labourer”. [n20; mr.wikipedia; n22; Kumar et al., WAT 2024] In Kadodi the sukeli becomes hukeli, the same sound shift by which the Vadvals say hakar for sakhar, sugar. [n20; n41; PLSI 2017]

Vadvali, or Phudagi, is listed by Glottolog in its 2026 edition as “threatened”; its speakers, the People’s Linguistic Survey reported, were recording and printing their folk songs for fear the language would vanish. [n41; Glottolog; indiandistricts.in] The East Indian Marathi of the Christian villages, “declining, if not in danger of extinction” in the Free Press Journal’s words of 2024, is being gathered into a dictionary by the Mobai Gaothan Panchayat. [n13; n34; FPJ, 14 Mar 2024] Katkari, Thakar, Kokna, Agri and Mangeli are not counted at all; the census folds them into Marathi. [n22, Gaps] No modern linguist has studied how Kadodi formed, where its name came from, or which of its words are Portuguese. [G §5; n22; n34]

Faith, sea and land

Three things have outlasted every ruler in this book.

The first is faith, and its sharing. In 1882 the November fair at Nirmal, on the anniversary of the Shankaracharya’s death, drew six to seven thousand pilgrims, “Hindus, Musalmans, Christians, and a few Parsis”. [n11; Gazetteer XIV, p. 292] In 2025 the same fair was selling purple yam and sweet potato “amid the rising tide of junk food”. [n20; Agrowon/eSakal] The Catholic feast of the coast’s saint is held in a ruin, on the Sunday nearest the neap tide after Christmas, a calendar set by the sea. [n38] Jivdani draws its Navratri crowds up 1,375 steps. [n20] In 1986 a pope came to Vasai first, before Bombay, and called it the place “where the Christian faith was first proclaimed” in this region. [n38; Vatican: Address at Vasai, 9 Feb 1986] Of the religions practised here today, only Hinduism, Jainism and Buddhism are older on this coast than Islam, which came by sea. [n03; Gazetteer XIII-II p. 421] The Buddhism that built the stupa gave way, in sources no one has found, to Brahmanical worship; in 2011, 2.53 per cent of the taluka was counted as Buddhist. [G §3; n22; n06]

The second is the sea. Local craft were hauled ashore from early June until the full moon of Shravan, when the coast offered a coconut to the sea, as the fishing villages still do at Narali Purnima. [n36; Gaz XIII-I p. 342] The sea gave Sopara its trade, and its silting took it away; it gave the Portuguese their moat and the Marathas their navy at Bajipur. [n02; n36; Wikipedia: Kanhoji Angre] It now gives the Kolis less each year, and it returns every monsoon up the old channels to the colonies on the flats. [n17; n31]

The third is the land, and its water. The garden belt was “watered by Persian wheels from shallow wells”; in 1881–82 the sub-division had 191 ponds and 2,624 wells. [n21; Gaz XIII-II pp. 678–679] Where volunteers restore the surviving ponds, the wells sweeten. [n17] The lake at Gass, “a winding hollow, 120 yards broad and 800 long with two cross dams”, with dressed stone steps and the foundations of an eleventh- or twelfth-century temple on its shore, is still there, and in 2025 the people of Gass filed more than 2,500 objections to keep a dumping ground away from it. The corporation withdrew its application in October 2025. [n42; Gazetteer XIV p. 341; n42 §6] As T3 put it, almost none of the places on this coast has a founding date, but each has a reason to be where it is, and the reason is nearly always water.

The empty places in the pot

An honest account of a rice pot names the grains that were never found. Appendix G lists about a hundred and fifty open questions. These are the largest, and the longest silences.

Under the ground. No radiometric date has been measured on the basalt of Vasai taluka, and no fossil locality is published in Vasai or Palghar. No Stone Age, Mesolithic or Neolithic site has been published for Vasai, Palghar, Dahanu, Talasari or Vikramgad, and no ancient DNA has been recovered from the Konkan. [G §3; n14; n23; n24] Archaic people made hand-axes at Kandivli, within a day’s walk of Sopara, and modern humans left microliths at Manori; nobody has searched the Tungar foothills and Kamandurg in the same way. [n14; G §3]

Before the edicts. For the long span from about 3000 to 300 BCE there is nothing on this coast: no Chalcolithic village, no Harappan find, and Sopara’s pre-Mauryan level lies unexcavated under the modern town. [G §3; n24; n36] What we know of those millennia comes from Lothal and Daimabad, Inamgaon and Ter, and it is told in Chapter 4 as the neighbours’ story, not ours. [n24]

The port without a ledger. For all of Shurparaka’s fame, no inscription names a Sopara shipowner, no guild seal or hull has been found, and the Jataka and Pliny passages behind several famous claims were not verified at source. [G §3; n36; n15]

Four or five silent centuries. From the fourth century to the ninth, “no cultural remains” at Sopara; no source explains the shift from Buddhist to Brahmanical worship; Xuanzang names no Sopara. [G §3; n02; n36] The Abhira, Traikutaka and Kalachuri dynasties ruled the region in those years, and the notes suggest that may be why the record is thin. [n02]

The sultans’ coast. No Persian inscription of the Gujarat Sultanate at Arnala or Vasai was retrieved, and the Arab and Persian historians who might describe the coast were not read. [G §3; n03]

The Portuguese registers. The founding documents of the parish churches, the full list of captains of Bassein, the conversion orders of 1546, 1555 and 1581, the specifics of any Inquisition cases, and the parish registers themselves are unread or unlocated. The dates of the oldest rural parishes, Nirmal among them, vary by thirty years between sources. [G §1.8; §3; §4; n09; n37]

The Maratha villages. For 1739 to 1818 there is no Maratha-period record for Gass or most other villages, no named governor after Shankarji Keshav, whose own dates are undocumented, and no figure for reconversion or conversion in those years. [G §1.13; §3; n25]

The railway’s own names. The year Nallasopara’s station opened, and where “Nala” came from, are unresolved. The Gazetteer of 1882 complained that Sopara had no station; a railway history lists an early halt as “Neela or (Nalasopara)”. [n31; G 1.20, 1.21]

1942, and after. Bal Samant’s rally rests on one government listing. No Vasai Christian freedom fighter is named in any source read. For the decades after independence, the town populations of 1951, 1961 and 1971 were not found; for the 1990s, nearly every other year has no sourced event, and the riots of 1992–93 in Vasai-Virar are unrecorded in the notes. [n43; G §3; n28; n29]

The people themselves. No source ties any Vasai Christian surname, D’Mello or Rodrigues, Gonsalves or Tuscano, to a named family or village. No study separates the new migrants by state of origin. No count of Kuparis, Vadvals, Agris or Kolis exists since the caste census of 1931. [G §5; n39; n44; n35; n41]

Gass in 2026. A search for news of Gass after the bishop’s consecration on 17 January 2026 found nothing. Who was born there this year, who married, who left, who still farms, is known to the village and not to the sources. [n42 §6 gaps]

Some of these silences may be absences. Most, the notes insist, are search gaps: places where a document exists but could not be reached, or where a question has never been put to the ground. [G, “How to use this appendix”] The freedom-struggle chapter is the proof. For a long time the research found almost nothing of the national movement in Vasai; when the police reports were read page by page, Bassein appeared in them again and again. [n43; Headline finding]

An invitation to Gass, and to every village

So the pot is not full, and this book cannot fill it alone. The grains that are missing are not in libraries in Lisbon or Pune only. Many are in houses.

In 1881 or 1882 the surveyors of the Gazetteer walked into Gass and found, in front of the house of a man they name as Degu Pobria Mhatara, a six-sided block of basalt used for washing clothes and grinding curry powder, with a single word on it in letters “perhaps about B.C. 200”: Badhuya, “of Badhu”. Two hundred yards away, at the house of a Samvedi Brahman, “Bab Naik by name”, a second block read Kodasa Kalavadasa, “of Kalavad the Kod”, and Ugudeveya, “of Ugradeva”. [n42; Gazetteer XIV p. 341] No report since 1882 mentions either stone. They may still lie in a plinth, a step or a yard in Gass. The people of the village told those surveyors that Gass was “the site of old Sopara”. [n42; Gazetteer XIV p. 341] Whether or not that was so, they were claiming a place in a long story, and the evidence for it was in their own front yards.

That is the invitation this book ends with, to Gass first and then to every village of the old island: to Nirmal and Agashi, Bolinj and Bhuigaon, Umrale and Nandakhal, Papdi and Sandor, Arnala and Pachubandar, the Warli hamlets under Tungar and the new societies of Nallasopara East. Each family holds grains that no archive does. The research has named what would help most. [G §7; K7]

Family memories. Record the oldest members of the family speaking, in Kadodi, Vadvali, East Indian Marathi, Koli or Warli if they speak it, about their parents and grandparents: the landing where boats once came to Gass and Bolinj, the betel and banana gardens, the wells and the Persian wheels, sukeli-making, toddy and salt, the first family members who left for Bombay, the Gulf, Canada or Australia, and why. Keep each recording with the speaker’s name and the date. [G §7.5, §7.7, §7.8]

Parish registers. Ask the parishes, and the Diocese of Vasai, whether the baptism, marriage and burial books can be read for family history: Holy Cross at Nirmal for the Gass families who belonged to it before 1942, and every parish for its own. The godparents’ and landlords’ names in those books are the real evidence for where the surnames came from and when the conversions happened. [G §4; §7.1–7.2; K7]

Songs. Write down or record the wedding songs, the Hadisoli, Lagin and Varad, the lullabies, proverbs and riddles, and the Kupari songs of the bride’s farewell, the women fetching fodder, the groom’s first shave and the daughter coming home after the rice planting, whose texts are not in any source this book could find. [G §7.5; n07; n34; Gaps]

Photographs and objects. Photograph red caps and lal lugda, jewellery, old houses and the crosses on their doors; the 1942 church at Gass and its 1957 rebuilding; early feasts, processions and first communions. Write on the back of every picture, or in a notebook beside it, who, where and when. [G §7.6; K7]

Inscribed stones. Note where carved or lettered stones lie, in Gass, at the Sonarbhat field, the lake shore at Mala, under a banyan or built into a step, and leave them where they are. Photograph them in raking light, measure them, and show the photographs to an epigraphist, without moving or cleaning them. The same goes for the stone anchor found near Taki Pada in 2018, whose present place the sources do not record. [K7; n42; G §7.4]

Papers in the cupboard. Land documents, wills, old certificates and letters; memories of fields sold, flooded or built over at Achole, Tulinj and Giriz; recollections of the 1921 conference, the 1930 prohibition work at Sopara, or Bal Samant and the firing at Chowk Bazar. [G §7.1, §7.9, §7.12]

Gather it in one place, with a parish, a temple trust, a school or a village group, so that the next person who writes this history does not have to write, as this book has had to write so often, that the record is silent.

The lid

Look once more into the pot. At the bottom lies the black rock, poured out as a continent broke and tilted toward the sea. Over it the sea itself, falling and rising with the ice. Then a town whose emperor’s words survive in two broken slabs, a merchant who brought a teaching home on his own ships, and a Greek pilot who wrote its name. Then copper plates naming Brahmins and villages, and long centuries in which nothing at all was written. Then a friar carrying the dead, a sultan’s port, a treaty on a galleon, a city of churches, an orphanage of forty boys, a saint from Bassein. Then mines under a bastion in May 1739, a Peshwa’s name on a city, temples by a lake. Then plantains in baskets on the train, nine kinds of them, and the evergreen garden of the Gazetteer. Then a speech that began “Your love has drawn me here”, and a boy of fifteen who died in a market square. Then a priest and a hundred thousand people who kept an island green, and a city of two million that grew beside it on the salt.

Every grain in that pot came from somewhere, and almost every one arrived in a village that was already there. The Warli’s ancestors came with the first humans; the Arabs and Persians by sea; the Samvedis are first glimpsed in a grant of 1026; the Portuguese came with guns and priests, the Chitpavans with the Peshwa’s army, and the families of Uttar Pradesh and Bihar on the suburban trains. [n35; T2] Each brought a language, and within a few generations each spoke the language of the coast, coloured by what it had brought.

The rice pot of a Kupari kitchen in Gass is a small thing, and it holds all of that: grain grown on a coast whose first rice no one can date, cooked in a dialect that a machine has only just learned, by a family whose name came from a godfather no register has yet been read to find, in a village that told the Gazetteer’s surveyors in 1882 that it stood on the site of old Sopara. The account is not finished. The grains that are missing are in the houses, the parish books, the songs and the stones. This book has counted what it could. The rest is for the people of Gass, and of every village, to add.
