Period covered: the shoreline from the Vaitarna to the Bassein creek, from the Gujarat sultanate’s island fort to November 2026

In this chapter. The shore between the towns: Arnala’s island fort, the fishing hamlets, the beaches, the salt pans and mangroves, and the pressures of erosion and reclamation.

At the northern end of the coast, where the Vaitarna river widens into the sea, a small island lies a short boat ride off the sand. In 1818, in the description the Gazetteer later printed, the fort on its north-western corner was “an oblong square of about 700 feet defended by round towers”, its walls about thirty feet high, with no ditch, its interior “covered with trees and swampy”. Among the forts of the north Konkan coast, the Gazetteer later wrote, it ranked next to Bassein in size and strength, and it was superior even to Bassein in the uniform breadth of its terreplein, the broad walk along the top of the wall. Local people called it Janjira, “the Island”. It commanded “the southern and main entrance to the Vaitarna river”, which the Gazetteer calls “the most extensive inlet in the north Konkan”. [n45; n11; Gaz XIV pp. 10–11]

Over the northern gateway of that fort a Marathi inscription records that it was rebuilt in the reign of Bajirao I in A.D. 1737 (Shak 1659) by an architect named Baji Tulaji. Inside the walls, in 1882, there were two temples, one to Trimbakeshvar and one to Bhavani, with yearly government grants of Rs 45 and Rs 91; a tomb of two Muslim saints, Shahali and Hajiali, with a grant of Rs 80; “a stone-lined eight-cornered pond and many wells”. [n45; n10; Gaz XIV pp. 10–11] Across the water, on the mainland, Koli fishermen hauled their boats up the beach. The Portuguese, who held the island for two centuries before the Marathas, had called it Ilha de Vaccas, Cows’ Island. [n45; Gaz XIV p. 10 n.]

From that island this chapter walks south along the shore, beach by beach, to the old fort of Bassein at the mouth of the Bassein creek. It is a short coast, about twenty-five kilometres of sand, rock, creek mouths and mangrove, but it holds the fishing villages, the salt pans and the boatyards that made this country’s living from the sea, and it is the part of the region whose history has been least written. The forts are well recorded. The beaches, almost not at all. Where the record is thin this chapter says so, and where there is a story only in the newspapers of the last fifteen years (a reclaimed wetland, a drowned boy, a car caught by the tide), it tells that story as the papers tell it.

The shore from the Vaitarna to the Bassein creek

Seen from the sea, the coast runs roughly north to south between two great waters. In the north the Vaitarna opens past Arnala island; in the south the Bassein creek, the estuary of the Ulhas, separates the old island of Bassein from Salsette and Bombay. Between them, in 1882, lay “the island of Bassein”, and before the Portuguese “the island of Sopara”: a strip of gardens about three miles broad, cut off from the mainland on the east by a backwater that wound from the Bassein creek north to the Vaitarna. [n39; n12; Gaz XIV pp. 314–315] That backwater, once “a considerable arm of the sea”, silted fast after the railway embankment of the 1860s dammed its southern flow; by 1882 boats of ten or twelve tons could still reach Gass from the south and Bolinj from the north, but “at Sopara the creek is dry”. [n36; Gaz XIV pp. 314–315] The story of that inner creek is the story of Sopara’s decline, told in Chapters 04, 05 and 11. This chapter faces the other way, west, towards the open sea.

The western shore was never a straight wall of sand. Da Cunha, writing in 1876, described the Arnala–Vaitarna side as “divided into several villages, at one of which, by name Bhoegaum, the sea enters, runs through that of Vagholi, and debouches at that of Vatari”. [n40; da Cunha p. 159] Bhoegaum is Bhuigaon. So in the 1870s a tidal channel still ran in from the sea at Bhuigaon, through Vagholi, and out again at Vatar, cutting the garden belt from within. Anquetil du Perron, a century earlier, had noted Vagholi “with a creek of its own”. [n40; Gaz XIV p. 322] At Agashi, the Gazetteer says, “except one channel the mouth of the bay on which the town stands is closed by a reef.” [n45; Gaz XIV p. 2] And at Nirmal, from the hilltop temple of the Shankaracharya, there was in 1882 “a fine view across the great Nirmal lake to the sea.” The Gazetteer offered an alternative etymology for Nirmal, from a Dravidian nir (water) and mal (hill), “the sea hill”, which it thought “a true description of the place which is a wooded mound rising from bare flats once under water”. [n40; n39; Gaz XIV pp. 292–293] Whatever the truth of the etymology, the description fits: behind the beaches lay lagoons, tidal flats, creeks and lakes, and the garden villages stood on the higher ground among them.

The order of the beaches from north to south, as the tourism sites and the district list them, is: Arnala, Navapur, Rajodi, Kalamb (near Nirmal, west of Nallasopara), Rangaon, Bhuigaon, Suruchi (in Vasai Nagar), then Pachubandar and the Vasai fort shore at the mouth of the creek. The Virar-Vasai tourism site places Navapur “between Arnala and Rajodi” and Arnala “at the northern tip of Virar”. [n45; vasaivirar.org; vasai.com] The Palghar district site also lists a “Vasai Beach” at the Ulhas mouth on the southern border, describing it as a “Mini Goa” of clean soft sand and few crowds. [n39; palghar.gov.in] There is no older list. The 1882 Gazetteer has no entry for any beach. It describes the shore only as fishing grounds and as the landings of the garden belt: Bolinj, Gass and Vagholi on the creeks, Agashi on its bay. [n45] No Portuguese or British mention of any individual beach was found. [n45]

The coast was also a line of defence. Between Shirgaon near Mahim, to the north beyond the Vaitarna, and Dantivra “close to Arnala”, the Gazetteer counted “remains of sixteen forts”. [n45; Gaz XIV pp. 200–201] In 1882 the Bassein sub-division had three sea forts, Arnala, Bassein and Kharlio, and two inland forts, Jivdhan above Virar and Kamandurg in the east. [n39; Gaz XIV] Of all of them, Arnala is the one that still stands entire.

Arnala: the island fort

Three forts on one island

Read together, the Gazetteer and the modern sources show three layers on Arnala island: a fort of the Gujarat sultans, an unfinished Portuguese fort, and the Maratha fort of 1737 that stands today. [n45]

The first layer is the hardest to date. The Gazetteer says that “about 1530” the Portuguese found Arnala “a strong fort in the hands of the Gujarat Musalmans with domes and Saracenic arches.” [n45; Gaz XIV p. 10] That is evidence for a sultanate fort on the island before 1530. The popular accounts go further. Wikipedia’s infobox and a Marathi fort website give the date 1516 and the builder as Sultan Mahmud Begada of Gujarat. [n45; Wikipedia: Arnala fort; Durg Bharari: Arnala] Neither cites a primary source, and there is a difficulty: Mahmud Begada died in 1511, so a fort “built by Mahmud Begada in 1516” cannot be right as it stands. [n10] UNESCO’s tentative-list entry for the coastal fortifications of the Konkan, known here only from a search summary, says the island “first belonged to Mahmud Begda” and was conquered by the Portuguese in 1530. [n45; UNESCO tentative list] The safe statement is the Gazetteer’s: a strong Gujarat fort stood on the island by about 1530. The date 1516 and the name of Mahmud Begada are unconfirmed; the year may belong to some other event, or to a later sultan’s work, or to nobody.

The Gujarat sultanate’s coast is the subject of Chapter 06. Its other works on this shore are better known: Agashi built ships “as good as Portuguese ships” about 1530, until the Portuguese burned the town and three hundred boats; Bassein was a harbour of “Moors and Gentiles” in 1514; in 1532 Bahadur Shah ordered Malik Tokan of Diu to fortify Bassein. [n40; n32; Gaz XIV pp. 1–2, 28–29] A fort at the Vaitarna mouth fits that picture of a sultanate guarding its northern Konkan harbours.

The second layer is Portuguese. The Gazetteer says that the domes and Saracenic arches “were destroyed … and the place made over to a Portuguese gentleman of Bassein”. [n45; Gaz XIV p. 10] Wikipedia adds that the captain of Bassein gave the island to a nobleman who began a fort about 700 feet square (Wikipedia: about 210 metres) but never finished it. [n45] The Gazetteer’s footnote, citing da Cunha, gives the Portuguese name, Ilha de Vaccas, “Cows’ Island”. [n45; Gaz XIV p. 10 n.] The Tombo de Baçaim, the Crown’s register of 1727–1730, shows that the coloria of Agaçaim and of the “Ilha das Vacas” was then held by D. Domingas Pereira de Azevedo, who also held the Agashi pacaria. [n40; n39; Tombo fl. 41] Coloria is a revenue lease, probably on the fisheries or the boats of the Koli fishermen; the word suggests it, but the register does not explain it, and the reading is an inference. [n39] A Portuguese bastion, a round tower of the kind later called a Martello tower, stands at the west end of the island, and another about 550 metres south of the main fort, with no entrance gate. One listing of Martello towers dates a tower at Arnala to 1803, which conflicts with a Portuguese origin; the conflict is not resolved. [n45; Wikipedia: Arnala fort; Martello Towers in Asia]

28 March 1737

The third layer begins with a battle. In the Maratha campaign against Portuguese Bassein, led by Chimaji Appa, the general Shankarji Pant urged an attack on Arnala for its naval value. A first assault, with Govindji Kasar and the navy of Manaji Angre, failed against the Portuguese fleet. A second, on 28 March 1737, surprised the Portuguese, who abandoned the island. Bajirao I, the Peshwa, then ordered a new fort built. [n45; n11; n13; Wikipedia: Arnala fort, citing Naravane, Scholberg and Kantak] The Gazetteer’s account of Bassein puts the consequence in one sentence: “In 1737 they seized the small fort of Arnala to the north of Bassein, and soon after, by occupying the islands of Versova and Dharavi and the creeks between Bassein and the mainland they completely isolated the city.” [n45; Gaz XIV p. 34] Arnala was the first door the Marathas opened. Bassein itself fell after the siege of 1739, as Chapters 08 and 09 tell.

Over the northern gateway the builders cut their record. The Gazetteer reads it as stating that the fort was “rebuilt in the reign of Bajirao I. in A.D. 1737 (Shak 1659), by an architect named Baji Tulaji”. [n45; Gaz XIV p. 10] A Marathi fort website says the inscription on the main gate gives the completion date as 23 May 1737 and names Baji Tulaji. [n45; Durg Bharari: Arnala] English Wikipedia names Shankarji (Keshav) as the man Bajirao ordered to build the new fort, and does not name Baji Tulaji. [n11; n45] The architect’s name, then, rests on the Gazetteer’s reading of 1882, which is the better authority; the precise date of 23 May 1737 is plausible but rests on one modern site. [n45] If it is right, the fort was rebuilt in less than two months after the capture, which would be fast for walls of this size; it may record the completion of the gate and its inscription rather than of the whole circuit. That last point is inference.

The fort the Marathas built is the one that stands. It has three gates, with the main entrance facing north, carved with elephants and floral and sharabha (mythical beast) designs. The Marathi fort site lists ten bastions, nine round and one square, and gives names for some of them: Yashwant, Bhavani, Ganesh and Vetal, with a “Hanumanth” bastion outside to the south. English Wikipedia says that only one of the ten bastions still stands. The two accounts conflict. [n45; Durg Bharari; Wikipedia] A Marathi site says records of 1772 mention a large Maratha navy based at Arnala; that is a single unverified source. [n45] The historian Shridatta Raut has argued, according to the same Marathi site, that the fort is wholly Maratha in construction; his own text was not located. [n45]

1781, 1817, 1818: the British

The British came twice. The Gazetteer says that in 1781 the fort’s commandant refused to yield to them until a bombardment from Agashi had been prepared, and that the fort came under the British in 1817. [n45; n11; Gaz XIV pp. 10–11] Wikipedia says the British captured Arnala on 18 January 1781, that the Treaty of Salbai returned it to the Marathas, and that the Marathas held it until 1817. The Marathi fort site says it fell in 1818 after strong resistance. [n45] There are, then, three versions: a capture in January 1781 against a surrender in 1781 under the threat of bombardment; and a final British possession in 1817 against 1818. The notes cannot choose between them. What is not in doubt is that the British were in possession by 1818, because that is the year of the description of the oblong square, the round towers and the swampy interior. [n45; Gaz XIV p. 10]

Inside the walls

The fort holds shrines of three faiths, or rather of two faiths and the memory of a third. In 1882 the Gazetteer recorded Trimbakeshvar’s temple (Rs 45 a year), Bhavani’s temple (Rs 91), and “a tomb of two Musalman saints, Shahali and Hajiali, with a yearly endowment of Rs 80, and a stone-lined eight-cornered pond and many wells”. [n10; n45; Gaz XIV p. 11] Set against the other grants of 1882, these are middling sums: far below Trivikram in Bassein fort (Rs 1,061 a year), the Nirmal temples (about Rs 1,330 together, with their alms-houses) and Vajrabai (Rs 196 in cash, plus villages), but above Agashi’s Bhavanishankar (Rs 50); the saints’ tomb, at Rs 80, out-ranked Agashi’s great temple too. [n10] The dargah of Shahali and Hajiali is the one dated, historical Muslim shrine of the whole coast. [n10] Who the two saints were, when they lived, and whether their tomb is older than the Maratha fort, the record does not say. A Muslim saints’ tomb inside a fort rebuilt by a Hindu Peshwa’s architect, endowed by a British government, is a fair picture of how this coast’s layers sat together.

Modern accounts list, inside the walls, the Trimbakeshwar Mahadev temple with its octagonal tank (the “eight-cornered pond” of 1882), the Bhavani Mata temple, the paduka (sacred footprints) of “Nityananda” (Wikipedia: “Shrinity Anand”), the dargah with its two tombs of Shah Ali and Hajji Ali, and a recent Datta temple; outside the main gate stands a Kalika Mata temple with a hero stone and a cannon. Wikipedia also names an “Ambakeshwar” temple, probably the same Trimbakeshwar. [n45; n11; Durg Bharari; Wikipedia]

Protected, inhabited, reached by boat

On 26 May 1909 the British government declared Arnala fort a Monument of National Importance, reference N-MH-M13 in Wikipedia’s list; it is still under the Archaeological Survey of India. [n45; n11] The ASI’s and Incredible India’s own pages on the fort came up in searches but were not read. [n45] The island is not a ruin in the wilderness. The Census of 2011 counted Arnala Killa, the fort island, as a village of its own: 467 households, 2,566 people, literacy 80.64 per cent, 1,037 workers, and no Scheduled Caste or Scheduled Tribe residents. [n40; n11; census2011.co.in] People live round the fort and inside the island’s lanes.

The way across is a short boat ride from Arnala beach, five to ten minutes. Timings quoted by sources disagree: the Marathi fort site gives 6:00 to 12:30 and 16:00 to 19:00; a guide says every fifteen minutes from 6:00 to 18:15; fares range from Rs 10 to Rs 50 per person across sources. All of these are unverified and change. [n45] One source says there is no jetty on the island, so visitors wade ashore. [n45; n11] In March 2015 the Free Press Journal carried a complaint about the lack of a passenger jetty and of safe boats. [n11; FPJ via PressReader, 4 Mar 2015] Whether a jetty had been built by 2026 is not recorded.

Arnala village and its Koliwada

Fishermen of the Vaitarna mouth

The mainland village of Arnala faces its island across a narrow channel. In 2011 the Census counted it as a rural village outside the municipal corporation: 3,005 households, 15,331 people (7,725 men and 7,606 women), 1,473 of them Scheduled Tribe and 117 Scheduled Caste, literacy 80.10 per cent, 7,639 workers. [n40; n11; census2011.co.in] A Marathi fort website says about three to four thousand Koli people live around the fort and fish. [n45; Durg Bharari] Arnala has its own gram panchayat; a sarpanch was quoted in the newspapers in 2026. Whether the village has since been brought inside the corporation is not confirmed. [n45]

Who were Arnala’s fishermen? The 1882 Gazetteer does not describe Arnala’s people village by village, but it describes the fishing castes of the coast, and its descriptions place them here. “The mass of the sea-fishing population” of the Thana coast, it says, were the Son Kolis, about 8,000, “chiefly along the coast south of the Vaitarna … probably a tribe of Kolis who have mixed with foreign settlers from beyond the sea”. Their name might mean “the younger or later-come Kolis”, or come from son, red, or sonay, stranger. [n08; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 148–149] They were short and sturdy, with powerful shoulders; the men wore a top-knot and moustache, a loincloth, a waistcloth, a woollen jacket and a red broadcloth cap; the women wore a loose long-sleeved bodice and a robe wound tight and ending above the knee, with glass bangles on the left hand only. Their Marathi had “so curious an accent” that outsiders found it hard to follow. They were “hardworking, hospitable and honest”. Their family gods were Khanderao, Bhavani, Bhairav, Babdev, Vir, Kalkai, Cheda and Marubai, kept in the houses of the older men, where the rest came daily to pray. Their tribal head was the Vagh Patil at Alibag in Kolaba, “whence the Thana Son Kolis say they originally came”, and “most of them have a good market for their fish, and on the whole are well-to-do.” [n08; Gaz XIII-1 p. 149]

The Vaitis, 4,596 in 1882, lived “in north Bassein and south Mahim”, that is, round the Vaitarna mouth and Arnala. Lower in rank than the Son Kolis but apparently the same tribe, they traced themselves to “one Valhya Koli” (an echo of the legend of Valmiki as Valya Koli), had their headman at Chaul in Kolaba, and had no surnames. They farmed, fished, sailed and laboured, and a few dealt in timber and hay. They worshipped “Ram and the sea”, and kept only a coconut at home as an object of worship. [n08; Gaz XIII-1 p. 182] The Mangelas, 10,718 in the district, lived mostly to the north and in Salsette, but a modern list includes Pachu Bandar, Vasai and Arnala among Mangela Koli villages. Among the Mangelas, the Gazetteer notes, the women sold the fish. [n08; Wikipedia: Mangela Kolis (search summary); Gaz XIII-1 pp. 147–148] Their Marathi, the Gazetteer noted, was “indistinct … with many Gujarati words”: kain gelā hotā? for “where had you gone?”, mana bhuk nāngli for “I am hungry”. [n08; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 147–148] The Machhis of Bassein, another small fishing group of seventy-seven people in 1881, spoke Gujarati at home and Marathi outside, fished, hired out boats and worshipped Maruti. [n08; n21; Gaz XIII-1 p. 147; Gaz XIII-2 p. 680] And the Gazetteer adds that “the Uraps and other Christian Kolis in Bassein … are professional fishermen”. [n08; Gaz XIII-1 p. 55] Which of these groups made up Arnala’s fishing population in 1882, and in what proportions, the record does not say. The likeliest reading is that Arnala, at the Vaitarna mouth, was a Son Koli and Vaiti place with Mangela and Christian Koli neighbours; the notes elsewhere describe Arnala and the northern coast as “Son Koli and Mangela areas”. [n45]

Before Act XIX of 1844, Kolis, Mangelas and Vaitis paid a fishing poll-tax, day-dene, of Rs 1¼ each for the privilege of fishing. [n08; n36] In 1851 the Collector counted 129 salt-water fisheries in Thana district, twenty-two of them in Bassein. [n21; Gaz XIII-1 p. 54] In 1881 cholera “began among the fishermen of Kelva Mahim in April and prevailed in Bassein from May to July.” [n21; Gaz XIII-2] These scattered official lines are almost all that the nineteenth century says about the fishermen of this shore.

A church built by fishermen

Arnala’s Christians have a short documented history, and the Kolis are at the centre of it. The parishioners of Arnala belonged first to Our Lady of Light and then to St James at Agashi. In 1919 Fr Ismail Da Costa, assistant priest at Agashi, built a hut chapel near Arnala beach; in 1921 a tin shed dedicated to St Peter went up “with the Kolis’ help”; on 27 December 1931 Archbishop Joaquim Lima blessed the church. A Hindu community association gave Rs 3,000 towards it. The community source says that “Kolis consider Fr Da Costa as the true Apostle of Arnala.” [n40; EI Arnala; CRIT Vol III] The 2008 heritage survey dates the church to 1921. [n40] The donation from a Hindu association to a Catholic fishermen’s church is the kind of detail that rarely reaches the record; here it did. The survey also lists a Ram Mandir at Arnala dated 1806. [n40; CRIT]

Salt, 1930

In April 1930, in the Civil Disobedience campaign, the Thane District Congress Committee opened its satyagraha centre at Arnala, where named leaders sold salt they had made; officials posted at Arnala were boycotted. At Virar a meeting on 15 April called for breaking the salt laws, and seventy-five people took part. These details come from a secondary summary of the 1982 Thane District Gazetteer’s chapter on the modern period, which could not be opened and quoted; the names of the Arnala leaders and the number arrested are not known. [n43] The 2008 heritage survey says the Salt and Forest Satyagraha of 1929–30 centred on Arnala and that a Gandhi Smarak, a memorial to Gandhi, stands there. [n40; CRIT Vol I] A Free Press Journal headline snippet says Arnala is “considered a holy place and hence ashes are immersed here because three rivers confluence at Arnala”, and that a Mahatma Gandhi memorial was built there on 2 October 1956. The full article could not be opened and the claim could not be confirmed. [n45] With its salt pans and its fishermen, Arnala was the natural place on this coast for the salt beat of 1930, but the primary record of what happened there is still unread. [n43]

The catch and the auction

In the twentieth century Arnala became one of the major dol-net landing centres of Thane district. The dol is a bag net set in the tidal current; its main catch on this coast is the Bombay duck, bombil; pomfret was taken by dol nets until the mid-1960s and then increasingly by nylon gill nets. [n36; ICSF (search summary)] A travel site says Arnala holds daily fish auctions and keeps traditional boats. [n08; Traveldojo] An Arnala Fishermen’s Association helps in rescues at sea; the Free Press Journal reported a fishing boat rescued after an engine failure off the Arnala coast, and Down To Earth told of a boat’s engine failing near Arnala in a piece on fisherfolk livelihoods. [n11; n17; FPJ; Down To Earth] Beyond these, the notes hold nothing specific to Arnala: no boat count, no landing figures, no named cooperative, no account of the auction itself. [n45; n17]

Flowers on the fishing coast

Arnala’s other living is newer. In the late 1980s Moreshwar Bhoir of Arnala brought the sonchafa, the golden champak (Magnolia champaca, the “Velankar chafa” from Uday Velankar’s nursery at Kudal), to the village. By January 2023 the Arnala Farmers’ Cooperative Society, chaired by his grandson Kiran Patil, had 1,100 members. About five hundred families in about twenty-five villages, along an eighteen-kilometre stretch from the Arnala coast to Vasai, grew flowers for Mumbai’s Dadar market, where sonchafa sold at Rs 150 for a box of a hundred and up to Rs 700 at Ganesh Chaturthi. [n17; n11; 101Reporters, Jan 2023] A 2011 study found that Arnala and Agashi had an extensive flower system that peaked at Ganesh Chaturthi, Diwali and Navratri, and that growers named price swings, up to twenty-fold at festivals, as their main difficulty. [n17; Vazhacharickal and Buerkert 2011] So the village at the Vaitarna mouth lives from two harvests: fish from the sea in front of it and flowers from the sandy gardens behind it.

South from Arnala: the beaches, one by one

From Arnala the sand runs south past a string of villages whose names are well known to weekend visitors from Mumbai and almost unknown to historians. For most of them the documentary record is a census row, a line in a Portuguese lease register, a church date and a handful of newspaper reports. This section gathers what there is, village by village, and marks what is missing.

Agashi: the port on the bay

Before the beaches proper begin, the shore south of Arnala holds the coast’s oldest working harbour. Agashi, the Gazetteer says, stands on a bay whose mouth is “closed by a reef” except for one channel. [n45; Gaz XIV p. 2] Behind that reef the town built ships about 1530 that could sail to Europe, until the Portuguese burned it and three hundred of its boats; Faria y Souza records a second destruction in March 1531. [n40; Gaz XIV pp. 1–2] Under the Portuguese, the town’s customs and revenue rose from 281,249 fedeas (about £234) in 1536 to 313,874 in 1537. [n11; Gaz XIV pp. 1–2] In 1570 “Agazim” was a port trading with Gujarat. [n40] It was from Agashi, in 1781, that the British prepared to bombard Arnala fort, a few miles up the coast. [n11; Gaz XIV p. 11] In 1837 Agashi was “the chief town on Bassein island”, trading grain, garden produce and salt to Gujarat and Bombay, and building “very good fishing boats and coasting craft”. [n40; Gaz XIV p. 2] In the five years to 1879 its sea exports averaged Rs 3,18,470 a year and its imports Rs 85,650, ranging from about Rs 2,46,000 in exports in 1875 to more than Rs 4,81,000 in 1879; visiting vessels anchored 100 to 200 feet from its landing. [n40; n21; n36; Gaz XIV p. 2; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 358–359] The heritage survey of 2008 says that Agashi’s port eventually “silted” and its shipbuilding declined because “modern shipping requires newer technology”, and that the town turned to farming in the nineteenth century. [n40; CRIT Vol I] Agashi’s land story, its temples, church and plantains, is told in Chapter 27 and in Chapter 38, “Agashi: the shipyard village”. Here it matters as the port that tied the beach villages to the wider sea, and as the one place on this shore where the Gazetteer recorded both a bay and a trade.

Navapur

Navapur’s beach lies “between Arnala and Rajodi”, in the Virar-Vasai tourism site’s words. [n45; n11; vasaivirar.org] The only older record of a Navapur in Bassein is the 1882 Gazetteer’s footnote naming it among the villages of the Urap Agris, the Christian Agris who returned to Hinduism in 1820 and 1828 (Chapter 27). [n40; Gaz XIII-1 p. 117 n.1] The “Navapur” in the 1882 Places index (p. 291) is a different place near Navli in Palghar, and the port of Navapur near Tarapur is Palghar’s too. [n40; n11] No source gives Navapur Kupari or Vadval history, a census figure for 2011, or any beach history. [n40; n45] One 2024 report places the resort where a young man died that July “at Arnala or Navapur” (see below). [n45]

Rajodi

Rajodi is the Kupari village of the coast. The community’s own list of thirteen Kupari villages includes it, and the 1882 Gazetteer names it among the nine villages where the famous Bassein dried plantains were made. [n40; n39; Gaz XIII-1 p. 292] The Tombo of 1727–30 has several “Rajoly” estates: “Vatar Rajoly”, the pacaria with the highest rent in its list (700 pardaus), “Rajoly e Caraly”, and “aldeia Rajoly Marelá”; which of them is this Rajodi and which Rajavali cannot be fixed. [n40] Rajodi’s church was inaugurated on 1 May 1999 as a centre of the old Kupari parish of Nandakhal; a mirror list gives it the dedication of Our Lady of Velankanni, which the same list also gives to Wagholi, so at least one entry is wrong. [n40] Its gram panchayat was Nale-Rajodi, and the Nale talav on the Arnala–Vasai road is where the gram panchayat found old ghats submerged below the water level. [n40; CRIT Vol I] Tourism sites call Rajodi a secluded family beach between Kalamb and Arnala. [n11; n39; vasaivirar.org] Chapter 38, “Rajodi: the beach village”, has the community history. Rajodi is also, as a later section shows, the beach where four teenagers drowned in 2016.

Kalamb

Kalamb is a beach village near Nirmal, west of Nallasopara. In 2011 it was a rural census village of 1,169 households and 5,329 people, 604 of them Scheduled Tribe and 46 Scheduled Caste, with a literacy rate of 87.20 per cent and 2,636 workers. [n40; n11; census2011.co.in] It must not be confused with Kalambhon (Kalambhom), an inland village on the Tansa eighteen miles east of Manikpur, which has a Shilahara inscription of Aparaditya and a carved memorial stone. [n11; Gaz XIV p. 112] The Tombo has a “Calamba” in a group of villages of the Thana caçabé (“Sedacer, Choly, Calamba, Nagão e Varchory”), which might be Kalamb with Holi and Naigaon, but the identification is uncertain. [n40] A Nirmal–Kalamb road runs past Nirmal Bazaar, founded in 1957. [n40] No history of Kalamb’s people, fishing or beach has been found. [n45; n11] Its one well-reported event is the car caught by the tide in September 2025, told below.

Rangaon

Rangaon is a census village of 875 households and 4,166 people in 2011 (399 Scheduled Tribe, 269 Scheduled Caste, literacy 85.19 per cent, 2,080 workers). [n40; n11] The 2008 heritage survey, without a source, lists Rangaon with Agashi and Papdi as “chief ship-building centres” before the Portuguese; it also records a Rangaon talav, an eighteenth-century Hanuman temple, the Devali talav and a Zilla Parishad school of 1917, all in the Kaular Budruk gram panchayat. [n40; CRIT Vol I] A travel guide lists Rangaon with Suruchi, Kalamb, Rajodi and Arnala among Vasai’s main beaches. [n11; NativePlanet] That is the whole record. The shipbuilding claim is worth testing, because if it is true Rangaon belongs with Agashi in the story of the coast’s shipwrights; at present it is an unsourced line in a survey.

Bhuigaon

Bhuigaon is the place where, in da Cunha’s description of 1876, “the sea enters” and runs inland through Vagholi to Vatar. [n40; da Cunha p. 159] It is a Kupari farming village two kilometres south-west of Nirmal, divided into two gram panchayats, Bhuigaon Khurd (small) and Bhuigaon Budruk (large). Its Catholics, about a thousand of them, found the walk to the mother church at Nirmal so long that “only a handful attended services”; a simple church was built on land the poor villagers gave, with money from the Archbishop of Bombay and the Bandra parishes, and Bhuigaon became the independent parish of the Holy Family on 2 June 1946, the second after Gass to be separated from Nirmal. [n40; n11; EI Bhuigaon] Thomas Dabre, the first Bishop of Vasai (1998), was born there on 23 October 1945. [n40]

A blogger who walked the village in April 2011 found about forty large irrigation wells, bhowkal, kept by the women, watering vegetables, hybrid bananas and flowers and holding back the monsoon for paddy; a stainless-steel cross in the western quarter, the Madikar Cross, that acts as “the local equivalent of a Gaondevi”, marking the territory of the extended Martis family; crosses on the front doors; and the Kadodi or Kupari dialect. [n40; n11; Mumbai Paused blog, 2011] A 2013 survey counted thirty-nine family ponds at Bhuigaon where the official records showed sixteen. [n17; Vikalp Sangam] The village’s history is in Chapter 38, “Bhuigaon: wells, crosses and a bishop”.

Bhuigaon’s beach is younger as a destination. In June 2019 the Free Press Journal reported that tourist footfall there had risen “fivefold”. In the same report it described the damage to its casuarina trees, and Bhuigaon also stands at the centre of the reclamation case of 2013–14. Both stories are told below. [n45]

Suruchi

Suruchi beach, in Vasai Nagar, is named after its suru, the casuarina trees that line it, and lies about 7.6 kilometres from Vasai Road station, according to a developer’s blog. [n11; n45; Sunteck blog] The Palghar district site recommends it for sunrise, sunset and morning walks. [n39; palghar.gov.in] No history of the beach, or of when its casuarinas were planted, was found. [n45] Casuarina belts were planted along this coast as protection against wind and sea, but even that planting history was not sourced. [n45]

Pachubandar, the Koliwada and the fort shore

At the southern end, close to the Bassein creek, are Pachubandar (Pachu Bandar) and the Vasai Koliwada. A modern list includes Pachu Bandar among Mangela Koli villages, and a Koliwada in Vasai is listed among the Koli settlements of the Mumbai region. [n08; n11; Wikipedia: Mangela Kolis; Wikipedia: Koliwada] Beyond that the record holds only property listings for “Pachu Bandar Road, Vasai West”. [n11] Nothing was found on its boats, its catch, its drying yards, its festivals, or its families. [n11; n45]

Beside the old fort is Killa Bandar, “the fort harbour”, whose name appears as the bus destination for the fort jetty; Killabandar is the village beside the fort. [n32; n11] The fort’s port is well documented up to 1882: a good landing place and a custom house, with average exports of Rs 5,14,140 and imports of Rs 2,25,200 a year in the five years to 1878–79, and a road to the landing cut through the dead city in 1856. [n32; n11; Gaz XIV pp. 36, 39] Killa Bandar’s fishing life, its boats, species, families, catch and jetty history, was not found in any source reached. [n32] The “Vasai Beach” of the district site, at the Ulhas mouth, is presumably this southern shore below the fort; the site gives no history. [n39] The fort itself, and the Portuguese city inside it, belong to Chapters 07 and 08.

The creek mouth below the fort has also seen the newest attempt to use the water as a road. On 20 February 2024 a roll-on, roll-off ferry began a three-month pilot service across the creek between Vasai and Bhayandar, about three and a half kilometres by water, with a vessel carrying a hundred passengers and thirty-three vehicles. On 25 February it was halted after the vessel hit the concrete jetty at Vasai. Its status after the pilot is not confirmed. [n36; ThePrint; Lokmat Times] In the Portuguese city above it, ferry boatmen of the Bassein river had been paid 200 réis a month. [n32; da Cunha pp. 222–223] The crossing is one of the oldest on the coast; earlier research saw in its revival a natural closing loop for the whole book. [n36]

Boats, nets and fish

The beaches were workplaces before they were playgrounds, and the record of that work, though it is mostly of the district rather than of single villages, is older and fuller than the record of the beaches themselves.

The old sea

The sea gave this coast more than fish. The Arab geographer Idrisi wrote of pearls fished in the Sopara creeks. [n36; Gaz XIII-2 p. 430] The broad backwater behind the island, the Gazetteer thought, “guarded it from pirate raids or the forays of the wild hill and forest tribes”; the Periplus and later travellers such as Marco Polo and Barbosa wrote of pirates and corsairs on the Thana coast. [n36; Gaz XIV pp. 316, 354–355; Periplus §53] Those deep stories belong to Chapters 04, 05 and T1. By the early eighteenth century the Portuguese were leasing the sea’s income as they leased the land: the Tombo de Baçaim records the coloria of Agaçaim and of Arnala island, and rents on toddy palms, held by Portuguese gentlemen and ladies. Earlier research read this, as an inference, to mean that fishing and toddy were leased to fidalgos in the early 1700s. [n39; n40; Tombo fl. 41, 44v]

The Gazetteer’s boats

In the 1880s the Thana coast had ten varieties of lateen-rigged craft. The local coasters were the balav, a fishing boat; the machva (also called suval); the padav, a cotton boat; and the mhangiri, a large padav. “Some are built in Bombay, Versova, Dantivra, Mahim, Karanja, and Daman, but most in the Bassein sub-division, chiefly at Bassein.” The builders were “Marathas, Christians, Gujarat carpenters, Cutch Musalmans, Kolis, and Pachkalshis”, working teak and ain from Bombay, Bassein and Jawhar. The owners were Gujarat and Marwar Vanis, Brahmins, Agris, Bhatias, Lingayats, Bhandaris, Machhis, Kolis and Khojas. A boat lasted twenty to thirty years. Sailors were paid by share, and the captain, the tandel, took a double share. Steering was “almost entirely by land-marks, and … by the stars”. And every year the local craft were hauled ashore from early June until Coconut Day, the full moon of Shravan. [n36; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 342–343] “Along the coast as far as Bassein,” the Gazetteer adds, “fishermen have taken to use jolly-boats of from 14 to 25 feet long.” [n21; Gaz XIII-1 p. 351]

The three ports of the Bassein division in 1881–82 were Agashi, Bassein and Navghar. Their vessels were mhangiris, machvas, padavs and batelas of fifty to seventy tons, “built locally by Marathas and Native Christians”, with crews from Bassein and the nearby villages; visiting ships from Gujarat, Cutch, Kathiawar, Diu, Daman and the south Konkan anchored 100 to 200 feet from the landing at Agashi. [n36; n21; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 358–359] In 1837, Agashi “built very good fishing boats and coasting craft.” [n40; Gaz XIV p. 2] Behind those lines lie the shipwrights of the sixteenth century, whose Agashi ships could sail to Europe, and the Bassein yards where in 1636 two pinnaces were built for the English factory at Surat, part of the nucleus of what became the Bombay Marine. [n40; n32] Chapter 39 follows the shipbuilders across every generation.

No account of boatbuilding at Arnala or Pachubandar in the twentieth century was found. [n13]

Fishing in the late twentieth century

The modern record is thin and mostly regional. A 1997 count, cited in a 2007 study, gave 5,007 active fishermen in Vasai taluka; there were eleven primary fishermen’s cooperatives in the taluka. [n36] Vasai’s fishers once caught many species in traditional dol nets, but by the 2010s, one account says, Vasai “has become largely a one-fish town”, depending mostly on pomfret, which was itself declining as trawlers and purse-seiners competed for it; the number of dol-net boats in Vasai reportedly doubled to about a hundred in a decade. [n17; n08; Citizen Matters; ICSF] (Earlier research suspected the “one fish” was the Bombay duck; the sources name pomfret.) [n08] The neighbouring Palghar coast shows the scale of the change: in the Satpati–Dahanu zone the bombil catch reached a record 40,065 tons in 1979, and the boats fishing bombil from Satpati fell from thirty or thirty-five in the 1980s to twelve by mid-2019; Satpati’s fishermen’s cooperative had built the first cooperative ice factory, of seven tons, in 1950–51. [n36; ICSF; PARI] A 2017 Maharashtra government resolution banned LED lights, purse seining and gill netting by mechanised boats in certain waters; traditional fishers have since demanded a special investigation into illegal purse-seine and LED fishing. [n08] Fishing stops every year in the monsoon months from June to August. [n36]

North of the Vaitarna, the Vadhvan port near Dahanu, approved by the Union Cabinet on 19 June 2024 at Rs 76,220 crore with 1,448 hectares of reclamation, had its foundation stone laid on 30 August 2024 amid fishermen’s protests. A fishing leader said it would reclaim about 5,000 acres of sea and add about 30,000 acres of restricted water; research cited by the press said it would directly hit sixteen fishing villages in Palghar. In December 2025 the Palghar collector called for a full social and livelihood survey of affected fishers. [n17; n36; FPJ; PIB] Vadhvan is not on this coast, but its waters are the same sea, and its effect on the fishermen of Arnala is one of the open questions of 2026.

Salt and mangroves

The salt pans

Behind the beaches and along the creeks the coast made salt. In 1837 Agashi traded “in grain, garden produce, and salt” with Gujarat and Bombay. [n40; Gaz XIV p. 2] In 1880–81 the Bassein salt division had seventeen works in six groups, with 11,374 pans on 1,439 acres and thirty owners, about eighteen per cent of the salt-pan area of Thana district and third after Uran and Ghodbandar. The salt tax was Rs 2 per Bengal maund of 82½ pounds; ground rent in Bassein was Rs 1½ an acre. [n21; n36; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 365–366] The workers were Agris; Kharvas also worked in Bassein; and “the Dublas work only on the salt-works near Bassein Road station”. Workmen at Bassein were paid Rs 5 to Rs 8 for a unit of eighty Bengal maunds. Since 1876, when the salt trade was opened to private enterprise, the quality of Bassein salt had improved with Gujarat Dubla labour, and Bassein and Ghodbandar salt together commanded about three-quarters of the trade of Uran. [n21; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 365–370] Bhayandar station’s goods traffic, mostly salt from the Rai Murdha pans three miles west, rose from 2,627 tons in 1873 to 19,770 in 1880. [n21; Gaz XIV p. 45] The Agris’ own subdivision of salt-makers, the Mithagris, takes its name from the work: mith is salt. [n08; Gaz XIII-1 p. 116] Salt revenue under the Portuguese and the Marathas was not found. [n36]

The precise locations of the salt pans by village, on this western shore or on the creek side, are not documented in the notes. [n11] Today about 2,000 acres of salt pans are cited for Vasai. [n08; n17; Outlook] Their future turned in the 2010s on the MMRDA’s draft Regional Plan for 2016–2036, which proposed a “growth centre”, with housing, sports, education and commerce, on 1,560 acres of former salt pans that had been a no-development zone. Residents pointed out that the land floods every monsoon. At least 40,000 objections came from Vasai-Virar (the activist Samir Vartak put the local figure at about 63,000), and Fr Francis D’Britto called the draft “builder-driven”. [n17; n11; The Quint; ICSF] Whether the growth centre survived into the sanctioned plan is not confirmed. [n17] Fraudulent sales of 208 acres of salt-pan land at Rai-Murdhe near Vasai have been attempted, and monsoon floods strand salt-pan workers at Vasai’s Mithagar. [n08; Asian Age; Deccan Herald]

Mangroves

The mangroves of the creeks and the coast are recorded mainly in their loss. Around 2018, residents said mangroves had been cleared from more than a hundred acres for buildings, and that “the sea moves further onto land every day”. [n17; ICSF/The Quint] An older and weakly sourced claim, from a blog repost of a 2009 newspaper story, put the Vasai creek’s mangroves at about 3,000 hectares over 47 kilometres, about half of them destroyed, with some two hundred acres cleared in one year on the Mira-Bhayandar side. [n17] Adani Electricity got permission to fell 209 mangroves near Vasai creek, against an initial estimate of seventy-nine, according to an opinion piece. [n17] In 2025 the river and wetland network SANDRP recorded assaults on mangroves and wetlands at Vasai creek among other sites. [n17; SANDRP, 8 Feb 2026] Around 2024 the climate activist Charan Bhatt asked the National Green Tribunal to act against debris dumping in Vasai-Virar’s wetlands, citing an ISRO remote-sensing study of wetland and mangrove decline. [n06; n26; FPJ] And a joint committee of the NGT reported in Original Application 201 of 2023 on reclamation of the Vasai coastline. [n17; NGT joint committee report] The committee’s findings were not summarised in the notes.

The coastal water bodies behind the beaches are brackish and linked underground, according to Macanzy Dabre of the Paryavaran Samvardhan Samiti; when the Yuva Vikas Sanstha restored ponds, the salt content (TDS) of nearby wells fell from 750 to 300 parts per million. [n17; Vikalp Sangam] The garden belt’s sweet water and the sea’s salt have always met under the sand. Those ponds, the bavkhal or bhowkal, were dug by family groups of five to sixty people for irrigation, fed by a shallow aquifer, and never used for drinking; a 2007 Minor Irrigation survey counted 479 of them in twenty-seven gram panchayats, and a 2013 survey found more than the records showed (Bhuigaon thirty-nine against sixteen, Nandakhal fifty-five against thirty-two, Wagholi fifty-four against sixteen). One study counted 108 lakes within a sixty-kilometre radius, of which only twenty-eight survived. [n17; Vikalp Sangam] When the ponds and mangroves go, the sea comes in.

The floods of the monsoon are the same story run backwards. In July 2018 Debi Goenka of the Conservation Action Trust said wetland reclamation in Thane and Vasai had “shrunk the natural drainage”, and Stalin Dayanand of Vanashakti called Vasai-Virar natural floodplains, with widespread wetlands and salt pans, unsuited to townships on raised fill; in one monsoon around 2017 a village was under water for five or six days and at least six people drowned. [n17; Asian Age via CAT; ICSF/The Quint] A NEERI and IIT-Bombay report followed the 2018 floods; its recommendations were reportedly never carried out. [n17; n06] The causal link between filled wetlands and floods is argued by activists and by the railway, not by any published study found in the notes. [n17]

The sea comes in: erosion and reclamation

The petition of 2013–14

The best-documented coastal case of the decade concerns the beaches themselves. On 29 April 2013 two groups, Vanashakti and the Harit Naisargik Vaibhav Bachao Abhiyaan, filed a public-interest petition about the reclamation and encroachment of the Vasai coastline. The Bombay High Court took cognizance on 16 September 2013. A Town Planning order of 18 December 2013 told municipal bodies not to allow the reclamation of wetlands. On 21 March 2014 the state’s Mangrove Cell, led by N. Vasudevan, Chief Conservator of Forests, visited the coast. The beaches named in the case were Kalamb, Rajouri (Rajodi), Arnala and Bhuigaon. [n45; FPJ, “Vasai coastline reclaimed, encroached, illegally”]

At Bhuigaon the report described a blocked nullah (drainage channel) and two illegal walls, about 500 feet and 300 feet long, that had killed mangroves; the reclamation site was less than two hundred metres from the beach. The article said that construction near the beach “thwarts all CRZ norms”. [n45; FPJ] The page that carries the report shows 1 June 2019 as its update time, so its exact publication date is uncertain. [n45] The outcome of the petition is not recorded in the notes.

The casuarinas of Bhuigaon, 2019

On 1 June 2019 the Free Press Journal reported that more than a hundred casuarina trees on the Vasai beaches, at Bhuigaon, had been badly damaged by repeated high tides. A land mafia was alleged to have burned and poisoned trees. The same report noted that tourist footfall at Bhuigaon had risen fivefold, and proposed a stone bed along the shore, “as was done at Arnala beach”. It did not mention CRZ. [n45; FPJ, 1 Jun 2019] So by 2019 the sea at Arnala had already been held back with stone, and the casuarina belts that gave Suruchi its name were failing further south. The planting history of the casuarinas, and the date and extent of the Arnala stone bed, were not found. [n45] The inference earlier research drew, and it is an inference, is that the loss of the casuarinas in 2019 links tourism, land pressure and erosion on one stretch of sand. [n45]

Sand

Sand mining appears in the record only as an accusation. After the drownings at Arnala in 2026, local people blamed sand mining for the depressions in the seabed that catch swimmers. [n45; FPJ] No study, permit or case on sand mining along this coast was found.

The Coastal Regulation Zone

India’s Coastal Regulation Zone rules restrict building within set distances of the high-tide line and protect mangroves and other coastal ecosystems. On this coast the CRZ appears in the notes only twice: in the 2013–14 report’s phrase that construction near Bhuigaon beach “thwarts all CRZ norms”, and in the absence of CRZ from the 2019 casuarina report. [n45] The specifics of the CRZ map for Vasai, which stretches of beach fall in which zone, and what development is allowed behind them, were not found. [n17] That is a real gap. Every question about resorts, reclamation and erosion on this coast turns on that map.

Weekend coast: tourism and resorts

From the 1990s, as Vasai-Virar filled with commuters and Mumbai’s own beaches grew crowded, the sands from Arnala to Suruchi became a weekend coast. The Free Press Journal’s “fivefold” rise in footfall at Bhuigaon by 2019 is the only number in the notes. [n45] Tourism guides dominate the online record of the beaches, and they describe them in the language of leisure: Rajodi “secluded”, Suruchi for sunrise walks, the Vasai Beach a “Mini Goa”. [n45; n39] Another Free Press Journal headline called Arnala beach “fast turning into dumping ground”. [n45]

Behind the sand, resorts and water parks multiplied. Their story enters the record through death. On about 28 July 2024 Milind More, son of a former Thane district chief of the Shiv Sena (UBT), died at a resort and water park (sources say at Arnala or at Navapur) after an argument; the Free Press Journal’s headline gives the cause of death as cardiac arrest. Chief Minister Eknath Shinde ordered action, and the municipal corporation razed unauthorised resort structures, including water slides, near Arnala beach. Mid-day published photographs of the demolition drive on 29 July 2024. [n45; FPJ; Mid-day, 29 Jul 2024 (titles only)] On 18 April 2026 a fifteen-year-old boy from Mumbai died after a fall from a slide at a water-park resort at Arnala. [n45; n06; n29; FPJ; vasaivirar.org] Whether the 2024 demolitions had touched that resort is not recorded.

The coast’s other modern visitors are film crews. Vasai Fort is a long-running film location (Josh, Khamoshi, Aag, Zero, the Kurbaan title song, and Coldplay’s “Hymn for the Weekend”, whose video also used Mumbai locations), and the police registered a case after a production company lit a stove inside the Franciscan church at the fort. [n13; n45; FPJ] No film shot at Arnala fort or on any of the Virar-side beaches was confirmed. [n45; Sceneloc8]

The sea takes: drownings

The beaches from Arnala to Rajodi are dangerous, and the newspapers of the last decade return to them again and again.

In August 2016 four teenagers on a picnic to Rajodi beach, along the Arnala coast, were pulled into the sea at high tide. Their bodies were found the next day. The two Business Standard/PTI reports of 11 and 12 August are known only from search summaries; the pages could not be opened. [n45; Business Standard/PTI]

On 22 March 2019 an IANS report headlined “Five Holi revellers drown in Maharashtra” surfaced in a search for Arnala; its location details were not read. [n45; Business Standard/IANS] It is recorded here because the search connected it to Arnala, not because the connection is confirmed.

On 19 March 2026 two sixteen-year-old boys drowned at Arnala beach while playing football; an accidental-death case was registered. In a separate case, a seventeen-year-old drowned and another youth went missing, found dead after a thirty-six-hour search, bringing that incident’s toll to two. After two drownings (the year is not confirmed in the reports), the Arnala Coastal Police imposed a ban on entering the sea until 30 September. Local people blamed sand mining for the depressions in the seabed; the Arnala sarpanch said that picnickers ignore the warnings. [n45; FPJ (headline level)] These reports were read at headline level, not in full.

The same geography appears in 2016 and in 2026: sand that slides, depressions in the bed, and a tide that comes in fast over a flat shore. Earlier research concluded that the Arnala–Rajodi stretch is the death-prone part of this coast, and that is the fairest reading of the reports, though no safety study confirms it. [n45] No count of drownings for any year, and no record of lifeguards or warning systems, was found.

The car at Kalamb, 2025

Not every story the sea tells ends in death. On 21 September 2025 a group of tourists drove a white Scorpio onto Kalamb beach, too close to the waterline. The tide came in and trapped the car in the surf. Local people waded out and pulled it free; the tourists were rescued and no one was hurt. The Free Press Journal ran the story with a video the next day. No ban on vehicles was reported. [n45; FPJ, 22 Sep 2025] It is a small event, but it is the whole modern coast in one picture: city visitors who do not know the tide, a beach used as a car park, and village people who know the sea and go into it to help.

Narali Purnima and the festivals of the sea

Coconut Day

The fishing year on this coast turns on one day. In the 1880s, the Gazetteer says, the local craft were hauled ashore from early June “to Coconut Day”, the full moon of the month of Shravan, in August. [n36; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 342–343] In June and July, when the boats could not go out, the Uraps and other Christian Kolis, like the Son Kolis, made new ropes, nets and sails. [n08] Coconut Day is Narali Purnima. Among the Bhois, the river fishermen, the Gazetteer names “Narli Purnima” as a chief holiday, the day of “going to the shore” to “worship the sea”, with Gauri in August and September and Shimga in February and March. [n08; Gaz XIII-1 p. 146] Today, as the Jio Institute’s exhibit on the Koli festival describes it from the Mumbai Koliwadas, Narali Purnima marks the end of the monsoon closure and the start of the new fishing season, with coconuts offered to the sea; All India Radio has reported fishing communities in Mumbai, Thane and Palghar celebrating it. [n08; Jio Institute exhibit; News on AIR]

No source specific to Arnala, Pachubandar, Rangaon, Kalamb or the Vasai Koliwada describes how Narali Purnima is kept there: which beach, which families, what songs, which boats are launched first. [n45; n08; n13] The festival’s place in the year is certain. Its local forms on this shore are unrecorded.

Bangles for the sea

The Son Koli wedding, as the Gazetteer recorded it, held the sea at its centre. The women wore glass bangles only on the left hand. “At their weddings the bangles intended for the right hand are consecrated and thrown into the sea, the ocean being invoked to take care of the husband”; on the right hand they wore silver instead. [n08; Gaz XIII-1 p. 149] Of all the customs in the notes, this one says most plainly what the sea meant to the fishing families of the coast: a provider and a danger, to be asked for a husband’s safe return on the day of the marriage itself. Whether the custom survives at Arnala is not known.

Gods of the shore

The Vaitis of north Bassein worshipped “Ram and the sea”, and kept only a coconut at home. [n08; Gaz XIII-1 p. 182] The Son Kolis’ household gods included Bhavani, Khanderao and Cheda; Cheda, a local spirit, was shared by Agri, Bhandari, Pachkalshi and Son Koli households. [n08] Jivdani, on the hill above Virar, is called the kuldevi of the Mangela, Koli and Bhandari communities (Chapter 27). [n08] Christian Kolis are found in Bassein and Agashi; they share customs such as the shakarpuda engagement with Hindu Kolis, and some revere Ekvira. [n08; Wikipedia: Koli Christians] At Arnala, Bhavani’s temple inside the fort, endowed in 1882, sits a short boat ride from the fishermen’s beach. [n10]

Shimga and the rest

The Kolis’ Holi, Shimga, can run for fifteen days from the day after Mahashivratri, and in Worli, Colaba and Vasai the Holi pyre is built of areca-nut branches. [n08; FPJ; Jio Institute] The Free Press Journal’s snippet about Arnala as a holy confluence of “three rivers”, where ashes are immersed, points to another sacred use of the shore, but it could not be confirmed. [n45] Koli songs (Koli geet), Koli dance at Arnala, and village jatras of the beach villages were not found in any source reached. [n13; n08]

What is still unrecorded

The beaches are the largest gap in the record of this coast. Earlier research said so plainly: of all the subjects of this book, the shore had the least written history. [n45] What follows is the list of what the record does not yet hold.

  • The beaches in the old record. No Portuguese or British mention of any individual beach was found. The 1882 Gazetteer has no beach entries; it treats the shore as fishing grounds and landings. [n45]
  • Village histories. For Navapur, Rangaon, Kalamb, Suruchi and Pachubandar, no history of founding, families, festivals or fishing exists in the notes; for Rajodi and Bhuigaon there are parish dates and a blog walk; for Rangaon an unsourced claim of pre-Portuguese shipbuilding. [n45; n11; n40]
  • Census. 2011 figures were not retrieved for Navapur, Rajodi, Koprad or Bhuigaon. Arnala’s 1881 population was not extracted from the Gazetteer. [n45; n11]
  • Arnala’s fort. The date 1516 and the builder Mahmud Begada are unconfirmed and in conflict with Begada’s death in 1511; the 23 May 1737 completion date rests on one site; the bastion count (ten listed, one standing) conflicts; the British dates (1781 capture or threat; 1817 or 1818) conflict; the Martello tower’s date (Portuguese or 1803) is unresolved; Shridatta Raut’s argument has not been read. [n45]
  • The Koli economy. No boat counts, landing figures, catch series, cooperative names or auction descriptions exist for Arnala, Pachubandar, Rangaon, Kalamb or the Koliwada. The CMFRI marine fisheries census tables for Palghar would supply some of them. [n17; n36]
  • Salt. Salt-pan locations by village, the workers’ numbers today, and any Portuguese or Maratha salt revenue are unrecorded. [n11; n36]
  • The 1930 salt satyagraha at Arnala. The primary record (Source Material vol. XI) has not been read; the leaders’ names and arrests are unknown. [n43]
  • Erosion. No measured erosion rates, no history of the casuarina plantations, no date for the Arnala stone bed, no outcome of the 2013 petition, no CRZ map. [n45; n17]
  • The sea’s festivals. Narali Purnima, Shimga, Koli songs and the Son Koli wedding customs are documented for the district or for Mumbai, but not for any village of this shore. [n08; n13]
  • The Marathi press. No article from Loksatta or Lokmat on the beaches could be retrieved; the village histories almost certainly lie there and in the memories of the fishing families. [n45]

From the beach

Walk the coast in November 2026 from south to north and the layers are all there. At the Bassein creek the dead Portuguese city stands above Killa Bandar. North of it the casuarinas of Suruchi, the damaged trees and illegal walls of Bhuigaon, the long sand of Rangaon and Kalamb where a car was caught by the tide, the Kupari village of Rajodi where four boys drowned, the resorts behind Navapur, and at last the Koli boats of Arnala drawn up opposite the island, with Baji Tulaji’s gateway, the octagonal tank of Trimbakeshvar and the twin tombs of Shahali and Hajiali within its walls. Every part of that shore has been fought over: by sultans and Portuguese, Marathas and British, builders and petitioners, tourists and the tide.

Inland from the beaches, in the gardens and the old villages, lived the people who made the plantain trade and the parish churches, the Kuparis whose story Part Seven tells. One of those villages, whose people told the Gazetteer’s compilers in 1882 that it stood on the site of old Sopara itself, is Gass.
