Period covered: the ways of life of the Kupari villages, from the first descriptions of the 1880s to November 2026, with their roots in the Samvedi world and the Portuguese parishes

In this chapter. How the villages lived: speech, dress, gold and glass, houses and wadis, the kitchen, songs and rites.

A feast in a roofless church

On Sunday 11 January 2026, hundreds of people gathered inside the walls of Vasai Fort for a Mass in a church that has had no proper roof since 1739. It was the Church of the Holy Name, the old Jesuit church of Baçaim, one of seven churches that once stood inside the fort. The Archaeological Survey of India, which guards the ruins, allows a Sunday service there. Fr Peter Fernandez presided. The feast was that of St Gonsalo Garcia, the son of a Portuguese soldier and a local mother, who had studied at the Jesuit college in that same compound before he went to Japan and was crucified at Nagasaki in 1597. People in Vasai call this day Killa cha Sann, the feast of the fort. [n07; Free Press Journal, 11 Jan 2026] [n09; FPJ, 11 Jan 2026]

The date is not fixed by the calendar of saints. The universal feast of St Gonsalo falls on 6 February, and the Indian bishops moved it to the 7th; but the fort feast falls on “the first Sunday nearest the neap tide after Christmas”. [n07; Archdiocese of Bombay; DNA, 12 Jan 2014] A feast set by the tide belongs to people who once came by water. On the same day, across the creeks at Erangal in Madh, the church of St Bonaventure, built by the Portuguese in 1575, destroyed in 1739 and repaired in the 1950s, held its own feast, and families camped there overnight. “People from Vasai and Versova came by boat,” one of them, Bosco Patel, told the newspaper. [n07; FPJ, 11 Jan 2026] At these feast-fairs families arrive the evening before and camp in enclosures; they cook the East Indian moile and stews of meat and of vegetables such as kohlrabi, navalkol; and pilgrims, the paper recalled, once came by animal cart or by boat. [n09; FPJ, 11 Jan 2026] In 2014 the youth of the Koliwada decorated the old church for the feast. [n07; DNA, 12 Jan 2014] In 1987 the church had a temporary roof put up for the annual Mass. [n09; The Examiner 1987 via Goanet]

That scene holds most of what this chapter is about. A saint’s feast that is also a fair. A date set by the sea. Food cooked in the open by families who have come a long way. A Catholic liturgy in a ruin that is also a national monument. And a name for the day, Killa cha Sann, “the fort’s festival”, that is not Portuguese, though the feast is, but Marathi as the villages say it. [n09; FPJ, 11 Jan 2026] The Kuparis, the Christian Samvedis of north Vasai, were made in the centuries that the last three chapters described: a Samvedi and Pachkalshi farming people of the old garden island of Sopara, baptised by Franciscans and Jesuits between the 1530s and about 1620, left without their Portuguese masters in 1739, ruled by the Marathas and then the British, and counted, when they were counted at all, as “Native Christians”, “Portuguese Christians” or “rural East Indians”. This chapter turns from what happened to them to how they lived: what they spoke, what they wore, what they built and cooked and sang, and how they marked a birth, a marriage, a death and the turning of the year. It also asks, at each step, what their Hindu neighbours, the Samvedi Brahmins and the Vadvals, did with the same moments.

What can be known, and from whom

Before the story begins, the reader is owed a plain account of the evidence, because this is the chapter of the book where the evidence is thinnest and most one-sided.

There is no period ethnography of the Kuparis by name. The research for this book searched the full scanned text of the 1882 Gazetteer of the Bombay Presidency, Enthoven’s Tribes and Castes of Bombay, Grierson’s Linguistic Survey of India, the census reports of 1901, 1911 and 1931, Gerson da Cunha’s books and the Jesuit letters of Documenta Indica. The word “Kupari” does not appear in any of them. [n34; Headline finding] What the period record does contain is the Gazetteer’s 1882 chapter on the “Native Christians” of the Thana district as a whole, with a handful of points marked as specific to Bassein, and its page and a half on the Hindu Samvedis. [n34; section 5] Read side by side, those two accounts give a documented baseline, a picture of village life in the 1880s against which later descriptions can be checked. [n34; Takeaway, section 5] Chapter 35 set out the Christian chapter of the Gazetteer as a whole, and Chapter 33 set out the Samvedi page; this chapter returns to both in detail, theme by theme.

Almost everything else about Kupari customs comes from the community itself. A blog written in 2009 by Rajan D’Mello gives the names of the life-cycle rites, the dress, the food. A blog of 2007 by Sachin Mendes gives the villages and the population estimate. A local site, Vasaiker, describes the Nandakhal songs. A 2026 article on the site All About Vasai Virar discusses the red cap and the red lugda. A Marathi newspaper, Saamana, and the Marathi Wikipedia page on the Samvedi dialect describe the speech and its scholars. [n07] [n13] [n20] [n34] These are the community speaking about itself. Much of it is affectionate, some of it boastful; none of it is footnoted. The research notes for this book say so each time, and so will this chapter: where a custom is known only from a community source, the text says so. That is not a judgement that the community is wrong. It is a statement that nobody outside it has yet written down what it knows. The author of this book is a Kupari from Gass, and for that reason, as the chapter on Gass says, is bound to be more careful here, not less. [K7]

There is one more caution. Several of the best modern descriptions are of the “East Indian” Catholics in general, the umbrella name adopted in Bombay in 1887 for the Catholics of Bombay, Salsette and Bassein. [n07; Catholic Connect] [n34; Mendiratta pp. 234–236] Sources about East Indian dress, East Indian cooking and the East Indian dialect are often about the villages of Salsette, Uttan, Gorai or Malwani, not about the Samvedi Christians of the Vasai north. When this chapter uses them, it says whose customs they describe, and it does not assume that what is true of Uttan is true of Nirmal.

The speech of the villages

A dialect without a name in 1905

Every Kupari village speaks, or spoke within living memory, a dialect that outsiders and insiders now call Kadodi, and also Samvedi. English Wikipedia, drawing on Ethnologue, describes it as a speech of the Samvedi Brahmin and Kupari communities of Vasai, an intermediate dialect between Marathi and Konkani with Portuguese influence in its vocabulary and sounds, and some Goan features, traditionally oral, written in Devanagari when it is written at all, with no literary tradition of its own. It gives the code ISO 639-3 smv and the Glottolog code samv1238. [n13; Wikipedia: Kadodi language] [n22; Wikipedia] Saamana, in a Marathi article of 22 March 2026 by Varnika Kakde, says that it is spoken by Samvedi Brahmins, Pachkalshes and Samvedi Christians together, that it mixes Marathi, Konkani and Portuguese with some Gujarati, and that it is spoken with a sing-song intonation; it calls the community “a singing community” with a rich store of folk songs, proverbs and stories. [n20; Saamana]

That definition, a single speech shared by Hindu Brahmins, Hindu Pachkalshis and Christians, is modern. In 1905 it did not exist on paper. George Grierson’s Linguistic Survey of India, the great colonial inventory of the subcontinent’s speech, recorded two separate things. Under “Samvedi” he described the speech of the Hindu Samvedis, “husbandmen and gardeners in Bassein” and a place his scanned text renders as “Alabim”, about 2,700 people, speaking “a mixed dialect, the chief components of which are the current Marathi of Thana and vulgar Gujarati”. [n34; LSI VII] The 1882 Gazetteer said the Samvedis were found “only in Bassein and Mahim”, which suggests that “Alabim” is Mahim, the Thana taluka, and not Alibag, as one earlier reading guessed. [n34; Corrections] Under “Kinstav”, separately, Grierson described the speech of the native Roman Catholics of Thana, about 26,600 of them, Salsette included: ordinary Marathi with a few Portuguese words, “nothing else than the usual Marathi of the district”. [n34; LSI VII] [n22; LSI VII] He did not record a Christian Samvedi dialect. [n34; Inferences, section 4] Either the Christian Samvedis of Nirmal and Agashi were folded into his Kinstav count, or their speech was not sampled, or both. His sample of Kinstav, a version of the Parable of the Prodigal Son, came from somewhere in the district; the notes do not say from which village. [n22]

So the question of how Kadodi formed is partly a question of how two things Grierson kept apart, a Gujarati-tinged Samvedi speech and a Portuguese-tinged Christian Marathi, came to be one dialect in the mouths of the Kuparis. No linguist has published a historical study of it. [n34; Gaps, section 4] What can be done is to lay out the layers the evidence shows, from the bottom up, and mark the strength of each.

The Marathi base and the Gujarati layer

The base is the Marathi of the north Konkan coast. Grierson said that Samvedi verb forms agreed with Thana Marathi, and every later description agrees that Kadodi is a form of Marathi, or of the Marathi–Konkani group. [n22; LSI VII] [n13; Wikipedia: Marathi-Konkani languages]

Over the base lies a Gujarati layer. Grierson listed the features. In Samvedi, ch and chh become s and then h, so that “son” is hakra and “field” is hit. The genitive ends in -ho, -ha or -a. Original s becomes h. Strong masculine nouns end in -o, “as in Gujarati and Konkani”. The dative ends in -do, as in mansa-do, “to a man”, or else in the Marathi -la or -ti. The third person masculine past ends in -o. His verdict was a “mechanical mixture of Konkan Marathi and Gujarati”. [n22; LSI VII] [n34; LSI VII] He set Samvedi with two neighbours, Vadval and Phudgi, as “three small dialects in Thana, which still have preserved many of the characteristic features of Gujarati Bhili”. [n22; LSI VII] [n41; LSI VII]

The 1882 Gazetteer, a generation earlier, had already noticed that the Samvedis spoke “incorrect Marathi”, and printed three examples in a footnote: áde for kothe, “where”; hád for kā, “why”; geltu for gelā hotā, “had gone”. [n08; Gazetteer 1882, p. 82 footnote] An official in 1882 heard these as errors. A linguist would hear them as a dialect’s own forms.

Where did the Gujarati come from? The notes offer two possibilities and do not choose. It may be a trace of a Gujarat-linked origin for the Samvedi farmers, in the way that the Vadvals’ own speech, with the same sound changes, fits the tradition that the Pachkalshis came from Gujarat. [n34; Inferences, section 4] [n41; Inferences, section 8] Or it may be the product of centuries of contact on a coast that faced Gujarat, traded with it, and was ruled from it for over a century before 1534. [n35; Inferences] Grierson himself noted that in the north of Thana Marathi was “gradually superseded by Gujarati”. [n22; LSI VII] The Samvedi, Vadval and Mangela dialects all sat on that line. [n35; section 10]

The sound laws, then and now

The most striking thing about Kadodi to a speaker of standard Marathi is what happens to the hissing sounds. The rule can be stated simply: ch tends to become s or h, and s and sh tend to become h. It is the same rule Grierson heard about 1900, and it is still alive. [n22; Inferences]

The Marathi Wikipedia page on the Samvedi dialect gives the examples a modern reader is most likely to hear. Sukeli, the famous sun-dried banana of Agashi, becomes hukeli (हुकेळी). Sheni, dried cow-dung cakes, becomes heni (हेनी). [n20; mr.wikipedia: सामवेदी बोली] The 2024 dictionary paper discussed below gives cow dung as haen (हॅन), against Marathi shen (शेण). [n22; WAT 2024] The same paper found that श and स “are commonly replaced by ह”, and that Kadodi’s own sound inventory lacks the vowels ऐ and औ and the consonants च, छ, ण and ष; and that it prefers plain dentals, त and द, to aspirated ones, थ and ध. [n22; WAT 2024]

The vowels shift as well. Standard Marathi ālo, “came”, is ālô in Samvedi (आलॉ), and jevlo, “ate”, becomes jivlô (जिवलॉ). [n20; mr.wikipedia] That rounded open o, written with the sign ॉ, runs through the dialect.

The neighbouring Vadval speech has the same core rule. Grierson wrote that in Vadval s and sh become h, “probably like the sound of ch in loch”: hona, “gold”; hambhar, “hundred”. Ch becomes s or h: sakar, “servant”, sand, “moon”, sar, “four”, for Marathi chakar, chand, char. [n41; LSI VII pp. 145–146] A modern relay of the People’s Linguistic Survey of India gives Vadvali hakar for sakhar, “sugar”, hanj for sanj, “evening”, and himpi for shimpi, “tailor”. [n41; PLSI via indiandistricts] The two dialects share the rule; the people of Bhuigaon told a visitor in 2011 that their Kadodi is nevertheless different from the Vadval speech of the Vasai villages to the south, and a Vasai community site says the same of the songs. [n07; Mumbai Paused] [n13; Vasaiker]

What the sentences sound like

The best published Kadodi sentences come from the 2024 paper. “He makes a living by working as a labourer” is, in Kadodi, tou majuri karon pout bhartae (तॉ मजुरी करॉन पॉट भरतॅ); in standard Marathi it is to majuri karun pot bharto. “He is sleeping” is tou nejlay (तॉ नजलॅ), where Marathi says to zhopla ahe. Kadodi often drops the present-tense “is”, Marathi ahe, Kadodi hai. [n22; WAT 2024]

The Marathi Wikipedia page gives a small dialogue that anyone who has been fed in a Samvedi house will know. The host asks, in Marathi, jevlat ka?, “have you eaten?” The guest answers jivyāsā hāy (जिव्यासा हाय): “I’ve eaten.” [n20; mr.wikipedia] The page tags part of its material “citation needed”, and the research notes treat it as medium-weak. [n20; Method] But the example agrees with the sound laws documented elsewhere.

Grierson’s specimens of the Prodigal Son parable in Samvedi (his No. 37) and Vadval (No. 36) are the earliest connected texts in these dialects. The Vadval one, printed with an interlinear gloss, begins Eka don por hote, “One man two sons were”, and has phrases such as ghan dukal, “a mighty famine”. The Samvedi one is printed in Devanagari, and the scanned text of it is unreadable; the page image would be needed to quote it. [n22; LSI VII] [n41; LSI VII pp. 146–147] Somewhere in that unread page is the oldest written Samvedi sentence known to the research for this book.

The Portuguese words

The third layer is Portuguese, and it came through the church. Grierson’s list of Portuguese words in the Christians’ Marathi of 1905 is short and telling: pay, father; may, mother; mass; padron, godfather; madron, godmother. He noted also the English word estate. [n34; LSI VII] [n22; LSI VII] These are words of the parish and the family: the people who carried a child to the font, the service that the whole village attended, and the names a child called its parents. The research notes draw the obvious conclusion: the Portuguese strand entered through the parish, not through settlement or trade. [n34; Inferences, section 4]

A Free Press Journal article of 14 March 2024, about the East Indian or “Vasai Marathi” dialect, adds the words that a Kupari household still uses. Mai and pai for mother and father, where standard Marathi says aai and baba. Kadair for a chair, from the Portuguese cadeira. Zanel for a window, from janela. Sukhala for a toast, “cheers”. And Ramacha Pass for the Palm Sunday procession, which the paper derives from Portuguese ramo, branches, and passo, steps. [n13; FPJ, 14 Mar 2024] The article is about the Vasai dialect of the East Indian Catholics in general, not about Kadodi alone, but these words are part of the shared Christian vocabulary of the Vasai villages.

The names of the rites themselves, given in the 2009 community blog and discussed in detail later in this chapter, carry the same stamp: Baltim for baptism, Komsar for confession, Cominyao for First Communion, Chrisma for confirmation. The blog calls them Portuguese-derived. [n07; D’Mello blog] The resemblance of each to a Portuguese church word is plain enough, baptismo, confessar, comunhão, crisma; but that is an observation from this book’s side, not a derivation any source in the notes has worked out letter by letter.

How much Portuguese is in Kadodi is disputed. One source says “very few” words; another calls the Kupari dialects a mix of Gujarati, Marathi and Indo-Portuguese. [n13; Wikipedia: Kupari] English Wikipedia’s page on the Marathi–Konkani languages says Vadvali and Samvedi have a higher share of Portuguese-derived words than ordinary Marathi. [n13] The 2024 paper calls Kadodi “a mix of Konkani, Gujarati, Marathi and Indo-Portuguese (now extinct)”. [n22; WAT 2024] That “now extinct” Indo-Portuguese raises a question the evidence cannot settle. The Portuguese of the Northern Province developed a creole, called Norteiro, which the Portuguese scholar Dalgado described in 1906. [n22; Cardoso 2006] The historian Mendiratta writes that in the 1880s “Indo-Portuguese creoles were more widespread in Salsette and Bassein and other areas, and survived until later”, and that most uneducated East Indians of Salsette and Bassein spoke “either in Marathi or in Indo-Portuguese creole”. [n34; Mendiratta p. 241] The 1882 Gazetteer said only that “a few know and a considerable number understand an ungrammatical Portuguese”, and that Portuguese was the home speech in some of the higher families. [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 204] Grierson’s Kinstav specimen of 1905 was ordinary Marathi. [n22] Whether the Kupari villages ever spoke a creole, and whether some of Kadodi’s Portuguese came through it rather than straight from the pulpit, no source read for this book can say. [n22; Gaps]

The church’s own languages left a further trace. The Gazetteer recorded that sermons were “either in Portuguese or in Marathi”, that prayer books existed in both, and that Latin was the language of the church. [n34; Gazetteer XIII pp. 204, 207] It also noted that the Christa Purana, a Marathi “Life of Christ” it dated to 1659, was “still well known and much read by the people”, and Grierson printed a Koli specimen from “the old Puran by Vas de Guimaraens”, another Christian Marathi text. [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 207 n.1] [n22; LSI VII] A Christian literature in Marathi, sung and read in the villages for two centuries before 1882, is part of the soil Kadodi grew in.

How many speak it

The numbers are few and not comparable. Grierson’s 2,700 Samvedis of 1905 counted only Hindu Samvedis; the Christians of Samvedi descent were counted elsewhere, under Kinstav, if at all. [n22; Inferences] Today, Ethnologue, as reported by Wikipedia, gives about 60,000 speakers of Kadodi, a figure for 2016. [n13; Wikipedia: Kadodi language] [n07] Saamana gives about 50,000, in sixteen villages of north Vasai. [n20; Saamana] The 2024 paper repeats about 60,000. [n22; WAT 2024] The community blogs estimate the Kuparis themselves at “about 40,000 to 45,000” (2007) or “around 60,000+” (2015). [n34; section 8] The 60,000 speakers and the 60,000 Kuparis may be the same figure travelling in a circle. No census counts either: the Indian census folds such dialects into Marathi, and it does not count Kuparis. [n22; Gaps] [n34; section 8] The research notes conclude that the defensible statement is “tens of thousands”, flagged as unsourced. [n34; Inferences, section 8]

Where it is spoken is better documented. Saamana and the Marathi Wikipedia page list sixteen villages: Bhuigaon, Wagholi, Kofrad, Gass, Umrale, Bolinj, Gavdhani, Vatar, Valunje, Vanda (Mardes), Padhai, Karmale, Navale, Somadi, Bankar and Mandpe. [n20] Saamana also says it is spoken in twelve coastal villages “from Agashi to Bhuigaon”. [n20; Saamana] The community’s own list of Kupari villages, from the 2007 and 2009 blogs, is Nirmal, Agashi, Nandakhal, Gass, Bhuigaon, Umrale, Nanbhat, Shrilaiwadi, Bolinj, Mardes, Wagholi, Gomes Ali and Rajodi. [n39; vasaikupari blog; D’Mello blog] The two lists overlap but are not the same, because one is a list of a dialect’s villages, Hindu and Christian, and the other of a Christian community’s. Together they draw the old garden island of Sopara and the strip north to Agashi: the “garden or agar of Sopara” that the 1882 Gazetteer described, with Gass, Nirmal, Vagholi, Bolinj, Koprad, Umbrala and Mardesh in it. [n39; Gazetteer XIV pp. 314–315]

Saamana’s claim that the dialect has been spoken in north Vasai “since about the sixth century” has no support in any source in the notes. [n20; Saamana] The research notes mark it as unsupported, and so does this book.

The name “Kadodi” is itself unexplained. Its origin is unknown to the researchers whose notes this book uses. [n34; Gaps, section 4]

A language at risk

Every source that describes the Vasai Christian dialects in the 2020s describes them as fading. The Free Press Journal’s 2024 article called the East Indian or Vasai Marathi dialect “declining, if not in danger of extinction”, spoken mostly by senior people. [n13; FPJ, 14 Mar 2024] English Wikipedia says the neighbouring Vadvali, or Phudagi, is “endangered” as young people stop using it as a first language, a claim it tags “citation needed”; Glottolog lists Phudagi as “threatened”. [n41; Glottolog; Wikipedia: Phudagi language] For Kadodi itself the notes found no endangerment rating beyond these descriptive statements, and Ethnologue’s entry was not seen. [n22; Gaps]

The pressure is old. Enthoven wrote in 1920 that the Son Koli dialect was “gradually obsolescent under the grinding of the Local Board school and the newspaper”. [n35; section 10] The same forces, school Marathi, print Marathi, and later Hindi and English from the city, have pressed on Kadodi ever since. At Bhuigaon in 2011 the community’s correspondence and its magazine were in standard Marathi. [n07; Mumbai Paused] The written language of the Kupari villages has long been the Marathi of the school and the parish bulletin, not the Kadodi of the kitchen.

The people who wrote it down

Against that, a handful of people have done the work of recording.

Paul Rumav, a teacher from Nandakhal, edited four books listed on Marathi Wikipedia: Samvedi Lokgeete (Samvedi folk songs), Madhachya Ghagari (Pots of honey), Samvedi Khristi Samaj (The Samvedi Christian community) and Vasai Parisaratil Mhani (Proverbs of the Vasai area). [n20; mr.wikipedia] None of them was read for this book. They are, the research notes judge, the most likely printed source for the Samvedi and Kupari wedding songs. [n20; Inferences]

Dr Naresh Naik of Virar wrote Samvedi Boli – Sanrachana ani Swarup (The Samvedi dialect: structure and form), which won the Krishna Mukund Ujalambkar Smriti award and a P. Na. Go. Kalelkar award in 2018. [n20; mr.wikipedia] It is the one book-length description of the dialect’s grammar the notes found. It too was not read.

A magazine called Kadodi, with a website, kadodi.in, is cited by Marathi Wikipedia and by the 2024 paper; its archive survives on the Wayback Machine. [n20; Inferences] [n22; WAT 2024]

In 2024 a paper presented at the Workshop on Asian Translation (WAT) of the Association for Computational Linguistics described “Suman”, a Kadodi–Marathi–English dictionary of 949 entries and 942 sentence triples, made by native Kadodi speakers. It claimed to be “the first work on machine translation involving Kadodi”, and it cited earlier writing on the community: Russell and Cohn (2012), Francis Correia (1992) and Pereira (2007). [n22; WAT 2024] The research notes give the paper’s authors two ways, as “Dabre, Dabre and Pereira” in one note and “Kumar et al.” in another; the paper is the same ACL Anthology item, 2024.wat-1.3, and the discrepancy should be settled against its title page. [n34; ACL Anthology] [n22] Its examples are those quoted above. A thousand words and a thousand sentences are not a language’s full record, but they are the first time Kadodi has been written down in a form a machine can learn from, and the work was done by its own speakers.

For the wider East Indian dialect there is more organised effort. The Mobai Gaothan Panchayat is compiling an East Indian Dictionary, with Gleason Barretto as ideator and founder editor and Amarylisa Gonsalves as editor; a second revised edition was targeted for the end of 2024. [n13; FPJ, 14 Mar 2024] The singer Royston D’Silva writes songs in the dialect. [n13] Fr Victor Dalmet, a priest from Uttan, composes songs in the “East Indian dialect of Marathi”, and the Mobai Gaothan Panchayat named him “East Indian Priest of the Year” in 2023; the article about him covers the Uttan, Gorai and Manori dialects and does not mention Kadodi. [n34; FPJ, 30 Nov 2023] On 15 June 2024 the same Panchayat asked Cardinal Gracias to use the East Indian dialect in Mass and hymns, among other cultural demands; the report does not mention Kupari or Kadodi either. [n34; FPJ, 16 Jun 2024] These efforts are real, and they show what can be done; but they are mostly the work of the southern and Salsette East Indians, and the northern Samvedi speech has had a smaller share of them.

Dress: the red and the checked

What the community remembers

Ask an older Kupari what the community used to wear, and the answer is almost always the same pair of images: the men in red caps, the women in red checked saris. The 2009 community blog gives the fullest description. Kupari men wore a dhoti, a white shirt, a black sleeveless jacket and a red cap, with an earring in the left ear. Kupari women wore a red blouse with a red checked kashta sari. [n07; D’Mello blog] The kashta is the sari draped with the back pleats tucked between the legs, the working drape of the Marathi-speaking countryside, and the colour of the Kupari women’s sari gave it a second name, the lal lugda, the red lugda. [n34; All About Vasai Virar, 29 Jun 2026]

That description comes from the community, and no period source confirms it in those words. [n07; Gaps] It describes, almost certainly, the dress of the generation before the blog’s writer: the grandparents of the 1950s and 1960s, before the turn away from farming that the same blogs date to the late 1960s and early 1970s. [n07; D’Mello blog] It is a portrait of a farming people in its best everyday clothes, the men’s jacket and cap over a dhoti that let them work the wadis, the women’s checked sari tucked for the fields and the wells.

A Loksatta column, “Vasaitil Khristayan”, by Disha Khatu, reportedly described the traditional dress of the Samvedi (Kupari), Somvanshi (Vadval), East Indian and Koli Christians of Vasai side by side. Only a short teaser of it was readable for this book. [n34; LinkedIn post] Its existence shows that the dress of the Christian communities of Vasai is understood locally as a set of distinct uniforms, each marking a community, and that the Kupari red is one of them.

What the record of 1882 shows

The 1882 Gazetteer describes the dress of the Thana Christians in general, and it complicates the community picture in useful ways.

The men. “The well-to-do wear European dress, generally black.” The poorer wore “tight trousers of coloured cotton cloth coming to the knee, and an inner jersey and cotton jacket”. On the head, “cart-drivers and husbandmen wear a long cylindrical white cotton hat or a woollen night-cap with or without a checked kerchief tied round the temples, fishermen wear red broadcloth caps”. In another passage the Gazetteer called “the tall white-cloth cap worn by the men of some of the lower orders” picturesque. [n34; Gazetteer XIII pp. 204–205] So in the Gazetteer’s general picture, the Christian husbandman wore a white hat; the red cap belonged to the Christian fisherman.

The women. “Almost all the women dress in local Hindu fashion.” Among the poor the robe was tight and above the knee; among the upper classes it fell full to the ankle. Married women drew the end of the robe over the right shoulder, “not like most Hindus over the right temple”. The robe was “generally of cotton and in colour dark purple, green, or black”. For church, “women cover themselves with a white sheet-like cotton robe … falling from the head in free outward curves”. [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 205] The Gazetteer does not mention red or checks.

At a wedding, the lower-class bride wore “the Hindu robe falling to the feet” over a white overall, and went to church in a palanquin, the duli. The groom walked under a long silk umbrella; in the lower classes he wore “the full European dress of bygone days, a scarlet or black military coat with cocked hat, epaulets, knee-breeches”, a costume hired for five to ten rupees. The Times of India of 9 March 1881, quoted in the Gazetteer, described a groom with a sword “preserved … as an heirloom”. [n34; Gazetteer XIII pp. 211–214] For one morning of his life the village farmer dressed as a Portuguese officer of the eighteenth century, and his family kept the sword.

Set beside the community description, the 1882 account shows two things. The Christian women dressed like their Hindu neighbours, in the local robe, with a difference in how the end was drawn. And the colours the Gazetteer recorded, dark purple, green and black, are not the red the community remembers. Either the Kupari red came later, or it was a northern Samvedi custom the Gazetteer’s general account missed, or it was a festival dress rather than a daily one. None of the sources read for this book decides between those.

The East Indian lugra

For the women’s sari the best modern description is of the East Indian lugra in general. A 2017 essay by the East Indian Memory Co. describes it as a hand-draped ten-yard cotton sari “with a distinctive checked weave and lined border”, worn across the north Konkan, in Bassein, Bombay and Salsette. Koli women tucked it tighter; paddy-farming women wore it shorter. There were silk lugras, reshmi, with gold zari borders. Once it was worn every day by all classes; now, the essay says, it is “a novelty item”, kept for events such as the Moya, a pre-wedding rite, and Umbra-cha Paani, the fetching of the bride’s or groom’s bathing water. [n07; East Indian Memory Co., 1 Dec 2017] The essay is about the East Indians of the whole region, not about Kuparis. But its checked weave and its short paddy-farmer’s drape match the community’s picture of the Kupari kashta, and its account of the sari’s retreat into wedding rites matches what Kupari families describe.

Among the Kolis north of Bassein the same sari was called lugat, lugda or lugra, and the men wore the kantopi, an ear cap. [n07; Jio Institute exhibit]

The red cap: whose was it?

Of all the questions about Kupari dress, the red cap has drawn the sharpest argument.

A polemical article by a local Hindu writer, Shrish Vaze, in Swarajya in 2019, claimed that the Portuguese made converts wear red caps (the men) and red saris (the women) as markers. [n07; Swarajya] It gave no source. On that reading, the Kupari red is a badge of conversion, imposed from outside.

The 1882 Gazetteer does not support it. Its page on the Hindu Samvedis says the men “wear short waistcloths, a shoulder cloth … and red broadcloth caps like Telegu Brahmans; on high days white turbans, coats and waistcloths with silk borders”. [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 82] [n08; Gazetteer 1882, p. 82] The Hindu Samvedis, who were never converted, wore red caps in 1882. So did the Christian fishermen. [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 205] So did the Son Kolis, whose men wore “a loincloth, waistcloth, woollen jacket and red broadcloth cap”, and the Mangela fishermen, whose men also wore a red broadcloth cap. [n08; Gazetteer 1882, pp. 147–149] The Bhandari toddy-tappers wore “a scarlet waistcoat and skull-cap at work”. [n08; Gazetteer 1882, p. 150] And in 1909 the Gazetteer of Bombay City and Island described the Hindu Vadvals’ head-dress as “either the Koli cap or a large red turban”. [n41; Bombay City Gazetteer I, pp. 240–241]

Red on the head, in other words, was a coastal habit in the north Konkan across Hindu and Christian, Brahmin, farmer and fisher. The research notes conclude that the red cap “looks like a shared regional Brahmin-farmer cap, not a convert’s mark”, and they add, rightly, that this is an inference from a few sentences that should be checked against drawings and photographs. [n34; Inferences, section 5] The modern Sahapedia summary, relaying East Indian sources, gives the red cap and red sari to the Christian Vadvals as well. [n41; Sahapedia] If the red cap marked anything, it marked a region and a class of people who worked the land and the sea, not a religion.

That is not to say the colour meant nothing to the Kuparis. A community that kept the red cap when its Hindu neighbours, by the twentieth century, had largely given it up might well come to see it as its own. The 2026 article on All About Vasai Virar treats the red cap and red lugda as “the identity of the Kupari community”, the story of the community told through two garments. [n34; All About Vasai Virar] What a cap meant to the people who wore it is a different question from where it came from, and the sources answer only the second, and that only partly.

The earring in the left ear

The single gold earring in a man’s left ear, which the 2009 blog gives as part of Kupari men’s dress, has no period source in the notes and no explanation. [n07; D’Mello blog] Among the Hindu Samvedis in 1882, boys wore silver ornaments, but the Gazetteer does not describe men’s earrings. [n08; Gazetteer 1882, p. 82] It is recorded here as the community records it.

Dress today

The 2026 article says that the red cap and the red lugda have “largely disappeared from everyday life”, and it names the causes: urbanisation, education, migration and the media. It lists ideas for preserving them, but it names no group, event or person doing so. [n34; All About Vasai Virar, 29 Jun 2026] The notes found no news coverage of a revival group for Kupari dress. [n07; Gaps] What survives, by every account, survives at weddings and at feasts, the same retreat into ritual that the East Indian lugra has made. [n07; East Indian Memory Co.]

Gold, silver and glass

Jewellery is the part of Kupari dress for which the evidence is weakest. The research notes found no source at all on Kupari jewellery as such. [n07; Gaps] [n20; Gaps] What the 1882 Gazetteer offers is the ornaments of the Thana Christian women in general, and those of the Hindu Samvedi women, and the overlap between them.

The Christian women, the Gazetteer said, wore gold cockle-shell earrings, silver double-loop necklaces, glass bangles and silver anklets, vales. [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 205] Its footnote list of their ornaments includes the mugdya and the gathe. [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 205 n.1] The Hindu Samvedi women wore, for the head, the mudaga khudi; for the ear, the lavanga bali, mugdya and gáthe; for the neck, the putlyáchi mál, the vajratik, and necklaces of flint, coral and gold beads; for the feet, tode, vále and phule. [n08; Gazetteer 1882, p. 82] So mugdya, gathe and vale appear in both lists. The scanned text is rough, and the reading should be checked against the printed page; but if it holds, Hindu and Christian Samvedi women of the 1880s wore ornaments with the same names. [n34; section 5] That is shared material culture: not proof of common descent, but what common descent would leave behind.

Jewellery carried a married woman’s status, and its removal marked a widow. The Christian widow gave up her bangles, the mugdya, the poth necklace and her earrings, and “unless she is very young, never wears them again”. [n34; Gazetteer XIII pp. 205, 214–215] At marriage the groom gave the bride a gold necklace that she wore while her husband lived. [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 214] The dowry, when there was one, a settlement on the bride of fifty to five hundred rupees and up to five thousand among the rich, “was paid in ornaments”. [n34; Gazetteer XIII pp. 211–214] A woman’s gold was her property, her security and her record of her own life’s turning points, and much of what she owned was worn.

The modern record says only that gold prices have pushed Koli women toward imitation jewellery. [n07; Jio Institute exhibit] What Kupari brides wear now, and which old pieces survive in family boxes, is a gap that families themselves could fill.

Houses, wells and wadis

The house

The Gazetteer’s two descriptions of houses, made in 1881–82, match each other closely. The Hindu Samvedis “live in one-storied houses with walls of wood planking and tiled roofs”. [n08; Gazetteer 1882, p. 82] The Christians lived in “substantial tile-roofed houses with walls of wooden planks, mud, or brick and stone”, with furniture according to class. [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 204] A plank-walled house under a tiled roof, one storey high: that was the north Vasai farmhouse of the 1880s, Hindu or Christian.

The Vadvals of the Bombay island in 1909 lived differently, in huts of split bamboo plastered with mud, with a verandah, a central room with a swing-cot, a devghar for the household gods, a kitchen, and a tulsi plant in front. [n41; Bombay City Gazetteer I, pp. 240–241] The Pachkalshis of the Thana district in 1882 had houses of one or two storeys, with mud or brick walls, thatched or tiled. [n41; Gazetteer XIII-1, p. 130] The difference is partly one of wealth and partly of place. The Gazetteer called the Christians’ houses “substantial”, and said that some of their villages were “as rich as any in the Thana district”. [n37; Gaz. XIII-I pp. 204, 216, cited in K3]

The research notes found no source describing Kupari house architecture in detail: the layout of a wadi house, its verandah, its rooms, the place of the well. [n07; Gaps] What a Kupari house looked like inside in 1950 or 1900 is known, as far as this book’s sources go, only from the 1882 sentence and from memory.

Blessing the ground and the house

Two rites mark the building of a house, according to the 2009 community blog. Paya bharni, the filling of the foundation, is a foundation ceremony at which jewellery is buried and a coconut broken. The blog likens it to the Hindu bhoomi pujan, the worship of the ground before building. Bejimat is the blessing of a new house, a housewarming at which the priest blesses the home. [n07; D’Mello blog] These are known only from the community source. The breaking of a coconut at a foundation is a custom of the whole Konkan, Hindu and Christian; that the Kuparis kept it, and placed gold in the earth under a Christian house, is one of the clearest small signs of how much of the older ritual world walked into the church with the converts.

The 2011 visitor to Bhuigaon recorded another: crosses placed on the front doors of houses. [n07; Mumbai Paused] The cross on the lintel does for a Christian house what other marks do for a Hindu one, a guard at the threshold.

The wadi

The house stood in a wadi, a garden-orchard, and the wadi stood among wells. The Bhuigaon visitor of 2011 counted about forty large irrigation wells, bhowkals, behind a steel cross; they watered bananas, vegetables and flowers, and they were maintained mostly by women. [n07; Mumbai Paused] [n08; Mumbai Paused] The visitor was told that the land the community farmed had been granted by the church. [n07; Mumbai Paused] The research notes give no document for that grant. It is what the village said.

The wadis of north Vasai had a long history before any Kupari existed. The 1882 Gazetteer described the “garden or agar of Sopara”, with Gass, Nirmal, Vagholi, Bolinj, Koprad, Umbrala and Mardesh, as part of the best-tilled tract in Bassein. [n39; Gazetteer XIV pp. 314–315] [n41; section 3] It praised the Christians of Bassein as “the most hardworking and skilful husbandmen” who had “turned the light sandy country about Bassein into an evergreen garden”. [n41; Gazetteer XIII-1, Ch. IV] It recorded about seventy-five families of “Native Christians originally Samvedis and Pachkalshis” at Agashi, Vatar and Koprad drying plantains alongside eighty-five Samvedi and fifteen Pachkalshi families, the dried plantains “the best in the district”; and it recorded sugar made in Bassein by “Pachkalshis, Malis, Native Christians and Samvedi Brahmans”. [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 395; XIV p. 2] Betel-nut growers were “partly Christians, Malis converted by the Portuguese, and partly Hindus of the Chavkalshi or Pachkalshi class”. [n41; Gazetteer XIII-1, Ch. IV]

The landscape of the wadi was therefore shared. Hindu and Christian, Samvedi and Pachkalshi, worked the same kind of garden behind the same kind of house, drew water from the same kind of well and sent the same plantains and betel to Bombay. The community blogs say farming remained the Kuparis’ chief living until the late 1960s and early 1970s. [n07; D’Mello blog] The story of how the wadis went, to education, to the city, to builders, belongs to the next chapter. Here it is enough to say that most of what the Kuparis ate, wore, sang and celebrated was shaped by a garden.

The kitchen

Daily food in 1882

The Gazetteer’s account of the Thana Christians’ food is plain. Two meals a day, of rice, nagli and vari (two hill millets), pulse and vegetables, with mutton, beef, pork, fowl and fish. Palm spirit, phul, of three to five per cent alcohol, and bevda, double-distilled palm juice. “The whole family eats together, men, women and children.” [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 204] “Pigs are found in most Christian villages.” [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 55]

The Hindu Samvedis’ daily food, by the same Gazetteer, was “rice pulse and vegetables”. [n08; Gazetteer 1882, p. 82] The Pachkalshis ate coarse and fine rice, rice bread, pulse, vegetables and fish, with holiday dinners of mutton, chicken, pulse bread and liquor. [n41; Gazetteer XIII-1, p. 130] Enthoven, in 1922, gave the Panchkalshis goat, sheep, wild boar, hare, deer, fish and fowl, and liquor. [n41; Enthoven III, p. 166] The modern summary of the East Indian Vadvals says they rear pigs but avoid beef. [n41; Sahapedia]

Three things in the Christian kitchen set it apart from its Hindu neighbours’: pork, beef and the family eating together. The pig in the yard, which the Gazetteer found in most Christian villages, was the most visible difference between a Christian and a Hindu household on the same lane, and pork would become the centre of every Kupari feast.

The community’s dishes

The 2009 community blog gives a list of Kupari foods, with short glosses. [n07; D’Mello blog]

  • Fish curries, above all of bombil, the Bombay duck.
  • Indel: meat or fish cooked to keep for days.
  • Vade: fried bread.
  • Dhapti: steamed flour and vegetables.
  • Hingoli.
  • Fogey: a round Portuguese bread.
  • Dodal: a sweet.
  • Fuddi: pork feijoada.
  • Sanna: rice bread.

The blog gives no recipes and no dates. Nothing in the primary texts confirms or contradicts the list. [n34; section 5] It is the community’s own record.

Each name carries some history. Fuddi, a pork-and-bean stew the blog calls feijoada, takes its model from Portugal. Fogey, a round bread, is called Portuguese. Vade and sanna are Konkan foods found across the coast, Hindu and Christian. The Hindu Samvedis served vadás at their weddings in 1882, beside pápads and shev. [n08; Gazetteer 1882, pp. 82–83] Dhapti, steamed dough with vegetables, and indel, food made to last, are the cooking of farming households that needed meals to keep through days in the fields. The Kupari kitchen is, in miniature, the same layering as the Kupari dialect: a Konkan base, a Portuguese layer that came through the church’s feasts, and a pork-eating difference that marked the Christian house.

A December 2025 article on the Christmas table of the Vasai East Indians, by the chef Freny Fernandes, describes fugias, a sweetish puffed fried bread, and pork indyal. [n07; Slurrp, 24 Dec 2025] The blog’s fogey is “a round Portuguese bread”; the chef’s fugias is “a sweetish puffed fried bread”. The two descriptions do not match exactly, and neither source says whether they are the same thing. The blog’s indel and the chef’s indyal are, on their face, one dish under two spellings, a meat preparation that keeps.

Bottle masala and the masalewalis

The spice that defines East Indian cooking is the bottle masala. It is made from twenty to thirty spices, sometimes up to sixty ingredients, roasted and ground, and it was traditionally stored in green and amber beer bottles to keep out the sunlight, which gave it its name. [n13; Zee Zest; Homegrown] “Where Goan recipes tend to favour wet masalas,” the 2025 article says, “East Indian dishes rely on the dry bottle masala.” [n07; Slurrp] East Indians make their own sorpotel with it. [n13; Finely Chopped]

The making of the masala was once a public event. “In East Indian neighbourhoods like Vasai (Bassein),” one writer says, “it was common to find groups of women (called masalewalis) going from house to house to do the roasting and grounding.” [n13; Zee Zest] The image is of a season, after the harvest and the dry heat of the year, when the masalewalis moved from courtyard to courtyard, roasting chillies and spices over open fires and grinding them for a household’s year, while the bottles waited in a row. The research notes do not give dates for the custom or say whether it continues. The Jio Institute’s digital exhibit on bottle masala is a citable archive for its history. [n13; Jio Institute exhibit] The notes do not say whether the Kupari villages of the north made their masala exactly as the East Indians of the south did, or whether Kupari families use the name. The sources speak of “Vasai” in general.

Feast food

On a feast day the Kupari table changed. The Gazetteer’s wedding feast of the 1880s had “two pork dishes with vinegar and spices”; the poor sat on mats and ate off leaf plates. [n34; Gazetteer XIII pp. 211–214] The baptism feast of the poorer families was country liquor, dates, gram and molasses. [n34; Gazetteer XIII pp. 210–211] The seventh-day meal after a funeral, the Saatvoh, the community blog says, was a meal with meat and alcohol. [n07; D’Mello blog]

The Christmas table of the Vasai East Indians, in the 2025 article, is the fullest modern description. Roast chicken stuffed with offal, peas and croutons, with bottle masala. Fugias. Pork indyal. Suckling pig. Beef tongue. Vindaloo and sorpotel made with sugarcane vinegar. And the sweets: fruit cream, guava cheese, coconut rava cake, nevri, and spiced wine. [n07; Slurrp, 24 Dec 2025] Sorpotel and vindaloo are dishes of the Portuguese Indian world, shared with Goa and Mangalore, but the sugarcane vinegar is local, the vinegar of a sugar-growing coast that the 1882 Gazetteer described. The feast table at the fort and at Erangal in January, with its moile and its kohlrabi stew cooked in the open, belongs to the same tradition. [n09; FPJ, 11 Jan 2026]

The 2009 blog’s list of feasts gives the shape of the Kupari year in food: Natal, Easter, the Pali fiesta, the Bandra fiesta and San Jao. [n07; D’Mello blog] It does not say what was eaten at each. Natal is the Portuguese word for Christmas, and the community still uses it.

Sukeli: the banana of Agashi

The one food of north Vasai that the wider world knows is the sukeli, the sun-dried banana. Marathi Wikipedia says “Vasai’s sukeli was once very famous”. [n20; mr.wikipedia: वसई] It is made by drying ripe bananas, mainly of the Rajeli variety, in the sun; Agashi is the centre of its making; and its orchards have shrunk. [n20; Vishwakosh and mr.wikipedia, snippets] In the dialect it is hukeli. [n20; mr.wikipedia: सामवेदी बोली] The Gazetteer had already called the dried plantains of Agashi, Vatar and Koprad “the best in the district” in 1882, and named the Christian, Samvedi and Pachkalshi families who made them. [n34; Gazetteer XIV p. 2; XIII p. 395]

In June 2026 the Marathi daily Sakal reported the death of Pius Alex Rodrigues of Nandakhal-Parsav, who, it said, “spread the name of sukeli, Vasai’s famous dry banana, across India and abroad”. He died on 19 June 2026, and his family donated his eyes and skin. [n20; eSakal, snippet] The research notes call him a Christian (Kupari or Samvedi Christian) farmer-entrepreneur from Nandakhal; the report itself, as relayed, does not use the word Kupari. [n20; Inferences] Sukeli is sold, among other places, at the stalls of the Nirmal yatra, the great Hindu pilgrimage fair of the north Vasai villages, beside dates, pedhe, water chestnuts and groundnuts. [n20; Lokmat, snippet] A dried banana made in Christian and Hindu gardens and sold at a Hindu fair is as good an emblem as any of the shared economy these villages lived by.

Toddy and drink

The Gazetteer’s palm spirit, phul, and the double-distilled bevda, were the drink of the Christian villages of the 1880s. [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 204] The Bhandaris, the toddy-tapping caste, were “often seen with a hollow gourd full of palm-juice on their head”, carrying “on their left thigh a heavy broad-bladed tapping knife”, and “they sing while they tap the trees”. [n08; Gazetteer 1882, p. 150] A rise in the palm-tree tax in 1877 pushed many of them into farming and labour. [n08; Gazetteer 1882, p. 150] The East Indian toast sukhala, “cheers”, is in the 2024 list of Vasai dialect words. [n13; FPJ, 14 Mar 2024] Drink was part of every feast described in this chapter, from the baptism to the seventh-day meal; the Gazetteer’s own opinions on drink, as on much else, are those of its time.

Songs

The songs of Nandakhal

The Kuparis’ folk songs are known to this book only through a single community source, and through the titles of books not yet read.

The Vasai community site Vasaiker, describing the music of Vasai, says that the Nandakhal folk songs, also called “Kupari songs”, “are rarely heard as lifestyles have changed”. Their themes, it says, are the bride leaving her father’s house and the father’s goodbye; women fetching fodder; the groom’s first shave, from the time when boys married young; and a daughter returning home after the monsoon rice planting. [n07; Vasaiker, “Music of Vasai”] [n13; Vasaiker] No text of any of these songs is in the research notes. [n34; Gaps, section 5]

Even the themes say a good deal. These are songs of a farming year and a farming family. The bride’s farewell is a song of every Indian village, Hindu or Christian, of the moment a daughter leaves for another house. The women fetching fodder are the women who kept the cattle and, at Bhuigaon, the wells. The groom’s first shave is a rite of passage from boyhood to marriage, and its place in a song tells us that the Kupari villages once married their sons young enough for a first shave to be a wedding event. The daughter returning after the rice planting belongs to the monsoon, when married daughters came home to help with the planting or came back to their parents’ house once it was done. The 1882 Gazetteer recorded that Christian boys in the district usually married after twenty, girls at fourteen to sixteen, with legal minimums of fourteen and twelve. [n34; Gazetteer XIII pp. 211–214] A song about the groom’s first shave therefore remembers either an earlier time, or a Kupari practice younger than the Gazetteer’s general rule; the sources do not say which.

Saamana calls the Samvedi-speaking people a “singing community”, with a rich store of folk songs. [n20; Saamana] Paul Rumav’s Samvedi Lokgeete and Madhachya Ghagari are the printed collections most likely to hold these songs’ words. [n20; mr.wikipedia; Inferences] They are the first place a reader who wants to hear the Kupari voice should go after this book.

Vasaiker adds a comparison that the Kuparis’ neighbours may recognise: the music of south Vasai comes from the Vadval community, which it calls “more fun-loving” than “the more conservative Kupari community”. [n07; Vasaiker] That is one site’s characterisation. It is the kind of thing neighbouring communities say about each other everywhere.

Violins in the church

The parish brought its own music. The 1882 Gazetteer recorded that “each church pays one or two music masters who … play on the violin”. At Nirmal the teacher of the private school played the violin in church. At Agashi the church school of seventeen pupils taught Portuguese, arithmetic, singing, doctrine and music. [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 207; XIV pp. 2, 293] A Lisbon source of 1843, quoted in the Gazetteer, said that the hamlet of “Trinity” sang sacred songs at work and that “the Thana choristers were famous singers”. But the Gazetteer itself thought the people of 1882 “seem to have lost their old fondness for singing”. [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 207 and n.2]

A village violin master, paid by the parish, teaching the boys of Nirmal to read music and to play at Mass, is one of the more surprising figures of the nineteenth-century record, and one of the more important for the Kupari story. The church gave the villages not only a liturgy in Latin and Portuguese but a Western musical training, a few village musicians at a time, that would later feed the brass bands.

Brass bands and the ghumat

The brass band is the sound of an East Indian wedding. East Indian folk songs are sung at engagements, weddings and gatherings, and brass bands play “party bops and melancholic dirges”. [n13; East Indian Memory Co., 2025] Brass bands are “an integral part” of East Indian village and wedding life; Mumbai held its first East Indian Battle of the Bands to show the tradition. [n13; FPJ] Vasaiker says wedding bands play at Vasai celebrations. [n07; Vasaiker] English Wikipedia’s page on the Kuparis describes a wedding rite, Hadisoli, in which the groom’s side goes in procession with a marriage band to invite its maternal relatives, and says that wedding bands are announced in church. [n13; Wikipedia: Kupari] That page is thinly sourced, and the rite is recorded here on its authority alone.

For Christmas, East Indian families sing and dance to traditional songs played on the ghumat, a clay drum. That is attested for the East Indians generally, not for the Kupari villages in particular. [n07; DNA, search summary] [n07; Gaps]

The music of the Hindu neighbours, by the community’s own tradition, has its own strand. The modern Wikipedia account of the Samvedi Brahmins says that their musicians play the sanai, choughada, mridanga, bheri and veena at the Nirmal Shankaracharya Samadhi Mandir, the Sureshwar Mandir and the Bhavani Shankar Mandir at Agashi; the page is tagged for original research. [n08; Wikipedia: Samvedi] The Samvedi origin legend itself makes them temple musicians. [n08] The research notes found no source on whether the Kupari songs and the Hindu Samvedi songs share tunes or texts. If the dialect is shared, and the themes are those of every farming village, they may well. That is a question for the collections of Rumav and Naik, and for the singers who are left.

The rites of a life

The community’s own list of the rites that mark a Kupari life comes from the 2009 blog by Rajan D’Mello, and it is the only list there is. It gives the names, most of them Portuguese in origin, and a one-line meaning for each. [n07; D’Mello blog] The research notes are blunt: no contemporary source describes the Kupari wedding terms, the Lenten Pirsao or the Kupari folk songs, and the names have not been verified. [n34; Gaps, section 5] What each rite involved in detail, the blog does not say. What can be done is to place each name beside the fullest period account of the same moment in the Christian villages, the 1882 Gazetteer’s description of the Thana Christians, and beside the Hindu Samvedi rite that the same Gazetteer records. The reader should keep the three kinds of evidence apart: the name is the community’s; the 1882 detail is the Gazetteer’s general description of the district’s Christians, with Bassein points marked; the Hindu comparison is the Gazetteer’s page on the Samvedis.

Baltim: baptism

Baltim is baptism. [n07; D’Mello blog]

The 1882 account is detailed. After a first confinement the wife went to her parents’ house to give birth. On the third or sixth night after the birth, among the lower orders, the household guarded against the spirit Sathi by strewing gram at the door. The child was baptised between the eighth and fifteenth day. It was carried to church by its godfather, padrinho, and godmother, madrinha. “The mother never goes to the christening.” When the child was brought home, the poorer families burned incense at the door before it entered, and there was a feast of country liquor, dates, gram and molasses. On the fortieth day the mother was purified at church. [n34; Gazetteer XIII pp. 210–211]

Two things in that account bind the Christian household to its Hindu past. The first is Sathi. Among the Hindu Samvedis of the same district, on the fifth day after a birth, the goddess Sati was worshipped, and on the twelfth the child was named and the caste feasted. [n08; Gazetteer 1882, p. 82] The same spirit, under nearly the same name, watched over the newborn in both communities, worshipped in one house and warded off in the other. [n34; Inferences, section 5] The second is the godparent. The padrinho who carried the child, and whom Grierson’s Christians called padron, is the figure whose Portuguese title, compadre, “co-father”, is the most widely repeated source of the word “Kupari” itself. [n34; section 2] That derivation has no scholarly source. But if it is right, the community is named after the man who stood at the font at Baltim, and the rite that made each Kupari a Christian also gave the community its name. [K2] [K3]

The mother’s absence from her own child’s baptism, and her purification forty days later, are Catholic custom of the period, and they are also close to the Hindu sense of birth pollution that the Samvedi rites of the fifth and twelfth days answered. The Gazetteer does not draw that parallel. It is the reader’s to weigh.

Komsar, Cominyao, Chrisma

Komsar is confession, Cominyao First Communion, Chrisma confirmation. [n07; D’Mello blog] The blog gives nothing more about them. The Gazetteer records that two or three months before a wedding the couple received instruction in doctrine, and made their confession and communion a day or two before the ceremony. [n34; Gazetteer XIII pp. 211–214] Confession and communion were therefore not only private sacraments but steps on the way to marriage, overseen by the parish priest. The research notes contain no description of a Kupari First Communion as a village event, and none of confirmation.

Marriage: Hamdao, Lagin, Varad, Saudio

The blog names four stages of a Kupari wedding: Hamdao, Lagin and Varad, the wedding stages, and Saudio, the celebration after the wedding. [n07; D’Mello blog] It does not say what happens at each. Lagin is the ordinary Marathi word for a wedding (lagna), and Varad the Marathi for a wedding procession; those meanings come from the language, not from the blog. Hamdao and Saudio are not explained in any source in the notes. English Wikipedia’s Kupari page adds Hadisoli, the procession of the groom’s side, with a band, to invite the maternal relatives, and says the wedding bands are announced in church. [n13; Wikipedia: Kupari] The East Indian lugra essay names, for the East Indians generally, the Moya, a pre-wedding rite, and Umbra-cha Paani, the fetching of the bride’s or groom’s bathing water, as occasions when the old sari is still worn. [n07; East Indian Memory Co.] Whether these are Kupari rites under the same names, the notes do not say.

The 1882 account fills in what a Christian wedding in the district looked like. Marriageable ages were fourteen for boys and twelve for girls, though boys usually married after twenty and girls at fourteen to sixteen. And one detail is marked as Bassein’s own: “In Salsette the parents of the girl propose; in Bassein the proposal comes from the boy’s side.” [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 211 n.1] There was a settlement on the bride of fifty to five hundred rupees, up to five thousand, paid in ornaments, with written contracts among the well-to-do. Two to five days before the wedding, booths were built, the mandav of every Konkan wedding. The couple had two to three months of instruction in doctrine, and made their confession and communion a day or two before. Weddings were held at eight to ten in the morning, except among the Kolis, who married in the afternoon to native music. The groom walked under a long silk umbrella; among the lower classes he wore the hired eighteenth-century military costume already described; the bride went in a palanquin. The groom gave the bride a gold necklace, worn while he lived. The feast had two pork dishes with vinegar and spices, and the poor ate off leaf plates, sitting on mats. [n34; Gazetteer XIII pp. 211–214]

The Hindu Samvedi wedding of the same years was another world, and yet a neighbouring one. Girls were married before ten. A bride-price of two hundred to a thousand rupees was paid to the girl’s father. The date was fixed at the astrologer’s house, tithinischay. The day before, twenty to thirty earthen pots were brought from the potter and worshipped. Rice-flour lamps were lit in a bamboo basket, zad, and held over the elders’ heads while the priest called “Vidávida ála ho”, time for sandal and betel. The food was pápads, vadás and shev. The groom rode to the bride’s house on a horse. The couple stood on stools with a cloth held between them, and when it was pulled away they garlanded each other. A varghoda procession followed. Widows could not remarry. [n08; Gazetteer 1882, pp. 82–83]

Set side by side, the two weddings differ in almost everything the church touched, the hour, the place, the vows, the age of the bride, and agree on the things it left alone: a procession, a booth, a feast, a groom who was the centre of a show, and the initiative lying with the boy’s family. In the Samvedi wedding the boy’s side paid a bride-price; in the Bassein Christian wedding the boy’s side made the proposal. Whether the second is a descendant of the first, the sources raise the question but do not answer it. [K3] Among the Vadvals too, Enthoven recorded in 1922, “the bridegroom’s parents approach the bride’s parents and request them to send their daughter’s horoscope”, the reverse of the practice in the other Panchkalshi divisions. [n41; Enthoven III, p. 163] The three communities of the Vasai gardens, Christian, Samvedi and Vadval, shared a custom of the groom’s family taking the first step, and each was unlike its own wider group in this. That is an observation from the documents, not a theory of where it came from.

The Nandakhal song of the groom’s first shave belongs to a wedding of this world; so does the song of the bride’s farewell. [n07; Vasaiker] They are the only Kupari wedding words the notes know of, and only by their themes.

Yentar and Saatvoh: death

Yentar is the funeral. Saatvoh is the seventh-day Mass, followed by a meal with meat and alcohol. [n07; D’Mello blog] Yentar plainly echoes the Portuguese enterro, burial, and Saatvoh the Marathi saat, seven; those are observations from the words, not from a source.

The 1882 account again gives the detail. The priest was called and the church bell tolled. Burial was usually within twenty-four hours. The corpse was dressed; the poor were buried in a calico habit “like a monk’s robe, in shape like a Franciscan’s and in colour like a Carmelite’s”. Candles stood round the coffin. The widow removed her ornaments and, “unless she is very young, never wears them again”. The village brotherhoods attended with candles. On the seventh day there were prayers in church and a meal; there were services at a month and at a year. Graves cost two to fifteen rupees for a temporary one and twenty-five to two hundred for a permanent one. [n34; Gazetteer XIII pp. 214–215 and n.1] The community’s Saatvoh, a seventh-day Mass and meal, is exactly the Gazetteer’s seventh-day observance, a hundred and twenty-seven years later in the record.

The Hindu Samvedis burned their dead, except children under three. On the third day the ashes were gathered and food on a leaf plate was given to the village Mhar. Mourning lasted ten days, with rice balls offered on the eleventh. A married but childless caste man, a vánzkuli, was taken out of the village, paid two rupees and fed rice, ámti and vadás, and feasted again on the thirteenth, and food cooked that day could not stay in the house. [n08; Gazetteer 1882, p. 83] The Vadvals cremated, cast the ashes into the sea or a river, carried the body on a bier of bamboo and coconut leaves, the zavalka, and kept thirteen days of rites, with a Narayan bali for an accidental death and a caste feast. [n41; Enthoven III, pp. 165–167]

The difference between burial and burning is the deepest line the church drew through the Samvedi villages. Yet even across it the pattern of feasting the dead’s memory on a fixed day, the third, seventh, eleventh or thirteenth, with a meal for the community, survived on both sides. And the widow who put away her ornaments for life was a figure in both houses: among the Christians by custom, as the Gazetteer recorded; among the Hindu Samvedis, who forbade widow remarriage, by rule. [n34] [n08]

The year’s turning

Lent: Upas and Pirsao

The Kupari year has a long season of penance before Easter. The community blog names Upas, the forty-day Lenten fast, and Pirsao or miravnuk, a candlelit Lenten procession through the villages. [n07; D’Mello blog] Upas is the ordinary Marathi word for a fast; miravnuk is Marathi for a procession. Pirsao echoes the Portuguese procissão. The research notes found no other description of either, and no source specific to Vasai on Lent and Easter customs, Passion plays, Good Friday processions or Lenten devotions at the crosses. [n09; Gaps, section 3] [n34; Gaps, section 5]

The deeper history of the Lenten processions is documented. The Gazetteer recorded that passion plays were “introduced by the Jesuits about the middle of the sixteenth century”, that a society of penitents was formed in 1551 and brought to Bassein in 1552 by Fr Melchior Nunes Barreto. [n34; Gazetteer XIII pp. 207–210] A night procession of candles through the lanes of Nirmal or Nandakhal in Lent is, on that evidence, a custom with roots in the first generation of the Bassein mission, nearly five centuries old. That is a continuity the sources suggest but do not trace step by step.

Ramacha Pass: Palm Sunday

Lent ends in Holy Week, which begins on Palm Sunday with the procession of palms, the Ramacha Pass. The Free Press Journal derives the name from Portuguese ramo, branches, and passo, steps. [n13; FPJ, 14 Mar 2024] It is reported as an East Indian and Vasai dialect term. The name is a small history in itself. A Portuguese liturgical phrase entered the village speech and was given a Marathi grammar, Ramacha, “of the Ramo”, until to a Marathi ear it sounds as though it could be “Rama’s procession”. Nothing in the sources says that anyone reads it that way. But a Palm Sunday whose name sounds, to the neighbours, like a procession of Ram is a fitting image of how close the two worlds of the Vasai villages stood.

Easter, Natal and the fiestas

The 2009 blog lists the community’s great feasts as Natal (Christmas), Easter, the Pali fiesta, the Bandra fiesta and San Jao. [n07; D’Mello blog] The Pali fiesta is presumably that of the old parish of Pali in Vasai, whose church dates from 1595 by one list. [n09] The Bandra fiesta is presumably the feast at Bandra in the city, and San Jao the feast of St John the Baptist kept across the Portuguese Catholic world; those identifications are this book’s, not the blog’s. The blog says nothing more about how the Kupari villages keep them. The notes contain no Vasai-specific description of San Jao.

The first fruits

One feast of the farming year is documented in 1882: the first fruits of the rice harvest were blessed at church. [n34; Gazetteer XIII pp. 207–210] The Jio Institute has documented the East Indian harvest thanksgiving, Aagera, at which the first sheaves are offered at church. [n13; Jio Institute: Aagera] The notes do not say whether the Kupari villages use that name. A farming Christian people offering its first rice at the altar is the Christian form of a harvest rite that every farming community of the Konkan keeps in some form.

What the priests disapproved

The Gazetteer recorded, with the reserve of a government report, that secret offerings to Ganpati and Gauri, and vows to Shitladevi, the smallpox goddess, were “said occasionally” to continue in outlying villages of Salsette and Bassein, and that the priests disapproved. [n34; Gazetteer XIII pp. 207–210] It does not name the villages. The 1931 census, writing of the Bassein fishing coast, said that “the intensive work of the last 60 to 80 years” had done much to purge the Indian Christians of practices “alien to … Roman Catholicism”, but that the priests found them still “more unorthodox than is desirable”; and it added that “there is no evidence that this community worships at Christian altars behind which are placed Hindu gods”. [n34; Census 1931 Vol VIII Part I pp. 363–364] Those lines were written of the seafaring Christians of the coast, not of the northern gardeners, and they should not be laid on the Kuparis without evidence. [n34; section 6] They show, nonetheless, what the church in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries was working against: the persistence of a shared village religion beneath the line the baptism had drawn.

Crosses in every village

The khursa

Walk through the old Christian villages of Vasai and you will meet crosses at crossroads and at the entrances to lanes. In the villages they are called khursa; the word’s likeness to the Portuguese cruz is this book’s observation. The research notes found no source text that uses the word itself; the sources say “cross”. [n09; Gaps, section 3] It is recorded here as the community’s word.

A Vasai city website says that crosses stand in almost every gaothan, every old village settlement, of Vasai; that at the annual cross feast the villagers gather to say the rosary; and that the tradition is older than the state of Maharashtra. [n09; VasaiCity, “Vasai Holy Crosses”] A local Catholic blog of 2015 names some of them: the Community Cross at Cross Naka, Agashi; the cross in the grounds of St Peter’s Church in the Koliwada; a cross on the way to Vasai Court; the Community Cross at Bangli Naka; the Milagres Cross near the church at Remedi, which is called miraculous and where devotees are often seen praying; the Parvati Cross toward Vasai station; and the Furtado family cross at Kirawali-Chobare. [n09; Vasai Catholics blog, 9 Oct 2015] Those named on the blog are mostly in south and central Vasai; the Agashi cross is the one in the Kupari north.

The fullest account of what a cross means to a Kupari village comes from Bhuigaon. The visitor of 2011 was told that the steel cross behind the village’s forty wells works like a Gaondevi, a village goddess: a protector and a territorial marker for the extended Marti family. [n07; Mumbai Paused] [n08; Mumbai Paused] The comparison was the villagers’ own, or the visitor’s paraphrase of it. It is a striking one. In the Hindu villages around, the gaondevi stands at the boundary and guards the settlement, and its annual jatra gathers the village. In the Kupari village the cross stands at the boundary, guards the fields and wells, and its annual feast gathers the village to pray. A family cross, like the Furtados’ at Kirawali, marks a lineage as a household shrine does. The cross on the front door marks the house. [n07; Mumbai Paused]

The cross feast

The annual cross feast is the most local of the Kupari feasts: not a parish feast, not a diocesan one, but the feast of a lane or a hamlet or a family. The research notes say only that villagers gather to say the rosary. [n09; VasaiCity] They do not describe the decoration, the food or the music, or say when the feasts fall. The community knows these things; the written record does not yet.

Feasts and fairs

Killa cha Sann and the January feasts

The feast with which this chapter began, Killa cha Sann, the January feast of St Gonsalo Garcia in the ruined Jesuit church of the fort, is the largest feast-fair of Vasai’s Catholics that the press regularly reports. Its date is set by the neap tide after Christmas; its Mass is said in a monument; its pilgrims camp, cook and, in the past, came by boat and cart. [n07; DNA 2014; FPJ 2026] [n09; FPJ 2026] The East Indian community also observes a “St Gonsalo Garcia Day” with veneration and cultural events. [n09; FPJ] The saint is the patron of the Diocese of Vasai and of the East Indian community. [n07; Wikipedia; Archdiocese of Bombay] In 2023 the clergy of Vasai said that more people should know about “India’s first saint”. [n09; Mid-day, 12 Feb 2023]

The parish feasts

Each parish keeps its own feast. The church of St Gonsalo Garcia at Gass, built in 1942 and enlarged in 1957, holds a week-long feast in February. [n09; Prayers & Petitions; CatholicsIndia] St Francis Xavier’s at Giriz keeps its feast on 3 December. [n09; Wikipedia: Giriz] The research notes found no feast dates for Nirmal, Agashi or Nandakhal, and no description of how their feasts are kept. [n09; Gaps] A parish feast in Vasai, by general account, is a fair as well as a Mass: stalls, families visiting, food. The 2026 report of the fort feast describes that pattern; the notes do not describe it parish by parish. [n09; FPJ 2026]

The Nirmal yatra: a Hindu feast in Kupari country

The largest fair of the Kupari north is not a Christian one. Shrikshetra Nirmal holds the samadhi of a Shankaracharya, and its yatra begins on Kartik Ekadashi and lasts about fifteen days. On Ekadashi a students’ dindi goes out, then a palanquin carrying the Shankaracharya’s image. Pilgrims bathe in the Vimal lake, where pinda-daan and tarpan rites are done. Stalls line the road from Nirmal Naka to Bhuigaon Phata, selling toys, sweets, dry fruit, Vasai sukeli, dates, pedhe, water chestnuts and groundnuts. [n20; Lokmat, snippet] In November 2025 the Konkan root vegetables, purple yam (konfal), kanak and sweet potato, were in high demand at the fair “amid the rising tide of junk food”. [n20; Agrowon, 19 Nov 2025]

The road from Nirmal Naka to Bhuigaon Phata runs through the heart of the Kupari villages, past the Holy Cross church of Nirmal, the oldest parish of the north. The Gazetteer recorded in 1882 that eight temples at Nirmal had been built about 1750 by the Maratha official Shankarji Keshav, and that the site of the temple the Portuguese pulled down had been purified in 1739 with the padukas of Dattatreya. [n34; Gazetteer XIV pp. 292–293] A Hindu revival and a Christian parish have stood side by side at Nirmal for nearly three centuries. The research notes give no figures on who comes to the yatra; that Kupari families walk among its stalls, and that Kupari-grown sukeli is sold on them, is part of village life that every local knows and the record touches only through the list of goods for sale. [n20]

Christmas: carols at the door

Christmas, Natal, is the great feast of the Kupari year, and it is the one for which the modern press gives the fullest picture of Vasai. The Free Press Journal, describing “Christmas fervour” in Vasai, wrote of churches holding services, choirs and carols, and of people going from door to door singing; it noted that Vasai has local East Indian Catholics along its coastal and western strip, and migrants of the last six decades. [n09; FPJ, “Christmas fervour picks up in Vasai”] [n13; FPJ] Door-to-door carolling is the custom most Kupari families would name first if asked what Christmas means in their village; but the notes describe it only in that one sentence, and how it is done in Nirmal or Gass, who goes, what is sung and in which language, is not yet written down.

The Christmas table has been described above: the stuffed roast chicken with bottle masala, the fugias, the pork indyal, the suckling pig, the beef tongue, the sorpotel and vindaloo in sugarcane vinegar, the fruit cream, guava cheese, coconut cake and nevri, and the spiced wine. [n07; Slurrp, 24 Dec 2025] The cribs and decorations of the Vasai Christmas season appear in the press. [n13; FPJ] For the East Indians generally, Christmas is also when families sing and dance to the ghumat. [n07; DNA, search summary]

The Christmas season runs into the season of fairs. The Vasai Taluka Kala-Krida Mahotsav, the great arts and sports festival of the taluka, is held from 26 to 31 December, with eighty-five competitions and more than 60,000 contestants in its thirty-sixth edition; it is not a Christian festival but a taluka-wide one, and it falls in the same week. [n13; FPJ] [n20; eSakal] Then, on the Sunday nearest the neap tide after Christmas, comes Killa cha Sann. For a Kupari family the year turns in a long run of feasts from the first week of Advent to the January fair at the fort.

Neighbours: Kupari, Samvedi and Vadval side by side

The Kuparis did not live alone, and they cannot be understood alone. In every village of the north their neighbours were the Hindu Samvedi Brahmins, from whom many of them descend; and to the south and among them were the Pachkalshis and Vadvals, Hindu and Christian, from whom others descend. The 1882 Gazetteer’s Christians of Agashi were “originally Samvedis and Pachkalshis”. [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 395] The evidence in this chapter allows a comparison, theme by theme, of what the three communities shared and where they parted. The dates of the evidence differ, and the reader should hold that in mind: the Samvedi record is mostly of 1882, the Vadval record of 1882, 1909 and 1922, and the Kupari record of 2009 to 2026, with the 1882 Christian chapter as its period anchor.

Speech. All three speak dialects with the same signature rule, s and sh to h, ch to s or h, which Grierson heard in Samvedi and Vadval around 1900, and which the Kadodi and Vadvali of the 2020s keep. [n22] [n41] Grierson grouped Samvedi and Vadval as dialects preserving “the characteristic features of Gujarati Bhili”. [n22] Saamana says Samvedi Brahmins, Pachkalshis and Samvedi Christians share the Samvedi dialect. [n20] The Kuparis of Bhuigaon say their Kadodi is not the same as the Vadval speech of south Vasai. [n07] The Christian speech added Portuguese words of church and family: mai, pai, padron, kadair, zanel. [n13] [n34] The Vadval speech was described in 1909 as close, among its poorer speakers, to that of the Kolis and Agris. [n41; Bombay City Gazetteer I, p. 239] Three dialects of one family, then, each marked by its community’s history: the Christians by the parish, the Vadvals by the fishing coast.

Head and body. Red on the head was shared. Hindu Samvedi men wore red broadcloth caps “like Telegu Brahmans” in 1882; Hindu Vadval men wore a large red turban or the Koli cap in 1909; Christian fishermen, Son Kolis and Mangelas wore red caps; the Kupari men of community memory wore a red cap. [n34] [n41] [n08] [n07] The Kupari women’s red checked kashta, the community’s chief emblem, has no exact Hindu Samvedi parallel in the 1882 record, which gives the Samvedi women only “the Maratha bodice and robe”. [n08] Vadval women at weddings wore “yellow saris of silk or cotton with embroidered borders”, and in daily life a cloth to the knee, tucked behind like Koli women’s, with a silver belt, kargota. [n41; Bombay City Gazetteer I, pp. 240–241] The Christian women of 1882 drew the end of the robe over the right shoulder, “not like most Hindus over the right temple”. [n34] A woman’s drape and colour told a passer-by which community she belonged to, and the communities knew it.

Ornaments. Hindu and Christian Samvedi women wore mugdya, gathe and vale in 1882. [n34] [n08] Vadval women wore a silver belt and necklace in 1909. [n41] The Christian widow put away her ornaments for life; the Hindu Samvedi widow could not remarry. [n34] [n08]

Houses. Samvedi and Christian houses of 1882 were one-storey plank-walled houses with tiled roofs, the Christian ones “substantial”. [n08] [n34] Vadvals on Bombay island lived in bamboo-and-mud huts with a devghar and a tulsi in front; Pachkalshis in Thana in mud or brick houses, thatched or tiled. [n41] Where the Hindu house had its tulsi and its devghar, the Kupari house had a cross on the door, and, at its founding, a coconut broken and gold laid in the earth, as the Hindu bhoomi pujan does. [n07]

Food. The Hindu Samvedis ate rice, pulse and vegetables; the Pachkalshis added fish and, on holidays, mutton, chicken and liquor; the Christians ate all of these and pork and beef, kept pigs in most villages, and made pork the heart of their feasts. [n08] [n41] [n34] The Christian family ate together, men, women and children, a point the Gazetteer thought worth noting. [n34] Vade were on the Samvedi wedding table in 1882 and are on the Kupari list of 2009. [n08] [n07]

Birth. The spirit Sathi, Sati, was guarded against at the Christian door on the third or sixth night and worshipped in the Samvedi house on the fifth. [n34] [n08] The Christian child was named at the font by its godparents; the Samvedi child was named on the twelfth day with a caste feast. The Samvedi boy had a thread ceremony, with its mock pilgrimage to Benares and a maternal uncle who called him back with the promise of his daughter; the Christian boy had confession, communion and confirmation, Komsar, Cominyao, Chrisma. [n08] [n07]

Marriage. The Samvedi wedding was fixed by an astrologer and paid for with a bride-price; the Vadval wedding was fixed by a Palshe priest, with no dowry and a sword piercing the canopy as a Kshatriya survival; the Christian wedding was fixed by the parish and paid for with ornaments settled on the bride. [n08] [n41; Enthoven III, pp. 162–164] [n34] In all three, among the Bassein Christians and the Vadvals by explicit record, and among the Samvedis through the bride-price, the groom’s side took the first step. [n34] [n41] [n08] The Samvedi groom rode a horse; the Christian groom walked under a silk umbrella in an officer’s coat.

Death. Samvedis and Vadvals burned their dead; the Christians buried. All three kept fixed days of remembrance with a meal: the Samvedi third, eleventh and thirteenth days, the Vadval thirteen days, the Christian seventh day, the Saatvoh, with month and year Masses. [n08] [n41] [n34] [n07]

Gods at the boundary. The Hindu villages had their gaondevi; the Pachkalshi and Vadval houses of 1882 kept images of Cheda and other local spirits beside Ganpati, Shiv and Krishna; the Vadvals honoured Ekvira, Vajreshwari and Mahalakshmi and kept a tulsi before the house. [n41; Gazetteer XIII-1, p. 130; Bombay City Gazetteer I, p. 240] The Kupari village had its khursa, which at Bhuigaon the villagers themselves compared to a gaondevi. [n07]

Who leads. The Hindu Samvedis had no caste headman in 1882 and settled their disputes by majority vote; the Kolis alone among the district’s Christians had a headman or council; a Samvedi Brahmin was the patil of Koprad. [n08] [n34] [n39] The modern Sahapedia account says the Christian Vadvals have a village council that fines those who shirk their duties at deaths and marriages. [n41; Sahapedia] For the Kuparis the notes record no such council. The parish, its priest and its brotherhoods held the place that a caste council held for others. [n34]

Character, as neighbours see it. Vasaiker calls the Kuparis “more conservative” and the Vadvals “more fun-loving”. [n07] Swarajya’s writer, a Hindu of the same villages, wrote in 2019 that the villages remain “highly segregated socially and communally”, that each Catholic caste group has its own organisations and they rarely intermarry, and that Hindu Vadvals, affluent Catholics and Samvedi Brahmins dominate the villages; he also wrote that he knew of no communal incident in his lifetime. [n07; Swarajya] [n41; Swarajya] An East Indian blog, by contrast, says there is “hardly any stratification according to caste”. [n07] The sources contradict each other here, as neighbours’ accounts of a shared village usually do.

The picture that comes out of the comparison is of three communities of one landscape who shared nearly everything that belonged to the land, the gardens, the wells, the houses, the plantains, the red cap, the dialect’s hiss turning to an aspirate, and who parted on what belonged to the altar and the grave. Conversion did not make the Kuparis strangers in their own villages. It made them a distinct household on a shared lane, eating pork where their cousins did not, burying where their cousins burned, calling the godfather padron and the chair kadair, and carrying the same plantains to the same Bombay market.

What the customs say

Look back over the chapter and a pattern repeats in every section. At the bottom lies the Konkan: the Marathi of Thana, the vade and the rice bread, the plank house under tiles, the checked sari tucked for field work, the procession and the booth at a wedding, the feast on a fixed day after a death. Over it lies a Gujarat-facing layer that the Kuparis share with the Samvedis and Vadvals and that only the dialect still shows plainly. Over that lies the parish: the godparents and their Portuguese titles, the violin master, the Latin and Portuguese and Marathi of the church, the pork feijoada and the sorpotel, the cross at the lane’s end, the procession of palms whose name is Portuguese and whose grammar is Marathi. Every Kupari custom this chapter has found is a meeting of those layers. None is purely Portuguese, and none is purely what the Samvedis had before 1535.

The research for this book cannot say when most of these customs took the forms the community now remembers. The 1882 Gazetteer gives a fixed point for the Christians of the district in general. The community blogs of 2007 and 2009 give a second fixed point for the Kuparis in particular. Between them lies more than a century in which the record is almost silent about how Kupari villages dressed, cooked, sang or married. The songs of Nandakhal, the red lugda, the Hamdao and the Saudio, the Pirsao by candlelight, the masalewalis going from yard to yard, the cross feasts: all of these are known from people who grew up with them, not from anyone who studied them. The collections of Paul Rumav and the grammar of Naresh Naik are the first written works on them, and both were beyond the reach of this book’s research. [n20]

The chapter can also say what is happening to these ways of life now. The dialect is spoken mostly by older people. The red cap and red sari have retreated from daily wear to weddings and feasts. The Nandakhal songs are rarely heard. The wadis that fed the kitchen and shaped the year are giving way to buildings. Every source that describes these things describes them as fading, and almost every one names the same causes: education, migration, the city and its media. [n13] [n34] [n07] That is the story of the next chapter: how the Kuparis left the gardens for the classroom, the office and the Gulf, how the Diocese of Vasai was made in 1998, how a priest from these villages became the voice of a green belt, and how a community that had lived for four centuries in the same dozen villages found itself on the edge of a megacity of more than a million people.
