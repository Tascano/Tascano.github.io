Period covered: 1870 – 1899

In this chapter. The railway makes a new economy. This chapter follows the mills, the plantain trade, the Gazetteer of 1882 and the plague of the 1890s.

Every Saturday, on the far side of the tracks at Virar, a market gathered by a pond. The Gazetteer of 1882 sets the scene in a single sentence: “To the east of the Virar station, across the railway, is the market place, on the west bank of a large pond under big banyan and mango trees. A large market is held every Saturday, thronged by Kolis and Varlis, selling fish, garden stuff, and cloth.” [n12; Thana Gazetteer XIV, p. 382]

It is the oldest description of Virar as a place where people met, and it holds the whole of this chapter in small. The fishing people of the coast and the forest people of the hills came down to a station that had not existed twenty years before. Beyond the pond, on the rock, were two cow’s hoofprints that the village said a goddess’s cow had made when it leapt from the top of Jivdhan hill. And on the line beside them, every day, trains carried the island’s plantains and betel leaves south to Bombay.

The thirty years from 1870 to 1899 were the years when Bassein taluka became, in effect, a garden for the city. They were also the years when a scholar opened Sopara’s oldest mound, when a paper mill failed in the creeks, when a community chose a new name for itself, and, at the very end, when plague came up the line and the forest people carried their firewood into Bassein in defiance of the law. They are also the years for which the island was counted, field by field and caste by caste, more carefully than ever before: nine kinds of plantain, eleven thousand salt pans, two and a half thousand wells, and the names of two householders of Gass.

1870s: Mills, a hill station and a famine

The decade began with a factory. In 1871 Messrs Johnson and Littlewood, the second name already familiar from the sugar estate in the fort, started a paper mill at Sasunavghar, “a small port about six miles east of Bassein” on the creek. Its buildings, English machinery and a dam across the nearby stream cost £8,000, or Rs 80,000. [n12; Gazetteer XIV]

Sasunavghar was a real port. In the five years to 1878–79 it averaged £4,661 of exports and £1,007 of imports a year, and its exports swung wildly, from £118 in 1874–75 to £10,827 in 1876–77. The notes do not explain the swing. [n12; Gazetteer XIV]

In 1872 Bassein town gained its first dispensary. It was named after Balvantrav Hari Naik and housed in a building given by Yashvantrao Balvant Naik. It had an assistant surgeon, and by 1880–81 it was treating 18,324 out-patients and 43 in-patients a year, paid for by the government (Rs 1,730), the municipality (Rs 1,700) and local funds (Rs 600). By 1881 the building was in bad repair. [n32; Gazetteer XIV, p. 36; n28; Gazetteer II, Health]

The two volumes of the Gazetteer do not quite agree about how busy it was. The Places of Interest volume gives the 18,324 out-patients and 43 in-patients of 1880–81, with grants of £173 from the government, £170 from the municipality and £60 from local funds. The district volume’s health chapter gives 15,038 out-door and 23 in-door patients against 16,149 and 40 the year before, at a cost of £536 (Rs 5,363), and records fifteen cases of cholera in August with five deaths. The figures probably belong to different years; neither book says which. [n21; Gaz XIV p. 36; Gaz XIII-II c. p. 667] Across the whole district there were by 1882 twelve dispensaries, seven of them paid for from local funds. [n21; Gaz XIII-II]

Cholera was the disease the island knew best. The Gazetteer’s health chapter, working from the Collector’s returns, says that it was absent from the district in only two years of the period it reviewed, 1873 and 1874. In 1878 it appeared in Mahim and Bassein in February; in 1879 it “began in April in Bassein” and continued to the end of the year; and in 1881 it started among the fishermen of Kelva Mahim in April and “prevailed in Bassein from May to July”. [n21; Gaz XIII-II Ch. XII] The OCR of these pages is partial, and the quotations are as close as the scan allows. Behind them lay an older memory: the same Gazetteer recalls that the cholera of 1818 and 1819 had lowered the district’s numbers so far that it did not recover for years, and that about 1690 the Portuguese city of Bassein had been “stricken by a pestilential fever or plague, ‘exactly like a bubo’”, which returned at intervals and robbed it of about a third of its people. [n21; Gaz XIII-II; Gaz XIV p. 33] The second sentence would read differently by the end of this chapter.

The same year a census was taken, and its tables give the first numbers for the island’s peoples that can be set beside the descriptions in Chapter 11. In Bassein taluka there were 2,025 Muslims, 1,866 Sunni and 159 Shia. The oldest of them, the Gazetteer says, were the Konkanis, descended from “the Arab and Persian refugees, merchants, and adventurers, who settled along the coast in the eighth, ninth, and tenth centuries”, whose home speech was a dialect of Marathi. There were 6,099 Varlis in the taluka. There were 2,563 Samvedi Brahmans, and they lived only in Bassein and Mahim. Across the whole district, only 1,823 people were counted as Jains. [n08; Gazetteer I, pp. 60, 81–82, 182–183, 216–217]

The census of 1872 also gives the whole sub-division, and those numbers survive in the Gazetteer’s table of “Thana Population Sub-divisional Details”, though the OCR of the table is poor and several figures have had to be repaired by arithmetic. Bassein had 61,089 people. About 44,253 of them were Hindus (the printed total is garbled, but the male and female figures of 22,710 and 21,543 are legible). The Muslims were the 2,025 already named, 1,041 men and 984 women. The Christians numbered 13,126. That left about 1,685 for the Parsis, Jews and others, a residual that cannot be checked because the rows for them are unreadable. [n21; Gaz XIII-I pp. 70–71] So in 1872 roughly one person in five in the sub-division, about 22 per cent, was a Christian. [n21; Inferences, derived]

The same table puts the Christians of the whole Thana district at 37,029, of whom 22,855 lived in Salsette and 13,126 in Bassein; the Gazetteer’s prose on another page says “about 22,800 in Salsette” and, by an obvious error of the scan, “138,000 in Bassein”, which must be read as about 13,800. [n21; Gaz XIII-I p. 199] Of the district’s Christians, 31,062 were Catholics and 5,955 Protestants, and most of the Protestants, 5,664 of them, were “native Christians” made by the Protestant missions. [n34; Gaz XIII-I pp. 71–72, 199] In Bassein the Christian population was almost entirely the old Catholic one.

The prose chapters on castes add a few more Bassein figures for 1872: about 1,050 Kunbis and Kunbi-Talheris, and “nearly 8000” Marathas, though the digit in that last figure is uncertain in the scan. [n21; Gaz XIII-I pp. 124, 128] Across the whole district the census counted 15,367 Pachkalshis, the gardening and carpentering caste whose Bassein branch was known as the Vadvals, 2,311 Palshe Brahmans and 585 Karhadas, and 70,015 Varlis, the great majority of them in Dahanu. [n41; Gaz XIII-I p. 129; n35; Gaz XIII-I p. 63; n08] And a separate census volume of 1872 lists the municipal town of Bassein with 9,356 inhabitants and a municipal income of Rs 6,579, again with digits the researchers could not fully trust. [n21; Census 1872, Part II, table of towns]

At Bassein Road station the trains were filling. In 1873 it handled 86,473 passengers (the district volume’s table reads 86,478; one digit of the scan is uncertain); by 1880 the figure was 140,837. Goods traffic went the other way, falling from 5,292 tons to 3,278. [n32; Gazetteer XIV, pp. 28, 36; n21; Gaz XIII-I p. 340]

The station stood 33¾ miles from Bombay on what the Gazetteer calls the Baroda line, ninety-five miles of track within the district that had been begun in May 1858 and opened for traffic on 28 November 1864. It crossed the Bassein channel on two runs of sixty-foot girders, 69 spans and 25, and the station had a waiting room and a travellers’ bungalow. Virar lay five miles further on, at 38¾ miles, and Bhayandar, at 28¼, was the last station before the creek. [n21; Gaz XIII-I pp. 328–329] The whole line grew in the 1870s at a pace no one on the island could have imagined in the days of the bullock cart: passengers from 865,990 in 1873 to 1,485,391 in 1880, goods from 20,075 tons to 45,438. [n21; Gaz XIII-I p. 340] Bassein’s falling goods traffic was the exception, and the Gazetteer’s own tables suggest the reason: the bulky trade of the island went on, as it had for two thousand years, by water, while the train carried people and the lighter, perishable cargo.

The goods station that grew fastest on this stretch was not on the island at all. Bhayandar, across the creek, went from 2,627 tons in 1873 to 19,770 in 1880, and the Gazetteer explains that most of it was salt from the Rai Murdha pans about three miles west of the station. [n21; Gaz XIV p. 45; Gaz XIII-I p. 340] Salt, as will be seen below, was one of the island’s oldest exports too.

On Tungar, the dream of a hill station ran out. Mr Hope’s road was finished; in May 1876 the summit’s thermometer read 74°F at sunrise and 82°F at four in the afternoon, and rainfall there was about 80 inches. The Gazetteer named the points from which visitors could look out: Vaitarna point, the highest, then Kaman point, Bellevue and Panorama, with views of Kamandurg, Matheran and the Sahyadri. But by 1874 the scheme had failed, and the fifty-three house sites marked out in 1869 stayed empty. One small loss came with the hotel: on the summit, the Gazetteer notes, a stone image of a Jain saint “has lately been broken to pieces by the Roman Catholic hotel-keeper”. [n12; Gazetteer XIV, pp. 366–368; n10; Gazetteer XIV]

In 1874, too, the sugar factory that had worked since 1852 inside the old church of Nossa Senhora da Vida closed. Da Cunha, publishing his history in 1876, called it “recently closed”. Of the whole half-century of sugar in the fort, from Lingard’s lease of 1829 to the closing of the church factory, the notes record no year in which it made a profit. [n32; Gazetteer XIV, p. 40; da Cunha 1876, pp. 226–227] The Gazetteer’s own summary of the venture is a list of leases and failures: Lingard’s forty-year rent-free lease of about 83 acres on the fort esplanade, with a £2,300 advance, in 1829, and his death in 1832; McGregor Brownrigg and Company’s perpetual lease of about 115 acres in 1841; the conversion of the church in 1852; the thirty-year lease of 1860 to Macfarlane and Littlewood as the “Bassein Sugar Company”, at £27 2s a year; the sale in 1861 to Lawrence and Company. By 1881 the company “has long ceased to exist”. [n21; Gaz XIII-I pp. 394–395] Sugar itself went on being made on the island, but not in a factory. It was made, the same chapter says, by Pachkalshis, Malis, Native Christians and Samvedi Brahmans in their own villages, in a season from February to June. [n21; Gaz XIII-I p. 390]

Da Cunha’s book of 1876, Notes on the History and Antiquities of Chaul and Bassein, is itself an event of the decade. It is the first full history of Portuguese Bassein in English, written by a Goan doctor-scholar, and it gives an image of the taluka as it then stood under the Maratha-era divisions it still used: 161 villages, four of them held as inam; Khani Vadem with a small bandar; the Manikpura mahal in the south-east with its railway station four and a half miles from the fort; Agashi in the north; Tungar hill; Nirmala; and “Supara”, in his words “once a famous bandar, now almost abandoned”. [n32; da Cunha 1876, pp. 156–157]

Then the famine came. In 1876 and 1877 the rains failed across the Deccan and over Thana, in one of the worst famines of the century. The Gazetteer speaks of poverty “caused by the 1876 and 1877 famines” and of a large export of grain to Karachi in 1879. It does not say what the famine did in Bassein itself, and no source in the notes does. The silence should be left as it is. [n28; Gazetteer I]

There is one indirect trace, and it should be read with care. The Gazetteer’s footnote on the trade of Bassein port gives the figures year by year for the five years to 1878–79. Exports were £78,004 in 1874–75 and £97,480 in 1875–76. In 1876–77 they fell to £20,710, less than a quarter of the year before, and they did not recover: £33,868 in 1877–78 and £27,007 in 1878–79. Imports moved less, from £19,225 and £19,176 in the first two years to £17,205 in 1876–77, then up to £33,547 in 1877–78 and £23,355 in 1878–79 (the text and the footnote disagree on the 1877–78 import figure, giving £38,547 in one place). [n21; Gaz XIV p. 36, footnote] Agashi’s exports swung too, from a low of £24,643 in 1875 to a high of £48,181 in 1879. [n21; Gaz XIV p. 2] A port that suddenly stops sending out rice and molasses in the famine year, and starts taking in more, looks like a port whose surplus has gone; but the Gazetteer does not draw that conclusion, and the collapse may have other causes, from a bad harvest in Gujarat to a quirk of record-keeping. It is an inference, not a finding.

The district had known famine and failed seasons before, and the Gazetteer lists them, which gives the 1870s a measure. In 1824–25 the rain failed in Dahanu, Bassein and Salsette, grain sold at famine prices, but no one died of starvation and the government spent £1,550 clearing reservoirs. In 1837 the crops of Sanjan and Bassein failed and remissions of about Rs 45,000 were granted; in 1838–39 the failure covered the whole district. In 1848 most of the salt-rice crop failed; in 1850 the coast suffered drought; in 1854 worms injured the salt-rice crop of Bassein. [n21; Gaz XIII-I p. 303] The island’s average rainfall, in the ten years to 1881, was 71.87 inches. [n21; Gaz XIII-II p. 678] It was a well-watered coast, and its gardens were watered from wells besides; that may be part of why the Gazetteer’s famine pages do not name it.

In 1877 one community felt a different blow. The government raised the tax on palm trees, and the Bhandaris, the singing tappers with their gourds and broad knives, began to give up their craft. “Since (1877) the recent rise in the palm tree cess”, the Gazetteer says, “many have become husbandmen and labourers.” [n08; Gazetteer I, p. 150]

The tax on the trees was not simple, and the Gazetteer’s excise chapter shows how it bore on this island in particular. In the district generally the tapping cess stood at Rs 2-2-0 on each brab palm, Rs 2-10-6 on each coconut palm and 8½ annas on each date palm. In Bassein and Agashi an older due, the bud-dene, had been compounded with an excise cess at about a rupee and two annas on each coconut and brab palm and 6 annas 9 pies on each date palm. In the Sivan, Naman and Manikpur divisions of Bassein, and in all of Mahim, the right to sell palm and country liquor retail was auctioned every year. The fractions in the scan are not secure. [n21; Gaz XIII-II Ch. X, c. pp. 643–649] What the chapter says about the people is clearer: even after the government made concessions, the Bassein Bhandaris “continued unruly and discontented”. [n21; Gaz XIII-II Ch. X] Twenty years later the price of liquor would bring the coast’s Kolis, and its Christians, out against the government.

How many tappers there were is uncertain. The 1881 caste table for Bassein reads 8,334 Bhandaris, but the leading digit may be a 3, which would make it 3,334. [n21; Gaz XIII-II p. 680] Either way, they were a large community in a sub-division of under seventy thousand, and the rise in the cess touched thousands of households whose income came from climbing trees before dawn.

The same year the paper mill at Sasunavghar finally began to work. It made paper from grass and rice straw, “but none was ever sold although many dealers had approved of the samples”. It needed another boiler to make a ton of paper a day, and there was no money to buy one. In 1880 the manager died in an accident and the mill closed: “the project failed through want of funds to buy new machinery”. [n12; Gazetteer XIV]

Also in 1877, the government granted the Bassein municipality a public garden, by Resolution 1265 of 26 February. Across from it stood the new Robertson fruit and vegetable markets, named after the Collector James Walker Robertson, with the municipal office on the upper floor. In 1878 the town built a school-house for Rs 5,601, with room for 150 pupils; by 1881 it was the chief of six vernacular schools in the town. [n32; Gazetteer XIV, p. 36]

The town had a longer claim in schooling than those figures suggest. “The first Government vernacular school”, the Gazetteer says of the whole district, “was opened at Bassein in 1827, and the second three years after at Kalyan.” Thana district had 27 government schools with 1,588 pupils in 1857–58, 151 in 1877–78, and 154 with 7,842 pupils in 1879–80. [n21; Gaz XIII-II pp. 656–657] In the villages, schools of another kind were appearing. A modern heritage survey dates the Zilla Parishad (local board) school at Juchandra to 1872 and the one at Bolinj to 1879; the one at Agashi, by the same survey, opened in 1886. [n40; CRIT 2008, Vol I items 43–46, 61–64, 111–114] The parish schools of the Christian villages were older still, and smaller: at Agashi in 1881 the parish school had seventeen pupils, who learned to read Portuguese, arithmetic, singing, doctrine and music from a master who also played the violin in church. [n21; n34; Gaz XIV p. 2]

The town also had a newspaper. The Gazetteer’s list of the district’s presses names the Vasai Samachar, “the Bassein News”, a Marathi weekly published at Bassein on Sundays at Rs 2½ a year, and describes it in 1882 as being of five years’ standing. [n21; Gaz XIII-II c. p. 662] It was founded, therefore, in about 1877, in the same year as the public garden and the failure of the paper mill. No copy of it is known to the research behind this book, and nothing is known of its editors or its politics. It is the first local press of the coast.

The municipality that ran the garden and the market was eighteen years old. It had been established in 1864 by Government Resolution 621 of 15 March, and its limits took in the old fort and most of the modern town, which stood in the village of Malonde, together with the survey villages of Dhauli, Sandor and Mulgaon, “and many gardens and fields between Bassein and Papdi”. [n21; Gaz XIV p. 36] It was one of seven of the district’s nine municipalities set up under Act XXVI of 1850. [n21; Gaz XIII-II pp. 653–654] Its accounts for 1880–81 survive in a single sentence. It raised £714 (Rs 7,141) from octroi, house and privy taxes, tolls and market fees, and it spent £664 (Rs 6,637), of which £117 went on scavenging, £46 on lighting and £77 on roads. [n21; Gaz XIV p. 36] Set beside the Rs 6,579 recorded for the municipality in the census of 1872, that is growth of about nine per cent in nine years, if the earlier figure has been read correctly. [n21; Inferences, derived] It was a small council with a small purse, sweeping streets, lighting a few lamps and keeping its market roofed, in a town of ten thousand people. The Mamlatdar’s office, the seat of the taluka’s revenue officer, had been completed in 1869 at a cost of £3,553. [n21; Gaz XIV p. 36]

At Virar the station was growing faster than anywhere else on the line. In 1878 it handled 48,294 passengers; in 1880, 83,176. Its goods doubled, from 1,730 tons to 3,349. A metalled road ran four miles west from the station to Agashi, and for visitors to Sopara, Virar was the nearest stop. The station had refreshment rooms and waiting rooms. Up the line at Saphale, below Tandulwadi fort, passengers grew from 10,177 to 19,142 in the same two years. [n12; Gazetteer XIV, p. 382]

What the trains carried, the Gazetteer spells out. “By rail, chiefly from the Virar station, molasses, rice, and betel-leaves pass north to Ankleshvar, Nadiad, Viramgam, and Patan, and large quantities of plantains and betel-leaves are sent south to Bombay.” Coming back the other way was oilcake from Gujarat, spread on the sugarcane as manure. [n12; Gazetteer XIV; n05]

The plantain was the crop that made the island’s name. “Agashi drives a great trade with Bombay in plantains and betel leaves”, the Gazetteer says, “its dried plantains being the best in the district.” Earlier drafts of this book, following a summary of the Gazetteer’s crafts chapter, called drying a specialist trade of three villages, Agashi, Vatar and Koprad. The fuller reading of the agriculture chapter widens the map: “the well-known Bassein dried-plantains”, it says, were prepared only in Agashi, Vagholi, Vatar, Bolinj, Koprad, Nala, Umrale, Rajodi and Murdes, nine villages of the garden belt. Agashi, Vatar and Koprad are the three where the crafts chapter counts the families who did the work (more on them below). Local dealers sent the dried fruit to Thana, Bombay, Surat, Poona and Sholapur. [n21; Gaz XIII-I pp. 292, 395; n40] A Marathi source adds what the Gazetteer does not: that the dried banana, sukeli, was made by drying ripe bananas of the Rajeli variety in the sun, and that Agashi was its centre. In the Samvedi speech of these villages the word itself changed shape, sukeli becoming hukeli. [n05; Gazetteer Part I, crafts; n11; Gazetteer XIV, p. 2; n20; mr.wikipedia: सामवेदी बोली; Vishwakosh via snippet]

The sea trade had not ended. In the five years to 1879, Agashi’s exports by sea averaged Rs 3,18,470 and its imports Rs 85,650. Bassein’s own port, in the five years to 1878–79, exported on average Rs 5,14,140 a year and imported Rs 2,25,200. In 1881–82 the three Bassein ports together, Agashi, Bassein and Navghar, were estimated to handle trade of £108,868: imports of £27,127 and exports of £81,741. (An earlier draft of this chapter, working from a snippet, could not name the currency; the full text of the trade chapter gives it in pounds.) Their chief exports were husked rice, molasses, coconuts and plantains, shipped to Bombay and Gujarat, with firewood and tiles to Bombay. They took in wheat, pulse and ghee from Bombay and Surat, timber from Bombay, piece-goods, oil and oil-cake, and lime. Exports were worth three times imports. The island sent out food and took little in return. [n11; Gazetteer XIV, pp. 2, 36; n21; n36; Gaz XIII-I p. 358] For the customs year 1880–81 the Bassein division’s figures read £28,267 of imports and £74,072 of exports, but the printed parts do not add up to the printed total of £102,329, and one of them is a misreading. [n21; Gaz XIII-I p. 355 approx.]

In 1879, too, castor seed was grown on 4,888 acres in the Bassein area, and in 1879–80 a single contract system for the toddy and liquor excise was brought in for Bassein, Mahim and Dahanu together. [n28; Gazetteer I; Gazetteer II, Excise]

1880s: The Gazetteer, the mound and the name

Counting the island

The census of 1881 counted Bassein town at 10,356 people: 6,850 Hindus, 2,623 Christians, 885 Muslims, 29 counted as “wild tribes”, 14 Parsis and 5 Jews. Their houses lay half a mile inland, north of the fort walls; the old city inside was, in the Gazetteer’s word, “desolate”. [n32; Gazetteer XIV, p. 28]

The whole sub-division, in the same census, held 68,967 people on 221 square miles, 312 to the square mile; a footnote adds that the revised count was about three hundred higher than the first total printed. [n21; Gaz XIII-II pp. 678–680] Of these 52,578 were Hindus, 76.23 per cent; 14,070 were Christians, 20.40 per cent; 2,292 were Muslims, 3.32 per cent; and 27 were Parsis. The percentages add up, which gives the counts some credibility in a scan where many numbers do not. [n21; Gaz XIII-II p. 680] Between 1872 and 1881 the sub-division had grown by about 12.9 per cent. [n21; Inferences, derived]

The same page breaks the Hindus down by caste. The scan is fuzzy and the researchers warn that any single figure should be checked against the page image before it is repeated, but the list as read gives a portrait of the island that no other source offers: 5,382 Brahmans, 827 Kayastha Prabhus, 880 Vanis, 80 Jains, 8,461 Agris (the first digit could be a 3), 5,978 Kunbis, 839 Sonars, 519 Sutars, 876 Shimpis, 216 Kumbhars, 214 Kasars, 821 Dhangars (perhaps 321), 2,375 Mangelas and 77 Machhis, both of them fishermen, 16 Bhois, the river fishers, 8,334 Bhandaris (perhaps 3,334), 7,308 Varlis, 7,048 Konkanis, 1,600 Kathkaris, 967 Vaitis, 698 Thakurs, 114 Dublas, 321 Chambhars, 1,482 Mhars and 50 Mangs. [n21; Gaz XIII-II p. 680] (The “Konkanis” in a Hindu list are presumably a Hindu caste of that name; the Konkani Muslims were counted among the Muslims.) What the table shows, even allowing for its errors, is a Hindu society of gardeners, salt-makers, toddy-tappers, fishermen and forest peoples, with a small Brahman and writer class, set beside a Christian fifth of the population that was itself drawn from many of the same castes.

Another figure from 1881 has travelled widely: 6,823 people, of whom 5,168 were Hindus, 1,500 Christians and 155 Muslims. One note, working only from a search-engine snippet, attached it to “Bassein island”. The note that read the Gazetteer’s full text found it in the entry for Agashi. The full-text reading is the better one, and this book treats 6,823 as the population of Agashi in 1881. Either way, about one person in five was Christian, a century and a half after Portuguese rule had ended. [n05; n11; Gazetteer XIV, p. 2]

Sopara in 1882 had about 1,700 people. [n11; Gazetteer XIV, p. 314]

The Christian parishes around the town counted their congregations in 1881, and their numbers make a small map of the faith on the island: Dhauli, the church of Our Lady of Remedies, 3,238, the largest; Sandor, St Thomas, 1,725; Papdi, Our Lady of Grace, 1,294; Merces, Our Lady of Mercy, 974; Pali, the Mother of God, 900; and Manikpur, St Michael, 800. Further north, Nirmal’s Holy Cross had 2,184. Most of these vicars were paid by the British government. At Merces, unusually, the vicar was paid Rs 17 a month by the Portuguese government. Several parishes kept schools, and several had a violin master; at Nirmal the teacher of the private school also played the violin in church. [n11; n32; Gazetteer XIV, pp. 36–37, 293]

Five years earlier, da Cunha had counted the same parishes and put the native Christians of Bassein at 12,400: Palli 800, Espirito Santo (the Holy Spirit church of Nandakhal) 2,000, Papari 1,200, Nossa Senhora das Merces 700, Monte Calvario 1,500, Manikpura 900, Nossa Senhora dos Remedios 3,000, Agasi 900 and São Tomé 1,000. [n40; da Cunha 1876, p. 161] The OCR headers of those pages are jumbled and the figures are rounded, but they agree with the Gazetteer on the shape of things: Remedy the largest congregation by far, the southern parishes round the fort numerous, the northern garden parishes of Agashi, Nirmal and the Holy Spirit together not much smaller.

The Gazetteer’s district volume has its own list, in a footnote, of the nine churches “for Bassein under nine priests” in 1881: Sandor (St Thomas), Papdi (Our Lady of Grace), Pali (Mother of God), Manikpur (St Michael), Davli (which it calls Our Lady of Help), Nirmal (Holy Cross, “St. Cruz”), Koprad (Holy Spirit), Agashi (St James, “St. Iagos”) and Bassein (Our Lady of Mercy). [n34; Gaz XIII-I p. 206 n. 3] Two things in that list are worth noticing. The Holy Spirit church, which modern sources place at Nandakhal, is listed under Koprad; the church stands in what was later Kofrad gram panchayat, on the Nandakhal road, so the two names describe one building. [n40; CRIT Vol I item 60; n34, Inferences] And the dedication given for Davli, the village of the Remedy church, is “Our Lady of Help”, not “of Remedies”; whether this is a slip or an older title the notes cannot say. Nandakhal and Bhuigaon, which would become famous parishes, do not appear as places in their own right.

Da Cunha also gives a hint of what the old Christian villages looked like through the eyes of an educated Goan in Bombay, and it is not flattering. The Catholics of Bassein, he wrote in 1876, were “pretty numerous clans of different types of the Kolis and Bhandaris”, with the “hybrid” offspring of European soldiers, and “there is no descendant of any high-class native amongst them”. [n34; da Cunha 1876, pp. 250–251] He was a hostile witness, a Goan Brahman writing in the middle of a bitter quarrel between Goan and local Catholics, and a modern historian reads his tone as contempt born of that quarrel. [n34; Mendiratta 2017, p. 234] The Gazetteer, compiled from district officers in the same years, contradicts him outright for the northern villages, as will be seen.

The islanders’ work showed in other corners of the record. In the fair season Dublas worked the salt-works near Bassein Road station, and the Agris of Bassein were called simply “salt-workers”. [n28; Gazetteer I, Salt]

The salt chapter of the Gazetteer, read in full, gives the island’s pans their only measurement of the century. “The seventeen Bassein works are arranged in six groups with 11,374 pans, an area of 1,439 acres, and thirty owners. The Bassein works are widely scattered on the north bank of the Bassein creek; a few are in the interior on the banks of rivers.” [n21; Gaz XIII-I p. 364] On the same page the other divisions of the district give the scale: Uran with 105 works, 18,373 pans on 3,241 acres and 1,018 owners; Ghodbandar with 37 works and 22,923 pans on 1,616 acres; Trombay with 17 works and 10,942 pans; Panvel with 22. [n21; Gaz XIII-I p. 364] Bassein’s 1,439 acres were about eighteen per cent of the district’s salt land, the third largest share after Uran and Ghodbandar; and with only thirty owners for seventeen works, its pans were held in far fewer hands than Uran’s. [n21; Inferences, derived]

The salt tax stood at Rs 2 on every Bengal maund of 82½ pounds, and ground-rent in Bassein at a rupee and a half an acre. [n21; Gaz XIII-I p. 365 footnote] The workers were Agris, Kharvas and, near the station only, Dublas from Gujarat; in Bassein and Ghodbandar they were paid by the unit of eighty Bengal maunds, five to eight rupees a unit. [n21; Gaz XIII-I pp. 365–366] In 1876, the Gazetteer adds, “the salt trade was thrown open to private enterprise”, and since then the quality of Bassein salt had improved through the Gujarat Dubla workmen. [n21; Gaz XIII-I p. 370] Bassein and Ghodbandar salt together commanded about three-quarters of the Uran trade and competed along the railways. [n21; Gaz XIII-I p. 370] The Thana works as a whole sold twenty to thirty lakh maunds a year, ten to fourteen lakh of it going by sea to the Madras ports and Calcutta, about four lakh by rail to the Nizam’s dominions and six to eight lakh to the Central Provinces. [n21; Gaz XIII-I p. 370] The table that would give Bassein’s own outturn and revenue survives only as two unlabelled numbers in a broken row, 72,929 and 13,02,192, and nobody can now say whether they are maunds, rupees or something else. [n21; Gaz XIII-I p. 365, gaps] The salt of the north bank went into curries in Hyderabad and Nagpur; the island’s own ledger for it is lost.

And every November the island gathered at Nirmal. On Kartik Vadya 11, the anniversary of the Shankaracharya’s death, a week-long fair drew six to seven thousand pilgrims, “Hindus, Musalmans, Christians, and a few Parsis, from all parts of Thana, Gujarat, Bombay, the Deccan, and south Konkan”. They bought brass and copper vessels, dried plantains, sweets, cloth and cattle, about Rs 10,000 worth in all. The Shankaracharya temple received a yearly government grant of Rs 421. [n11; Gazetteer XIV, p. 292; n10]

The Gazetteer

In 1882 the Government of Bombay published the Gazetteer of Thana, edited by James M. Campbell, in three parts. It is the single richest source for this whole book, and for these decades almost the only one. Its two volumes on the district and its third on “Places of Interest” set down the island’s castes, crops, ports, churches, temples, fairs, inscriptions and stones, house by house in places. The Sopara entry, running from page 314 to 342, was written with Pandit Bhagwanlal Indraji. [n05; NVLI; n15; Gazetteer XIV]

Its compilers had local helpers, and some of them have their names in the text. The Gazetteer thanks Ramdas Kasidas Modi, “a native of Sopara”, as an informant. It names Bhiku Patil, the Samvedi Brahman headman of Koprad, near whose house lay an inscribed stone of the fifteenth century. At Bolinj, which “was formerly a large town and probably had rich temples”, a stone inscribed in Devanagari was serving as a doorstep in the house of a Christian, “Juju Manu Lop, that is Joseph Manuel Lopez”. [n11; Gazetteer XIV, pp. 314, 342]

At Gass the record is most vivid of all. In front of the house of Degu Pobria Mhatara lay a six-sided basalt block that his family used for washing clothes and grinding curry powder. On it, in Pali, was one word: “Badhuya”, “of Badhu”, cut about 200 BC. At the house of the Samvedi Brahman Bab Naik another block read “Kodasa Kalavadasa”, “of Kalavad the Kod”, and “Ugudeveya”, “of Ugradeva”. In a Gass field called Sonarbhat, the “gold field”, lay a broken cross-legged female figure, perhaps Ambika, the tomb of the Muslim saint Shaikh Akbar-moi-ud-din bordered with very large old bricks, and an eleventh- or twelfth-century door stone. On the shore of Gass lake, at a place called “Mala”, were the foundations of a Brahmanic temple of the same age. “The village of Gas”, the Gazetteer adds, “according to the people of the place, is the site of old Sopara.” [n11; Gazetteer XIV, p. 341]

The book describes the modern Hindu shrines too: Hanuman by the sea gate of the fort and Trivikram, which drew a yearly government allowance of Rs 1,061, the largest temple grant in the taluka; Bhavani and Trimbakeshvar in Arnala fort, with the tomb of two Muslim saints, Shahali and Hajiali, endowed with Rs 80 a year; and up on Jivdhan hill, above Virar, the ruins of a fort with “very old-looking caves and cisterns said to be the work of the Pandavs”. There, in the fair season, people, “especially barren women from the surrounding villages”, made offerings to an invisible goddess who was believed to have fled from a niche in one of the caves “at the touch of a Mhar”. They threw betel nuts into a hole in the niche. [n32; n11; n12; Gazetteer XIV, pp. 11, 37, 382–383]

The Gazetteer tells the story of that goddess as the village told it. The Mhar, the village herdsman of Virar, always found among the village cattle a cow whose owner never paid him. He followed it up Jivdhan hill, where a woman appeared and agreed to pay. As she was about to put the money in his hand he said, “Do not touch me, I am a Mhar.” At that the goddess vanished, “and the cow leapt from the hill-top and lighted on this rock”. The rock, with two cow’s feet cut into it, lay about 200 yards beyond a land-grant stone carved with a cow and calf, a hundred yards east of the station. [n12; Gazetteer XIV, pp. 382–383]

The mound of the Burud king

The Gazetteer’s most dramatic pages describe something that happened while it was being written.

Bhagwanlal Indraji was born on 8 November 1839 at Junagadh, a Nagar Brahman from Kathiawar, and had come to Bombay under the patronage of the physician and scholar Bhau Daji Lad in 1862. By 1882 he was the most accomplished Indian epigrapher of his day, an honorary member of the Royal Asiatic Society since 1877. [n25; Wikipedia: Bhagwanlal Indraji]

Near Sopara, in the village of Merdes (Mardes), stood a brick mound that local people called Burud Rajache Kot, the fort of the Burud king. In 1882 Indraji opened it. Most sources say he dug in April; some say February, and the month is not settled. Inside the mound he found a brick chamber, and in the chamber a large stone coffer. [n02; Wikipedia: Sopara; n18]

The coffer held caskets nested one inside another: copper, silver, stone, crystal and gold. With them were many small gold flowers, scented powder, jewellery, and fragments of what the Asiatic Society of Mumbai still describes as “a begging bowl believed to have been used by Gautama Buddha”. There was a silver coin of a Satavahana king. Sources disagree on which one: Wikipedia and Shah name Gautamiputra Satakarni, LiveHistoryIndia names Yajnashri Satakarni, and the Gazetteer itself read the ruler as “Gotamiputra Yajnashri Shatakarni”. Around the caskets sat eight bronze images: seven Buddhas, the six of the past with Shakyamuni, and Maitreya, the Buddha to come. [n02; Asiatic Society of Mumbai; Shah 2020; LiveHistoryIndia; n15; Gazetteer XIV, p. 320]

The bronzes were not as old as the stupa. They are dated by style to the seventh to ninth centuries CE, perhaps put in when the stupa was enlarged or repaired, possibly under the Rashtrakutas. The mound had been first raised in the Mauryan period and enlarged between the first century BCE and the third century CE. It had been a living shrine, opened and added to, for something like a thousand years. [n02; LiveHistoryIndia; Shah 2020]

Indraji found one more thing. Near the Bhatela pond, east of Sopara, lay a broken slab of basalt carrying part of the lines of the eighth of Ashoka’s Rock Edicts, about a third of it, in Brahmi letters. In that edict Ashoka contrasts the pleasure-tours of earlier kings with his own “tours of morality”. D. C. Sircar would later reason that a full set of the fourteen rock edicts had probably once stood near Sopara. (Some popular accounts say Indraji found pieces of both the eighth and ninth edicts, near Ramkund. Sircar’s primary publication gives the eighth to 1882 and the ninth to a later find, and this book follows Sircar.) [n02; Sircar, Epigraphia Indica 32; n22]

From the coffer and the stone, the Gazetteer drew a conclusion. The finds showed, it said, that “about the middle of the second century after Christ (A.D. 160), Gotamiputra Yajnashri Shatakarni was the ruler of the Konkan; and that Sopara was then a Buddhist religious centre, and had skilful masons, coppersmiths, and goldsmiths”. The edict “seems to show that [Sopara] was the capital of Aparanta in B.C. 250”. Indraji also recorded a local tradition that the Buddha had converted the Brahman sage Vakkali and five hundred widows here and given them relics of his nails and hair, over which they built a “Widows’ Stupa”. He published his account the same year in the Journal of the Bombay Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society, as “Antiquarian Remains at Sopara and Padana”. [n15; Gazetteer XIV, p. 320; n02; Shah 2020]

The Bombay Government gave the relics to the Asiatic Society, where the stone coffer still stands in the vestibule. The edict went to the museum that is now the Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj Vastu Sangrahalaya. Wikipedia calls Indraji the first Indian to excavate and publish such a report; the University of Leiden gave him an honorary doctorate in 1884. One later writer, Fergusson in 1910, credited the excavation to Campbell instead, a conflict the notes cannot settle. Indraji died in Bombay on 16 March 1888. [n02; Asiatic Society of Mumbai; n25; Wikipedia: Bhagwanlal Indraji; Fergusson 1910]

So in the same year that the Gazetteer wished aloud for “a station at Sopara”, the town turned out to be one of the oldest documented places on the whole western coast of India. The man who proved it reached the mound from a railway station three and a half miles away.

What the 1882 records also show

The Gazetteer’s station list for 1882 is worth reading slowly: Bandra, Andheri, Goregaon, Borivli, Bhayandar, Bassein Road, Virar, Saphala, Palghar, Boisar, Vangaon, Dahanu Road, Gholvad, Vevji, Sanjan, Bhilad. There is no Naigaon, and no station at Sopara under any name. Whenever those two stations opened, it was after 1882. A Western Railway heritage book of 2009 gives 1865 for the Nallasopara station, which cannot be squared with the Gazetteer; the true year is unresolved. [n28; Gazetteer I; n31; Wikipedia: Nallasopara railway station]

The Gazetteer also wrote down, without meaning to, the sound of the island’s speech. The Samvedis said ade for kothe, “where”, had for ka, “why”, geltu for gela hota, “had gone”. The Mangelas said kain gela hota? for kothe gela hota? and mana bhuk nangli for mala bhuk lagli, “I am hungry”. The Varlis clipped their words, kot jas for kothe jatos, and used nangne for “to see”. The Son Kolis spoke with “so curious an accent” that their sounds shifted. The Pachkalshis used l for ḷ and n for ṇ. The Palshes said mad for madhe and kai for kothe. And the Konkani Muslims spoke a dialect of Marathi at home. These are the first written traces of the dialects that the government’s linguists would try to map twenty years later. [n08; Gazetteer I, pp. 81–82, 130, 147–148, 183, 216]

In 1882 the Collector’s letter of 24 January, already quoted, still complained that the Littlewood lease made “the proper preservation of the ruins almost impossible”. [n32; Gazetteer XIV, pp. 37–38]

The island’s ledger, 1880–81

The Gazetteer’s chapter on the sub-divisions gives Bassein a page of land and livestock that turns the gardens into numbers. Of its 221 square miles, about five and a half were alienated, held by grantees rather than the state. Of the rest, 64,098 acres, 46.4 per cent, were arable; 2,859 acres were unarable; 328 were grass; and 70,635 acres, more than half, were village sites, roads, ponds and river beds, a figure that in a coast of creeks and salt flats must include a great deal of water and mud. [n21; Gaz XIII-II p. 678] In 1881–82 the sub-division had 191 ponds and 2,624 wells, twenty-five of them with steps. [n21; Gaz XIII-II pp. 678–679] The wells were the gardens’ engines. The garden belt, the Gazetteer says, was a coastal strip about three miles broad of rich alluvial soil, “watered by Persian wheels from shallow wells”, its chief products plantains, sugarcane and coconuts. [n21; Gaz XIII-II p. 679]

In 1880–81, about 36,541 acres of government land were under tillage, and grain covered 31,885 acres of them, 87 per cent. Rice alone took 29,687 acres; nachni (ragi) 1,846; vari and other small millets 338. Pulses covered 1,555 acres, oilseeds 575, fibres 91, sugarcane 1,188, and “fruits, vegetables and other garden crops” 1,297. Several of those digits are uncertain in the scan. [n21; Gaz XIII-II p. 680] The proportions are the surprise. The island that sent plantains and betel to Bombay and molasses to Gujarat was, by acreage, a rice country; the gardens that made its name covered a few thousand acres of the best land near the wells, and on them was concentrated nearly all its wealth.

The livestock count of 1881, as read, gives 2,997 carts, 5,308 ploughs, 8,160 oxen, 4,879 cows, 6,466 buffaloes, 128 horses and 8,142 sheep and goats. [n21; Gaz XIII-II p. 680] Three thousand carts on an island of sixty-nine thousand people is a reminder that the carters were a large trade; who they were the Gazetteer says elsewhere.

The land paid its revenue on a settlement made a generation earlier. The British survey of Bassein had begun in 1858–59 and finished in 1861–62, and its rates had been fixed in 1861–62 for thirty years, in eighty-eight government villages. The settlement grouped the villages in four classes, with top dry-crop rates per acre of Rs 6, Rs 5½ and Rs 5, Rs 4½ and Rs 4, and Rs 3½ and Rs 3; garden land was in three classes with top rates of Rs 8, Rs 7 and Rs 6 an acre. Its effect had been to raise the revenue from about £10,644 to about £11,865, almost all the rise falling on the gardens, whose rental went from £2,277 to £2,988 while rice rents rose only about three per cent. [n21; Gaz XIII-II pp. 609–612] The survey officers’ reasoning was that capital had accumulated in the gardens since the downward revision of 1836–37. [n21; Gaz XIII-II pp. 610–611] By 1880 the land revenue stood at £12,671; there were 8,064 holdings in 1879–80, at an average rental of a little under fifteen rupees; and occupied land averaged 9 annas 3 pies an acre for dry crop, about Rs 2-15 for rice and about Rs 6 for garden. [n21; Gaz XIII-II p. 679] The thirty-year term ran out in 1891–92. What the revision settlement of that year did to the gardens is not in any source the research found; the next chapter of the island’s revenue history is a blank. [n21; Gaps]

Nine kinds of plantain

For the plantain itself the Gazetteer is unusually generous, because an officer named K. J. Ebden supplied it with a table. Nine varieties were grown in Bassein: basrai, mutheli, tambdi, rajeli, lokhandi, sonkeli, bankeli, karanjeli and narsingi. [n21; Gaz XIII-I p. 291] Ebden’s footnote gives the quantities usually sold in a year and the prices, with some digits uncertain: about 220,000 bunches of green basrai, sold by the hundred bunches; 12,000 of the round-ended mutheli at twelve annas a bunch; 50,000 of the red tambdi at a rupee and a half; 5,000 of rajeli at eight annas; 1,000 of lokhandi at a rupee; 200 of the bright yellow sonkeli at eight annas; 2,000 of the wild bankeli at twelve annas; 5,000 of karanjeli at four annas; and 5,000 of narsingi at a rupee. [n21; Gaz XIII-I p. 292] Altogether about three hundred thousand bunches a year, most of them basrai. [n21; Inferences] The names carry their descriptions with them: tambdi is red, sonkeli the gold banana, bankeli the forest banana, lokhandi the iron one.

The growers planted about a thousand basrai to the acre but only 550 tambdi, which fruited in the tenth month where the others fruited in the eighth. They manured first with oil-cake, the oil-cake that came down the railway from Gujarat, and then with fish. Fish, the Gazetteer notes, was cheaper and gave the best return, but bred worms. [n21; Gaz XIII-I p. 292] The fishing villages and the garden villages were tied together by the manure basket as well as by the market.

The dried plantain was made from one variety only. “The well-known Bassein dried-plantains are the fruit of the rajele variety.” [n21; Gaz XIII-I p. 292] At Agashi the yearly yield was about 160 tons, 3,000 Bengal maunds, worth about £2,700 (Rs 27,000). [n21; Gaz XIII-I p. 292] The work was done, the crafts chapter says, by “about eighty-five families of Sámvedi Bráhmans, fifteen families of Páchkalshis, and about seventy-five families of Native Christians originally Sámvedis and Páchkalshis” at Agashi, Vatar and Koprad, in a season that ran from October to January, after the monsoon. [n21; n34; Gaz XIII-I p. 395] It is one of the most important sentences in this book’s sources for the history of the Kuparis, and it will be read again below.

The sugarcane went out raw as well as boiled. About 750,000 canes were sent from Bassein every year, chiefly to Bombay, Surat and Broach, at about a rupee and a quarter the hundred. [n21; Gaz XIII-I pp. 291, 338] The island’s molasses were prized in Gujarat for gudaku, the tobacco paste smoked in the hookah. [n36; Gaz XIII-I p. 338]

The betel nut, the areca palm, was grown “chiefly in garden lands at Bassein and Bombay-Mahim”. A tree bore from its fifth or seventh year for twenty to twenty-five years, giving anything from 150 to 1,250 nuts, about three hundred on average. A garden of a thousand plants cost Rs 1,276 to bring into bearing over five years. The growers sold their nuts wholesale to the Vanis of Papdi, two miles from Bassein, who sorted them into six classes, phulbardi, tambdi, chikni, lavangchuri, pandhri and dagdi or kapkadi, and sold them at Rs 21 to Rs 36 for a maund of eighty pounds. “Bassein is famous for its phulbardi betelnuts.” [n21; Gaz XIII-I pp. 298–300] And the growers, the Gazetteer says, were “partly Christians (converted Malis) and partly Chavkalshi or Pachkalshi Hindus”. [n21; n41; Gaz XIII-I pp. 298–300] The nut went to Bombay, Poona and Gujarat. The betel leaf, the vine, the Gazetteer treats as a chief crop of Mahim rather than Bassein, though Agashi traded in it and the Virar trains carried it north and south. [n21; Gaz XIII-I p. 292; Gaz XIV pp. 2, 316]

The coconut, finally, was grown chiefly in Salsette, Bassein, Kelva-Mahim and Tarapur, and in Bassein it was “the usual practice” to let the seed nuts drop from the tree rather than pick them. Coconuts sold at Rs 5 for the nominal hundred, which was 172 nuts. [n21; Gaz XIII-I pp. 295–298]

Over all of it the Gazetteer passes one judgement. “The most hardworking and skilful husbandmen are the Christians of Bassein”, who grow sugarcane and plantains and have “turned the light sandy country about Bassein into an evergreen garden”. [n21; Gaz XIII-I p. 300] After them, the same passage ranks the Kunbis, and about equal with them the Christians of Salsette, the Agris who owned sweet rice land, and the Chavkalshis of Uran. [n41; Gaz XIII-I Ch. IV]

Fish, oysters and jolly-boats

The fishing coast is thinner in the Gazetteer than the gardens. It gives no Bassein boat count and no catch. It gives the castes: 2,375 Mangelas and 77 Machhis in 1881, and the observation that “the Uraps and other Christian Kolis in Bassein … are professional fishermen, but the mass of the sea-fishing population are Son Kolis”. [n21; Gaz XIII-I p. 55; Gaz XIII-II p. 680] It recalls that in 1851 the district had 129 salt-water fisheries, 22 of them in Bassein, and 101 fresh-water fisheries, 18 in Bassein, and that until Act XIX of 1844 the Kolis, Mangelas and Vaitis had paid a poll tax, the day-dene, of a rupee and a quarter for the right to fish. [n21; Gaz XIII-I p. 54 footnote] “Good oysters are found along the Bassein, Mahim and Dahanu coasts.” And it notes a change of gear: “along the coast as far as Bassein, fishermen have taken to use jolly-boats of from 14 to 25 feet long”, the ship’s boat of the European navies adopted by the Kolis. [n21; Gaz XIII-I pp. 56, 351 approx.] The Christian fishermen, the Christians chapter adds, wore red broadcloth caps. [n34; Gaz XIII-I p. 205]

Machvas, mhangiris and the railway’s bite

The sea trade that the Gazetteer records in pounds was carried in boats it describes in loving detail. The Thana coast had ten kinds of lateen-rigged craft, among them the balav for fishing, the machva, the padav or cotton boat, and the mhangiri, a large padav. “Some are built in Bombay, Versova, Dantivra, Mahim, Karanja and Daman, but most in the Bassein sub-division, chiefly at Bassein.” [n36; Gaz XIII-I pp. 342–343] The builders were “Marathas, Christians, Gujarat carpenters, Cutch Musalmans, Kolis, and Pachkalshis”, working teak and ain from Bombay, Bassein and Jawhar. The owners were Gujarat and Marwar Vanis, Brahmans, Agris, Bhatias, Lingayats, Bhandaris, Machhis, Kolis and Khojas. A boat lasted twenty to thirty years. Sailors were paid by a share of the earnings, the tandel, the captain, taking a double share; they steered “almost entirely by land-marks, and … by the stars”; and from early June to Coconut Day, the full moon of Shravan, when the monsoon sea was closed, the local craft were hauled up on the shore. [n36; Gaz XIII-I pp. 342–343]

For the three Bassein ports themselves, the trade chapter names the vessels as “mhangiris, machvas, paddvs, and batelas of from fifty to seventy tons … built locally by Marathas and Native Christians”, with crews from Bassein and the villages round it. [n36; n21; Gaz XIII-I pp. 358–359] The traders who shipped in them were “Musalmans, Marathas, local Vanis, and a few Brahmans”; about half were natives of Bassein, and the rest came from Kalyan, Bhiwandi, Bombay and Gujarat and stayed “from December to June”, the dry trading season. Visiting craft came from Gujarat, Cutch, Kathiawar, Diu, Daman and the south Konkan, anchoring twenty-five to two hundred feet off the landing at Bassein and a hundred to two hundred feet off Agashi. [n36; Gaz XIII-I pp. 358–359] At Agashi, Horsburgh’s sailing directions warned, the mouth of the bay was closed by a reef except for one channel, and the bay was “full of shoals and navigable only by boats and small vessels”. [n36; Gaz XIV p. 1]

Set against the district, the island’s sea trade was modest. Thana’s thirty-three ports, in seven customs divisions, did about £2 million of trade a year in the early 1880s; about 1,100 locally owned vessels worked the coast; and in 1880–81 some 19,959 vessels entered its ports with cargo and 34,717 cleared. [n36; Gaz XIII-I pp. 343, 358] The Bassein division’s £108,868 of 1881–82 was about five per cent of the district’s sea trade by value. [n36; Inferences, derived]

And the railway was eating into it. “Along the coast,” the Gazetteer says flatly, “the opening of the Baroda railway has destroyed the old cart traffic, and has reduced the sea-trade with Gujarat and Bombay.” [n36; Gaz XIII-I pp. 341–342] The ports that remained were feeders, carrying what was too bulky or too cheap for the railway’s rates: rice, molasses, firewood, tiles, salt. [n36; Inferences] The railway had a second effect on the island’s water, and the Gazetteer saw it on the ground: at Sopara the old creek was silting “especially since the long railway embankment has dammed the flow of water from the south”. [n36; n42; Gaz XIV pp. 314–315] Small boats of ten or twelve tons still came from the south as far as the landing at Gass and from the north as far as Bolinj; “at Sopara the creek is dry”. [n42; Gaz XIV p. 315] A Portuguese report of 1728 had already asked for the creek between Sopara and Bolinj to be dug “as deep as the height of a man”, or it would soon be filled level with the fields. [n36; Gaz XIV p. 322] The two reports, a century and a half apart, are a time series of a dying channel; the embankment hastened what the rivers had begun.

Traders from Cambay and Marwar

The Vanis of Papdi who graded the betel nuts belonged to a community whose arrival the Gazetteer can date. “Of about 120 families of Lad Vanis about forty are in Thana, thirty-five in Supara, and the rest in Bassein, Agashi, Nala, Papdi, and Dahanu.” [n35; Gaz XIII-I pp. 112–113] The Lads took their name from Lat-desh, the old name of south Gujarat. [n35; Enthoven III, p. 428 approx.] The Gazetteer thought that some of them might have been on this coast for a thousand years, since the Arab geographer Masudi had written in 915 that the Lar language was spoken in the coast towns as far as Chaul; “but it would seem that most of the present families are late settlers, who about the middle of the eighteenth century fled from Cambay to escape the tyranny of Momin Khan II”. [n35; Gaz XIII-I pp. 112–113] Another page dates the flight to 1760–1766 and says that “several Gujarat Brahmans” came with them to Supara, Bassein and other coast towns. [n35; Gaz XIII-I p. 63] By 1882 they were shopkeepers, moneylenders, merchants and superior landholders. They spoke Gujarati among themselves and Marathi with others. They were Vaishnavas of the Vallabhacharya sect who also kept the Shaiva fasts and feasts, which the Gazetteer put down to their “close connection with Maratha Brahmans”. They had stopped giving their daughters to the Thana Lad Vanis and took brides from Cambay, Jambusar and Bombay. [n35; Gaz XIII-I pp. 112–113] Thirty-five families at Sopara, in a town of 1,700, were a substantial merchant presence: the “crowded weekly market” of 1882 was, in part, theirs.

They are the reason the island’s money moved. The Gazetteer lists four Bassein villages where bills of exchange, hundis, could be cashed for sums up to £1,000: Nala, Agashi, Sopara and Navghar. [n40; Gaz XIII-I trade chapter, OCR p. 304] It lists ten trade centres in the sub-division: Bassein, Nala, Sopara, Agashi, Kaman, Bolinj, Manikpur, Virar, Bhatane and Kharbhav, though the page reference is uncertain. [n40; Gaz XIII-I, OCR p. 300]

The Marwari Vanis were newer. They had come to Thana from Rajputana “almost entirely since the British conquest”, by way of Gujarat and Bombay, “and since the railway has been opened, they have come in great numbers”. [n35; Gaz XIII-I pp. 64, 113–114] Their first wave had followed the general reduction of rents between 1835 and 1838: “The reductions left a large margin of profit to the landholder and the Marvadis came, advanced money at from 100 to 200 per cent to the husbandmen, and sold them up.” In 1846 the Collector, Mr Law, noticed that the Marwari “had begun to settle even in the remotest villages”. The Gazetteer’s own words for them, “thrifty avaricious”, and its complaint that they “carried their gains to their own country”, are the judgement of a colonial officer, and should be read as his. [n35; Gaz XIII-I pp. 113–114] They were Jains, and they kept weekly fasts in the month of Bhadrapad. Lohanas and Bhatias from Cutch and north Gujarat were grain and cloth merchants in most of the district’s towns. [n35; Gaz XIII-I p. 114]

The Gazetteer’s list of “recent settlers” in the district reads, a century and a half later, like a prophecy: Pardeshis from Upper India working as messengers and servants, Kachis as market gardeners, Sindhi sweet-makers, Gujarat Brahmans and traders, Marwar Vanis, Lingayat Vanis. It also noted that in the far north of the district Gujarati was superseding Marathi. [n35; Gaz XIII-I pp. 64–65] The Muslim traders, too, had newcomers among them: Bohora and Memon merchants “from Gujarat through Bombay”, beside the old Konkani Muslims who were long “the largest and most prosperous class of Thana Musalmans”. [n35; Gaz XIII-I pp. 64, 216–224] And there were Parsis everywhere, “Gujarat Parsi liquor-contractors and Government servants, found all over the district” — the fourteen Parsis of Bassein town among them. [n35; Gaz XIII-I p. 65; n21]

The Christians of 1882: “Native”, “Portuguese”, “Firgi”

The Gazetteer’s chapter on the Christians of Thana is the fullest description of the island’s Catholic communities ever written before the twentieth century, and it was compiled in exactly these years. It does not use the word “Kupari”. A search of the full text of the Gazetteer, of Enthoven’s caste volumes, of Grierson’s linguistic survey, of the census reports of 1901, 1911 and 1931, and of da Cunha’s books found the word nowhere. [n34; Headline finding] In the official English of 1882 the Christians of Bassein were “Native Christians” or “Portuguese Christians”. Among themselves, the upper families called themselves Firgi, from Firangi, the Frank, while “the lower classes call themselves by their caste name”. Their Hindu neighbours called them “Firangis and more often Kiristans”. Native priests were called Kanarin, a word “considered a term of reproach”. [n34; Gaz XIII-I pp. 199–200, 203 n. 1] The name the villages of Nirmal and Agashi use for themselves today had not yet reached print, and may not have existed in its present sense.

Who were they? The Gazetteer’s answer has several layers. Of the Portuguese rule, it says, “there remains in Salsette, Bassein and Mahim the important class of Christians, chiefly converted Brahmans, Prabhus, Pachkalshis and Kolis”. [n34; n41; Gaz XIII-I p. 63] Its list of constituent castes runs “Brahmans, Prabhus, Pachkalshis, Charkalshis, Sonars, Khatris, Bhandaris, Kharpatils, Kunbis, Kumbhars, Nhavis, Dhobis, Kolis, Bhois, Mhars, Chambhars”, and adds that “the bulk of them are Bhandaris, Kolis and Kunbis”, and that “in matters of marriage the lower classes keep to their old caste distinctions”. [n34; n41; Gaz XIII-I p. 200] And for Bassein in particular it is specific. At Sopara “the husbandmen … are Christians, many of them converted Samvedi Brahmans”. [n34; Gaz XIV p. 315] At Agashi, Vatar and Koprad the plantain-drying Christians were “originally Samvedis and Pachkalshis”. [n34; Gaz XIII-I p. 395] These two sentences are the best period evidence that the Christian farmers of the northern garden villages, the people now called Kuparis, descended at least in part from Samvedi Brahman farmers, and that Pachkalshi families had converted beside them. They describe part of the community, not all of it, and da Cunha, as noted, denied it outright. [n34; Inferences]

The Gazetteer also recorded how the Christians themselves remembered their conversion, and how far it believed them. “According to the story of the Uraps and other classes their forefathers were forced to become Christians by having pieces of flesh thrown into their wells.” It cited a missionary journal of 1859 for “the Portuguese thrusting biscuit and pork into the mouths of unwilling multitudes”. Then it gave its own view: “Force may have been used in some cases, but the Portuguese accounts seem to show that persuasion and interest were the chief means of conversion.” [n34; Gaz XIII-I p. 201 n. 2] It knew the reverse movement too. The Urap Agris of Bassein villages such as Navapur, Umela, Juchandra, Achole, Virar and Kolwad had gone back to Hinduism in 1820 and 1828, and still carried the surnames Gomes, Soz, Fernan, Frutad and Minez. [n40; Gaz XIII-I pp. 116–117; n34; Enthoven I] And it repeated, without endorsing it, a curious report: “one recent report mentions a Christian village in Bassein that claims to be older than the Portuguese. No confirmation of this statement has been received.” [n34; Gaz XIII-I p. 200 n. 7]

The Gazetteer’s account of their daily life is detailed enough to walk through a Christian village of 1882. The houses were “substantial tile-roofed houses with walls of wooden planks, mud, or brick and stone”. [n34; Gaz XIII-I p. 204] The family ate together, men, women and children, two meals a day: rice, nagli and vari, pulse, vegetables, fish, and on occasion mutton, beef, pork or fowl, with palm spirit, phul, of three to five per cent alcohol, and bevda, double-distilled. [n34; Gaz XIII-I p. 204] “Pigs are found in most Christian villages.” [n34; Gaz XIII-I p. 55]

The men’s dress marked their work. The well-to-do wore European clothes, generally black. The poorer wore tight knee-length trousers of coloured cotton, an inner jersey and a cotton jacket; “cart-drivers and husbandmen wear a long cylindrical white cotton hat or a woollen night-cap with or without a checked kerchief tied round the temples, fishermen wear red broadcloth caps”. [n34; Gaz XIII-I pp. 204–205] The women dressed “almost all … in local Hindu fashion”, in a robe tight and above the knee among the poor and full to the ankle among the better-off, usually dark purple, green or black, the married women drawing the end over the right shoulder. For church they covered themselves in a white sheet-like robe falling from the head “in free outward curves”. They wore gold cockle-shell earrings, silver double-loop necklaces, glass bangles and silver anklets, vales; a widow gave up her bangles, her mugdya, her poth necklace and her earrings. [n34; Gaz XIII-I p. 205] The same chapter’s account of the Hindu Samvedis lists the same ornament words, mugdya and gathe, and says that the Samvedi men wore “red broadcloth caps like Telegu Brahmans”. [n34; Gaz XIII-I p. 82] The red cap that later writers would call a mark imposed on converts was, in 1882, worn by Hindu Samvedi men and Christian fishermen alike. [n34; Inferences]

The rites of life ran in a Christian frame with Hindu threads through it. After a first confinement the young mother went to her parents’ house; on the third or sixth night the lower orders guarded the child against the spirit Sathi by strewing gram at the door. The child was baptised between the eighth and fifteenth day, carried to church by the godfather and godmother, padrinho and madrinha; “the mother never goes to the christening”; the poorer families burned incense at the door before the child came back in and feasted on country liquor, dates, gram and molasses. [n34; Gaz XIII-I pp. 210–211] The Hindu Samvedis, by the same Gazetteer, worshipped Sati on the fifth day after a birth. [n34; Gaz XIII-I p. 82] It is the same spirit in both houses.

Marriages were arranged, and here Bassein differed from Salsette: “In Salsette the parents of the girl propose; in Bassein the proposal comes from the boy’s side.” [n34; Gaz XIII-I p. 211 n. 1] (Among the Hindu Vadvals, Enthoven would later record, the groom’s parents also made the first approach, the reverse of other Panchkalshi divisions. [n41; Enthoven III, p. 163]) The bride’s family settled from fifty to five hundred rupees on her, sometimes far more, in ornaments. Booths were built two to five days before; the couple took instruction in doctrine for two or three months; they confessed and took communion a day or two ahead. The wedding was at eight to ten in the morning, except among the Kolis, who married in the afternoon to native music. The groom walked under a long silk umbrella, and among the lower classes he wore “the full European dress of bygone days, a scarlet or black military coat with cocked hat, epaulets, knee-breeches”, hired for five to ten rupees; the Times of India of 9 March 1881 described a groom with a sword “preserved … as an heirloom”. The bride rode in a duli, a palanquin; the groom gave her a gold necklace that she wore for as long as he lived; and the feast had two pork dishes cooked with vinegar and spices, eaten by the poor on mats from leaf plates. [n34; Gaz XIII-I pp. 211–214]

At death the priest was called and the church bell tolled; burial was usually within a day; the poor were dressed in a calico habit “like a monk’s robe, in shape like a Franciscan’s and in colour like a Carmelite’s”. The village brotherhoods came with candles; there were prayers and a meal on the seventh day, and services at the month and the year. A temporary grave cost two to fifteen rupees, a permanent one twenty-five to two hundred. A widow put off her ornaments and, “unless she is very young, never wears them again”. [n34; Gaz XIII-I pp. 214–215]

They went to church, the Gazetteer says, regularly. Each church paid one or two music masters who played the violin, though the Christians “seem to have lost their old fondness for singing”. First fruits of the rice harvest were blessed at the church, the custom that survives as Aagera. Passion plays, introduced by the Jesuits in the middle of the sixteenth century, were still performed. And in the outlying villages of Salsette and Bassein, it was “said occasionally”, secret offerings were still made to Ganpati and Gauri and vows to Shitladevi, against the priests’ disapproval. [n34; Gaz XIII-I pp. 207–210] The Christa Purana, the Marathi life of Christ, was “still well known and much read”. [n34; Gaz XIII-I p. 207 n. 1]

Their speech was Marathi. “Their home tongue is Marathi, very little different from the Marathi spoken by the Kunbis and Kolis of the district. A few know and a considerable number understand an ungrammatical Portuguese, and among some of the higher families … Portuguese is the home speech. Latin is the language of the Church.” Sermons were in Portuguese or Marathi. [n34; Gaz XIII-I pp. 204, 207] A modern historian adds that in the 1880s Portuguese was a home language only for well-to-do families in Bandra, Thane, Bassein and Kurla, and that most of the uneducated Christians of Salsette and Bassein spoke Marathi or an Indo-Portuguese creole. [n34; Mendiratta 2017, p. 241]

And their work was changing. “Wealthy families who are village headmen and owners of rich garden lands often bring up one or more of their sons for the Church, and a considerable number who know English are employed in Bombay chiefly as clerks. The morning trains from Andheri and Bandra are crowded.” The lower classes were husbandmen, “some of them as the Vadvals among the most skilful cultivators in the district”. And the Christians “pride themselves on never taking household service with Europeans”. [n34; n41; Gaz XIII-I pp. 205–206] The clerks on the morning trains came from Salsette, nearer the city; but the habit of sending sons to Bombay, and to the seminary, was already set.

The Vadvals and the Pachkalshis

The Vadvals enter the record of these years in two places at once, as Christian carters and as a Hindu caste.

The carters are in the Gazetteer’s account of the forest trade. “The traders, Christian cartmen chiefly of Chulna, Manikpur, Gokhivra, Sandor, and Barhanpur in Bassein, are known as Vadvals or gardeners.” They bought fallen timber from the government forests in Vada and cut green wood in Jawhar, and travelled in gangs of five to thirty carts, by night. “The Vadvals are the best axemen in the district, and their carts are larger and their cattle stronger than those found in other sub-divisions.” A cart with its man, a boy and the cattle earned at least twelve annas a day. [n41; n40; Gaz XIII-I pp. 97–98] Those five villages are the southern Christian parishes around the fort, Sandor and Manikpur with their old Portuguese churches, Chulne, Gokhivare beyond the railway. Here is the island’s other Christian people, not the gardeners of the Sopara agar but the cart-owning Vadvals of the south, whose loaded carts creaking north to the Vada forests explain the island’s three thousand carts.

The Hindu caste is in the Gazetteer’s entry for the Pachkalshis. They were “known as Sutars, Malis, Vadvals, Chaukalshis, Somvanshi Kshatris, and Pathares, all of whom except the Chaukalshis eat together and intermarry”. The Chaukalshis wore the sacred thread only at marriage, did not shave a widow’s head and allowed widows to remarry; some Pachkalshis always wore the thread and forbade widow marriage. [n41; Gaz XIII-I pp. 129–130] The name, the Gazetteer said, came “from their using in their marriage ceremonies five earthen pots, kalas”. [n41; Gaz XIII-I p. 129] Their surnames were “Raut, Vartak, and Chodhri”. They earned their living “as husbandmen, gardeners and carpenters, and a few as writers and day labourers”, and their wives helped by selling vegetables, butter and milk. Near Bombay the men dressed like Brahmans, in Bombay like Prabhus, and in the outlying villages “they call themselves Marathas”, in a loincloth, a coarse blanket and a cap; on festive days they wore a silk-bordered waistcloth, a turban and Gujarati shoes, and their women the full Maratha robe and short-sleeved bodice. Their houses were of one or two storeys, of mud or brick, thatched or tiled; they ate rice, rice bread, pulse, vegetables and fish, and at holiday dinners mutton, chicken and liquor. They kept images of Cheda and other spirits in their houses beside brass and stone images of Ganpati, Shiva and Krishna. And they spoke “incorrect Marathi using l for ḷ and n for ṇ”. [n41; Gaz XIII-I p. 130] A proverb in the footnote praised them: Panchkalshi ani kon mhanel alshi, “who can call a Panchkalshi idle?” [n41; Gaz XIII-I p. 130 fn]

Where had they come from? The community’s own tradition, recorded at length by Enthoven forty years later, brought them with the prince Bimb from Paithan or from Anhilvada at the end of the thirteenth century. The Gazetteer of 1882 put the Paithan story on trial and found it “doubtful”: no record showed Paithan sacked before 1318; the settlements of the Prabhus and Pachkalshis first appeared on the coast; their home speech used Gujarati words; their Palshe priests followed the Gujarat Yajurveda. It preferred a Gujarat origin, and noted that “the Thana coast [was] a favourite resort for refugees and settlers from Kathiawar”. [n41; Gaz XIII-I pp. 61–62] It grouped the Pachkalshis, Chavkalshis, Malis and Somvanshi Kshatris as cultivators “probably of part Gujarat descent”. [n41; Gaz XIII-I p. 124] The question was not academic to them. The caste’s status was being argued, and the Vadvals’ place in it most of all: documents produced to the Ethnographic Survey in the next century included letters from the Shankaracharya of 1836 and 1852 and a resolution of the Palshikar Brahmans of Bassein taluka of 1855, all supporting the Vadvals’ right to call themselves Panchkalshis. [n41; Enthoven III, pp. 161–162] And it records a memory of 1739: “such assistance did they render at the siege of Bassein”, a footnote says (misprinting the year as 1743), that the Peshwa made one family chief patils of Salsette and two others proprietors of villages in Bhiwandi. [n41; Gaz XIII-I p. 130 fn]

In Bombay, in the same decade, the caste was organising. In 1884 the community founded the Kshattriya Union Club “for the promotion of education and the charitable relief of the aged, widowed and orphaned”, and about 1889 reforms in marriage ceremonies were enforced among its members. [n41; Bombay City Gazetteer I, pp. 238–239] These were Bombay events, but they are the first steps of the associations, the Somvanshi Kshatriya Hitechhu Mandali and its successors, that would speak for the Vadvals of Bassein in the twentieth century.

So the Vadvals of 1882 stood on both sides of the religious line, as the Samvedis did. Hindu Pachkalshi families dried plantains beside Christian ones at Agashi; Christian Vadvals drove timber carts out of Sandor and Chulne; Hindu Raut and Vartak families farmed and carpentered. The line that divided Christian from Hindu ran through families that shared caste names, customs, crops and speech. [n41; Inferences]

Village by village, 1882

Read together, the Gazetteer, da Cunha and the parish histories allow a tour of the Kupari and Vadval villages as they stood in the early 1880s. It begins in the north, in the garden the Gazetteer calls “the garden or agar of Sopara”: “Bolinj, Koprad and Umbrala in the north, Nirmal and Vagholi in the west, Gass in the south, and Sopara and Mardesh in the centre”, the middle of a strip of garden land three miles broad that ran from the Bassein creek to the Vaitarna. [n42; n40; Gaz XIV p. 314]

Nirmal was the religious centre of the whole belt. On its wooded hill stood the eight temples built about 1750 by the Maratha Sarsubhedar Shankarji Keshav, the Shankaracharya’s samadhi with its government grant of Rs 421 a year, the Mahavishnu, Vimaleshvar, Bhuteshvar and other shrines, a Brahman annachhatra and a Bairagi alms-house; “from the Shankaracharya temple is a fine view across the great Nirmal lake to the sea”. [n40; Gaz XIV pp. 292–293] Below it stood the Holy Cross church, rebuilt by its parishioners in 1856 for about Rs 24,000 after more than a century in which, by the inscription on its bell-tower, “the present Cemetery was pro tempore their Church”. Its congregation of 2,184 was the second largest on the island. [n40; Gaz XIV p. 293; EI Nirmal] The Gazetteer recorded two explanations of the name: the Nirmal Mahatmya’s story of the demon Vimal overcome by Parashuram, “from Vimal it came to be called Nirmal or stainless”, and a Collector’s suggestion that it was a translation of Dravidian words for “water” and “hill”, “a true description of the place which is a wooded mound rising from bare flats once under water”. [n40; Gaz XIV p. 292 n. 2] A dressed stone in front of a house about five hundred yards north of the chief temple carried letters that seemed part of a Sanskrit inscription of the seventh century. [n40; Gaz XIV p. 293]

Agashi was the largest place on the island outside Bassein town: 6,823 people, 5,168 of them Hindus, 1,500 Christians and 155 Muslims. [n40; Gaz XIV p. 2] Its Christian church, “built after the destruction of Nossa Senhora da Luz”, was seventy-five feet long, its vicar paid fourteen and a half rupees a month by the British government. Its Hindu quarter held the Bhavani Shankar temple, which the Gazetteer dates to 1691 and to Shankarji Keshav Phadke (a date the modern heritage survey doubts, since Shankarji Keshav belongs to the mid-eighteenth century), with a bathing place thought to cure skin diseases, a Hanuman temple, Vishnu and Ganpati temples, and a Jain temple of Parasnath built “about sixty years ago by Motilal a Vani of Bombay”. [n40; Gaz XIV p. 2; CRIT Vol III] A blog adds a Ram Mandir built by the Garde family in 1885. [n40; Heritage Chronicles, T] It was the one village, as noted, where Hindu Samvedi, Hindu Pachkalshi and Christian families are counted side by side at the same work.

Koprad, half a mile south-west of Bolinj, had a Samvedi Brahman headman, Bhiku Patil, near whose house lay an inscribed stone of the fifteenth century; its Hindu Samvedis, Hindu Pachkalshis and Christians all dried plantains; and the Holy Spirit church of the Christian parish stood in its lands on the road to Nandakhal. [n40; Gaz XIV p. 342; Gaz XIII-I p. 395] Da Cunha called that church, Espirito Santo, “the oldest among the rural churches, and the only one standing in its primitive state”. [n40; da Cunha 1876, p. 161] The parish’s own tradition held that Maratha soldiers who stormed it in 1739 had been driven off by a swarm of bees. [n40; EI Nandkhal, C/T]

Vatar carried the oldest datable name in the belt. A stone found at Agashi, dated 1150 in the reign of the Shilahara king Haripaldev, granted the village of Vattaraka in Shurparaka, and named as witnesses its headmen, “Risi Mhatara … Naguji Mhatara, Ananta Nayaka, and Changdev Mhatara”; “Vattaraka”, the Gazetteer explained, “is the modern Vatargaon, two miles north-west of Sopara”. [n40; Gaz XIV p. 385 and n. 1] In 1882 it was a plantain village with Samvedi, Pachkalshi and Christian families.

Bolinj, at the north end of the dying creek, “was formerly a large town and probably had rich temples”; carved stones were said to lie in its Devhara pond; and a stone inscribed in Devanagari served as the doorstep of “Juju Manu Lop, that is Joseph Manuel Lopez”, a Christian. The metalled Virar–Agashi road took wheeled traffic within a mile of it, but “between Bolinj and Sopara the road is a narrow low lane passable for wheels only in fair weather”, and “for many years boats have been unable to pass beyond Bolinj”. [n40; Gaz XIV pp. 316, 338, 341–342]

Vagholi, the western landing, was where the small boats came “from the west as near as Vagholi about two miles” from Sopara; an inscribed stone from a well there, fourteen illegible lines, had been taken to the Collector’s garden at Thana. [n40; Gaz XIV pp. 315–316, 342] Umrale and Mardes appear only as plantain villages and members of the agar. Rajodi and Nala were plantain villages too; Nala was also a trade centre where hundis were cashed, and a stone “brought from Nila about a mile north of Sopara” named the Yadava king Ramchandradev and the city of Shurparak twice. [n40; Gaz XIII-I p. 292; Gaz XIV p. 387] Bhuigaon, south-west of Nirmal, does not appear in the Gazetteer at all; its first printed mention is da Cunha’s of 1876, describing the creek system, “divided into several villages, at one of which, by name Bhoegaum, the sea enters, runs through that of Vagholi, and debouches at that of Vatari”. [n40; da Cunha 1876, p. 159]

Sopara itself was “still a rich country town with a crowded weekly market and a population of about 1700”. Its husbandmen were Christians, many of them converted Samvedi Brahmans; its Muslims were many of them Naitas; and its Hindus were chiefly Samvedi Brahmans and Bhandaris, with the thirty-five Lad Vani families. Molasses, rice, plantains and betel leaf were its trade. [n40; Gaz XIV pp. 314–316]

Gass, finally, the southern landing of the agar, needs its own pages, and they follow.

South of the creek mouth lay the Vadval country. Sandor, three miles north of Bassein, had its St Thomas church with sixteenth-century side walls, a chapel added in 1838 and a frontispiece of 1858, and a congregation of 1,725; the parish would enlarge the church in 1889. [n40; Gaz XIV p. 37; EI Sandor] Papdi had been, by da Cunha’s account in 1876, “chiefly inhabited by Brahmans of Chitpawan, Karada, and Deshastha divisions, as well as Palshas, Sonars, and other inferior castes of the Hindus”, a settlement of the Maratha officers’ families; but its church of Our Lady of Grace, rebuilt in 1865 for Rs 18,000 and 102 feet long, served 1,294 Christians, and had a parish school with a violin master. [n40; da Cunha 1876, p. 157; Gaz XIV p. 37] The parish’s own history remembers its founding congregation as “poor farmers” who “mainly survived on manual labour”. [n40; EI Papdy] Remedy, the village the Gazetteer calls Dhauli, had the biggest congregation of all, 3,238, in a church 108 feet long built in 1821 for Rs 18,600, Rs 18,000 of it raised by subscription. [n40; Gaz XIV p. 37] Merces, in the village of Kaular, had a congregation of 974, mostly Koli fisher-folk, and the oddity of a vicar paid by the Portuguese government; its first secular vicar had been appointed in 1862. [n40; Gaz XIV p. 37; EI Merces] Pali, three miles east of Bassein, had rebuilt its Mother of God church in 1840 for Rs 15,000 and kept a school of thirty pupils for a congregation of 900; beside it stood a sixteenth-century dargah and an eighteenth-century mosque. [n40; Gaz XIV p. 37; CRIT Vol I items 107–109] Manikpur, with St Michael’s church rebuilt in 1851 for its 800 parishioners, had already lent its name to the railway station and the creek; the parish’s own history says the village’s older name, Porim or Purim, was changed to Manikpur at the start of the twentieth century, but the Gazetteer’s usage shows the new name was current by 1882. [n40; Gaz XIV pp. 37, 314; EI Manikpur] Chulne lay two miles west of the station on the Gazetteer’s walking route back from Sopara; Gokhivare had a five-mile railway feeder running to Papdi. [n40; Gaz XIV p. 341; Gaz XIII-I p. 319] And Giriz, between Bassein and Nirmal, had no church of its own and was part of the parish of Sandor and Merces; its villagers would build one only in 1917. [n40; CRIT Vol III asset 90; EI Giriz]

Virar, at the northern edge of the island, was “a rich village well-wooded and with many patches of sugarcane”, with its Saturday market, its ruined Portuguese church and well fifty yards south of the station, “a notable Portuguese tower or fortified mansion” a mile south, and the hill of Jivdhan to the east. [n40; Gaz XIV p. 382] The Virar ward later called Vartakwada carries the name of a Pachkalshi family, but no source of these years locates them there. [n40; CRIT Vol I; Inferences]

Gass in 1882

The Gazetteer reached Gass on foot. Its suggested one-day circuit for a visitor began at Manikpur station, went north-west about four miles to Brahma hill, then to Nirmal, to the “Burud-king’s tower” (the stupa mound Indraji was opening), half a mile east to Sopara, “about a mile south to Gas”, and finally “about three miles south-east to Manikpur”, some twelve miles in all. [n42; Gaz XIV, Sopara “Objects of Interest”] On the way the walker crossed “a bare flat, formerly flooded during the rains and now being gradually reclaimed for rice”, with “the raised lands of Gas and Sopara, full of trees and sugarcane gardens” to the north and the long wooded hill of Nirmal to the west. [n42; Gaz XIV]

Gass stood on that raised land at the southern edge of the agar, four or five feet above the salt waste, beside the last working landing on the creek. “Small boats of from eight to ten tons … come at high tide from the south as near as Gas landing-place about a mile from Sopara.” From the landings at Gass, Bolinj and Vagholi, molasses, rice and betel leaf went by sea to Surat, Broach, Jambusar, Dholera, Bhavnagar and Gogha. [n42; Gaz XIV pp. 315–316] South of the village, toward Achole, lines of large basalt blocks ran across the low muddy ground, the remains of an old raised footway that the people called the dagdi bandh, the stone dam; similar footways crossed the low land between Gass and Brahma hill. [n42; Gaz XIV pp. 341–342] Brahma hill had long been a quarry: its basalt blocks lay “as a footway along some of the lowest parts of the road between Gas and Nirmal” and were “built into the plinths of Gas and Sopara houses”. Under a banyan between Gass and the hill stood a slab carved with a cobra god, a nagoba. [n42; Gaz XIV, Brahma hill passage]

The village had its water. Gas lake was “a winding hollow, 120 yards broad and 800 long with two cross dams”, fringed with gardens and Persian wheels. On its east shore lay a richly carved stone lotus-pendant; at its south end, dressed stone steps; at a spot on its shore called Mala, foundations of “probably a Brahmanic temple of the eleventh or twelfth century”. [n42; Gaz XIV p. 341] Just inside the Gass boundary on the Sopara side lay the Khere or Shirmoli pond, fifty yards south of which a statue of Brahma had been found that now stood near the Chakreshvar temple. [n42; Gaz XIV, Sopara section] In a field called Sonarbhat stood a mound with a broken cross-legged female figure, perhaps Ambika, and the foundations of a temple “said to have been dedicated to Parasnath”; in a garden lay the tomb of the Muslim saint Shaikh Akbar-moi-ud-din, bordered with old bricks sixteen inches long. [n42; Gaz XIV p. 341]

And the Gazetteer names two of its householders, which for a village of 1882 is rare. In front of the house of Degu Pobria Mhatara lay the six-sided basalt block his family used for washing and bathing and “apparently for grinding curry powder”, with its Pali letters, Badhuya, “of Badhu”, “perhaps about B.C. 200”. “About three years ago”, the Gazetteer adds, the stone had been struck by a plough in Kaklai’s field close to Brahma hill; “it is probably a grave-stone”. About two hundred yards south-west, at the house of “a Sámvedi Bráhman, Bab Naik by name”, a smaller block carried Kodasa Kalavadasa, “of Kalavad the Kod”, on one face and Ugudeveya, “of Ugradeva”, on another; “like Degu’s stone, this was brought from near Brahma hill”. “Passing through Gas,” the account goes on, “many of these blocks of basalt are notable in front of verandas and built into plinths.” [n42; Gaz XIV p. 341] Mhatara is the name borne by the headmen of Vatar in the grant of 1150; Naik is both a Samvedi and a Kupari surname. [n40; n42; Inferences]

The village’s own claim was the largest of all. “The village of Gas, which, according to the people of the place, is the site of old Sopara.” [n42; Gaz XIV p. 341] The Gazetteer recorded it and did not endorse it: the stones in its houses had been carried from Brahma hill, and it placed the stupa and the city’s remains around Sopara village. [n42; Inferences] What the sentence does record is a village in 1882 that thought of itself as the heir of the ancient port.

The Gazetteer gives no population for Gass and no list of its castes. A Samvedi Brahman lived there; a family named Mhatara lived there; the husbandmen of the Sopara agar were, it says, Christians, many of them converted Samvedis. [n42; Gaz XIV pp. 314–315, 341] Gass had no church. Its Christians would have walked, presumably, to Holy Cross at Nirmal, the mother parish from which their own parish would be carved sixty years later; no source says so in as many words. [n42; Inferences]

A new name: “East Indian”

The Christians of the northern Konkan had been called many things. Under the Portuguese the converts of the province were Norteiros, after the Court of the North. Under the British, Europeans called them “Portuguese Christians” or “Salsette Christians”, and their Hindu neighbours called them Kinstav. [n04; Wikipedia: Bombay East Indians; n22; Grierson 1905]

In 1886 their church changed hands. The Christians of Bassein had been under the Padroado Archdiocese of Goa since the 1530s. For half a century before 1886 the Catholics of British Bombay and Salsette had been fought over by two jurisdictions: the Padroado, the Portuguese crown’s patronage exercised from Goa, and the vicars apostolic sent by Rome’s Propaganda. In 1838 the brief Multa praeclare took jurisdiction from the Padroado clergy within the vicariates, and in 1839 the Vicar Apostolic of Bombay was given Salsette. Bassein and Chaul were exempted, an anomaly a Goan annalist found “a puzzle”. [n38; Hull 1927, I] Bassein had been Portuguese until 1739 and then Maratha, outside the British quarrel, and its parishes had stayed with Goa. [n38; Inferences]

The quarrel was settled by treaty. The Concordat of 23 June 1886 between the Holy See and Portugal, and the bull Humanae Salutis of 1 September 1886, made Bombay an archdiocese and created a new Diocese of Daman (Damaun), whose territory took in Bassein and the rest of the Portuguese-patronage northern coast outside Bombay island. [n38; Hull I, p. 6; Catholic Encyclopedia, Damão] (Earlier drafts of this book called it an archdiocese; the sources say diocese.) Early in the next century the Catholic Encyclopedia counted 71,000 Catholics in it and 51 churches, twelve churches and a chapel in Bassein district alone, and described Bassein as “memorable for the martyrdom of five religious”. [n38; Catholic Encyclopedia, Damão] Hull’s history gives the Bassein varado 19,527 Catholics by the Goa Annuario of 1901. [n38; Hull I, pp. 5–6] For the parishioners of Nirmal or Sandor nothing visible changed: the same priests, the same churches, the same feasts, under a bishop now in Daman instead of an archbishop in Goa. [n38; Inferences]

The next year they changed their name. On 26 May 1887 the native Christians of the North Konkan formally adopted the name “East Indian” by resolution, and the Bombay East Indian Association was founded. The year was Queen Victoria’s Golden Jubilee, and a souvenir published fifty years later in 1937 called the timing a “happy coincidence”. The purpose, the community’s own history says, was to set the local Catholics apart from the Goan and Mangalorean migrants then pouring into Bombay as “the original Catholic people of the Bombay East Indies”. The new name covered several caste-based groups who kept to their own marriages in the villages: Kuparis or Samvedi Christians, Vadvals, Kolis, Kunbis, and the Salsette and urban Christians. [n28; Catholic Connect; n07; east-indians.com]

It was a decision taken in Bombay, for the whole community of Bombay, Salsette and Bassein, not a Bassein event alone. But the Christian villages of the island, from Papdi and Remedy to Gass and Nirmal, were among the people it named.

A modern historian, Sidh Losa Mendiratta, has reconstructed the meeting from the recollection of one who was there, John de Mello, printed in the community’s 1937 souvenir. About thirty “leaders of the community” met on 26 May 1887 “to change the designation ‘Bombay Portuguese’ to ‘Bombay East-Indian’”. At the same meeting they founded the Bombay East Indian Association and a weekly paper, the Bombay East Indian. The name claimed a century and a half as subjects of the East India Company and the standing of “sons of the soil”, and drew a line between the local Catholics and the Goans and Portuguese. The Goans answered with a Bombay Portuguese Association and a paper of their own, the Portuguez Brittanico. [n34; Mendiratta 2017, pp. 234–236] Da Cunha’s sneers of 1876 belong to the same rivalry.

The name meant something different in the villages. The Catholics of Bassein and Salsette, Mendiratta writes, came to be labelled “rural” or “samvedi” East Indians, and while the urban East Indians drifted toward the Archdiocese of Bombay, those of Salsette and Bassein “remained by and large under the jurisdiction of the Padroado”. [n34; Mendiratta 2017, pp. 218, 236] The five-group list now used for the community, Kulbis, Samvedi Christians “commonly called Kuparis”, Koli Christians, Vadvals and Salsette Christians, comes from Elsie Baptista’s book of 1967, which the research behind this book could not read. [n34; Catholic Connect] In 1887 the word “Kupari” was not in print, and the farmers of Nirmal and Agashi would have answered to “Christian”, to their village and to their caste name. [n34; Inferences] No Bassein voice from the meeting, and no account of how the villages received the new name, has been found. [n34; Gaps] The bishops of Daman would try, from 1887 to 1925, to run Portuguese schools for these people; they “don’t appear to have been long-lasting”. [n34; Mendiratta 2017, p. 241]

In 1886 Yule and Burnell’s Hobson-Jobson gave Sopara its own entry. Against a Sanskrit scholar who had called the place “mythical”, they wrote that Supara “was real enough”, “for many centuries the chief city of the Konkan”. By then Indraji’s coffer had made the point beyond argument. [n15; Hobson-Jobson]

The Gazetteer’s text says the fort of Bassein had been “almost deserted” since “1888”, which must be a slip for 1838, the year of Mrs Postans’s visit. Nothing else is recorded for the taluka in the last years of the decade. [n28; Gazetteer XIV]

Firewood for the sugar boilers, 1851 and 1887

The sugar mills of Bassein ran on firewood from the forest, and the law knew it. In 1851 Government sanctioned a proposal by Dr Gibson that the exemption of “the inhabitants of Bassein” from fees for firewood “required by them for sugar-boiling” should continue, on condition that they obtained a permit from the kamavisdar, the local revenue officer, at the start of each season for as many cart-loads as they and he judged necessary. [n69 §2] Raw sugar in the Bassein sub-division, the Dictionary of the Economic Products of India repeated from the Gazetteer, was made by “Pachkalshis, Malis, Native Christians and Samvedi Brahmans” between February and June. [n69 §1] When the Forest Commission sat in 1887, the people of Thana district told it that forest land served the sugar factories of Bassein taluka and “the plantain gardening” of the coast talukas, and that rab, woodash manure, was “the very life of Konkan agriculture”. In “garden talukas such as Bassein, Mahim, Dahanu”, they said, bullocks were worked to draw water from wells with Persian wheels, so the loss of grass hit the cattle that the gardens depended on. [n69 §2] Bassein was one of five talukas whose forest settlement was already complete; the settlement officers found in them only rights of way, of access to water, and of approach to shrines, and in Bassein they recognised the ownership of those who held the survey numbers. [n69 §2]

1890s: A bridge strengthened, and the plague

After 1882 the record of Bassein taluka goes quiet. The great Gazetteer had been published, and the next district gazetteers and census reports of 1891 and 1901 were not found in readable form for this book. No population figure for the taluka in the 1890s survives in the notes; the census of 1911 implies about 80,251 for 1901, a little more than the 68,967 of 1881. [n21; Census 1911, Provincial Table I] For most of the decade, what we know comes from the railway, the parish books and, at its end, from the government’s own record of a rising.

The thirty-year revenue settlement expired in 1891–92, and a revision would have followed; what it did to the gardens is not recorded in any source found. [n21; Gaps] In the parishes the decade left a few marks. At Sandor the St Thomas church was enlarged in 1889. [n40; EI Sandor] Nandakhal’s parish history records nothing for these years; its next dates are 1907 and 1908. [n40; Wikipedia: Holy Spirit Church Nandakhal] At Agashi, Fr Jovito V. Dias would extend the St James church in 1900. [n40; EI Agashi]

The old bridge over the Bassein creek, opened in 1864, was strengthened between 1896 and 1902, with extra piles and new girders. Speed limits followed: 15 miles an hour for local trains and 8 for through trains. Over the water between Panju island and the mainland, the trains slowed to a walking pace. [n05; n28; Wikipedia: Vasai Creek Railway Bridge]

Then, in 1896, bubonic plague reached Bombay, and from Bombay it spread along the railways. The records that survive for Bassein are thin and must be treated with care. A search summary of the Government of Bombay’s plague reports gives Bassein town about 11,291 people in 1897, with 497 cases of plague and 377 deaths between 1 February and 1 June of that year. The same summary ranks Thana as the worst district of the whole Presidency by mortality, at 550 deaths per 100,000, and gives Bhiwandi, across the creeks, 546 cases and 452 deaths. Plague came back violently to Bhiwandi in July to September 1898. [n28; Government of Bombay plague reports, NLS, search summary]

These Bassein figures come from a search summary of scanned reports, not from a page that was read. They are given here as provisional and should be checked before anyone repeats them. No figures were found for Bassein in 1896, 1899 or 1900. [n28, gaps]

If the 1897 numbers are right, about one person in thirty in Bassein town died of plague in four months. That would make it the worst single disaster in the town’s recorded nineteenth century, worse than any famine the Gazetteer names. But the notes cannot confirm it, and the people who lived through it left no account that has been found.

The census of 1901 offers one more clue, from the other side. Its report on the Bombay Presidency has a section on “Famine and Plague” and a statement of registered plague deaths for 1896 to 1901, but the Thana and Bassein figures could not be read from the scan. What it does say is that the population of Thana district fell only about one per cent short of the estimate when the count was made, the smallest shortfall of any district. [n21; Census 1901 Vol IX Pt I] That sits uneasily with the plague summary’s claim that Thana was the worst district by mortality. Both may be true of different years, or one may be wrong; the notes cannot settle it. And the Bassein of 1897 had been here before: the Gazetteer’s account of the plague of about 1690, “exactly like a bubo”, which took a third of the Portuguese city, had been printed only fifteen years earlier. [n21; Gaz XIV p. 33]

1897–98: the forest people come to Bassein

The plague years were also the years of the district’s most serious unrest since the income-tax riot of 1860, and this time the government’s own account begins at Bassein.

The Commissioner who put the disturbances down, A. C. Logan, wrote a report that the Maharashtra government later reprinted in its source volumes on the freedom movement. The grievance, he wrote, “was confined to a few hundred forest people, who at great damage to the forests supplied the Brahmin and other wealthy inhabitants of Bassein and other coast villages with cheap firewood”. When the forest department banned the trade, the forest people “defied the interdict, overpowered the forest guards and brought their loads to Bassein with riotous demonstrations”. [n43; SM-II pp. 636–637]

From there it spread. The Kolis “rose to the cry of free liquor and free salt”; the Agris turned “turbulent”; at Mahim a crowd attacked the treasury and was fired on, and “a few” were killed. And, Logan added, the “Native Christians who shared the Koli discontent at the price of liquor emulated them”. [n43; SM-II pp. 636–637] It is the only mention found in any government record of the North Konkan Christians in unrest, and it comes from a hostile official. [n43; Christian participation]

Logan’s letter is a colonial officer’s account, and its framing, a few hundred forest people serving the Brahmans and the rich of the coast, is his. [n43; Inferences] What it records is nonetheless clear. The town’s firewood came down from the forests of the east, carried by the forest peoples, the Varlis, Kathkaris and Thakurs whom the census had counted in thousands on the island’s inland edge; when the forest law shut that trade, they brought their loads into Bassein anyway, in a crowd. The coast’s grievances were the old ones that this chapter has been recording: the tree tax that had driven the Bhandaris from their trade in 1877, the price of liquor under the excise farm of 1879, the salt tax of two rupees a maund on a coast of salt pans. [n21; n43; Inferences] These were not nationalist demands, and nobody at the time called them so. But they were the first time since 1860 that the people of Bassein and its villages defied the state in the street, and they did so in the same years that plague was emptying the town. How large Bassein’s own part was, beyond Logan’s letter, and who if anyone was punished there, is not recorded. [n43; Gaps]

The garden on the line

By 1899 the island had settled into the shape it would keep for almost a hundred years. The old fort was a ruin with fields inside it. The town lay north of the walls, with its council, its market named for a Collector, its dispensary and its schools. Out in the green strip, in Agashi, Vatar, Koprad, Nirmal, Gass, Bolinj and Sopara, Samvedi, Vadval and Kupari gardeners grew plantains, betel leaf and sugarcane beside their wells and ponds. On the coast the Kolis, Vaitis and Mangelas fished, and the Agris made salt on 1,439 acres of pans along the north bank of the creek. In the hills the Varlis kept to their forests, and in 1897 came down from them to defend a trade in firewood. Two stations, Bassein Road and Virar, sent the produce south, and the boats of Bassein, Agashi and Navghar, built on the island by Christian and Maratha carpenters, still carried the rice and molasses north to Surat and the Gulf of Cambay.

Its peoples had names in the record, though not always the names they used. The Christians were “Native” or “Portuguese” Christians to the government and “East Indians” to their own leaders in Bombay; they were Firgi in their upper families, and in their villages farmers whose grandfathers had been Samvedi Brahmans or Pachkalshis, carters who were Vadvals, fishermen who were Kolis. The Hindus were Samvedis and Pachkalshis, Bhandaris and Agris, Brahmans of the Maratha conquest and Lad Vanis from Cambay. The Muslims were Konkanis and Naitas, with Bohoras and Memons arriving. The census-taker counted them all in a sub-division of under seventy thousand. The word “Kupari” was not yet written down by anyone.

The creek that had once carried Sopara’s ships was silting into fields, and Sopara itself had a stupa without a station. Within a generation the railway would electrify its trains, and a Western line magazine would print, for the first time, a name for the halt that the Gazetteer had asked for: “Neela, or Nalasopara”. Before that, though, a government linguist in Calcutta would sit down with the speech the Gazetteer had only glimpsed. George Grierson’s survey, published in 1905, would put the Samvedis at about 2,700 and call their speech “a mechanical mixture of Konkan Marathi and Gujarati”; it would count the Vadvals at 3,500 and note that they “are supposed to have immigrated from Gujarat”; and it would find in the Marathi of the native Christians, whom the Hindus called Kinstav, the Portuguese words padron and madron, godfather and godmother, the padrinho and madrinha of the Gazetteer’s christenings. [n34; n41; Grierson 1905] He would print a parable in the Vadval dialect, and its first line would read: “One man had two sons.”
