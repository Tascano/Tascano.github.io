In this chapter. The peoples of the island and its villages: Kuparis, Samvedis, Bhandaris, Muslims, Jains and the rest, and how each came.

The market place of Sopara

In the early 1880s Sopara was still a town, not a suburb. It stood on the garden island between the Bassein creek and the Vaitarna, three and a half miles from Bassein Road station and three and a half from Virar, “a rich country town with a crowded weekly market and a population of about 1700.” [n40; n31; Gaz XIV pp. 314–315] At its heart was “a broad market place, bordered by large one and two storied houses with rich wood carving and walls which owe much to old dressed stones and large old bricks”. [n54; Gaz XIV pp. 315–316] The old stones and bricks were the remains of the ancient port of Shurparaka, built into the plinths and walls of a nineteenth-century town. The finest house belonged to Ibrahim Seth, “the chief merchant of Sopara”: a two-storeyed building “about fifty years old and with much of the rich wood-carving then in fashion”. [n54; Gaz XIV pp. 338–340]

The officers who compiled the Thana Gazetteer wrote down who lived there, in one sentence. “The husbandmen of Sopara are Christians, many of them converted Samvedi Brahmans; Musalmans, many of them Naitas; and Hindus, chiefly Samvedi Brahmans and Bhandaris.” The traders were Muslims and Gujarat Vanis of the Lad division, who had come from Cambay about 1760. The town lived on molasses, rice, plantains and betel leaf. [n40; n31; Gaz XIV pp. 314–316] Small boats still came up the silting creek at high tide to the landing at Gass, a mile south, and to Vagholi and Bolinj; from those landings molasses, rice and betel went by sea to Surat, Broach, Jambusar, Bhavnagar and Gogha, and plantains and betel south by rail to Bombay. [n54; n42; Gaz XIV pp. 315–316] But the town had no station. “A station at Sopara or a made road to the town from the Agashi high road,” the Gazetteer wrote, “would be a boon to its people.” [n31; Gaz XIV p. 316]

That sentence about the husbandmen names five peoples, Christian, Muslim, Samvedi, Bhandari and Vani, living in one small town. This chapter is about them, and about everyone who came after: the Jains whose medieval tirtha lay here, the Kolis, Agris and forest people of the creeks and the eastern hills, the Buddhists who returned to the stupa in the twentieth century, and the hundreds of thousands of migrants for whom the railway halt that Sopara lacked in 1882 became the gate to a city. For each community it asks where they came from and when, and how sure we can be; which villages they lived in; what work they did; what they spoke; what they worshipped, and why some changed their faith; what customs the record holds; and how they live now. The second part walks through the sacred places and festivals of Sopara, Nala, Gass, Nirmal and the East.

One old town, two modern halves

The place this chapter covers has had three shapes. In the record before 1900 it was Sopara, the old town, with its “garden or agar”: “Bolinj, Koprad and Umbrala in the north, Nirmal and Vagholi in the west, Gas in the south, and Sopara and Mardesh in the centre”. [n42; Gaz XIV p. 314] Nala, a mile north of Sopara, was a separate village; the Gazetteer listed it among the plantain-drying villages, among the four villages of Bassein where bills of exchange could be cashed up to £1,000, and probably among its ten trade centres. [n40; Gaz XIII-1 p. 292, OCR pp. 300, 304] Then a railway halt called “Neela or Nalasopara” appeared on the line, at a date the record does not fix, and the two names joined. [n31] Chapters 22 and N2 tell how Sopara became Nallasopara and how the suburb grew, and Chapter 24 its land, water and livelihood; Chapter 26 tells Gass, the birthplace of this book’s author, from its formation to today; Chapter 20 tells the communities of Vasai, whose parishes and caste histories overlap with these. This chapter points to those chapters rather than repeating them.

Today Nallasopara is two halves. The West holds the old villages, Sopara, Nale, Gass, Nirmal, Umrale and Mardes, with the stupa, the Chakreshwar lake and the parish churches. The East, across the railway, holds the new city: Achole, Tulinj, Santosh Bhuvan, Shirdi Nagar and Pelhar. The Nallasopara town council had 184,664 people in 2001; Marathi Wikipedia gives about 4.6 lakh for 2011, and a student project at TU Delft gives about 564,664, two figures that cannot both be right for the same area. [n54; n31; Wikipedia: Nallasopara; mr.wikipedia] In 2001 the town’s minority languages were Hindi (22.92 per cent), Gujarati (17.82 per cent) and Urdu (12 per cent). [n31; n54] In 2011, across the whole Vasai-Virar corporation, Hindi was the first language of 26.58 per cent, Gujarati of 9.26, Bhojpuri of 3.10 and Urdu of 2.72; Hindus were 77.16 per cent, Muslims 9.03, Christians 8.28, Buddhists 2.65 and Jains 1.90. [n54; Wikipedia: Vasai-Virar] The 2001 town table and the 2011 corporation table cover different areas and cannot be compared directly. [n54]

The record for Nallasopara is uneven in a particular way. The old town of the West is described in detail by the Gazetteer of 1882, because the antiquarian Bhagwanlal Indraji was excavating there that year, with the help of a local man, Ramdas Kasidas Modi of Sopara. [n42; Gaz XIV p. 314 n.] The new city of the East, where most of Nallasopara’s people now live, is almost undescribed: no census table by ward, no study of its neighbourhoods’ origins, no history of its mosques, temples or associations was found. This chapter says plainly where that silence falls.

Nala, the second village

Nala, the first half of the modern name, has a thin history of its own, and it deserves to be told before its people disappear into the suburb’s. In 1882 it was a separate village “about a mile north of Sopara”. From it had come an inscribed stone, later kept in the rest-house at Bassein Road station, that names the Yadava king Ramchandradev, whose reign the Gazetteer read as AD 1280 to 1298 (“Shak 1202 or 1220”), and names the place Shurparak twice. [n40; Gaz XIV p. 387] It is one of the few documents that tie the medieval name of the port to the land round Nala. In the Portuguese register of 1727–30 the estate “Nala Magir” was leased to Miguel Francisco Mira de Vasconcelos. [n40; Tombo fl. 43] In 1882 Nala was one of the nine Kupari-belt villages that dried plantains; one of the four villages of Bassein, with Agashi, Sopara and Navghar, where hundis, bills of exchange, could be cashed up to £1,000; and, by an uncertain reading of the OCR, one of the ten trade centres of the sub-division, with Bassein, Sopara, Agashi, Kaman, Bolinj, Manikpur, Virar, Bhatane and Kharbhav. [n40; Gaz XIII-1 p. 292, OCR pp. 300, 304] Lad Vani families lived there. [n35]

Note 31 of the research for this book found no Nala entry in the Gazetteer and concluded there was no 1882 Nala village; the later reading of the trade chapter and the inscription corrects that. [n40] What remains unknown is where the name came from. A popular account says two villages, Nala east of the railway and Sopara west of it, came to be treated as one region; it gives no dates and no source. [n31; Lokshahi] A railway article of 2022, drawing on the 1923 magazine of the Bombay, Baroda and Central India Railway, lists an early halt as “Neela or (Nalasopara)”; the Gazetteer of 1882 knew of no Sopara station; Wikipedia’s “1865” for the station rests on one railway heritage book and should not be stated as fact. [n31] Today “Nale Gaon” and “Sopara Gaon” survive as locality names of Nallasopara West, and “More Gaon” and “Achole Gaon” in the East. [n31] Chapter 22 tells the name in full.

Part One: The Peoples

I. The Kuparis of Gass, Umrale, Sopara and Nirmal

Who they were in 1882

The Kuparis, the Samvedi Christians, are the subject of a whole Part of this book, Chapters 33 to K7, and of a fuller section in Chapters 20 and V5. In Nallasopara their villages are the southern and central parts of the Sopara garden belt: Gass, Sopara itself, Mardes, Nirmal on the western edge, and Umrale toward the north. The community’s own list of Kupari places includes Nirmal, Gass, Umraala (Umrale), Mardes and Wagholi among its thirteen; and the 1882 list of the villages that dried plantains, “Agáshi, Vagholi, Vatár, Bolinj, Koprád, Nala, Umrále, Rajodi, and Murdes”, independently names Nala, Umrale and Mardes. [n40; Rajan D’Mello blog 2009; Gaz XIII-1 p. 292]

The Gazetteer’s sentence on Sopara is the strongest single piece of period evidence for who the Kuparis were: “The husbandmen of Sopara are Christians, many of them converted Samvedi Brahmans.” [n34; n54; Gaz XIV p. 315] It places the Christian husbandmen in the same sentence as their Hindu Samvedi neighbours, and the same Gazetteer shows the two living side by side. At Gass in 1882 the surveyor found an inscribed stone at the house of “Bab Naik by name”, “a Samvedi Brahman”. [n42; Gaz XIV p. 341] At Bolinj, on the northern edge of the agar, a Devanagari stone served as the doorstep of a Christian, “Juju Manu Lop, that is Joseph Manuel Lopez”. [n40; Gaz XIV pp. 341–342] “Naik” is also a Kupari surname. [n42] The line between the Samvedi Christian and the Samvedi Hindu households of Sopara was one of religion, not of work, land or house: they grew the same sugarcane, plantains and betel, and lived in the same lanes. [n54]

The word Kupari was not used in any primary source from 1876 to 1931. [n34] What the Gazetteer said of the “Native Christians” of the district, and of Bassein in particular, is told in Chapter 20: the proposal of marriage coming from the boy’s side in Bassein, unlike Salsette; the godparents padrinho and madrinha; the spirit Sathi guarded against at birth, as the Hindu Samvedis worshipped Sati; the Lenten passion plays; the violin masters of each church. [n34; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 82, 207–214] No source records Kupari customs, songs or food specifically for Gass, Umrale or Sopara beyond that baseline and the community’s own compilations in Chapter 36. [n54]

Christians at Sopara before the Portuguese

Sopara has a Christian memory older than the Kuparis. About 1321 the Dominican friar Jordanus found Nestorian Christians and a church of St Thomas at Sopara; he baptised many and gave communion to about ninety. [n40; Gaz XIV p. 322] Chapter 06 tells that story. The Gazetteer itself concluded that “no trace seems to be left either of the Nestorian or of the Latin converts”, and a footnote mentions, without confirmation, “a Christian village in Bassein that claims to be older than the Portuguese”. [n34; Gaz XIII-1 p. 200 and n. 7] No source connects the Christians of Jordanus to the Kuparis, and this chapter does not.

Under the Portuguese, Sopara was a fortified post. A report of 1728 described four wooden stockades there, and complained that the “Nil hill” near Sopara had been fortified without an engineer, its bastions too small for a four-pounder gun. [n40; n31; Gaz XIV p. 322 and n.] The same report wanted the creek between Sopara and Bolinj dug “as deep as the height of a man”, or it would silt level with the fields. [n40; Gaz XIV p. 322] The Tombo de Baçaim, the Portuguese register of crown estates of 1727–30, listed four titles in Sopará and two estates that can be matched to Nallasopara’s villages by sound and context: “pacaria Mardes Sirgoly”, leased to Lucas de Almeida de Andrade at 180 pardaus, and “pacaria Nala Magir”, held by Miguel Francisco Mira de Vasconcelos, with an extra garden, horta Matarvary, held by Gonçalo Carneiro. [n40; Tombo fl. 40, 43, 47v] No Tombo entry for Gass has been found under any spelling. [n42]

Gass

Gass is told in full in Chapter 26. What belongs here is its people.

The Gazetteer recorded two named residents of Gass in 1882. One was Bab Naik, the Samvedi Brahman, at whose house lay a basalt block carved on two sides in early letters, “Kodasa Kalavadasa”, “of Kalavad the Kod”, and “Ugudeveya”, “of Ugradeva”. The other was “one Degu Pobria Mhatara”, in front of whose house a smooth six-sided basalt block, used for washing clothes, bathing and grinding curry powder, bore “in large Pali letters, perhaps about B.C. 200, the letters ‘Badhuya’, that is ‘Of Badhu’”. Both stones had been brought from near Brahma hill, where the second had been struck by a plough about three years before. [n42; Gaz XIV p. 341] The Gazetteer does not give Degu’s community. Mhatara is the form of the surname Mhatre found among Samvedi, Vadval and Agri families, and the headmen of nearby Vatar in a grant of AD 1150 were Mhataras; the surname alone settles nothing. [n40; n49] Many other basalt blocks stood “in front of verandas and built into plinths” of Gass houses. [n42] The villagers told the surveyor that Gass was “the site of old Sopara”, a tradition the Gazetteer recorded and did not endorse. [n42; Gaz XIV p. 341]

Which parish served Gass before 1942 is not recorded; Nirmal, the mother of the southern Kupari parishes, is the likely one. [n42] Gass petitioned for its own church in 1941, and in 1942 the parish of St Gonsalo Garcia was formed, with Fr Thomas as first parish priest; the church was expanded or renovated in 1957 and blessed on 8 March 1962 by Cardinal Gracias. [n40; n42; EI Ghass; CatholicsIndia] The patron, St Gonsalo Garcia, was born in Bassein about 1556 and crucified at Nagasaki in 1597; a community column of February 2021 by the Mobai Gaothan Panchayat claimed Gass as his birthplace, a claim without evidence that conflicts with the usual “born in Bassein”. [n42; Gaothan Voice, Feb 2021] The parish keeps a week-long feast in February, and in recent listings said its weekday Mass at 7:15 in the morning in Marathi and at 7 in the evening in English. [n42; Prayers & Petitions]

On 11 August 2023 vandals ransacked the church: the altar was defaced, the statue of Our Lady thrown down and hymn books scattered. A complaint was filed at Manickpur police station, Archbishop Felix Machado condemned the attack, and the lack of CCTV hampered the police, after which the church decided to install cameras. These details come from search summaries, since the original reports could not be opened. [n42; UCA News; AsiaNews]

A Gass boy became a bishop in 2026. Simon Almeida was born at Gass on 4 November 1960, studied at St Gonsalo Garcia Primary School and at Holy Cross High School, Nirmal, entered the minor seminary at Pune in 1978, and was ordained priest for Poona on 1 April 1990. Pope Leo XIV appointed him Bishop of Poona on 1 November 2025, and he was ordained and installed on 17 January 2026 by Cardinal Filipe Neri Ferrão, with the motto “To make all things new in Christ”. His elder brother, Fr Peter Almeida, was parish priest of Gass in 2023. [n42; Wikipedia: Simon Almeida; Diocese of Poona]

The Hindustan Times in 2025 called Gass “a predominantly Christian village often described as ‘Mini Goa’ for the ancestral mansions that dot the surrounding farmland”. [n42] Wikipedia counts about 5,000 Roman Catholics in 2009; reprints of the 2025 report give the village’s population as about 5,500 in one version and about 9,000 in another. [n42] In March 2025 Gass villagers filed more than 2,500 objections to a municipal plan to move a dumping ground and sewage-treatment reservation from Agarwal Nagar in Vasai East to a plot in Gass, and in October 2025 the corporation withdrew its application, though the villagers still wanted a formal commitment. [n42; HT via Magzter; newsminimalist] Wikipedia describes the village as known for salt farming and green-vegetable farming. [n42] How the Kupari families of Gass live and work in November 2026 is not documented beyond that.

Umrale

Umrale, the Gazetteer’s Umbrala, lies in the north of the Sopara agar with Bolinj and Koprad, and was one of the plantain-drying villages of 1882. [n40; Gaz XIV p. 314; XIII-1 p. 292] Its Catholics belonged to the Holy Spirit parish of Nandakhal; Mass was said at Umrale and Nanbhat from 1949 under Mgr Mendes, and Umrale became an independent parish, St Joseph’s, in 1970. A prayer house there is dated by one source to 1935, a date the same source contradicts. [n40; Wikipedia: Holy Spirit Church Nandakhal] Two ponds of the village, the Kudhvat pond on the Umbrale-Kofrad road and the Umbrale pond on the Umbrale-Karmala road, are used for immersing Ganesh images at the Hindu festival, a sign that the Christian village has Hindu neighbours using its water. [n40; CRIT Vol I items 65–66] Chapter 31 tells Umrale’s place in the northern Kupari belt.

Sopara, Mardes and Nala

Sopara’s Christians in 1882 were, by the Gazetteer’s count of husbandmen, the first-named community of the town. [n34] Today the old village’s Christians belong to the parishes round it; no Sopara-specific parish figure was found. [n54]

Mardes, the Gazetteer’s Mardesh and “Murdes”, stood “in the centre” of the agar with Sopara, and dried plantains in 1882. It is the village of the stupa, which Indraji excavated in “Merdes” in 1882. [n40; n02] The CRIT heritage survey of 2008 recorded the Madres pond there, where “local Catholic communities gather around the cross for prayers and ritual processions” at Christmas and at the feast of the Nativity. [n40; CRIT Vol I items 70–71] At Mardes, then, the Kupari wayside-cross devotion is kept a few hundred yards from a Buddhist stupa of the third century BC. A Wikipedia-derived table says Mardes and Wagholi form one parish dedicated to Our Lady of Velankanni, split from Nirmal; the dedication is doubtful. [n40]

Nala was a plantain village and a market of bills in 1882; Lad Vani families lived there. [n40; n35] Its Christians are not separately recorded. [n40]

The garden year

What the Kupari and Samvedi families of the agar did with their land is better recorded than who they were. The Sopara garden tract, the Gazetteer said, was part of a strip about three miles broad running from the Bassein creek to the Vaitarna, “full of trees and sugarcane gardens”, raised four or five feet above the salt waste. Its crops were sugarcane, plantains, betel leaf and rice, under coconut and brab palms, mango, tamarind, banyan, pipal and karanj; oilcake was brought in to manure the cane. [n42; Gaz XIV pp. 314–316] Sugar in Bassein was made “chiefly … by Pachkalshis, Malis, Native Christians and Samvedi Brahmans”, and dried plantains, the speciality of the agar, were made “only in the villages of Agáshi, Vagholi, Vatár, Bolinj, Koprád, Nala, Umrále, Rajodi, and Murdes”, “the dried plantains being the best in the district”. [n41; n40; n34; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 292, 395; XIV p. 2] At the Nirmal fair each November, dried plantains were among the goods sold. [n10]

The produce went two ways. Molasses, rice and betel leaf left by boat from the landings of Gass, Vagholi and Bolinj for Surat, Broach, Jambusar, Dholera, Bhavnagar and Gogha, and by rail from Virar north to Ankleshwar, Nadiad, Viramgam and Patan; plantains and betel went south by rail to Bombay in large quantities. [n54; n42; Gaz XIV pp. 315–316] The garden families of Sopara were thus tied by sea to the old Gujarat ports that the Lad Vanis had come from, and by rail to the growing city to the south. Between Bolinj and Sopara the road was “a narrow low lane passable for wheels only in fair weather”. [n40; Gaz XIV p. 316]

In the twentieth century the Vasai farmers specialised in betel leaf and the miniature velchi banana, and Vasai’s sweet yellow bananas went to Bombay’s temples and kitchens; the farmers in half-trousers who carried vegetables on a shoulder pole to the 4 pm train for Bora Bazaar and Dadar, calling “baaju-baaju Vasaiwale aale”, had disappeared by 2015. [n08] Those accounts are of Vasai generally, not of Gass or Sopara by name, and Chapters 19 and N3 tell the economy of the land and water. The Hindustan Times’s “ancestral mansions” of Gass and Wikipedia’s “green vegetable farming” are what remains of the garden year in the record of 2025. [n42]

Nirmal

Nirmal, the mother parish of the southern Kuparis, is told in Chapter 20: Holy Cross church, dated between 1557 and 1586, destroyed in 1739, its people worshipping in their cemetery until the parishioners rebuilt it in 1856, with 2,184 people in 1881. [n40] For Nallasopara two facts matter. Nirmal’s high school, Holy Cross, is where the Gass boys went, Bishop Almeida among them. [n42] And Nirmal, though the shrine and the church stand in Vasai West, is reached in the modern city by bus from Nallasopara to Nirmal Naka; a heritage listing of 2015 gave exactly those directions. [n54] The Nirmal Bazaar, opened in 1957, lies in the Vagholi gram panchayat. [n40] The parishes split from Nirmal, Gass in 1942, Bhuigaon in 1946 and Gomes Ali in 2004, show the same pattern on both sides of the boundary between Vasai and Nallasopara: villages too far from the old church asked for their own. [n40; n42]

The daughter parishes and what is not known

The modern Kupari parishes of Nallasopara and its edge are the daughters of Nirmal and Nandakhal: Gass (1942), Umrale (1970), Nanbhat (1975), Rajodi (1999) and Bolinj (2001). [n54; n09] The 1837 and 1881 evidence for Christian congregations in the south, the Manikpur report and the six churches, applies to the Vadval parishes round the fort, not to Gass; no Gass-specific figure exists before 1942. [n54]

What is not known is most of the Kupari history of Nallasopara’s villages before the twentieth century: who converted at Gass or Sopara, when, and under which order; which parish they belonged to; how many there were in 1881; and what the community of Gass did between the Gazetteer’s visit in 1882 and its petition for a church in 1941. [n42] Chapter 26 tells what is known.

II. The Samvedi Brahmins and the Hindu cultivators

Hindu husbandmen of Sopara

The Hindu half of Sopara’s husbandmen in 1882 were “chiefly Samvedi Brahmans and Bhandaris”. [n54; Gaz XIV p. 315] The Samvedis, 2,563 in the district in 1872 and “found only in Bassein and Mahim”, were gardeners and husbandmen, Brahmins by name and Veda but farmers by work; their customs, dialect and origin traditions are told in Chapters 20, V5 and K1. [n08; n49] In the Sopara country the record names two of their households: Bab Naik at Gass, and Bhiku Patil, “a Samvedi Brahman”, headman of Koprad, half a mile south-west of Bolinj, near whose house lay an inscribed stone of the fifteenth century. [n42; n49; Gaz XIV pp. 341–342] A Samvedi headman at Koprad and a Christian householder at Bolinj, in the same entry of the Gazetteer, show the two communities sharing the northern edge of the agar.

The community’s own tradition places its sacred centre at Nirmal, beside Sopara: the lake villages “earlier called Shurparak”, Agashi, Vatar, Kofrad, Bolinj, Nala and Umrale, with Nirmaleshwar as the community’s deity and its musicians serving the Nirmal samadhi temple with sanai, choughada, mridanga, bheri and veena. [n08; Wikipedia: Samvedi] Nala and Umrale, both in Nallasopara, appear in that list. It is a modern, weakly sourced tradition. [n08]

The village temples of Nale, Karmale, Bolinj and Achole

The Hindu sacred geography of the villages round Sopara survives in small temples that the older sources did not record, and that a heritage blog walked in October 2025. [n54; Heritage Chronicles, 11 Oct 2025]

At Nale, about four kilometres beyond Bolinj by the blog’s reckoning, the Shankar Nageshwar temple is built “in typical Konkani architecture”, with a Shiva linga in the sanctum, painted images of Parashurama, Vishnu and the sage Gautama, and a Narayan Vishnu image with the ten avatars carved round it, which an unnamed expert told the blogger was Chalukya; that dating is unverified. [n54]

At Karmale, on the Bolinj road about a kilometre and a half from Chakreshwar, an open shrine holds Vishnu as Shreedhar. In Bolinj village another open shrine, under a tree, holds Vishnu as Upendra. [n54] Bolinj, the Gazetteer said in 1882, “was formerly a large town and probably had rich temples”; carved stones were said to lie in its Devhara pond. [n40; Gaz XIV pp. 341–342] The CRIT survey recorded a seventeenth-century Bolinj pond used by devotees visiting the temple, and a Manukarnika Devi pond with a new temple. [n40]

At Achole, in Nallasopara East, a Vitthal-Rukmini temple stands near a lake, with a Trivikram carved on its façade. [n54] Vitthal is the god of the Pandharpur pilgrimage, to which the Son Kolis of 1882 also went. [n08]

The Vishnu images under trees at Karmale and Bolinj, the Trivikram at Achole and the Gazetteer’s eleventh- and twelfth-century sculptures at Chakreshwar and Gass belong to one medieval Hindu landscape, broken under the Portuguese and partly re-gathered after 1739; no source dates the village shrines themselves. [n54; n10] Chapter 05 tells the Shilahara temples of the coast.

No population figure for Hindu Samvedis in Sopara or Nala exists in the sources reached. [n54]

The Samvedi household in 1882

The Gazetteer’s account of the Samvedis, told at length in Chapters 20 and V5, is the only description of the Hindu husbandmen of Sopara’s households. They were “strong, tall, and fair, with regular features”, “clean, neat, sober, thrifty and orderly”, living in one-storeyed houses with plank walls and tiled roofs; the men wore short waistcloths, a shoulder cloth and “red broadcloth caps like Telugu Brahmans”; they said áde for “where” and hád for “why”. They paid a bride-price for brides married before ten; the groom rode to the bride’s house on horseback; the boy at his thread ceremony set out on a mock pilgrimage to Benares until his maternal uncle called him back with the promise of a daughter; the dead were burned and food given to the village Mhar on the third day. They had no headman and “send their boys to school”. [n08; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 82–83] Bab Naik’s household at Gass, where the Badhu stone stood, would have kept these rites.

Pachkalshi neighbours and Gujarati priests

Two other Hindu communities touched the Sopara agar without being named among its husbandmen. The Pachkalshis, the Hindu Vadvals, were fifteen families among the plantain-driers of Agashi, Vatar and Koprad in 1882, on the northern edge of the agar, and the Christians there were “originally Samvedis and Pachkalshis”. [n34; n40; Gaz XIII-1 p. 395] The Christian Vadval cart village of Chulne lay at the far end of the old basalt causeway south of Gass, on the road to Manikpur. [n54; Gaz XIV p. 341] But no source says that Vadvals lived at Gass or Sopara itself; the research for this book looked and found none. [n41] Chapter 20 tells them.

The Brahmin priests of the old town came, in part, with its traders. When the Cambay Vanis fled Momin Khan II in 1760–66, “with them several Gujarat Brahmans settled in Supara, Bassein, and other coast towns”. [n35; Gaz XIII-1 p. 63] The Lad Vanis’ family priests were Tolakia Brahmans. [n54] The Palshe priests of the coast and the Chitpavan and Deshastha Brahmins who came with the Marathas after 1739 are told in Chapter 20; no source places a family of either in Sopara or Nala. [n54]

III. The Bhandaris

The Bhandaris were the second Hindu community of Sopara’s husbandmen in 1882. [n54; Gaz XIV p. 315] They were the palm-tappers of the coast, 13,088 in the district, divided into Kirtes (coconut-palm tappers), Gavads (brab-palm tappers), Sindes and Kirpals, the Kirpals being “once Christians”. They carried a heavy tapping knife on the left thigh and a gourd of palm juice on the head, and “they sing while they tap the trees”; they worshipped Cheda and fasted on Ganesh Chaturthi; and after the rise in the palm-tree cess of 1877 many became husbandmen and labourers. [n08; n54; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 149–150] Chapter 20 tells them at length.

One trace of them survives in the map of old Sopara. Among the quarters of the old village, a Wikipedia-derived list gives Nawayat, Kharkhandi, Shakar, Waza, Bhandarali, Loharali, Chawdi Naka, Samelpada and Bazar Peth, also called Burhan Chowk. [n54; n31; Nalasopara mirror] “Bhandarali” is, by its form, the Bhandaris’ ali, their lane; “Loharali” the blacksmiths’ lane; and “Nawayat” the Naitas’. No source states those meanings or dates the quarters, and the list comes from a mirror of a Wikipedia page; but the old town’s lanes appear to have been named, as in many Konkan towns, after the communities that lived in them. That is an inference.

There is no source on Bhandari toddy-tapping in Sopara or Nallasopara today, and no figure for the community. [n54]

IV. The Muslims of Sopara and Nallasopara

The oldest settlers

Muslims may be the oldest continuously present community of the old town after its Hindu cultivators. The Gazetteer recorded, from Reinaud’s edition of the geographer Abulfeda, that Arabs had settled at Sopara, “Sofala”, in very early times. [n08; Gaz XIII-1 p. 61 n.] The Konkani Muslims of the district traced their origin to Arab and Persian refugees, merchants and adventurers of the eighth, ninth and tenth centuries, and their tradition to a flight from the governor Hajjaj in AD 699. [n35; n54; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 216–217] The Gazetteer’s footnote on Sopara’s Naitas says: “The chief settlements of Arab and Persian Naitas were made between the eighth and eleventh centuries.” [n54; Gaz XIV p. 315 n. 1] The Naitas, the Navayats, belonged, in Enthoven’s telling, to “the same wave of Arab settlers” as the Konkani Muslims, which tradition brought from the Euphrates valley about 700. [n35; Enthoven III]

After the first Muslim conquest of the coast, which the Gazetteer dated to 1290, the conquerors left the local chief the right to make land grants; a land-grant stone of AD 1351, carved with the sun and moon, stood near Pada, a hamlet of Sondavli, in the Sopara country. [n42; n31; Gaz XIV] At the Portuguese conquest of the 1530s, Garcia d’Orta found “Naitas of Bassein who had married women of the country and were very rich and enterprising traders”, and the Gazetteer said the Portuguese then forced the Muslims of the coast inland, to Kalyan, Bhiwandi and Thana. [n54; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 216–217] The Naitas of Sopara in 1882 were, if that is right, families who had come back after 1739, or who had never left.

The Naitas of 1882

In 1882 the Muslims of Sopara were both husbandmen and traders. “Musalmans, many of them Naitas” were among its husbandmen, and the traders were Muslims and Lad Vanis. [n54; Gaz XIV p. 315] The Gazetteer added a sentence about the name that a historian of the community has to keep: “The Hindus know the word Naita well but the Musalmans seem to consider it a term of abuse and disclaim it.” [n54; Gaz XIV p. 315 n. 1] The name the Gazetteer used was, then, not the name the community used of itself. A Wikipedia-derived page of about 2019 lists a “Nawayat Mohalla” among the old quarters of Sopara village, which is the same word in a form the community would recognise. [n54; n31]

The leading Muslim house of the town was the merchant’s. Ibrahim Seth, “the chief merchant of Sopara”, lived in the fine carved house on the market place, about fifty years old in 1882, so built about the 1830s. A hundred and fifty yards south of it lay a Muslim pond, about eleven by fourteen yards, “lined with old dressed stones”, with “many Musalman tombs” round it. [n54; Gaz XIV pp. 338–340] At the larger Bhatela pond, 240 yards by 146, the north and west banks were “covered with Musalman tombs, some with nicely carved head-stones”, with rows of lotus and chain lamps carved on them. [n54; Gaz XIV pp. 338–340] The Bhatela pond is also the place where Indraji found the fragment of Ashoka’s eighth Rock Edict in 1882; Wikipedia’s version of that find places it “in an old Muslim graveyard near Ramkund”. [n02] Along the creek ran a stockade of palm stems that the people called the “Burud Raja’s”. [n54]

The Gazetteer also noted, round Sopara, “modern mosques and shrines whose builders have made free use of the older masonry”. [n54; Gaz XIV p. 315] At Gass, in a garden, stood the tomb of a Muslim saint, Shaikh Akbar-moi-ud-din, bordered with old bricks sixteen inches by ten by three. [n42; Gaz XIV p. 341] And the Gazetteer told a local tale of robbers who troubled Sopara and of the garden of a chemist, Shaikh Amir; it is a legend. [n31]

What the Konkani Muslims kept

Enthoven’s account of the Konkani Muslims, compiled about 1920, describes the community to which the Naitas of the coast belonged. Its leading branch, the Jama’ti Konkanis, were mostly Shaikhs; their “home speech is Konkani Marathi with a slightly peculiar pronunciation and a mixture of Urdu, Persian and Arab words”; they followed the Shafi’i school of law, unlike the Hanafi majority of the Deccan; they were landholders, some holding inams as qazis, traders in rice, timber and salt, and sailors. At their weddings a lamp of dough was carried to a source of water, a custom shared with their Hindu neighbours. [n35; Enthoven III, “Musalmans”] The Gazetteer of 1882 said their organisation was complete under a headman, the mukadam, that well-to-do Konkanis owned brick houses of two or three storeys, and that traces of African descent appeared among them. [n35; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 64, 216–224] Enthoven added that Konkani Muslims in Bombay were known among other Muslims as “Kufis”, and that “as late as the Marathas the services of Arab seamen were still in demand”. [n35]

Whether the Muslim families of Sopara in 1882 were Jama’ti Konkanis, and whether Ibrahim Seth’s house kept a dough lamp at its weddings, the record does not say. The general account is given here because it is the only description of the community’s customs on this coast that the notes hold.

Dargahs and mosques

The CRIT heritage survey of 2008 recorded the Muslim sacred places of the old town: the Rehmatullah Alleya dargah, dated 1555 and since rebuilt, and the Hazrat Diwan Shah dargah on the Sopara-Gass road. [n40; CRIT Vol I items 8–11] At Karmale, by the Soneri pond, stands a small dargah. [n40] A modern archaeological paper notes a “Bandar Masjid”, the mosque of the port, near the spot at Gass-Taki Pada where a stone anchor was found in 2018, at the place where the authors think the old port lay before it silted up. [n42; Shah 2020] The name of the mosque keeps the memory of a harbour that has vanished.

In the modern city, a user-compiled listing names the Hazrat Kutti Shah Baba dargah, called the “Pahad wali dargah”, the dargah on the hill, near Nallasopara West station, with a mosque on the hill; its reliability is low. [n10] A Wikipedia-derived text says Nallasopara has “many dargahs of Islamic Sufi masters of the medieval period” and names none. [n10] No reliable history, founding date or urs calendar for any Nallasopara dargah was found, and a Marathi search for Nallasopara’s dargahs and urus returned nothing local. [n54; n10]

The market square of the old town is called Burhan Chowk, or Bazar Peth; the CRIT survey says the Burhanpur Chowk market was named after a nineteenth-century “Mr Burhanpur Harris”, an attribution no other source supports. [n40; n54]

Muslims of Nallasopara East

In 2001 Urdu was the first language of 12 per cent of Nallasopara town’s people, a higher share than anywhere else in the taluka figures. [n31; n54] Beyond that, the Muslim life of Nallasopara East, its mosques, madrasas, neighbourhoods, families and their origins, is undocumented in every source reached. There is no census count of Muslims for Nallasopara East or West. [n54] Reports of communal incidents exist only in partisan outlets, as allegations, and are not used in this book. [n54] The Muslims of Sopara are among the best-described people of the town in 1882, with their merchant, their ponds, their tombs and their carved headstones; the Muslims of Nallasopara in 2026, many times more numerous, are among the least described. That is the largest single gap in this chapter.

V. The Jains and the Lad Vanis

Sopara, a Jain tirtha

For the Jains, Sopara was a holy place long before it was a garden town. The Gazetteer recorded that “Jinaprabhasuri mentions Sopara (Soparaka) as one of the eighty-four sacred places of the Jains, and notices that while he was still alive Sopara had an image of Rishabhadev the first Tirthankar. One of the eighty-four gachchhas or Jain sects is called after Sopara.” [n10; Gaz XIV, Sopara] Jinaprabhasuri wrote his Vividha Tirtha Kalpa in the fourteenth century. Jain tradition, as a modern paper by Shah, Kale and the Jain monk Bhagyayashsuri sets it out, adds much more: an image of Adinath believed to be jivitswami, made in the Tirthankara’s lifetime; a recitation of the scriptures at Sopara by Acharya Yakshadevsuri after a twelve-year famine, in a year Jain reckoning places about AD 58; a Parshvanath temple built at Sopara by the minister Pethad Shah (1223–1283), the fifty-first of his eighty-four; Sopara as the earliest taleti, foot-shrine, of the holy hill of Shatrunjaya; and a “Sopariya gachha” still active in 1953. [n02; n10; Shah 2020] The Jain story of King Shripal, who married Tilakasundari, daughter of King Mahasena of Soparaka, is set here. [n10] These are traditions and texts; the Rishabhadeva image of Jinaprabhasuri’s lifetime is a fourteenth-century testimony.

The Jain Kuvalayamala of AD 778 tells of a horse merchant from Taxila, Lobhadeva, selling horses at Sopara with the help of a local shreshthin, a guild merchant, and describes traders’ clubs and eighteen breeds of horses. [n02; Shah 2020] It is the earliest literary picture of trading at Sopara in which merchants of the Jain world appear.

The stones bear some of this out. In the hollow of the great banyan by the Chakreshwar lake, forty-eight feet round, the Gazetteer saw a six-inch naked Parshvanath with a five-hooded snake, “probably of the tenth century or earlier”; and on an eleventh-century slab beside the temple, Shitala Devi stood with a small Jain Chandraprabha, the eighth Tirthankara. [n10; Gaz XIV pp. 337–338] At Gass, in the field called Sonarbhat, the surveyor found a broken cross-legged female figure, “perhaps Ambika”, the Jain mother goddess, and the foundations of a temple “said to have been dedicated to Parasnath”. [n42; Gaz XIV p. 341] Modern exploration round the Taki Pada part of Gass has found lintels of a Jain temple. [n42; Shah 2020] A Surya image from Gass inscribed in 1412 is held today by the Sopara Acchalgachha Jain Sangh, and seven inscribed Tirthankara bronzes dated 1458 to 1555 are held by the Jain Sangh at Chalpeth, Agashi. [n42; n02; Shah 2020] The 1555 bronze is the last dated Jain object before, or at the edge of, the Portuguese century. That a Jain Sangh at Sopara holds a medieval image today is the one documentary sign in the notes of an organised Jain community in the old town now.

On Tungar hill, to the south-east, Jain cells were cut, by tradition, about 1234. [n10] The great Jain tirtha of the coast today, the Agashi Maha Tirth at Chalpeth in Virar West, keeps a legend that its image of Munisuvrat Swami rose from a pond at Chakreshwar, or from “the lake of Nalasopara”, and was carried to Agashi; Chapter 31 tells it. [n10; n08]

The Lad Vanis of Supara

The Jain and Vaishnav traders who lived at Sopara in 1882 were Lad Vanis. “Of about 120 families of Lad Vanis about forty are in Thana, thirty-five in Supara, and the rest in Bassein, Agashi, Nala, Papdi, and Dahanu.” [n35; n54; Gaz XIII-1 p. 112] Supara, with thirty-five families, held more of them than any place but Thana town, and Nala had some too. They came, the Gazetteer said, about the middle of the eighteenth century, “a considerable number of Cambay Vanis, chiefly of the Lad sub-division, and with them several Gujarat Brahmans”, who “settled in Supara, Bassein, and other coast towns to avoid the exactions of Momin Khan II (1748–1783)”. [n35; Gaz XIII-1 p. 63] The Sopara entry dates their arrival from Cambay to about 1760. [n40; Gaz XIV p. 315] The Gazetteer also argued that Lad traders had been on this coast from very early times, because Masudi had written in 915 that the Lar language was spoken in the coast towns as far south as Chaul. [n35; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 112–113]

They spoke Gujarati at home and Marathi outside; they were shopkeepers, moneylenders, superior landholders and merchants, kept bullock carriages, ate no meat, and employed Tolakia Brahmans as priests. They were Vallabhacharya Vaishnavs, not Jains, and kept Shaiv fasts too, out of their “close connection with Maratha Brahmans”. [n35; n54; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 112–113] Their family goddess, Enthoven said, was Ashapuri of Ashmai near Petlad, and their women were “noted for their taste in dress”. [n35; Enthoven III p. 428] The Lad Vanis of Sopara and Nala, who came by the Cambay sea route a hundred and twenty years before the railway, are the best documentary forerunners of the Gujarati presence in modern Nallasopara. [n35]

Ramdas Kasidas Modi of Sopara, who helped Indraji with the antiquities in 1882, carried a surname that is common among Gujarati Vanis, but the Gazetteer does not state his community. [n42] Nothing in the notes describes a Lad Vani family, trust or temple at Sopara today. [n54]

Gujaratis, Marwaris and the modern derasar

In 2001, 17.82 per cent of Nallasopara town spoke Gujarati as a first language, more than in Vasai or the corporation as a whole in 2011. [n31; n54] The Marwaris came to the district after the British conquest, the first wave following the rent reductions of 1835–38, as moneylenders; Chapter 20 tells them. [n35] The only Jain institution of modern Nallasopara found in the notes is the Shri Munisuvrat Swami Jain Derasar and Upashray in the Shripal Complex on Tulinj Road, Nallasopara East, known from a listing that gives no history; its name joins the Munisuvrat of Agashi and the King Shripal of the Sopara legend. [n54; n10; jainmandir.org] No history, trust record or festival report for it, and no account of Paryushan or of any diksha in Nallasopara, was found. [n54]

VI. Kolis, Agris, Urap Agris and the people of the eastern hills

The creek folk of Sopara

Sopara’s creek once made it a fishing and salt-making town. Before silting, boats came up from Manikpur to Sopara itself, and “twenty years” before 1882, the Gazetteer said, there had been “much fishing and salt making” there. [n54; Gaz XIV p. 338] By 1882 the creek was dry at Sopara, and the long railway embankment, built in the 1860s, had sped the silting by damming the flow from the south. [n42; n31; Gaz XIV p. 315] The fishermen and salt-makers of the Sopara creek are not named by community in the Gazetteer’s Sopara entry. The district’s fishermen were Son Kolis, Christian Kolis and, in the north, Mangelas and Vaitis; its salt-makers were the Mithagris among the Agris, and the Kharpatils. [n54; n08] Gass, by its Wikipedia page, is still known for salt farming. [n42] There is no source on the Kolis of Nallasopara, or on any salt-pan or fishing family of its creeks, in the notes. [n54]

Achole and the Urap Agris

Achole, in Nallasopara East, is the one village of this chapter whose community the 1882 record states. It appears in the Gazetteer’s footnote list of the Bassein villages of the Urap Agris, the Agris who “are said to be Christian Agris who reverted to Hinduism, some in 1820 and others in 1828”. [n54; n40; Gaz XIII-1 p. 117 n. 1] Their story, the cholera years, the Palshe priests who readmitted them for a fee, the separate priest and headman, the Christian surnames Gomes, Soz, Fernan, Frutad and Minez, is told in Chapter 20. Achole belonged to the Jesuit parish of St Michael at Manikpur before 1739, by that parish’s history, which lists “Achola” in its jurisdiction. [n40; EI Manikpur] Its people had therefore been Christian under the Portuguese, and some at least became Hindu again in the 1820s. In 1882 the Portuguese fort at “Achola, a mile and a half north of Manikpur” was still on the visitor’s itinerary, and the old basalt footway south of Gass ran “on the way to Achola village”. [n40; n42; Gaz XIV pp. 323, 341–342]

North of Achole, about a mile, rises Tulinj hill, the Gazetteer’s “Rakshi Dongar”, a dyke of basalt whose six-sided pillars, six to twenty-two feet long, were quarried for the railway: “many of the pillars were used when the railway was made”, and people were still carrying them away in 1882. Across a ravine stood a circle of upright basalt pillars. [n54; n31; Gaz XIV pp. 341–342] The railway that made Nallasopara was built, in part, of the stone of the hill under which its eastern half now stands.

Today Achole, Tulinj and Pelhar are listed as “urban, gram panchayat not applicable”, places swallowed whole into the city. [n54; villageinfo.in (search summary)] Achole Talao, the village lake, lies in the Ambawadi area of the East. [n54] In the floods of July 2018 residents of Achole Road and Chheda Nagar were stranded for five days, and an Achole gaon resident said the water entered his hall, kitchen and bedroom. [n31; NewsBytes; DNA] Whether the descendants of the Urap Agris of 1882 still live in Achole gaon, the record does not say. A Marathi search for the history of Achole, Tulinj and Pelhar returned only general pages on Sopara. [n54]

Pelhar and the forest edge

Pelhar, at the eastern edge of Nallasopara, sat in 1882 in the forest belt of the Tungar and Kamandurg slopes: the Gazetteer named it first among the south-eastern forest villages, with Kaman, Chichoti, Poman and Paya. [n54; Gaz XIII-1 p. 33] Pelhar and Mandvi, in the Tansa valley, were “once places of importance”. [n54] The forest people of those slopes were Varlis, Katkaris and Thakurs, and the Kolis of the hill; they are told in Chapter 20. [n54] No source names a Warli, Katkari or Thakur pada in Pelhar or in any part of Nallasopara East, or gives its people’s numbers, deities or land claims. [n54] The Scheduled Tribe share of the corporation in 2011 was 4.70 per cent, lower than the taluka’s 7.32, and the census does not say how much of it is old hamlet and how much new migrant. [n49]

VII. The migrants of Nallasopara East

How little is recorded

Most of the people of Nallasopara today live east of the railway, and most of them, or their parents, came from somewhere else. This book would like to tell where they came from, when, why, and how they built the East. It can tell very little, and it should say so plainly. No ward-level census data were found. No table of migrants by state of origin for Nallasopara was reached. No history of Santosh Bhuvan, Shirdi Nagar, Pragati Nagar or Nilemore, no founder, no date and no account of who first lived there, exists in the sources reached. No migrant association, no regional temple or mosque history, no oral history of the East was found. [n54; n31]

What follows is the whole of what the notes hold.

Fragments

The census shows the migrants only through language. In 2001, besides Marathi, Nallasopara town spoke Hindi (22.92 per cent), Gujarati (17.82) and Urdu (12). In 2011, across the corporation, Hindi had risen to 26.58 per cent and Bhojpuri stood at 3.10, with Malayalam at 1.53, Bengali at 1.38 and Kannada at 1.00. [n54; n31] Between 2001 and 2011 Hindi and Bhojpuri appear to have grown while Gujarati fell, but the two tables cover different areas and the comparison may mislead. [n54]

The best account of how the East was built comes from a student research project of the Tata Institute of Social Sciences, “Make Break”, read only through search summaries because its pages would not open. It found that many people in Makarandnagar had been displaced from the Sanjay Gandhi National Park; that in the 1970s industrial zoning led entrepreneurs to build chawls for workers; that after 2000 landowners converted farmland into chawls for migrants, many of them coming from Mumbai itself; and that the low-lying, flood-prone land overlaps the zones marked for building. [n31; TISS] In one settlement, Sai Society, the scrap dealers and recyclers all came from a single village of eastern Uttar Pradesh. [n08; n35; TISS] A TU Delft student project describes low-rise baithi chawls in Nallasopara East being replaced by blocks of a ground floor and four storeys. [n31]

The 1882 Gazetteer described this eastern ground as salt waste, flooded and impassable in the rains, “a bare flat, formerly flooded during the rains and now being gradually reclaimed for rice”. [n42; n31] The same ground floods now. In July 2018 Vishal Nagar, H and C Colony, Vidyamandir Road, Tulinj Road and Achole were submerged; in a later 690-millimetre rain, Tulinj, Vijay Nagar and Giriz stood knee-deep, the Tulinj police station sat under about two feet of water and the station tracks went under. [n31; Lokmat Times; FPJ] The geography has not changed; only the buildings on it have. [n31]

Some of those buildings were built outside the law. At Agarwal Nagari, or Lakshmi Nagar, forty-one unauthorised buildings put up from 2006 on land reserved for a dumping ground and a sewage-treatment plant received notices in October 2024; reports gave their residents as 2,000 or 8,000. In November 2024 the High Court ordered their demolition, and the buildings were demolished. [n31; n02; Land Conflict Watch; Lokmat Times] It was from Agarwal Nagar that the corporation proposed, in 2025, to move the dumping-ground reservation to Gass, which the Kupari villagers fought off. [n42] The old Christian village of the West and the new migrant estate of the East were, in that dispute, two ends of the same municipal problem.

The migrants’ festivals appear once. In October 2025 the corporation allowed Chhath devotees to make their offerings in natural ponds across Vasai-Virar on 27 and 28 October, under court directions; no Nallasopara pond or ghat was named. [n54]

What can and cannot be said

The people of Nallasopara East come, by the language figures, from Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Gujarat, Rajasthan, Kerala, Karnataka and Bengal, and from Mumbai itself; by the TISS project, some were displaced from the national park and some came for factory work and cheap rooms. Their numbers, their communities, their villages of origin, their caste and religious make-up, the dates of their arrival and the history of their neighbourhoods are not recorded in any source this book could reach. The Gazetteer of 1882 could name every household community of Sopara in one sentence; no document of 2026 names the communities of the city that replaced it. That silence is not a judgement on the people of the East. It is a gap in what has been written about them.

VIII. The Buddhists and the revival of the stupa

The stupa and the edicts

Buddhism is the oldest faith documented in Nallasopara. Two fragments of Ashoka’s Rock Edicts put Sopara on the Mauryan map in the third century BC: the eighth edict, found by Bhagwanlal Indraji near the Bhatela pond in 1882, and the ninth, found at Bhuigaon on 1 January 1956 by N. A. Gore; both are now in the Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj Vastu Sangrahalaya. [n02; Sircar, Epigraphia Indica 32] In April 1882 Indraji opened the mound at Mardes that the people called the Burud Raja’s fort, Buruda Rajacha Kot, and found a stone coffer holding nested caskets of copper, silver, stone, crystal and gold, gold flowers, fragments believed to be of the Buddha’s begging bowl, a Satavahana silver coin, and eight bronzes of seven Buddhas and Maitreya of the seventh to ninth century. The relics are with the Asiatic Society of Mumbai. [n02] Chapter 04 tells the stupa and Chapter 22 its afterlife.

Buddhist tradition made Sopara the home of Purna, a merchant who built a sandalwood hall near Surparaka and invited the Buddha, who came, the story says, flying through the sky with five hundred monks. The historian writing for Live History India called the visit “not historically true”. [n02; n54] Indraji in 1882 recorded a local tradition that the Buddha converted the Brahmin sage Vakkali and five hundred widows here and gave them nail and hair relics, over which they built a “Widows’ Stupa”. [n02; Shah 2020] These are legends, recorded two thousand years and 140 years ago respectively.

The texts

The Buddhist literature gives Sopara a larger place than its stones. The Pali chronicle of Sri Lanka, the Mahavamsa, has Prince Vijaya, the island’s legendary first king, sail from Supparaka; it also says that after the Third Council the emperor Ashoka sent the “Yavana”, the Greek, Dhammarakkhita to Aparanta, the western coast, where he preached to seventy thousand and ordained more than a thousand men and a thousand women. Indraji believed Dhammarakkhita made Sopara his base. [n02; Shah 2020; Live History India] In a previous birth, a Jataka says, the Bodhisattva was a master mariner named Supparaka, and the Suppara Jataka pictures a prosperous port trading with south-west Asia, Gujarat, Malabar and Sri Lanka. [n02] A painting of Purna’s story was made in Cave 2 at Ajanta in the fifth century AD. [n02] These texts are late compilations, and they are legend and literature, not record; but they are why Buddhists today come to a mound in Nallasopara.

The modern community

Between the last enlargement of the stupa, which one modern account places in the Rashtrakuta period, and the twentieth century, there is no record of Buddhists at Sopara. [n02] The notes hold only fragments of the modern community’s history, and none dates its settlement here. A personal blog says Ambedkar visited the stupa, without a date; the claim is unverified. [n54; A Soul Window] The same blog says Buddha Purnima is celebrated at the stupa “on a grand scale”, with a Dhamma procession from the Buddha statue to the stupa, a Dhamma sermon (Dhamma Desna), and a camp for novice monks organised by the Pali department of the University of Mumbai; it also describes the site’s signboards as dilapidated and its facilities as poor. [n54; A Soul Window] The blog contradicts itself in places, and these details should be taken as one visitor’s account.

Beside the stupa stands a Buddha Vihar founded by a Buddhist nun, Bhikkhuni Sanghamitra (spelled “Bikhoni Sangamitra” in one blog). In January 2012, a blog reposting the Times of India reported, workers digging for a garden there found a pillar carved with a figure of the Buddha, which she said proved the Buddha’s visit; the state government and the municipal corporation then planned a meditation centre, a museum and a small theatre at the site. [n54; n02; Karmadungyu blog 2012; TOI repost] Search summaries add that she had worked there for twenty-six years and that a nine-foot Buddha statue donated by a trust in Pune was installed about two hundred metres from the stupa. [n54; n02] In 2014 the state decided to make the stupa the hub of Maharashtra’s Buddhist circuit; a later report, through Buddhistdoor, found it “still lies largely neglected”. [n02] Outlook Traveller called it a candidate for “India’s least visited tourist sight”. [n02]

In 2011 Buddhists were 2.65 per cent of the Vasai-Virar corporation and 2.53 per cent of the taluka. [n54] Where in Nallasopara they live, when the Ambedkarite community settled here, and how many come to the stupa at Buddha Purnima are not recorded. [n54] The stupa that Indraji opened in 1882 as an antiquity is now, at least once a year, a living place of worship again.

IX. Other Christians of the new city

The new city has Christians who are not Kuparis. The Syro-Malabar Church, the Kerala Catholic rite, has a parish of St George at Nallasopara West under the Kalyan diocese. [n31; Kalyan Diocese] Its founding date and congregation are not in the notes. The Kerala presence in the corporation, Malayalam at 1.53 per cent in 2011, is seen at this parish and at the Syro-Malabar centres of Naigaon and Manickpur told in Chapter 20. [n54] No other church of Nallasopara East, Catholic or Protestant, is documented in the sources reached. [n09]

X. The Parsis, and an absence

No source in the notes places a Parsi family at Sopara or Nala at any time. The Parsis appear on this coast in the Kanheri inscriptions of 999 and 1021 and at Thana before 1322; Garcia d’Orta found Parsi traders, “Coaris”, at Bassein in the 1530s; and after their return under British protection about 1780 they were liquor contractors, Government servants and timber dealers in the district. [n35; n54] A few Parsis came to the Nirmal fair in the 1880s, on the western edge of the Sopara agar. [n54; Gaz XIV p. 292] That is the only Parsi trace near Nallasopara in the record. Chapter 20 tells the Parsis of Bassein.

Part Two: Sacred Places and Festivals

The sacred places of Nallasopara lie almost all in the West, in and round the old town, and almost all of them hold layers of more than one faith. The 1882 Gazetteer described them because Indraji was digging there; the modern record adds a heritage survey, blogs and a few news items. What follows is each place as the communities of this chapter have used it. Chapter 44 tells what a visitor can see, and Chapter 20 tells Nirmal’s temples and church in full.

Chakreshwar

The Chakreshwar lake lies about 550 yards north-east of the stupa mound. In 1882 it measured about 180 yards by 120, shallow and never lined with masonry except for a central cistern. On the middle of its west bank stood a modern lamp pillar, a rest-house, “the mean modern temple of Chakreshvar” and a shrine of Maruti. [n10; Gaz XIV pp. 336–337] Round the temple the Gazetteer recorded a remarkable gathering of sculpture: a headless bull with a carved chain-and-bell necklace, dug from a well in the Sonarbhat field and “probably of about the same age (A.D. 1060) as the Ambarnath temple”; an eleventh-century Surya on seven horses; a Shiva and Parvati with Ganpati and Kartikeya; the slab of Shitala Devi with the Jain Chandraprabha; a sati memorial stone; a hero stone in three panels; and the chief piece, a Brahma six feet four inches high, three-headed and four-armed, with “a pointed rishi-like beard”, holding ladle, rosary, Veda and oil-flask, with his swan, “probably carved in the eleventh or twelfth century … found about thirty years ago buried in a field in Sonarbhat”, and “still worshipped as Dattatraya”. In the hollow of the great banyan sat the tenth-century Parshvanath. [n10; Gaz XIV pp. 337–338] The Brahma had been found, the Gazetteer said elsewhere, about fifty yards south of the Khare, or Shirmoli, pond, inside the limits of Gass. [n42]

So the temple of a Hindu god held a Jain image, the Brahma was worshipped as Datta, and a Buddhist stupa stood half a kilometre away. Local legend says the Portuguese threw the idols into the lake and they were later recovered; another says the people hid them to save them. Hindu belief says Krishna’s discus dug the tank; Jain belief links it to Chakreshvari, the guardian goddess of Adinath. [n10; Inditales; Heritage Chronicles 2014] The present temple is reconstructed; Inditales says most historians date it to the nineteenth century, and the Gazetteer already called the shrine modern in 1882. [n10] Beside it stands the math of Swami Samarth of Akkalkot, with the samadhi of Swami Mayuranand; a tradition says Swami Samarth himself stayed there. [n10; n54] Sculptures seen there today include Agni on a ram, Mahishasuramardini, Gajalakshmi, Harihara, Trivikram, a four-headed Brahma, a Jain Ambika and Uma-Maheshwara. [n10; n54] A jogging track now rings the lake. [n10]

The ponds of Sopara and Gass

The old town was a town of ponds, and each pond had its people.

Ramkund, Rama’s Pool, was in 1882 a bathing place for pilgrims in the month of Shravan. [n31; Gaz XIV] A modern paper cites Indraji for “the area near Ram Kund (Gaas tank)” as a place with the remains of an old Jain temple; whether Ramkund is the same as the Gazetteer’s Gass lake is not confirmed. [n42; Shah 2020]

The Bhatela pond, 240 yards by 146, had Muslim tombs with carved headstones on its north and west banks; near it lay the site of a Portuguese tower and an old landing, and there Indraji found the fragment of Ashoka’s eighth edict. [n54; n31; n02] Wikipedia speaks of remains of a Portuguese jetty and customs house at the pond. [n31]

The Muslim pond south of Ibrahim Seth’s house, lined with old dressed stones and ringed with tombs; the Khere or Shirmoli pond inside Gass limits, near which the Brahma was found; and the Gass lake, “a winding hollow, 120 yards broad and 800 long with two cross dams”, fringed with gardens and Persian wheels, with a carved stone lotus pendant on its east shore and dressed stone steps at its south end, complete the ring. [n42; Gaz XIV pp. 340–341]

Boghatirth, also called Bud-talavdi, was once a sacred pond lined with dressed stones; by 1882 it had been filled in. A treasure legend was attached to it: the first two men to dig there would forfeit their lives. [n54; Gaz XIV p. 339] It is a tradition, recorded as one.

Brahma hill, the cobra stone and the hill forts

West of Gass, toward Nirmal, rises Brahma hill, with the Vakal pond. The hill had long been a quarry: blocks of its basalt were laid as a footway along the low road between Gass and Nirmal and built into the plinths of Gass and Sopara houses. Between Gass and Brahma hill, under a banyan, stood a slab carved with the cobra god, Nagoba. [n42; n54; Gaz XIV pp. 323–324] No source records who worshipped there.

The Portuguese left three small hill forts round Sopara: Vajirgad, two hundred feet high, a mile south of Nirmal; Nildongri, with the ruins of a small fort, the “Nil hill” whose fortification the 1728 report criticised; and the fort at Achole. [n54; n31; Gaz XIV pp. 323–324, 339, 372] On Tulinj hill the circle of upright basalt pillars across a ravine was recorded in 1882 without explanation. [n31]

The stupa

The stupa, Buruda Rajacha Kot, at Mardes, is protected by the Archaeological Survey of India. It was excavated by Indraji in 1882 and again by M. M. Qureshi of the ASI in 1939–40, who found stone lintels, two small stupas on its south side and glazed sherds of the Muslim period. [n02] Today it is the scene of the Buddha Purnima procession, with the Buddha Vihar of Bhikkhuni Sanghamitra and the nine-foot Buddha statue nearby. [n54; n02] A few hundred yards from it, at the Madres pond, Catholic families gather round a cross at Christmas. [n40]

Churches

The parish churches of Nallasopara’s old villages are St Gonsalo Garcia at Gass (1942, expanded 1957, blessed 1962) and St Joseph’s at Umrale (parish 1970), with Holy Cross at Nirmal (told in Chapter 20) as their mother on the west and the Holy Spirit church at Nandakhal on the north. [n40; n42; n09] In the new city, the Syro-Malabar parish of St George stands in Nallasopara West. [n31] The wayside crosses of the Kupari villages, such as the cross at the Madres pond, are kept with an annual feast and rosary, as in every Catholic gaothan of the taluka. [n40; n09]

Temples, dargahs and the derasar

The Hindu temples are told above: Chakreshwar and the Akkalkot math; the Shankar Nageshwar at Nale; the open Vishnu shrines of Karmale and Bolinj; the Vitthal-Rukmini temple at Achole; the Manukarnika Devi temple by its pond at Bolinj. [n54; n40; n10] The dargahs are the Rehmatullah Alleya (1555, rebuilt), the Hazrat Diwan Shah on the Sopara-Gass road, the small dargah at the Karmale Soneri pond, the tomb of Shaikh Akbar-moi-ud-din in Gass recorded in 1882, and the Kutti Shah Baba dargah on the hill by Nallasopara West station. [n40; n42; n10] The Bandar Masjid stands near Gass-Taki Pada. [n42] The Munisuvrat Swami derasar is on Tulinj Road. [n54] No jatra of a village goddess was found for Achole, Tulinj, Pelhar or Nala. [n54]

The calendar of Nallasopara

  • February: the week-long feast of St Gonsalo Garcia at Gass.
  • April or May: Buddha Purnima at the stupa, with the Dhamma procession.
  • July to August: pilgrims bathing at Ramkund in Shravan (1882).
  • August to September: Ganesh immersions at the Umrale ponds.
  • October to November: Chhath at the ponds of the corporation (27–28 October 2025); the Nirmal fair on Kartik Vadya 11, the great gathering of 1882 that drew Hindus, Muslims, Christians and a few Parsis.
  • December: Christmas, with the Catholic gathering at the Madres pond cross.

Open gaps

The record for Nallasopara is rich in 1882 and thin in 2026, and the gaps fall on the many, not the few.

  • The East. No ward-level census figures; no origin-state tables; no founding history of Santosh Bhuvan, Shirdi Nagar, Pragati Nagar, Nilemore or any neighbourhood of the East; no migrant associations. [n54; n31]
  • The Muslims of today. No census count by ward; no history of any mosque, madrasa or dargah of Nallasopara; no urs calendar. The Naita merchants of 1882 are better recorded than their successors. [n54]
  • The name and the station. The origin of “Nala”, the date the halt opened, and its first name (“Neela”?) remain unresolved. [n31]
  • Nala before the suburb. Beyond the 1882 trade and plantain notices, the Nila inscription and the Tombo entry, no history of Nala village survives in the notes. [n40; n31]
  • The Kuparis of Gass and Sopara. No conversion record, no parish of record before 1942, no Tombo entry for Gass, no population figure before the twenty-first century; the two inscribed stones of 1882 have not been traced. [n42]
  • The Hindu Samvedis and the Bhandaris. No population figure for Sopara or Nala; no source on Bhandari toddy-tapping. [n54]
  • The Lad Vanis. Thirty-five families in 1882 at Supara; no family, trust or temple of theirs today in the notes. [n54]
  • The creek folk. No source on the Kolis or salt-workers of Nallasopara’s creeks. [n54]
  • The Adivasis. No pada of Pelhar or the East is named in any source. [n54]
  • The Buddhists. No history of the Ambedkarite community’s settlement, no Buddhist share by ward, no pilgrim count. [n54]
  • The Jains. No history of the Tulinj Road derasar or of the Sopara Acchalgachha Jain Sangh. [n54; n42]

Marathi newspapers, the 1982 Thane district gazetteer, the census village tables, the Waqf records, the parish books of Nirmal and Nandakhal, and above all the memories of the people of the East could fill these gaps. They lay outside the sources this book could reach.

From the market place to the city

In 1882 the people of Sopara could be named in one sentence, and they lived within sight of one market place: the Christian husbandman and his Samvedi neighbour, the Naita trader and the Bhandari tapper, the Lad Vani shopkeeper, the merchant in his carved house. The creek that had brought their goods to the sea was already dry, and the railway that had dammed it ran three-quarters of a mile away without a station. When the station came, and the city grew round it, the old town became one small part of something its people could not have imagined. Chapter 26 follows one of its villages, Gass, from its formation to that city; the Epilogue, the Epilogue, asks what all these peoples, of Vasai, Virar and Nallasopara together, still share.
