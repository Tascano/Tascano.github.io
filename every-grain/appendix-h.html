This appendix gathers material found after the main chapters were drafted. Each item is sourced in the research notes. Where a source is a community publication, a thesis or a catalogue record only, that is said here.

The Katkaris (Katkari, Kathodi)

The Katkaris were the forest-edge community of the hills of Thana and Raigad, and a few are reported in the Vasai region. Colonial writers described them harshly. The Thana volume of the Bombay Gazetteer (1882) called them “the poorest and least hopeful” people of the district, a “drunken lot given to thieving”, and unwilling to work except when forced by hunger. [n57; Monograph of the Katkaris, 1979, quoting Gazetteer Thana vol. XIII] A century earlier, Bishop Heber’s 1825 account, relayed from Mr Wishinton, described Katkari charcoal-burners in Salsette who left their charcoal at set spots, and were paid in rice, cloth and iron tools by the villagers who took it away. [n57; Monograph, quoting Heber] Mackintosh in 1836 described them as thieves who stole grain and plundered travellers, but also drew attention to their desperate circumstances. [n57; Monograph, quoting Mackintosh] The monograph of 1979, prepared by the Tribal Research and Training Institute at Pune at the request of the Government of India, is a useful corrective: it describes the community in the state’s own terms, with a sample survey of villages and a district-level population table. That table is hard to read in the scan and its figures are not quoted here. [n57 §1]

The modern community is classed as a Particularly Vulnerable Tribal Group. Later surveys, including a 2014 Tata Institute of Social Sciences report that appeared in search results (not read in full), describe their poverty and the loss of forest livelihoods. [n57 §1] The history of Katkari sites in the Vasai region itself is thin in the record and this appendix does not fill it.

Marathi-language books on Vasai Fort and the Vasai campaign

Several Marathi books on the fort are not cited in the English-language sources used in the main chapters. Two are recent popular histories: Ramesh Nevase’s Kille Vasai (Shri Rajmudra Prakashan, 2017; 127 pages) and P. K. Ghanekar’s Kille Vasai: Advitiya Vijayacha Sakshidar (Snehal Prakashan, Pune, 2022; 97 pages, illustrated). Older scholarship includes Y. N. Kelkar’s Vasaichi Mohim (Pune, 1955), cited in the main chapters, and P. S. Pisurlekar’s Portuguese-Marathe Sambandha (Pune, 1967), which the main chapters do not yet cite. [n57 §§2–3] These titles were found through catalogue records and were not read. Their claims should be checked on the books before they are used, including the dates of the 1739 siege, where sources disagree. [n57; n55]

The Vadvals: sources outside the main citations

The Vadval Samaj’s own Marathi site gives the community’s original name as Somvanshi Kshatriya and says the community is found in North Konkan from about 1060. [n57 §5] That is a community claim, not an academic finding, and the main chapters already treat it that way. A food-heritage site, Ruchkar Vadval, describes the community’s rice and fishing livelihoods around the creeks between Kelwa, Saphale and Vasai. It is useful for food and daily life, and is flagged as community-sourced. [n57 §5]

Agri (Aagri) Koli and the salt pans

A student thesis on the Aagri Koli describes a salt-pan subgroup, Mith Aagri, and migration from Colaba toward Thane and Vasai. [n57 §6] A thesis is not a monograph, and its claims are leads only. The Agri dialect of Marathi, spoken in Vasai, is noted in the language chapters. [n22]

The Indraji text

Bhagwanlal Indraji’s Antiquarian Remains at Sopara and Padana (1882, Journal of the Bombay Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society) is the first published excavation account of the Sopara stupa. The main chapters rely on it. The text was fetched and skimmed this round; the main chapters’ specific claims have not yet been checked line by line against it. [n57 §4]

A Portuguese chronicler on Baçaim

Diogo do Couto, writing in the Décadas da Ásia (Década V, part I, Lisbon 1779 edition, p. 23 as read from the scan), describes the Portuguese capture of Dio and the nearby kingdom of Baçaim in the same breath. He calls Baçaim “uma Cidade insigne; cheia de muitas riquezas, com o seu proprio Rey e todo o Reyno”. In English: a famous city, full of riches, with its own king and its whole kingdom. [n58 §3] The passage is in old Portuguese orthography, and it is the chronicler’s own voice, not a later summary. The book’s chapters on the Bassein sultanate use the Portuguese chroniclers only at second hand; this is the direct text.
