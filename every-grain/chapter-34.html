Period covered: from the Treaty of Bassein (December 1534) to the fall of Portuguese Baçaim (May 1739)

In this chapter. How and why the villages became Christian between 1534 and 1739: the first friars, the laws, the Jesuits, the great baptisms, and what conversion cost.

Forty boys at Agashi

In 1535, the year after the Sultan of Gujarat signed Bassein over to the Portuguese, a Franciscan friar named António do Porto went north from the new Portuguese town to Agashi. There, “as early as 1535”, he founded an orphanage for forty boys, under the invocation of Nossa Senhora da Luz, Our Lady of Light. Agashi, Gerson da Cunha wrote in 1876, “was the first place of this district visited by Franciscan missionaries”. [n34; Da Cunha 1876, pp. 159–160; Gazetteer XIV p. 1–2] [n37; Gaz. XIV pp. 1–2]

Five years later, in April 1540, an army from Gujarat came down the coast in reprisal for the Portuguese conquest. It destroyed the orphanage. Da Cunha, following a Franciscan chronicle, reports that five people who had been left behind were burned alive after refusing to accept Islam. The Franciscans counted them as their “second batch of martyrs”, and all of them were native converts. [n34; Da Cunha 1876, pp. 159–160] [n37; Gaz. XIV p. 1; da Cunha pp. 159–160] The Catholic Encyclopedia of 1907 says Do Porto’s orphanage at Agashi produced “the first Indian martyrs”. [n04; Catholic Encyclopedia]

Who were the forty boys? No source names them. The Gazetteer gives the method that filled such places: Do Porto, “by providing orphanages and in times of war and famine filling them with deserted children, prepared a class of native priests”. [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 201] They were children of the coast, taken in by the friars when their families could not or did not keep them, and brought up as Christians. The orphanage stood in Agashi, the northern edge of the Samvedi world described in the last chapter, and its boys were the first Christians that world produced under the new rulers, a hundred years before most of the conversions. When the church of Agashi was rebuilt, the Gazetteer recorded, it was “built after the destruction of Nossa Senhora da Luz”. [n34; Gazetteer XIV pp. 1–2]

A second Franciscan chronicle: four martyrs, and 1571

The story above follows da Cunha and the Gazetteer. The Franciscan chronicle behind it is Frei Paulo da Trindade’s Conquista Espiritual do Oriente, written in the 1630s, and its modern edition, with notes by the Franciscan historian Félix Lopes, tells it differently. [n64] Its fifth rectory in the cassabé of Baçaim is Agashi: “a large settlement where many Portuguese also live”, with a church of Our Lady of the Assumption and “two thousand Christians whom our religious converted”. As “the principal place of this cassabé”, Paulo says, fr. António do Porto “built a college, by order of the King of Portugal, for forty orphan boys native to the place”, who learned Christian doctrine, reading, writing and singing, and, if able, Latin. [n64 §3] The forty boys are there; the name Our Lady of Light, and the year 1535, are not.

The martyrdom is there too, but it is not in 1540 and the number is not five. Paulo places it “in the time when Zamaluco”, the Nizam Shah of Ahmadnagar, “had laid that great siege to the city of Chaul”. The Muslim troops came as far as Agashi, a village without walls or soldiers; the villagers left; and four neophytes were seized, offered great promises if they would take up Islam, and threatened. They would not, and were put into the church, “which was still of cane and straw”, and burnt with it, “receiving through martyrdom the sovereign crown of glory”. [n64 §4] The editor dates that siege to 1571. The manuscript copy says 1572, which he takes for a copyist’s slip, and Couto records that when the Nizam Shah reached Chaul on 6 January 1571 he at once “sent four thousand horse against the lands of Baçaim”. [n64 §4] The parish accounts also say that Gujarat forces destroyed the chapel at Sandor in 1571; the two may be one raid, though no source says so. [n64; n40]

The editor goes further. Some writers, he notes, have placed Franciscan work in the lands of Baçaim before 1540. As they state it, he says, that “seems unacceptable”: Franciscans may have visited, but “before 1547 they did not settle there”, and in 1545 Miguel Vaz told the king that there was no church “in all the land, which is fifteen leagues and more, nor any order or way to make known to these vassals the errors in which they live”. [n64 §4] The martyrs, he adds, raise no difficulty, since they belong to 1571. If he is right, the paragraphs above date the Agashi story thirty years too early: the “forty boys” and the burning belong to the second half of the sixteenth century, not to 1535 and 1540. Da Cunha’s date and the number five should be read as da Cunha’s, and probably mistaken.

This chapter tells how the Samvedi and Pachkalshi families of the villages around Agashi, Nirmal and Sopara came to the font over the next two centuries: who brought them, with what means, under what laws, and why. It is the hardest chapter in this part of the book to write, because every source has a side. The friars wrote to their superiors to show results. The Goan Catholic scholar Gerson da Cunha disliked the Jesuits and looked down on the people of Bassein. The British Gazetteer’s editors wanted to explain a community they ruled. A Goan Hindu archivist, A. K. Priolkar, wrote in 1961 to expose the Inquisition. A Jesuit historian, Fr H. Heras, wrote to defend his order. Hindu writers of today remember force; Christian bloggers remember force too, and something else as well. No one source tells the truth. Together, read against one another, they tell a good deal.

The new masters and the old faiths

The treaty by which Sultan Bahadur Shah ceded Bassein, signed on 23 December 1534 aboard the galleon São Mateus, contained a clause that later Portuguese policy would turn upside down. It provided that 5,000 larins of Bassein’s revenue that went to the mosques “was to continue to be so applied”. [n37; Danvers vol. 1 p. 416] [n04; Treaty of Bassein] Within about twelve years the Crown reversed it. In March 1546 King João III wrote to the viceroy Dom João de Castro ordering that the “3,000 pardaos formerly employed in the maintenance of the Mahomedan mosques” be given to the priests “who give a helping hand towards the conversion of the infidels”. [n37; da Cunha p. 215 note] From 1550, da Cunha found, the Crown paid the Franciscans of Bassein 2,070 pardaos a year “derived from a source which before the conquest was solely applicable to the lighting of the mosques in Bassein”. When the Jesuits came the money was split between the orders, entered in the accounts under the heading Conversão da Fé, “Conversion of the Faith”, and in 1634 the Jesuits’ share was raised to 3,000 pardaos. [n34; Da Cunha 1876, p. 235] [n37; da Cunha p. 235] The parish church of the fort, St Joseph’s of 1546, the “cathedral wrongly so-called”, was itself funded from mosque money under the King’s letter. [n37; Hull vol. 1 pp. 4–5; da Cunha pp. 215–216]

That was the first lesson the people of the villages learned about the new order: the endowments of the old faiths would now pay for the conversion of their followers.

The second lesson was the destruction of temples. The Gazetteer credited António do Porto, between 1534 and 1552, with having “destroyed 200 temples, made over 10,000 converts, built twelve churches, and, by founding orphanages and monasteries, secured a supply of native priests”. [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 461] The Catholic Encyclopedia gives him precisely 10,156 conversions. [n34; New Advent, “Bassein”] The temples he threw down were at Agashi, Bassein and Thana. At many sacred tanks “temples were thrown down” and some pools were filled in. [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 461 note 1] The Gazetteer’s general summary of the Franciscan’s work was blunt: “He threw down Hindu temples, rebuilt them as churches.” [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 201]

At Nirmal, the holy lake at the heart of the Samvedi world, the Portuguese “pulled down the temples and destroyed the linga”. [n37; Gaz. XIV pp. 292–293] A church of the Holy Cross, Santa Cruz, stood there in time. The Gazetteer, writing after the Marathas had rebuilt the temples, thought the old temple stones had been used in Portuguese churches and were later “probably used to raise the temples of the older gods”. [n37; Gaz. XIV pp. 292–293] At Sopara the real stripping of the lake banks and temples came in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, when the stones were carried off for Portuguese churches, forts and “the great walls … of Bassein”. [n37; Gaz. XIV p. 323]

Sometimes the old sacred place was not erased but taken over. At one famous pool between Bassein and Agashi, the Gazetteer recorded, the pool was converted, a chapel to Our Lady of Healing was built, and “the pilgrimage and cure-working continued”. [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 461 note 1] Later, da Cunha says, the Inquisition “had put a stop to all pilgrimage” to the healing tank near Our Lady of Remedies on the Agashi–Bassein road “under very severe penalties”; in his own day Hindus once again held a jatra there. [n37; da Cunha p. 163] The Great Cave at Kanheri became a church of St Michael, and the Brahman caves at Mandapeshvar a church of Our Lady of the Conception. [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 461 note 1] The two heads of the Kanheri monastery, apparently Buddhist, were converted and renamed Paulo Rapozo and Francisco de Santa Maria, and Paulo Rapozo was given three villages, which he left to the college at Mandapeshvar. [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 461 note 1] That last detail is worth holding on to: a convert of standing could receive villages.

Do Porto’s orphanages were the third instrument. Besides Agashi’s forty boys, there were 130 at Thana, 800 at Vehar and 100 at Mandapeshvar. [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 461 note 1] They gave the church its first native clergy, and that clergy, as the next chapter will show, would save the church of the villages two centuries later.

The Father of the Christians

In each Portuguese settlement there was an officer called the Pai dos Cristãos, the Father of the Christians, whose task was “to further Christianity, to foster Christians and to gather others to Christ”. Salsette and Karanja were given to the Franciscans; Goa and Cochin to the Jesuits; Chaul, Diu and Macao to the Dominicans. Da Cunha called it “a dangerous appointment” whose holders’ “indiscreet zeal” led to “unseemly affrays”. [n37; da Cunha p. 102; Gaz. XIII-II p. 462 note 2]

The Jesuit visitor Valignano wrote instructions for the office, quoted by Priolkar, that state the method of the whole age with unusual frankness. Conversion, he wrote, “does not commonly occur as a result of sermons and doctrine” but by “obstructing the idolatrous practices … meting out just punishment … refusing them favours which can justly be refused, and offering them to those who are newly converted, and honouring, assisting and protecting the latter”. [n37; Priolkar p. 58] Sermons were not expected to do the work. Favour and disfavour were.

The Jesuits come

The Jesuits reached the Province of the North in 1542. Francis Xavier visited Bassein three times, about 1544 and twice in 1548, and in December 1548 founded the College of the Holy Name of God inside the fort. [n04; Catholic Encyclopedia] [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 461] The Gazetteer says the college’s foundation was laid in 1548 by Fr Melchior Gonçalves. [n32; BG 41] A division of labour grew up, the Catholic Encyclopedia says, in which the Franciscans served the lower classes and the Jesuits the higher. [n34; New Advent, “Bassein”] It was the Jesuits who left the clearest written evidence of how the conversions actually worked, in letters sent back to their superiors in Rome and Portugal, printed in the twentieth century in the collection Documenta Indica. Those letters are the nearest we can come to hearing the missionaries describe their own work in the years when it was done.

Gonçalo Rodrigues, 1558

On 5 September 1558 Father Gonçalo Rodrigues wrote from Bassein. In the three and a half years since he had come from Goa “to Taná and Baçaim”, he reported, “more than nine hundred souls” had been baptised. [n34; Documenta Indica IV, doc. 16, p. 98]

Then he wrote something remarkable for its honesty. “Those who come to conversion”, he said, “are commonly the poorest and most needy, and are converted more drawn and moved by hope of some interest and remedy of life than by preaching, which they do not understand and for which there is no interpreter.” [n34; Documenta Indica IV, pp. 100–101, translated in the research notes; the scanned text is slightly noisy] He described the method plainly: “we set them the snares of human things and, once caught in them, we teach them the divine … we invite the rustics, farmers and those who live in woods and hedges”. [n34; Documenta Indica IV, p. 100–101]

He described what the snares were. At a new Christian village of the Holy Trinity in Salsette, built around a converted temple, lived about five hundred souls, all farmers and labourers. For them the Jesuits had provided “houses made, very cheap lands, and for many, properties we buy for them”; clothes for the men, women and children; rice to eat until their first harvest; seed; “as many oxen as they want to plough, with their ploughs mended”; a common cattle shed; and a granary for the paddy that Christians on the land paid as rent. [n34; Documenta Indica IV, pp. 100–101] The royal alms for conversion, he added, had risen from 1,500 to over 1,700 pardaos. [n34; Documenta Indica IV, p. 102] He also described a house where catechumen women lived and were married to converts, who were then given “a way of living and help”. [n34; Documenta Indica IV, pp. 101–102]

This village was in Salsette, near Thana, not in the Samvedi north. It is nevertheless the clearest account of the Jesuit economic method in this region, written by the man who ran it. [n34; note on doc. 16] The Gazetteer, drawing on a Jesuit chronicle, describes what seems to be the same enterprise from another angle. Gonçalo Rodrigues at Thana, about 1560, bought young children and collected orphans, baptised five to six thousand people in three years, and founded a Christian village at Vehar of eight thousand people around an enclosed shrine of a “three-headed god”, which was rededicated to the Trinity. [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 461 and note 4] The 1558 letter’s five hundred and the Gazetteer’s eight thousand are very different figures, and the research notes do not say whether they describe the same village at different dates. Both describe a village of the Trinity founded by the same Jesuit. In 1843 a Lisbon periodical remembered a hamlet called “Trinity” whose people sang sacred songs at their work. [n34; Gaz XIII p. 207 n.2]

The same Gazetteer note adds a sentence from the chronicle that no honest history can pass over: “The lower Hindus sold their children to Musalmans and Christians. A child at the breast cost as much as a goat in Portugal.” [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 461 note 4] Machado’s modern history, cited through Wikipedia, says that Rodrigues’s five to six thousand baptisms were “many orphans or children sold by their parents”. [n04; Machado 1999] Famine and poverty drove families to sell their children, and the mission bought them. Whether that was rescue or traffic, the sources come only from the buyers.

Francisco Cabral, 1562

Four years later, on 5 December 1562, Father Francisco Cabral wrote from Bassein to the general of his order, Laínez. His letter describes the landlords. [n34; Documenta Indica V, doc. 90, pp. 628–631]

The Portuguese citizens of Bassein, Cabral wrote, “all have villages”, aldeas. They had believed that if one villager were made a Christian, the village would be depopulated, and so Christianity “was little favoured”. After the Jesuits’ preaching “they no longer hinder Christianity, at least publicly”. In one baptism he had baptised about 196 people together (the scanned figure is uncertain), and more than 300 since he had arrived. And then: “The inhabitants who before would riot if a vassal of their villages was made [Christian] now come to beg us to go and convert their villages.” [n34; Documenta Indica V, pp. 628–629]

He described the ceremony. The catechumens were kept at the college, men and women separately. A lay brother who knew the language explained the faith to them every night. They were baptised in procession, with the captain and the whole city, with garlands, psalms and flutes, and afterwards feasted. Those who had been married as Hindus were married again. About eighty converts’ children were kept at the college to learn to read, write and sing, “and if they are taught they may become priests”. [n34; Documenta Indica V, p. 629] A variant reading in the critical apparatus of the printed letter, read through the scan and needing checking against the page, describes the Thana practice: gentile converts were lodged in a village and given “land to plough and oxen … all bought with the money the King gives for the Christianity”. [n34; Documenta Indica V, apparatus note, p. 630–631]

Cabral’s letter matters for the Kupari story for two reasons. First, it shows that the Portuguese landholders of Bassein first resisted and then competed in conversion. The Census of 1931, looking back over four centuries, gave the same explanation for the Christian villages of the coast: “The Portuguese parcelled out the countryside among certain of their countrymen … With the advent of the missionary a great deal of mass conversion took place, not unaided by competition among the owners of these estates, and in many villages every tenant in the village was baptized and given the surname of the owner of the village, e.g., Demonte, Pereira, Ferreira, etc.” [n34; Census 1931 Vol VIII Part I pp. 363–364] Second, it shows the pageant. A baptism in procession with the captain of Bassein and the whole city, with garlands and flutes and a feast, was an event designed to be seen.

The villages ask

Later letters show villages asking for resident Jesuits, at least in part on their own initiative. A settlement near Bassein offered to maintain a priest “at their own houses”, Father Gomes Vaz reported in the years 1566–68. The Jesuit residence at a place called Maim, near Bassein, was founded “because the inhabitants asked with great insistence, not only the Provincial but the Viceroy and the Archbishop”. [n34; Documenta Indica VII, letter of P. Gomes Vaz; annual letter, para 6] Maim is probably Mahim in the Bassein district, though this is uncertain, and none of these letters can be matched to a named Samvedi village. [n34; note]

A request from a village is not the same as a free choice by every family in it. Cabral had just described landlords who now “beg” for the conversion of their villages, and Mendiratta, the modern historian of the community, writes that whole villages converted “normally following the conversion of the respective ‘matará’ or headman”. [n34; Mendiratta p. 211] But the letters show that by the 1560s there were villages near Bassein where at least some people wanted a priest among them, and were willing to house him.

A comparison from Goa

One more Jesuit letter belongs here, though it is about Goa in 1547, not Bassein, and must not be read as evidence for Bassein. Father Nicolau Lancilotto wrote to Ignatius Loyola from Goa on 10 October 1547: “The people of this country who become Christians do so purely for temporal advantage … Slaves of the Moors or Hindus seek baptism in order to secure their manumission … Others do so to get protection from tyrants, or for the sake of a turban, a shirt … or to escape being hanged … The man who embraces the faith from honest conviction is regarded as a fool. They are baptized … without any instruction, and many revert to their former paganism.” [n37; Wicki, Documenta Indica I, pp. 188–189, quoted in Priolkar p. 55] Lancilotto was a harsh observer, writing about another place a decade before Rodrigues. But his letter and Rodrigues’s agree on one point: in the Jesuits’ own eyes, in the first generation, interest outran doctrine.

The great numbers, 1565–1620

The great numbers came in the second half of the century.

On 8 September 1565 a Brahmin astrologer named Parashuram Joshi was baptised as Henrique da Cunha, followed by about 250 Hindus, more than fifty of them Brahmins. [n04; Machado 1999 via Wikipedia: Bombay East Indians] The figure is from a modern history, seen only through Wikipedia, which does not say where the baptism took place. Joshi is a Palshe surname, and the Palshes were the astrologers of the coast, but whether Parashuram Joshi was a Palshe is not known. [n08; Gazetteer 1882, p. 81]

Between 1570 and 1590, the Gazetteer says, the Jesuits “took pains to make Brahman and other high-caste converts, knowing that if the Brahmans became Christians, many of the lower classes would follow”, and made baptism “an occasion of great splendour and rejoicing”. Baptisms rose “from 1600 in 1573 to 9400 in 1588”. [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 461; Gaz. XIV p. 41] In 1588, the Gazetteer says, 9,400 Hindus were baptised in the church of Bassein alone. [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 201] [n32; BG 41] Da Cunha gives the same figure, citing the Jesuit chronicle Oriente Conquistado. [n34; Da Cunha 1876, p. 234] The research for this book traced the 9,400 to both da Cunha and the Gazetteer, both citing Jesuit chronicles, but did not see the Jesuit letter itself. [n34; Corrections]

Da Cunha described the Jesuit method with sharpness. They converted “a high-caste man, a Brahman, who was sure to be imitated by a number of his and other classes”; then they solemnised his baptism “with all possible pomp”, with “a grand procession in his honour with the assistance of the Governor, the nobility, gentry and clergy”, and “a sumptuous banquet”. His verdict: “Their bait was alluring and irresistible, and the native convert was led by the nose.” [n34; Da Cunha 1876, p. 234] [n37; da Cunha p. 234] Three new Bassein parishes, he said, arose from the Jesuits: Nossa Senhora da Graça, São Tomé and São Miguel Arcanjo. [n34; Da Cunha 1876, p. 234] Those dedications match the later churches of Papdi (Our Lady of Grace), Sandor (St Thomas) and Manickpur (St Michael), south and east of the fort; the match is an inference, since da Cunha names no villages. [n32; BG 36–37]

These numbers do not sit easily together. Machado’s history, via Wikipedia, estimates only 10,000 to 15,000 Catholics in the whole Province of the North by the end of the sixteenth century. [n04; Machado 1999] If António do Porto baptised over 10,000 before 1552 and the Jesuits 9,400 at Bassein in 1588 alone, the provincial total looks too small. Either many of the baptised died, fled or lapsed; or the yearly figures counted children and slaves who were not counted as households; or one set of figures is wrong. The notes cannot resolve it, and the reader should see the tension rather than a clean total.

What the modern historian Sidh Mendiratta concludes, from the scholarship available to him, is that between about 1570 and 1620 the Jesuits and Franciscans converted “a majority of the population” of Salsette and the Bassein and Agashi subdivisions. “Whole villages were converted, normally following the conversion of the respective ‘matará’ or headman, usually from the Prabhu caste, in the case of Salsette.” And: “Christian converts who were farmers or fishermen were meted out particular benefits, while Hindu and Muslim communities that did not convert had to revert to low-key and mostly private religious practices.” Missionary activity “slowed down considerably from the early 17th century”. [n34; Mendiratta p. 211]

That is the window in which the Samvedi and Pachkalshi families of Agashi, Nirmal and the garden island most likely went to the font: the half-century from about 1570 to 1620, in the Agashi subdivision that Mendiratta names. No source dates the conversion of any individual Kupari village. No source says whether the Samvedi converts were baptised in one wave or gradually. [n34; Gaps] But the window is clear, and so is the mechanism Mendiratta describes: a headman first, then the village.

The laws

Alongside the friars and their gifts stood a growing body of law. The question for the Kupari villages is not only what the laws said but whether they were enforced there.

The sequence, in the Gazetteer’s Bassein-relevant chronology, runs like this. In 1546 the King of Portugal ordered idols to be broken, idol-makers and performers of Hindu rites to be punished, and mosques to pay tribute; the order was not enforced. It was renewed in 1555: Hindu feasts, Brahman preachings, ritual washings and burnings of the dead were forbidden; anyone found with idols was to be sent to the galleys with his property forfeit, and “half of the property of a man found with idols went to the church”. These orders were “evaded by the grant of licenses, but … enforced in 1581”. [n37; Gaz. XIII-II pp. 462–463 and note 7] The period of Philip II, from 1580 to 1598, ended all toleration of Hindu worship. [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 201]

Mendiratta adds the Goan legislation known as the Provisões a favor da Cristandade, the “Provisions in favour of Christianity”, of 1549 to 1557, which “contributed decisively towards conversion, and severely restricting the freedoms of Hindu and Muslim inhabitants”. In 1549 the governor Jorge Cabral ordered that “no person of whatever quality shall rebuild a [Hindu] temple or a mosque” and that “no person shall dare to prevent another from becoming Christian”. In March 1555 the viceroy Pedro de Mascarenhas ordered measures against the gentios, the Hindus, of the Bassein territory. [n34; Mendiratta pp. 210 and 210 n.5]

Then come two sentences of Mendiratta’s that matter more for the Kupari villages than any decree. First: the seizure of Hindu or Muslim orphans “was enforced in the Northern Province only in or near towns”. Second: “As far as we know, the ‘Provisions in favor of Christianity’ were never enforced in the Northern Province’s subdivisions closest to the mainland.” [n34; Mendiratta pp. 210 n.5, 211] The northern villages, Nirmal and Agashi among them, lay in the Cassabé and Agaçaim subdivisions. [n34; note] If Mendiratta is right, the harshest Goan provisions were a law on paper in the Samvedi country, not a policeman at the door.

The orphan law has its own history. In March 1559, according to Priolkar, King Sebastião ordered that no Hindu temple be built, and none repaired without special permission, on pain of destruction; and that Hindu orphans with no living father, mother or ascendant be handed over to the Jesuit College of St Paul at Goa. Priolkar gives the date three different ways, 22, 25 and 28 March, and the research notes recommend checking the archive collections before quoting a day. The temple order “did not apply to Salsete and Bardez”, the Goa territories, until a viceroy’s order of 29 August 1566 extended it “to the entire territory under Portuguese rule”. [n37; Priolkar pp. 76–78, 128–129] The research did not find the 1559 text applied to Bassein by name. [n37]

The orphan law was not a dead letter in the North. The Inquisitors complained that orphans were not reported promptly, and ordered them reported to the Father of Orphans within six days on pain of excommunication. [n37; Priolkar p. 128] In 1577 the Hindus of Goa petitioned that the Father of Orphans removed sons from living mothers “together with the estates … to compel them by force to receive baptism”. [n37; Priolkar p. 128] And in the north, the English East India Company’s papers recorded of Shivaji: “Sevagee and they daily quarrell, the chiefest cause of his hatred to them being for forcing orphans of his [caste to] turn Roman Catholicks”. In a letter of 26 November 1675, the Captain General of Bassein himself took the side of some orphans against the Jesuits and forced the Jesuits to restore the orphans’ estate. [n37; Orme Manuscripts, quoted in Priolkar p. 129] A year earlier, in 1674, the Maratha envoy More Pandit had demanded the chauth, a quarter of the revenue, because, he said, the Portuguese “had lately inflicted great severities on many families of the Morattoe religion … because they refused to become Christians”. [n37; Orme, cited by da Cunha p. 143]

In December 1567 the first Provincial Council of Goa resolved that “it is not lawful to bring to our faith and baptism any person by force with threats and terror”, and that conversion should come by “example of our lives, preaching … gentleness”. [n37; Priolkar p. 53] Yet the same Council’s decrees, as a Portuguese writer cited by da Cunha reports them, barred Christians in Goa and Bassein from lending money to, entering partnership with or sheltering “Parabus” (Prabhus); from employing non-Christians in public office as clerk, interpreter, broker or shroff; and from renting property to pagans. They ordered a roll of the infidels in each parish of Goa and Bassein, half of whom were to attend Sunday catechism on pain of fines of one, two or three tangas. [n37; Priolkar p. 53; da Cunha p. 142] Here the “two-track system” the research notes describe is laid bare: an official language of free conversion, alongside pressure through property, office, credit and the Sunday roll. [n37; Inferences]

A caution belongs here. Priolkar’s famous account of the destruction of 280 temples “of Salsete” in 1567 by the captain of Rachol concerns Goa’s Salcete, not Bombay’s Salsette, and the older literature sometimes confuses the two. It should not be used for Vasai. [n37; Priolkar pp. 78–79]

There was another effect of the laws, and it ran against conversion. A Portuguese writer, Goez, reported about 1630 that “the persecution of the Portuguese had made many Hindus, Mussulmans and Parsees abandon their homes and live in the dominion of Shah Jehan … between Bassein and Damaun there are few natives and the greater part of the village lands being uncultivated”. The Gazetteer added, cautiously: “if this account is correct the districts soon recovered their prosperity”. [n37; Gaz. XIII-II pp. 463–464; da Cunha p. 143] Some families, faced with the choice, simply left. The Pachkalshis’ own tradition, recorded by Enthoven, remembered that persecution “forced many members of the caste to fly to the outlying parts of the Southern Konkan”. [n35; Enthoven III, p. 162] The Samvedis who remained Hindu in 1882 may in part descend from families who stayed and held out; the sources do not say.

What the Goa register says about Baçaim itself, 1547–1585

Priolkar and the Gazetteer give the laws in general. The register in which the Goa government copied its decrees, printed by Josef Wicki as O Livro do «Pai dos Cristãos» (1969), also holds four items addressed to Baçaim by name. [n71] They show the conversion of the villages being managed by decree, fine and salary.

  • The Franciscans’ own list, November 1547. The same weeks produced a list from the Piedade friars at Baçaim. The list the Bishop forwarded from Cochin on 26 November 1547 includes the freedom of converts from the coloarias and from the village posts held by the unconverted: naique, pião, deiçila (dessai), patel and the like, which the friars wanted given to native Christians. It asks that the lands of rebels and of those who died childless, and the “viretes” brought by Brahmins (outcaste Hindus who had not received the thread), go to Christians, and that the rents of the pagodes and mosques support the colleges of Baçaim and Chaul. [n72 §4] Two months before, Miguel Vaz had also asked that the Goa Brahmins who “go about in Baçaim and Diu” be thrown out of those lands. [n72 §1] Cosme Anes wrote from Baçaim on 30 November that the friars “requested of the Governor some things for the good of the Christians of the land, of which he passed provisions”, so the decree of 28 October, printed next, was very likely one of them. [n72 §5] (Inference.)
  • 28 October 1547, no village labour for converts. Governor Dom João de Castro told the captain of the fortress, Dom Jerónimo de Noronha, that the gentiles of the fortress and its lands who had been converted, and all who later converted, were “not obliged nor compelled to the coloarias, as they were before they became Christians, but live in their liberty in any part of this island of Baçaim they wish”. The coloaria was the register of a village’s cultivators, bound to service (Dalgado’s gloss). [n71 §1] Exemption from it was a real change of status, and the earliest dated material inducement to convert that the book’s sources record for Baçaim.
  • 2 August 1549, no new temples or mosques. Governor Jorge Cabral ordered the captain, the ouvidor and the tanadars (village headmen and revenue collectors) that “no person of whatever quality shall again build a pagode or mosque, nor raise one”. The first offence cost 10 pardaos, half to the tanadar who enforced it and half to the hospital. Anyone who hindered a non-Christian was fined 5, and a Muslim or Brahmin who performed a ceremony on a Christian “by which he gives to understand that he returns him to his sect or gentility” was fined 10 each time. Every tanadar had to proclaim the order in his village and record that he had done so. [n71 §2]
  • 15 March 1550, the Bishop’s request. Answering a royal letter of 1548 that there be no idolatry in the King’s lands, the Bishop of Goa reasoned that “as Baçaym is of his lordship, and so are all its islands”, the same applied there, and asked Fr Belchior Gonçalves and the Jesuits, the vicar Fr Simão Travaços and the Franciscans to pull down any pagode they found built, begun or being repaired. The editor says Belchior Gonçalves founded the Jesuit residence at Baçaim and was dismissed by Xavier in early 1552, and that Travaços was still vicar in 1556. [n71 §3]
  • 24 February 1585, a salaried Pai dos Cristãos in each house. Viceroy Dom Duarte de Meneses ordered that each of the Franciscans’ “houses of the Christianity” in the lands of Baçaim, “the college of Manapacer, Maym, Bombaym, Caranjá, Monte Calvario and Agaçaim”, should have a Pai dos Cristãos named by the Custodian and paid at the Baçaim factory. He could judge Christians orally up to 50 xerafins without appeal, and take verbal insults and blows short of bloodshed. [n71 §4] The list is wider than Paulo da Trindade’s five rectories of the cassabé and includes Mandapeshwar, Mahim, Bombay and Karanja. (Inference.)

Pedro de Almeida, S.J., who died at Baçaim in 1579, is called by the editor “the first Jesuit Pai dos Cristãos”. [n71 §5]

Privilege and servitude

The law pushed. Privilege pulled. And privilege was not distributed equally.

“Converts, especially high caste converts, were treated with honour and distinction, and, for the first fifteen years after conversion, the poorer class of Christians were freed from the payment of tithes and first fruits.” [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 462] Fifteen years free of the church’s tithe on the harvest was a real incentive for a farming family.

But the Gazetteer drew a sharp line between converts of rank and the rest. “Though Christian names were given to all alike, the Portuguese treatment of converts of good birth was very different from … lower class converts. Men of rank were admitted into the best Portuguese society and were allowed to marry with Europeans … received grants of land … On the other hand, those of low birth were left in a state little removed from servitude. In 1675 the Portuguese gentry are described as living … like petty monarchs holding the people in a perfect state of villainage.” [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 202] The grants of land were nominally for three lives, and “no right in the land was conceded to the husbandmen or tenants”. [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 460] By gradual grants “about one half of Salsette became church property”, and by 1695 church revenue was said to exceed the king’s. [n37; Gaz. XIII-I p. 201; Gaz. XIII-II p. 463]

Where would a Samvedi family stand in this order? Probably in between. They were Brahmins by name, which the Jesuits valued, and landholding farmers and village headmen, which the landlords needed. A family like that could be courted as a “high-caste” convert whose example would bring others, and could also be a tenant in a village whose Portuguese holder wanted all his people baptised. The research notes put it this way: the Jesuit letters show three conversion modes at work at the same time, (a) livelihood grants aimed at the poorest cultivators, (b) pageant baptism of leading men who expected social inclusion, and (c) landlord-led conversion of tenants; “a farming Samvedi Brahmin community would fit (b) and (c)”. [n34; Inferences, section 3] That is an inference, but it is a reasonable one, and it is consistent with the later reputation of Samvedi Christians as, in one sociologist’s phrase, “the only Bassein group to receive Jesuit education”. [n34; Rodricks, Sahapedia]

The privilege did not erase caste. The Gazetteer recorded that among the Christians of the district in the nineteenth century the Bhois, Kumbhars, Nhavis and Dhobis formed separate castes, and that Mhar converts lived outside the villages in 1859 and could not touch the village well or enter the church. [n37; Gaz. XIII-I pp. 201–202 and note 1; Gaz. XIII-I p. 201] Enthoven noted in 1920 that the Bhandaris, Kolis and Kunbis contained “Christian sections that only marry within such sections”. [n34; Enthoven vol I, p. 10] A modern sociologist writes that the Portuguese did not make converts give up caste, so caste identities persisted. [n34; Rodricks, Sahapedia] The Samvedi family that went to the font remained, in its own eyes and its neighbours’, a Samvedi family.

The Inquisition

In 1560 the Inquisition was established at Goa, “and by 1580 agents of the Inquisition, called commissaries, were at work in Chaul, Bassein and Daman, collecting offenders and sending them for trial and punishment to Goa”. “After 1560”, the Places volume says, “there was a commissary of the Inquisition at Bassein.” [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 463; Gaz. XIV p. 41] Da Cunha describes the system: every principal settlement “sent yearly a large number of victims through their so-called Commissaries”; prisoners at Bassein were held in the jail near the Captain’s palace until “the fleet from the north” carried them to Goa; and an inscribed slab of the Inquisition prison still stood near the travellers’ bungalow in his day. [n37; da Cunha pp. 235–236] The Jesuit church’s own entry in the Gazetteer records a Commissary of the Inquisition from 1560. [n32; BG 41] (A 1987 newspaper article claimed the Inquisition was “never set up in Bassein”. The two primary sources contradict it: there was no tribunal at Bassein, but there was a commissary who sent prisoners to the tribunal at Goa. [n09; The Examiner 1987])

The Inquisition proper tried baptised persons. Christian converts were subject to it as “New Christians”. [n37; Priolkar p. 60] That is what made it a threat to the Samvedi families who had gone to the font: a convert who kept a Hindu rite, a vow to a goddess, a household image, could be denounced as a relapsed heretic. Seventeenth-century travellers, Pietro della Valle, Dellon and Gemelli Careri among them, described its rigour against “the Christians if they strayed from the path of orthodoxy, the Hindus if they practised their religious rites”. [n37; Gaz. XIII-I p. 202] The research notes point out that this sentence conflates two jurisdictions: the Inquisition tried the baptised, while the law against idolatry among non-Christians was enforced by other courts. [n37; Inferences] Both bore on the villages.

There is one hard local case. In 1840 Filipe Nery Xavier found a stone set up in 1786 at a place where the Inquisition had razed a house. The family who had lived there, “dogmatists of the said sect”, were “burnt in the Auto de Fé celebrated on December 30, 1747”; their houses were “demolished and ploughed with salt”. [n37; Priolkar pp. 60–61] The stone belongs to Bassein. The sources read do not give the family’s name or the house’s location, and the date, 1747, falls after the Portuguese had lost Bassein, so the case may have concerned people living elsewhere in the Portuguese territories or the record may have been carried over; the notes do not resolve it. Its wording, “of the said sect”, points to charges of secret Hindu practice among converts. [n37; Inferences]

How many people from the Bassein villages the Inquisition took, nobody can say. Its records at Goa were destroyed, Priolkar complained, so “it is not possible to obtain an idea of the numbers” burnt. [n37; Priolkar p. 60] Da Cunha gives “about 1,250 persons burned or tortured in ten or twelve autos da fé” at Goa, uncited. [n37; da Cunha] A traveller, Pyrard, claimed that “only the rich, the confiscation of whose property profited the Inquisition, suffered, while the poor escaped”. [n37; da Cunha p. 235] For the Samvedi villages there is no list of names, no case and no number. There is only the commissary, the jail, the fleet from the north, and a stone of 1786.

It is worth noting what survived in spite of it. In 1882 the Gazetteer recorded that secret offerings to Ganpati and Gauri, and vows to the goddess Shitladevi, still continued in outlying Salsette and Bassein villages, “said occasionally”, and were disapproved by the priests. [n34; Gazetteer XIII pp. 207–210] Centuries of law and two and a half centuries of the Inquisition had not entirely closed the door between the old world and the new.

Persuasion, interest or force: the judges

Every serious historian of the Bassein conversions has had to answer the same question: did people choose, or were they made to? The sources answer differently, and each answer should be heard in its author’s voice.

The Gazetteer (1882), edited by J. M. Campbell’s team, leaning on Portuguese accounts. “The fact that the people of Bandra remained Hindus till about 1580, seems to show that the earlier conversions were the result of persuasion and encouragement, not of force. At the same time, from before the middle of the sixteenth century, the persuasion … [was] accompanied by rules discouraging and suppressing Hinduism.” [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 462] And, in its most quoted judgement: “Force may have been used in some cases, but the Portuguese accounts seem to show that persuasion and interest were the chief means of conversion. Their treatment of their chief rivals, the Musalmans, was specially severe.” [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 201 n.2] [n37; Gaz. XIII-I p. 201 note 2]

Gerson da Cunha (1876), a Goan Catholic physician and antiquarian. No defender of the Jesuits: “Their bait was alluring and irresistible, and the native convert was led by the nose.” [n37; da Cunha p. 234] For him the instrument was not force but enticement.

A. K. Priolkar (1961), a Goan Hindu archivist writing on the Goa Inquisition. The “generally accepted view”, he wrote, was that conversion rested on “lure of material rewards and threat of violence and force”. He quoted Dr Antonio Noronha: conversions took place “some by fear of physical force; others from moral cowardice … not a few to avoid the loss of their properties and interests; some with their eyes on lucrative jobs — and almost none from conviction”. He called Pyrard’s account of 1,500 Goan catechumens baptised after a sermon that “none should come there by constraint” “an empty gesture”. He noted that even the Jesuit biographer Brodrick admitted that Michael Vaz’s policy involved “a great deal of pressure, social and financial”, while insisting “No Hindu … was ever forced by that policy”. [n37; Priolkar pp. 49–56]

Fr H. Heras SJ, The Conversion Policy of the Jesuits in India. The opposite view: the Jesuits’ work came “only a posteriori … after the Hindus themselves had decided to join the Church”. [n37; Heras p. 26, quoted in Priolkar p. 51]

Sidh Mendiratta (2017), a modern historian of the East Indian community. The Provisões “contributed decisively towards conversion”, but were never enforced in the mainland subdivisions; whole villages converted after their headmen; converts who were farmers or fishermen received particular benefits. [n34; Mendiratta pp. 210–211]

How should these be weighed for the Samvedi villages? Priolkar’s source base is Goa, often Goa’s Salcete, not Vasai, and should be used with care here. [n37; note] Heras’s view is that of an advocate for his order. The Gazetteer leaned on Portuguese chroniclers who had reasons to stress willing conversion. Da Cunha despised both the Jesuits and, as we shall see, the people of Bassein. Mendiratta is the only one writing specifically about the Northern Province with modern scholarship.

The research behind this book reached a judgement that this chapter adopts. Payment, tithe exemption, land, status, protection and the orphan law pushed the poor and the vulnerable, orphans and slaves and fishermen dependent on Portuguese ports. High-caste converts were honoured and induced by ceremony. Coercion in the strict sense, forced baptism, is documented most clearly for orphans and for Muslims, and was tied to Inquisition rule. No source read documents a mass forced baptism in Bassein by name. Voluntary or semi-voluntary conversions certainly occurred, “but ‘faith’ cannot be separated from ‘interest’ in these sources”. [n37; Inferences, section 3] The Gazetteer’s own phrase, “persuasion and interest”, is the safest neutral wording, so long as it is remembered that it was written beside a list of laws suppressing Hinduism. [n37; Inferences, section 2]

There is one more voice, and it is not a scholar’s. It is the memory of the converts’ descendants and their neighbours. The Pachkalshis told Enthoven their kin were converted “by force”. [n35; Enthoven III, p. 162] A Christian blogger of 2009, writing the history of the Kupari community, said most Samvedi Brahmins converted “after losing property and being ostracised by orthodox Brahmins”. [n07; D’Mello blog] A Hindu writer of 2019 spoke of “force and deceit”. [n07; Swarajya] Memory is evidence of what people came to believe about their past, and that belief has its own history. But the three memories agree on something the documents also show: that the conversion was experienced as a loss as well as a gain, and that the loss was of property, of caste, and of kin.

Who converted: the evidence on the Samvedis

The claim that the Kuparis are “converted Samvedi Brahmins” is the centre of the community’s identity. What do the documents say?

For it. The best period evidence is a pair of statements in the 1882 Gazetteer. At Sopara: “The husbandmen of Sopara are Christians, many of them converted Samvedi Brahmans; Musalmans …; and Hindus, chiefly Samvedi Brahmans and Bhandaris.” [n34; Gazetteer XIV p. 315] At Agashi, Vatar and Koprad: “about eighty-five families of Samvedi Brahmans, fifteen families of Pachkalshis, and about seventy-five families of Native Christians originally Samivedis and Pachkalshis” dried ripe plantains, the Christians being “gardeners who grow the fruit”. [n34; Gazetteer XIII Part I p. 395] The same Gazetteer said generally that “of the Portuguese rule along the coast, from 1530 to 1740, there remains in Salsette, Bassein and Mahim the important class of Christians, chiefly converted Brahmans, Prabhus, Pachkalshis and Kolis”. [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 63] Its Christians chapter begins its list of constituent castes with “Brahmans, Prabhus, Pachkalshis, Charkalshis, Sonars”. [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 200] The shared houses, ornament names and birth-spirit described in the last chapter point the same way. [n34; Gazetteer XIII pp. 81–83, 204–205, 210–211] A modern sociologist, Rita Rodricks, writes that the first converts were Samvedi Brahmins who had settled near the Vaitarna between Agashi and Nalasopara, that Samvedi Christians are “largely farmers”, were once forest contractors, and were “the only Bassein group to receive Jesuit education”; her article as read cites no sources and is best treated as an informed popular synthesis. [n34; Rodricks, Sahapedia]

Against it, or complicating it. The same Gazetteer added that “the bulk of them are Bhandaris, Kolis and Kunbis”. [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 200] Gerson da Cunha, in 1876, denied any high-caste origin at all. The Catholics of Bassein, he wrote, were “pretty numerous clans of different types of the Kolis and Bhandaris … besides the hybrid product or bastard offspring of … a European soldier and a low-class native woman”; “there is no descendant of any high-class native amongst them”. [n34; Da Cunha 1876, pp. 250–251] Mendiratta reads Cunha’s tone as contempt rooted in the quarrel between the Portuguese Padroado and Rome’s Propaganda, and in the rivalry between Goans and the local Catholics; on the same page Cunha resents the “parochial” pamphlets written against Goans. [n34; Mendiratta p. 234] The research notes conclude that Cunha is “a hostile, caste-conscious contemporary”, and that “his claim is a rival opinion, not a census”. [n34] The Census of 1931 said that at least ninety per cent of the Indian Christians of the whole Bombay Presidency descended from the “Sudra section of Hindus”; that is a Presidency-wide statement, not one about Bassein. [n34; Census 1931 Vol VIII Part I, para 8] Kenneth Ballhatchet, quoted through Elroy Pinto, found that by 1850 Catholics in the Konkan and Bombay were “primarily the cultivating classes or ‘low castes’”. Pinto adds that “historically, only a tiny minority have been of high caste origin”, and that family Brahmin claims are unsupported. [n34; Ballhatchet via Pinto] Mendiratta says the Indian Christians who stayed in Salsette in 1739 were “overwhelmingly either fisherfolk or farmers, mostly of Koli, Kunbi or Bhandari castes”, and that Brahmin and Prabhu converts were a minority in the Salsette villages, which was “one of the main reasons why, later on, the Catholic inhabitants of Salsette Island and Bassein’s subdivisions would be labelled as ‘rural’ or ‘samvedi’ East-Indians”. [n34; Mendiratta p. 218]

That last point deserves to be heard carefully. By the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, “Samvedi” had apparently become, in East Indian usage, a loose label for rural Bassein and Salsette Catholics generally. [n34; Inferences, section 1] So the word “Samvedi” in a later document does not always mean Brahmin descent.

The verdict of the evidence. The research notes put it in a sentence that this chapter cannot improve: “‘converted Samvedi Brahmins’ has genuine 1880s support for the northern villages, but it describes part of the community, not all.” [n34; Takeaway, section 1] The northern villages of Agashi, Nirmal, Koprad, Vatar and Sopara are exactly where 1882 observers saw Christians who had been Samvedis, and that fits the modern Kupari map. [n34; Inferences] The Agashi entry adds the Pachkalshis. And the Brahmin claim and the cultivator claim are not enemies: the Samvedis of 1882 were already husbandmen and gardeners, not priests, so even a Brahmin origin means descent from a landed farming community. [n34; Inferences] Later mixing, through marriage with Vadval and Goan families and perhaps some Portuguese ancestry, is described by a community blog and should be held as tradition. [n07; D’Mello blog]

The Gazetteer’s Agashi figures carry one more meaning. Eighty-five Hindu Samvedi families and seventy-five Christian families of Samvedi and Pachkalshi origin lived in the same three villages in 1882. [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 395] “The Samvedi community was split by conversion, not wholly converted.” [n37; Gaz. XIII-I] The conversion did not take a caste; it divided one.

Godfathers, compadres and the name

At the moment of baptism a new kind of kinship was made. The Gazetteer of 1882 describes it for the Christians of the district: the child “is taken to the church by its godfather, padrinho, and godmother, madrinha”, between the eighth and fifteenth day after birth, and “the mother never goes to the christening”. [n34; Gazetteer XIII pp. 210–211] Grierson, in 1905, recorded the Portuguese words the Christians of Thana used in their Marathi: “pay, father, may, mother, … padron, god-father, madron, god-mother”. [n34; LSI VII, Kiristav section] The godparent was a figure of the Christian village important enough to keep his Portuguese name for three and a half centuries.

That is the strongest supporting fact for the most widely repeated derivation of the word “Kupari”: that it comes from Portuguese compadre, literally “co-father”, the word for the relationship between a child’s father and its godfather, with a feminine comadre said to give “Kumari”. [n34; Wikipedia: Kupari; Post in Mumbai blog, 25 Mar 2015] Linguistically the derivation is straightforward: cum-padre, co-father. The Christians demonstrably used Portuguese godparent words. But the derivation is cited on Wikipedia only to forum posts and a dead dictionary link, and the research for this book found no scholarly or period source that states it. Dalgado’s great Glossário Luso-Asiático of Portuguese words in Asian languages has no entry for Kupari or Kumpari. [n34; Etymology; Gaps] The compadre derivation is therefore a plausible hypothesis, consistent with the evidence, and unproven.

There is a second derivation, and it is painful. A commenter on Wikipedia’s discussion page in 2007 proposed that “Kupari” means “those who drank water from the well”, from Marathi kūp, a well, linking it to a legend of a priest dropping a host into a well, and said that Hindus use the word as an insult. No source was given, and another editor asked for a “strong source”. [n34; Talk:Kupari] Kūp is a real Marathi word, and the sound-alike works. [n34; Inferences] But a sound-alike is not an etymology, and this one carries a story of humiliation. It should be told, because people in the villages have heard it, and labelled for what it is: folklore, unsourced, and offensive to many.

The well brings us to the story that every Kupari child has heard in one form or another.

The bread in the well

The story is told in several versions. In the one carried by a community blog of 2009, missionaries threw bread into the village wells, and the Brahmins who drank from them were outcasted, then given new names and a new dress code. [n07; D’Mello blog] In a Hindu writer’s version of 2019, conversions were by “force and deceit”, bread was put in wells, about two hundred temples were destroyed and ten thousand people converted early on, and converts were made to wear red caps and red saris as markers. [n07; Swarajya] The logic of the story is the logic of caste. To eat or drink what a Christian had touched, or what contained forbidden food, broke a Brahmin’s purity; once broken, his own caste would not take him back, and he had nowhere to go but the church.

What do the sources actually say?

A well-pollution story is documented in the 1880s, but not of the Samvedis. The Gazetteer wrote: “According to the story of the Uraps and other classes their forefathers were forced to become Christians by having pieces of flesh thrown into their wells.” The Uraps were Christian Agris and Kolis, fishermen and salt-workers, who had gone back to Hinduism in 1820 and 1828. The Gazetteer cited the Oriental Christian Spectator, a mid-nineteenth-century Protestant-leaning periodical, of June 1859, page 238, for “the Portuguese thrusting biscuit and pork into the mouths of unwilling multitudes”. And it added at once its own judgement: “Force may have been used in some cases, but the Portuguese accounts seem to show that persuasion and interest were the chief means of conversion.” [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 201 n.2] [n37; Gaz. XIII-I p. 201 note 2]

So the oldest written form of the story, as far as this research reached, is from 1859, three centuries after the events, in a periodical with its own sectarian view of Catholic missions; and its 1882 form is a story told by the Uraps “and other classes”, not specifically by Samvedis. [n34; Corrections] No Jesuit or Franciscan letter read for this book records it. The Jesuit letters record gifts, feasts and pageantry, which are different mechanisms. [n34; Inferences, section 3] The research notes judge it “a tradition attested from the 1850s”. [n34]

Some of the modern version’s details can be checked, and they come apart in an instructive way. The figures of two hundred temples and ten thousand converts are not invented: they are the Gazetteer’s own figures for António do Porto’s work between 1534 and 1552. [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 461] The red caps are another matter. The Gazetteer of 1882 records red broadcloth caps worn by Hindu Samvedi men, “like Telugu Brahmans”, and by Christian fishermen. [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 82, p. 205] A cap worn by the unconverted half of the community cannot have been a mark imposed on the converted half. [n34; Inferences; Corrections]

What should a Kupari reader make of the bread in the well? Not that it is a lie, and not that it is a fact. It is a story that the coast’s converted communities, and their neighbours, have told for at least a hundred and sixty years to explain an event that the documents explain otherwise. The documents speak of land and oxen, tithes and processions, headmen and landlords, laws and a commissary. The story speaks of a single moment of defilement after which there was no way back. Both are ways of saying that conversion closed a door. The documents say the door was closed by many hands, slowly, over two generations. The story says it was closed in one morning, by a trick. A people may keep the story as a story. A history has to tell what the documents show.

How the converts got their names

“Christian names were given to all alike,” the Gazetteer says. [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 202] The baptised received a saint’s name and, sooner or later, a Portuguese surname. How?

The Census of 1931 gave one clear mechanism: in many villages “every tenant in the village was baptized and given the surname of the owner of the village, e.g., Demonte, Pereira, Ferreira, etc.” [n34; Census 1931 Vol VIII Part I pp. 363–364] The report was written about the Christian Koli coast, but the mechanism was general. [n34; note]

The surnames survived even among families who later left the church. The Urap Agris who reverted to Hinduism in 1820 and 1828 still carried, the Gazetteer and Enthoven recorded, surnames that showed their Christian past: Gomes, Soz (Souza), Fernan, Frutad (Furtado) and Minez (Menezes). [n34; Enthoven vol I, “Urap Agris”] [n37; Gaz. XIII-I p. 117]

What about godfathers? It is natural to wonder whether a convert of standing, baptised in procession with the captain of Bassein, took his godfather’s surname, and whether that is how the godparent words and the Portuguese names travelled together into the villages. The research notes do not document such a practice for Bassein. It would be consistent with the compadre derivation of “Kupari” and with the Census’s account of names given by men of standing. But no source read for this book states it, and it remains a question, not a finding. The notes conclude that Portuguese surnames among Kuparis “are best explained by baptism under landlords, godparents and parish priests”, and that “no source ties a particular family name to a Kupari village”. [n34; Inferences, section 5]

The parishes

The converts needed churches, and the churches became the frame of village life.

The dates usually given for the parishes outside the fort come from a modern list, repeated by a community website, a Goanet post crediting the historian Prakash Shroff, and Wikipedia: Nirmal 1557, Remedi 1557, Sandor 1566, Agashi 1568, Nandakhal 1573, Papdi 1574, Pali 1595, Manickpur and Merces 1606. [n09; Vasaiker; Goanet 2020] [n04; Wikipedia: Vasai Fort] The research for this book looked for primary support for the northern dates and did not find it. The primary record shows a Franciscan orphanage at Agashi in 1535, burned in 1540, with the later church “built after the destruction” of it. There is no primary source for 1557, 1568 or 1573. [n34; Corrections] The dates may be right, but they are not yet documented in the sources read.

The Gazetteer’s list of the nine churches of Bassein in 1881 gives the northern dedications: Nirmal, Holy Cross (“St. Cruz”); Koprad, Holy Spirit; and Agashi, St James (“St. Iagos”). Nandakhal and Bhuigaon are not on it. [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 206 n.3] Nandakhal today has a Holy Spirit church, said to have been built by Franciscans in 1573 and to be the only Holy Spirit church in the diocese; the Gazetteer’s Holy Spirit church of 1881 was “at Koprad”. The research notes suggest that the later Nandakhal parish may be the same church as the 1881 Koprad one, but could not confirm it. [n34; note] [n09; Wikipedia: Holy Spirit Church Nandakhal] Nandakhal’s Wikipedia page says that Nirmal’s priest looked after Nandakhal’s worshippers before Nandakhal had its own church. [n09] The dedication of Agashi’s church is given by different sources as St James and as “Mother of Light”, the old title of Do Porto’s orphanage. [n09] A Jesuit account says the parishes of Remedy, Papdy, Palle, Merces and Porim were carved out between 1574 and 1606. [n04; via Diocese of Vasai]

The religious life of the city behind these parishes was dense. By 1634 Bassein had sixty-three friars: thirty Franciscans, fifteen Jesuits, ten Dominicans and eight Augustinians. In 1673 it had “six churches, four colleges and two convents”. [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 462] By the end of the sixteenth century there were houses of all the great orders, and “the College of the Purification, a seminary for noble children, natives of those parts, who were brought up as missionaries”. [n34; Gazetteer XIII pp. 201–202] A seminary for secular clergy had opened at Bassein about 1570. [n37; Hull vol. 1] The Jesuits brought passion plays: a society of penitents in 1551, brought to Bassein in 1552 by Father Melchior Nunes Barreto. [n34; Gazetteer XIII pp. 207–210] The Christa Purana, a “Life of Christ” in Marathi verse, was still “well known and much read” in the 1880s, in the home dialect of the Thana Son Kolis; the Gazetteer dates its publication to 1659. [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 207 n.1] The singers of the Thana churches were famous. [n34; Gaz XIII p. 207 n.2]

The parishes in 1726. A head-count by parish comes from Dean Henrique Bravo de Morais’s Notícias do Arcebispado de Goa, finished in 1726, as printed by A. B. de Bragança Pereira. [n77 §1] It gives 3,093 Christians in the city of Baçaim itself and, in the villages of the island and the mainland, these Christians by parish: Nirmal (Calvário) 2,650; Manickpur (S. Miguel) 1,876; Nandakhal (Espírito Santo) 1,648; Pale (Mãe de Deus) 1,637; Agashi (Nossa Senhora da Luz) 1,395; Papdi (Nossa Senhora da Graça) 1,348; Sandor 1,319 (S. Tomé) and 700 (Mercês); S. João Baptista 1,164; Doli (Remédios) 542; Manorá 600; Asserim 110. Beyond the island it lists Uttan 1,482, Manori 914, Dongri 867, Gorai about 650, and in Salsette Thana (two parishes, 2,050 and 476), Kurla 1,926, Versova 503 and Bandra 1,467. The printed total for the whole Province is 40,884. [n77 §1] The rows as read add up to within a few of that total. Nirmal, the old pilgrimage site where “the gentiles made their ‘washings’” in 1561, was by far the largest of the village parishes, with more Christians than Nandakhal and Pale together. The sources do not say whether the Dean counted from parish books or estimated.

The villages were exposed in a way the walled city was not. In 1634 raiders from Mughal Gujarat destroyed a Dominican monastery at Agashi. [n34; Gazetteer XIV pp. 1–2] In 1618 a cyclone unroofed the city’s largest churches. [n09; Vasaiker] In 1674 six hundred Arab raiders from Muscat plundered the churches outside the walls. [n04; Vasai Fort; source flagged unreliable] About 1690 a plague “exactly like a bubo” carried off about a third of the city’s people over several years. [n21; Gaz XIV p. 33]

And yet, by the early eighteenth century, the Christian population of Bassein had become the overwhelming majority of the district. The Gazetteer records that the population at the start of the eighteenth century was returned as 60,499: 58,181 Native Christians and 2,368 Europeans. [n21; Gaz XIV p. 33] The figure is a Portuguese return of the territory of Bassein, and its boundaries are not stated in the notes; it may not include Hindus at all, or may count only the Christian population of the Portuguese district. But it shows what the Portuguese thought they had made: a Christian country of nearly sixty thousand souls, in which the Europeans were one in twenty-five.

A son of this world: Gonsalo Garcia

Out of this world came the first saint born in India.

Gonsalo Garcia was born in Bassein in 1556 or 1557; one source gives about 1564. [n07; Wikipedia: Gonsalo Garcia; Catholic.org via search] His father was a Portuguese soldier who later stayed on as a civilian employee in the fort; his mother was a local woman, a “Canarim”, the Portuguese word for a native of the coast, which made him what the Portuguese called a mestiço. [n07; Wikipedia; DNA, 12 Jan 2014] Documents in the Portuguese national archive in Lisbon describe him as “natural de Agaçaim”, “a native of Agashi”. [n07; DNA, 12 Jan 2014] He went to the Jesuit school in the fort and served at the church of the Holy Name of Jesus, under Father Sebastião Gonsalves. The Archdiocese of Bombay says he went to Japan at about fifteen and was refused admission to the Jesuits; other accounts have him leave for Japan in 1580 and serve there as a catechist for eight years. He became a merchant in Macao, joined the Franciscans in Manila as a lay brother, and returned to Japan in 1592 with Fr Pedro Bautista as interpreter, helping to build churches, convents and hospitals. [n07; Wikipedia; Archdiocese of Bombay]

He was arrested on 8 December 1596, after the San Felipe incident, walked about six hundred miles from Kyoto to Nagasaki, and was crucified on Nishizaka hill on 5 February 1597, aged about forty, one of the Twenty-Six Martyrs of Japan. He was beatified in 1627 and canonised by Pius IX on 8 June 1862. [n07; Wikipedia] He is the patron of the Diocese of Vasai, and a church of St Gonsalo Garcia stands today at Gass, built in 1942 as a parish carved out of Nirmal. [n07; Catholics India; Wikipedia] [n09]

Is he a Kupari? The honest answer is no, and also that the question is anachronistic. His father was Portuguese and his mother a woman of the coast whose caste no source names. The 2009 community blog cites him as an example of the “minor Portuguese ancestry” that “cannot be ruled out” among Kuparis. [n07; D’Mello blog] Nothing in the sources makes his mother a Samvedi. But “natural de Agaçaim” places his birth, if the Lisbon record is right, in Agashi, the village where António do Porto had opened his orphanage twenty years earlier, at the northern edge of the Samvedi world. [n07; Inferences] He was a son of that world in the only sense the evidence allows: born where it was being made, schooled by the Jesuits who were making it, and carried by the Portuguese sea to a death on the other side of Asia, while at home the villages around his birthplace were going to the font.

The end of the Portuguese century

By the 1730s the Portuguese Province of the North was a revenue machine under siege. In 1674 More Pandit had forced the Portuguese to pay a quarter of Baçaim’s revenues. [n04; Vasai Fort] The Marathas of the Peshwa’s court were rising. In 1737 they began a campaign that took Thana and Salsette, and on 17 February 1739 Chimaji Appa laid siege to Baçaim. Mines brought down the bastion of São Sebastião, and on 16 May 1739 the garrison surrendered and marched out with honours to Bombay. [n04; Battle of Vasai] [n32; DC 148; BG 34]

The Portuguese left. All the Portuguese reinóis and descendentes “fled or were expelled … leaving only the Indian converted population behind”. [n34; Mendiratta pp. 216–218] The friars went to Goa. The village churches stood, or were looted, or burned. In the villages around Agashi, Nirmal and the garden island, the Samvedi and Pachkalshi Christians, their Portuguese surnames and godparent words, their Latin prayers and their passion plays, their tithes and their oxen, were left behind with their Hindu kin under a Hindu power.

Some of them would go back. Most of them would not. Why, and how they lived for the next two centuries, is the subject of the next chapter.
