An overview of the book, its names, and how to read it

At low tide, the creeks north of Mumbai give back their mud. The water slides off grey flats and the roots of mangroves stand up in rows, and for a few hours the line between land and sea is not a line at all but a broad, shining, uncertain margin. Then the tide turns, the Arabian Sea comes back up the channels, and the land shrinks again. On this coast the land has always been something negotiated with the water. Ancient storytellers said it was won from the sea by a hero’s throw. Geologists say something stranger: that the ground was poured out as molten rock while a continent tore apart, and that the sea has advanced and retreated across it many times since.

This book is the biography of one stretch of that coast. In the administrative language of today it is Vasai taluka, in Palghar district of Maharashtra. It is bounded on the north by the estuary of the Vaitarna and on the south by Vasai Creek, the mouth of the Ulhas, which divides it from Mumbai’s Salsette Island. To the east the land climbs into the basalt hills of Tungareshwar and Kamandurg, and beyond them stand the Western Ghats. Inside those limits lie Vasai, Sopara, Virar, Naigaon, Agashi, Arnala, Nirmal, Gass, Papdi and many other villages and towns, together with the creeks, salt pans, plantain gardens and fishing harbours that have tied them together [n01; Pandit 2017]. Around this small territory the story keeps reaching outward, to Kalyan and Thane, to Chaul and Sanjan, to Gujarat and the Deccan, and to the ports of the Indian Ocean, because this coast has rarely lived to itself.

The names

A place that has been held by many hands carries many names. Following them is a way of following its history.

The oldest town here is Sopara. Its Sanskrit name, in the Mahabharata and the Puranas, is Shurparaka. Buddhist texts in Pali call it Supparaka. The Greek geographer Ptolemy, writing in the second century CE, lists a Soupara. The first-century Greek sailing guide known as the Periplus of the Erythraean Sea names Souppara among the market towns after Barygaza (Bharuch). Jain writers used Soparaka. Arab geographers of the tenth to twelfth centuries wrote it as Subara, Sufara and Surbaraha [n22; Wikipedia: Sopara] [n15; Hobson-Jobson]. Tradition explains Shurparaka through the legend of Parashurama and his sacrificial ladle, the shurpa, though no linguistic study in the research behind this book confirms that derivation, and the book treats it as tradition [n22; Wikipedia: Sopara] [n01; Brahmanda Purana summary]. In this book the ancient and medieval town is always Sopara, with its older forms given where a source uses them.

Today most people know the place as Nallasopara, a railway station and a crowded suburb. That double name is surprisingly hard to date. The Bombay Gazetteer of 1882 still called the town simply Sopara, knew no village called Nala nearby, and complained that the town had no railway station. The earliest trace of the joint name found in the research is a railway-history article which lists an early halt as “Neela or (Nalasopara)”. The year the station opened, and where “Nala” came from, are still unresolved [n31; Gazetteer 1882; Free Press Journal 2022]. The book will introduce the name Nallasopara when the record first shows it, in the railway age, and will explain the change there.

The second great name is Vasai. Its root is usually traced to the Sanskrit vas, “to dwell”, meaning a settlement. Some writers add that it was a settlement of Jain monks, or of Jain migrants, but those glosses are speculative [n22; Wikipedia: Vasai] [n01; Shah et al. 2020]. The town’s later names follow its rulers. A Marathi account gives the sequence Vesele, Basai, Baçaim, Bajipur, Bassein, Vasai [n20; mr.wikipedia: वसई]. A Portuguese writer of 1514, Duarte Barbosa, described a good seaport called Baxay belonging to the king of Gujarat [n15; Thana Gaz. XIV]. Under the Gujarat Sultanate it was Basai. After the treaty of 1534 the Portuguese made it Baçaim, the capital of their Province of the North. After the Maratha conquest of 1739 it was renamed Bajipur, after Peshwa Baji Rao. The British wrote Bassein. After independence it became Vasai again [n22; Wikipedia: Vasai; Fort Vasai] [n15; Thana Gaz. XIV]. Each chapter uses the name of its own era, and explains each change as it happens.

Virar, at the northern end of the modern city, is said to take its name from Eka-Veera, the goddess Ekaveera of Virar Tirtha, whom the Pandavas are said to have worshipped there on their wanderings. That is a folk etymology, recorded on popular and district pages, not a proven derivation [n13; Wikipedia: Jivdani Mata; Palghar district]. Arnala, the island fort at the mouth of the Vaitarna, was “Ilha das Vacas”, the island of cows, to the Portuguese; its own name has no sourced origin [n22; Durg Bharari, via note 13]. For many villages, including Nala, Agashi, Nirmal, Papdi, Naigaon and Gass, the research found no etymology at all, and the book does not invent one [n22]. Throughout, the village is spelled Gass.

What the book covers

The book runs from the birth of the Earth to November 2026. That is an absurd span for one taluka, and it is meant to be. The ground under Vasai has a history long before any person stood on it, and the people who came later lived on that inheritance: the black basalt they quarried and built with, the laterite hills, the monsoon, the creeks that rose with the sea after the last ice age, the tidal island that ancient sailors used as a harbour.

The first three chapters are about deep time and the first people. Chapter 1 follows the making of the rock, from the planet’s formation to the great Deccan lava floods and the tearing of India’s west coast about 62.5 million years ago, when the ground of Mumbai and probably Vasai was laid down. Chapter 2 follows the land through the long weathering, the arrival of the monsoon, the ice ages, and the first archaic and modern humans of the region. Chapter 3 follows the sea’s return after the last glacial maximum, the hunter-gatherers of the coastal hills, the long millennia when farming cultures flourished elsewhere in Maharashtra and Gujarat, the origins of the Warli and the coastal communities as tradition tells them and as evidence allows, and the first mentions of Shurparaka and Aparanta in sacred literature.

From there the story becomes a history of towns and kings and ships. Chapter 5 is Shurparaka as a port of the western sea, from Ashoka’s edicts to the Roman trade. Then come the long quiet centuries and the Shilahara kings; the sultans of Gujarat and the legend of Raja Bimba; the arrival of the Portuguese and the building of Baçaim; its century and a half as the “Court of the North”, ending in the siege of 1739; Maratha Vasai; Bassein under the East India Company; the railway, the plantain gardens and the plague; the freedom struggle; the quiet republic years; and, finally, the transformation of a green belt into a city of more than a million people at the edge of a megacity. The epilogue gathers what is still unknown.

The communities of this coast are not set apart in separate chapters. The Warli and the Kathkari of the forest hills; the Son Kolis, Mangelas and Vaitis of the creeks and the sea; the Agris of the salt pans; the Bhandaris who tapped the palms; the Samvedi Brahmins and the Vadval gardeners of the wadis; the Christians, Kuparis and East Indians of the Portuguese centuries; the Jains of Sopara and Agashi; the Konkani Muslims; and the migrants of the last half century from Gujarat, Uttar Pradesh, Bihar and elsewhere: each enters the story at the moment the evidence brings them in, with their languages, temples, churches, food and work.

How to read the citations

The book is called Every Grain because of the author’s own image for what a history should be: a rice pot, with an account of every grain in it, where it was grown, who carried it, who cooked it, and which grains were lost on the way. Its subject is one coast and three places that are thought of together today: Vasai, Virar and Nallasopara, with every village around them. They are given equal weight. After the main history, Vasai, Nallasopara (with Gass) and Virar each have a part of their own, followed by the coast, a part on the Kuparis and four thread chapters; every community of the coast, Kupari, Samvedi, Vadval, Brahmin, Koli, Agri, Bhandari, Warli and the other Adivasi peoples, Muslim, Jain, Parsi and the newest migrants, appears wherever the record lets it.

Every fact in this book comes from a set of research notes compiled for it, each note covering one theme or era. After a paragraph or a key claim you will see a bracketed reference such as [n14; Pande et al. 2017]. The first part, n14, is the note number; the second is a short name for the source that note relies on. At the end of each chapter, “Sources for this chapter” lists those sources with their full web addresses, so that a reader can follow any claim back to the published paper, gazetteer, inscription edition, news report or website it came from.

The sources differ greatly in weight. Some are primary: a seventeenth-century traveller’s own words, a nineteenth-century gazetteer, an epigraphist’s reading of an inscription, a peer-reviewed dating study. Others are secondary or weak: Wikipedia and its mirrors, tourism and devotional websites, press summaries. Where a claim rests only on a weak source, the text says so. Where sources disagree, both sides are given. Where a date is a guess, it is called a guess. Words such as “tradition holds”, “legend says”, “the notes infer” and “this is not documented” are not hedging for style. They mark the difference between what is known, what is believed, and what is only possible.

Global facts that every geology textbook states, such as the age of the Earth or the basic workings of plate tectonics, are given as general knowledge and framed that way. They are the backdrop, not the local evidence.

The silences

The most important thing to know about this book is that much of it is about silence.

There is no published fossil site inside Vasai taluka. There is no published Stone Age site inside it either. No radiometric date has been measured on the basalt beneath it. For thousands of years between the last microlith-makers of the Mumbai coast and the Mauryan layer at Sopara, there is no excavated record at all. After the great age of Shurparaka there are centuries in which almost nothing is written about the town. Even in the modern era, basic facts, such as the year Nallasopara’s station opened, have slipped through the record [n14] [n23] [n01] [n31].

The book does not fill those silences with invention. When the record here goes quiet, the text says so plainly. Then it looks at what was happening nearby, at Kandivli and Manori on Salsette, at Kalyan and Thane, at Lothal and Daimabad, at Sanjan and Chaul, in Gujarat and the Deccan and across the Indian Ocean. It names how we know those things, and asks how they probably touched this coast. When evidence returns to Vasai and Sopara, the thread is picked up again.

This approach is less tidy than a confident chronicle, but it is more honest, and it has its own drama. A gap in the record is not proof that nothing happened. Often it is the sign of a question nobody has yet asked of the ground. The hills of Jivdani and Tungareshwar have not, as far as these notes could find, been searched for stone tools. The sediments of the old Sopara channel have not been cored for pollen. The Warli of the eastern hills carry in their genomes one of the deepest local ancestries yet measured in India, but no ancient DNA has been recovered from the Konkan [n14] [n01]. Some of the silences in this book may be broken by its readers.

The story begins, then, long before any name, with fire.
