Part One — Before People

The coast begins in fire and ends this part under ice-age seas. These two chapters cover almost all of time, and almost none of it has a person in it. They are here because the lava, the monsoon and the rising sea explain why a port could stand on this island and why the land looks as it does.

Period covered: 4.54 billion years ago to 60 million years ago

In this chapter. The ground under every later chapter was made in fire. This chapter follows the rock from the formation of the planet to the lava flows that built the Konkan, and explains how its age is known.

Across Vasai Creek from the old fort, on the Bhayandar side, the low hills of Uttan and Dongri run down toward the sea. Their rock is pale for this coast: rhyolite, a lava rich in silica, where most of the land around is dark basalt. Geologists have dated those rhyolites with some care. Most of their units came out of the ground between about 62.5 and 61 million years ago, at the same time as, or a little after, a moment when a piece of the Indian continent broke away and drifted off into the ocean [n14; IAVCEI-SA2025 abstract] [n23; IAVCEI-SA2025 abstract]. They are the closest dated rocks to Vasai. Nobody has published a date for the basalt inside Vasai taluka itself. But the rock under the plantain gardens, the salt pans and the railway is, by every regional measure, part of the same story: a land made of fire, at the edge of a continent being torn open [n14] [n23].

This chapter tells how that ground came to be. It is the longest chapter in time and the thinnest in local evidence. For more than four billion years there is nothing at Vasai that anyone can point to and date. What we know comes from elsewhere: from the ancient cores of the Indian continent far inland, from ocean-floor magnetism, from lavas in Madagascar and the Seychelles, from dinosaur nests in Gujarat, and from the rocks and fossils of Mumbai a few kilometres south. The silence here is not the silence of a gap in documents. It is the silence of rock that lies buried. Everything older than the lava under Vasai is hidden beneath it, several hundred metres to kilometres down [n23; IISc Journal; Purana basins perspective].

A word on how to read what follows, because this chapter mixes four kinds of statement, and the reader deserves to know which is which. Some statements rest on evidence found at or right beside Vasai, and they are called Vasai-specific or next to Vasai; there are very few of them, and the nearest are the rhyolites of Uttan and Dongri. Most rest on evidence from India, Maharashtra, Mumbai or the Konkan, and they are called regional: true of the country or the coast, and only by extension of Vasai. Some are the book’s own reasoning from that evidence, and they are called inference. And some are the common ground of the earth sciences, the facts any geology textbook states (the age of the Earth, how plates move, what a hotspot is), which the research notes for this book flagged as background they did not source [n23; Scope and labels; Gaps]. Those are marked as general knowledge. They are given because the local story makes no sense without them, and they are stated with the confidence the science itself has: firm where the science is firm, hedged where it is not. No general-knowledge statement in this chapter is offered as a finding about Vasai.

A record that begins late

The official groundwater survey of the old Thane district, which then included Vasai, gives the local geology in one line: Quaternary alluvium lying over “U. Cretaceous to L. Eocene: Deccan Trap Basalt”. In other words, a thin, young skin of river and tidal sediment rests on thick lava, and the lava’s age range spans the end of the age of dinosaurs and the early part of the age of mammals. The weathered and fractured basalt is the main source of the region’s groundwater [n01; CGWB Thane district brochure]. Every well dug in a Vasai wadi has been drawing water from that rock.

A 2021 study of the Thane–Vasai region, reported in a journal abstract, describes what lies under that skin in more detail. There are west-dipping sequences of basalt several hundred metres thick, capped by volcaniclastic sediments (layers made of volcanic fragments), and cut through by gabbros and dykes, bodies of once-molten rock that forced their way up through cracks. Gabbro crops out at Chena, on the Thane–Vasai side of the Ulhas. The study ties the whole structure to the Panvel flexure, the great bend in the lava pile along India’s west coast [n01; x-mol listing] [n23; x-mol listing]. The notes flag that the paper’s title and authors could not be verified from the page that carried the abstract; it is the closest published local geology, but it should be read with that caution.

Mumbai and Vasai lie where the Deccan sequence exposes the western limb of this flexure, which is why geologists value the area as a link for correlating the stratigraphy of the whole western Deccan [n01; Deposits Magazine, part 2]. To understand why the land bends, and why it is made of lava at all, the story has to go back to the very beginning.

The making of a planet

What follows in this section is general knowledge, stated in every geology textbook. The research notes for this book did not retrieve a specific source for it, and it is given here only as the backdrop to the local story [n23, Gaps].

The Earth formed about 4.54 billion years ago, out of the disc of dust and gas that surrounded the young Sun. For its first several hundred million years it was a hot, battered world. Its surface was repeatedly melted by impacts, and its interior separated into a dense iron core and a rocky mantle. As the surface cooled, a crust formed, and water collected into oceans. The oldest surviving fragments of the planet’s crust are tiny mineral grains, zircons, which are older than any intact rock that survives around them.

From early on, the Earth’s outer shell has been broken into plates that move over the slowly churning mantle. Where plates pull apart, molten rock rises to fill the gap and new crust is made. Where they collide, crust is crumpled into mountains or dragged back down into the mantle. Over hundreds of millions of years this motion assembles continents into great supercontinents and then breaks them apart again. Hot plumes rising from deep in the mantle can punch through a plate and pour out lava on a vast scale. Every one of those processes, plate motion, rifting, collision and plume volcanism, shaped the ground on which Vasai stands.

The local point is simple. None of the rock from the Earth’s first four billion years is visible at Vasai. If any of it lies beneath the taluka, it is buried deep under the basalt, and no borehole or geophysical study found in the research says what it is [n23; Gaps].

The Hadean: a world without a record

General knowledge throughout this subsection; not sourced in the research notes [n23; Gaps].

Geologists call the Earth’s first eon the Hadean, after the Greek underworld, and the name is apt. In its earliest phase the young planet is thought to have been struck by a body roughly the size of Mars. The collision threw a vast cloud of molten and vaporised rock into orbit, and from that debris the Moon gathered. For a time the Earth’s surface may have been an ocean of magma. As it cooled, a thin crust froze over it, cracked, sank and froze again. Volcanoes poured out water vapour, carbon dioxide and nitrogen, and the first atmosphere held no free oxygen at all.

Almost nothing of this world survives. The oldest known pieces of the Earth are not rocks but single crystals of zircon, a tough mineral that resists heat, weathering and time. Zircons from the Jack Hills of Western Australia, re-deposited in much younger sandstones, have given ages of about 4.4 billion years. Their chemistry suggests that liquid water already existed at the surface when they grew, so the oceans may be nearly as old as the planet. The oldest intact rocks known anywhere are about four billion years old, in northern Canada. India’s own oldest crust, in its southern and eastern cratons, reaches back well beyond three billion years.

The Sun, in those days, was fainter than it is now, giving perhaps a quarter less light. That the early Earth was not frozen solid is usually credited to a thick blanket of greenhouse gases. Whether the inner solar system suffered a late, intense storm of impacts around 3.9 billion years ago, the so-called Late Heavy Bombardment, is now debated; some researchers think the record of lunar craters on which it was based has been misread.

None of this can be seen at Vasai, and none of it ever will be. It belongs here only because the rock under Vasai is, in the end, made of the same mantle that cooled out of that molten world, and because the processes that began then (melting, crust-making, the cycling of water) never stopped.

The Archean: first continents and first life

General knowledge throughout this subsection; not sourced in the research notes [n23; Gaps].

From about four billion to 2.5 billion years ago, in the eon called the Archean, the first lasting continental crust appeared. It formed in small pieces, as rafts of lighter, silica-rich rock that melted out of the mantle and refused to sink back into it. Granites and the dark, folded volcanic belts geologists call greenstone belts are the signature rocks of this age, and both are found in the ancient cores of India. The Dharwar craton of Karnataka, the nearest exposed ancient block to the Konkan, is one of the classic regions of the world for Archean greenstone belts and gneisses.

Life appeared in the oceans early in this eon. The oldest widely accepted evidence takes the form of stromatolites, mounds built up layer upon layer by mats of microbes, about 3.5 billion years old; chemical traces in still older rocks are argued over. For more than two billion years after that, life on Earth was microscopic and almost entirely marine. Some of those microbes, the cyanobacteria, learned to use sunlight to split water, releasing oxygen as a waste gas. At first the oxygen was mopped up by iron dissolved in the sea, which rusted out of the water and settled on the sea floor in banded iron formations. India’s great iron-ore belts, in the old cratons of the east and south, are of this ancient kind.

On the land, there was nothing alive at all: bare rock, rain, wind and rivers.

Oxygen, ice and the long middle age

General knowledge throughout this subsection; not sourced in the research notes [n23; Gaps].

Around 2.4 billion years ago, at the start of the Proterozoic eon, oxygen began to build up in the atmosphere for the first time. Geologists call it the Great Oxidation Event. It transformed the chemistry of the planet’s surface: iron in rocks rusted, new minerals formed, and the stage was set, very slowly, for complex life. The next billion and a half years are sometimes called the “boring billion” because so little appears to change in the fossil record, but they were the years in which India’s cratons were welded into larger continents, which the next section describes.

Toward the end of the Proterozoic, between about 720 and 635 million years ago, the Earth went through the most extreme ice ages it has known. In the strongest version of the theory, the “Snowball Earth”, ice reached the tropics and the oceans were frozen over almost entirely. After the thaw, life changed. The strange soft-bodied Ediacaran organisms appeared, and then, from about 539 million years ago, the burst of animal evolution known as the Cambrian explosion filled the seas with creatures that had shells, eyes and limbs. The ancestors of every animal that would later live on the Konkan coast, from the frogs of Worli to the people of Sopara, trace their lineages to that moment.

All of this happened while the crust that would one day lie beneath Vasai was part of a continent far from any sea it now faces. What that continent was, and how it was assembled, is the subject of the next part of the story.

The old heart of India

India’s oldest rocks lie far from this coast, in ancient blocks of continental crust called cratons. They were assembled over billions of years, and geologists still argue about how.

A craton, in the general vocabulary of geology, is a piece of continent that has been stable for a very long time: thick, cold, buoyant crust with a deep keel of mantle beneath it, which has survived the opening and closing of oceans around it without being broken up or remelted. Cratons are the old hearts of continents, and the belts of folded, metamorphosed rock between them, the “mobile belts” or “fold belts”, mark where they once collided [general knowledge]. India, on the usual reckoning, contains five main cratons. In the north are the Aravalli (sometimes called the Marwar–Aravalli or Bundelkhand–Aravalli block) and the Bundelkhand. In the south and east are the Dharwar, under most of Karnataka and parts of Andhra Pradesh and Goa; the Bastar, under Chhattisgarh and southern Odisha; and the Singhbhum, under Jharkhand and northern Odisha [n23; IISc Journal; Univ. Melbourne repository paper]. None of them reaches the surface anywhere near Vasai.

One synthesis holds that the northern Indian block, made of the Aravalli and Bundelkhand cratons, was amalgamated by about 3.3 billion years ago. Other models treat them as a single proto-continent from the start, and the debate is open [n23; IISc Journal; Univ. Melbourne repository paper]. The southern block, made of the Singhbhum, Bastar and Dharwar cratons, is said in one account to have come together by about 2.5 billion years ago. Another paper argues that the pieces split and rejoined along the western margin of the Bastar craton about 1.6 billion years ago [n23; IISc Journal; Univ. Melbourne repository paper].

A palaeomagnetic study of the Dharwar craton, published in 2023, supports the view that the Dharwar, Singhbhum and Bastar cratons were united as a Southern India Block by at least 1.79 billion years ago. That places them in the era of the supercontinent geologists call Columbia, or Nuna, between about 1.8 and 1.6 billion years ago [n23; NSF PAR record]. By about one billion years ago, according to one source, the Indian cratons were stitched together along three great belts: the Aravalli-Delhi Fold Belt, the Central Indian Tectonic Zone and the Eastern Ghats Mobile Belt, during the assembly of the next supercontinent, Rodinia. How India fitted into Columbia and Rodinia is still debated [n23; IISc Journal].

During the long Proterozoic eon, basins opened and closed on the margins of the Dharwar craton. Indian geologists call them the “Purana” basins, from the Sanskrit word for “ancient”. The sources note that, farther north, these basins and the cratons beside them are concealed beneath the Deccan basalts [n23; Purana basins perspective; IISc Journal]. That concealment is the reason the older story is invisible at Vasai.

It is worth seeing what “concealed” means on a map. Draw a line from the Dharwar country of northern Karnataka north-west toward Mumbai, and the ancient rocks of the craton and its basins disappear, around the Krishna and Bhima valleys, under the southern edge of the Deccan lavas. In general geological accounts, two of the Purana basins on the north of the Dharwar craton, the Kaladgi and the Bhima, vanish under the basalt in just that way, which is how geologists know that the lava pile rests directly on the old continent there [general knowledge; the basin names are not in the research notes]. From that point north and west, for hundreds of kilometres, there is only lava at the surface. The Konkan, Mumbai and Vasai lie deep inside that blanket, at its far western edge.

What the old continent was doing

The most useful way to think about these billions of years, for a book about one coast, is as a long cycle of assembly and dispersal. In the general account of the earth sciences, continents drift together into a supercontinent every few hundred million years, stay joined for a time, and then rift apart; geologists call it the supercontinent cycle [general knowledge]. India’s cratons took part in at least three such cycles before the one that matters most for Vasai.

The first for which the notes give evidence is Columbia, also called Nuna, between about 1.8 and 1.6 billion years ago. The 2023 palaeomagnetic study of the Dharwar craton, which measured the magnetic directions frozen into its rocks, supports a Southern India Block, Dharwar, Bastar and Singhbhum together, by at least 1.79 billion years ago, inside that supercontinent [n23; NSF PAR record]. The second is Rodinia, around one billion years ago, during whose assembly India’s cratons were stitched together along the Aravalli-Delhi Fold Belt, the Central Indian Tectonic Zone and the Eastern Ghats Mobile Belt, according to one source [n23; IISc Journal]. The Central Indian Tectonic Zone, which runs roughly east–west across the middle of the country near the line of the Narmada and Son, is in this reading the great seam where northern India and southern India were welded together [n23; IISc Journal]. Where Vasai sits relative to that seam is not stated in any source the research found; it lies far to its south-west, and well inside the lava cover [n23; Gaps].

Rodinia, in turn, broke apart, in general accounts between about 800 and 700 million years ago [general knowledge]. Its fragments, including the pieces that would become India, drifted, collided again and, by the end of the Precambrian, came together in a new configuration. That configuration was Gondwana, and India would stay in it for some four hundred million years.

The research notes are explicit that the fit of India within Columbia and Rodinia is debated, that the dates of the cratons’ amalgamation differ between papers by more than a billion years in one case, and that no numeric latitude for India’s position through these ages was retrieved [n23; IISc Journal; Gaps]. The story told here is a framework, not a settled history.

The Dharwar is the nearest exposed craton to this coast, and it is tempting to imagine that Dharwar-type crust continues westward under the lavas of the north Konkan. The research notes are explicit that this is a guess, not a finding. No source found says which craton block, if any, lies directly beneath Vasai [n23; Inferences and Gaps].

So the first four billion years of this book’s story are, locally, a sealed room. What happened in it can be reconstructed only from India as a whole.

Gondwana

Again, this section begins with general knowledge that the research notes did not source, and it is offered as framing only [n23, Gaps].

Toward the end of the Precambrian, roughly 550 to 500 million years ago in textbook accounts, the southern continents, Africa, South America, Antarctica, Australia, Madagascar and India, were joined into a single great landmass, Gondwana. India lay deep in the southern hemisphere, pressed against Madagascar and Africa on one side and Antarctica and Australia on another. For hundreds of millions of years the future site of Vasai was part of the interior or margin of that supercontinent, far from any sea it would later face. Life crawled out of the oceans and spread across the land; forests grew and fell; reptiles rose; the first dinosaurs appeared. None of it left a trace that can be seen at Vasai, because whatever surface existed then is now buried under the Deccan lavas [n14; Inferences].

A supercontinent named after central India

General knowledge throughout this subsection; not sourced in the research notes [n23; Gaps].

There is a pleasing irony in the name. “Gondwana” means the land, or forest, of the Gonds, the people of the hill country of central India, and it was the British geologists of the Geological Survey of India who first used it in the nineteenth century, for a thick sequence of river and lake sediments, with coal seams and fossil plants, that they mapped in the basins of the Damodar, the Son, the Mahanadi and the Godavari. When the same distinctive rocks and the same fossil plants turned up in South Africa, South America, Antarctica and Australia, the Austrian geologist Eduard Suess borrowed the Indian name for the whole lost southern continent. A word from the forests of the Gond country became the name of half the ancient world.

The fossil that tied those continents together was a plant, Glossopteris, a seed-bearing tree with tongue-shaped leaves. Its leaves are found in the coal-bearing rocks of India and in the same kinds of rocks on every other southern continent. Long before anyone understood plate tectonics, that shared flora was one of the strongest arguments that the southern lands had once been joined.

India’s Gondwana coal, which today fuels much of the country’s electricity, is the compressed remains of the swamp forests of that age, laid down in rift basins inside the supercontinent between roughly 300 and 250 million years ago. None of those basins lies near the Konkan. If any Gondwana sediments exist under the Deccan lavas near the west coast, no source found for this book says so.

Ice in the south

General knowledge; not sourced in the research notes [n23; Gaps].

For much of the Palaeozoic era, Gondwana lay over or near the South Pole, and India sat in high southern latitudes. Around 300 million years ago, in the late Carboniferous and early Permian, great ice sheets spread across the supercontinent. In India their traces are boulder beds called the Talchir tillites, after a place in Odisha: jumbles of stones of every size, dumped by melting ice, sometimes resting on bedrock that the ice scratched as it passed. The cold, high-latitude world of Glossopteris forests and coal swamps followed the retreat of that ice.

The research notes did not retrieve a numeric palaeolatitude for India in this age, or for Vasai’s site at any time; one note names a figure of about 30 degrees south at 100 million years ago only as an example of what was not found [n23; Gaps]. What can be said in general terms is that the ground of the future Konkan spent hundreds of millions of years in cold or temperate southern latitudes, far from the tropics, before the long voyage north that this chapter goes on to describe.

Pangaea, the great dying and the first dinosaurs

General knowledge; not sourced in the research notes [n23; Gaps].

By about 300 million years ago Gondwana had joined the northern continents to form a single world-continent, Pangaea, stretching from pole to pole, with one great ocean around it. At the end of the Permian period, about 252 million years ago, life suffered the worst mass extinction in the planet’s history. Most species in the sea and a large share of those on land disappeared. The leading explanation is a vast outpouring of lava in Siberia, the Siberian Traps, whose gases poisoned and overheated the world. It is worth holding that event in mind, because the end of this chapter’s story, the Deccan, is the same kind of catastrophe on a somewhat smaller scale.

From the survivors, in the Triassic period that followed, came the first dinosaurs, the first mammals and the ancestors of crocodiles and pterosaurs. India has Triassic and Jurassic fossil beds of its own, in the Pranhita–Godavari valley of Telangana and Maharashtra’s far east, where some of the earliest long-necked sauropod dinosaurs have been found. Those sites lie more than six hundred kilometres east of Vasai. On the Konkan, every surface of that age is buried.

The breakup, stage by stage

The breaking of Gondwana was not one event but a sequence, spread over more than a hundred million years. In outline, the research notes and general accounts give the following stages.

First, in general accounts, East Gondwana (Antarctica, Australia, India and Madagascar together) began to separate from West Gondwana (Africa and South America) around 180 million years ago, in the Jurassic period. The research notes flag this figure as background for which they retrieved no source [n23; Gaps]. Huge outpourings of lava accompanied it in southern Africa and Antarctica [general knowledge].

Second, India, still joined to Madagascar, began to pull away from Antarctica and Australia. The sources available to this book place India’s separation from the other Gondwana fragments somewhere between about 140 and 100 million years ago, from a Wikipedia mirror and a review paper [n23; Indian Plate (Wikipedia mirror); Typeset.io PDF]. In general accounts, the eastern margin of India was rifted from Antarctica in the Early Cretaceous, and the Rajmahal Traps of Jharkhand and West Bengal, lavas of about 118 million years ago, together with the Kerguelen Plateau in the southern Indian Ocean, are commonly attributed to the Kerguelen hotspot acting at that stage [general knowledge; the Rajmahal and Kerguelen Plateau attributions are not sourced in the notes]. The research notes do record that one source links the later India–Madagascar breakup site to the Jurassic position of the Kerguelen and Crozet hotspots [n23; Interaction of mantle plume with India].

Third, at about 88 million years ago, India and the Seychelles together broke from Madagascar, the event the next section describes, with the best primary dating the notes contain [n14; Storey et al.] [n23; Storey et al.].

Fourth, and last, at about 62.5 million years ago by the dated study (about 66 million in another account), the Seychelles and the Laxmi Ridge broke from India’s western flank, and Vasai’s own rock was born in that tearing [n14; Pande et al. 2017; Seychelles microcontinent (Wikipedia)].

Each stage moved India’s coastlines. Each stage changed which neighbours lay against the ground that would become the Konkan. At first it was deep inside a supercontinent; then it was on the inner side of India, facing Madagascar; then it faced the Seychelles across a narrow rift; and only at the very end did it face open ocean.

To return to the sources’ own wording on the second stage: when India separated from the other Gondwana fragments is given in the sources available to this book as somewhere between about 140 and 100 million years ago. Those figures come from secondary material, a Wikipedia mirror and a review paper, and the notes flag that a primary reference on the Indian plate’s voyage was identified but not read [n23; Indian Plate (Wikipedia mirror); Typeset.io PDF; Texas Tech record]. What is clear is that, by the later Cretaceous, India and Madagascar still lay together, and the Seychelles, a small sliver of continent, lay against India’s western flank. That flank would one day be the Konkan coast.

The island continent

The decisive break came at about 88 million years ago. Thick lavas at Volcan de l’Androy, in southern Madagascar, mark the focus of the Marion hotspot at about that date, and that hotspot is credited with helping to split Madagascar from India [n14; Storey et al., Southampton eprint]. A broader window, from a reconstruction of seafloor spreading, puts the separation of Seychelles-and-India from Madagascar between 84 and 95 million years ago, with spreading moving from south to north between 94 and 84 million years ago [n14; Seychelles microcontinent (Wikipedia)] [n23; Storey et al.; Seychelles microcontinent]. Another source links the breakup site to the earlier position of the Kerguelen and Crozet hotspots [n23; Interaction of mantle plume with India].

From that moment India was an island continent, carrying the Seychelles on its western side, and it began to move north across the ocean. Its motion is recorded in the magnetic stripes of the Indian Ocean floor and in the trails that hotspots burned into the moving plate, such as the long Ninetyeast Ridge [n23; Northward motion paper (typeset.io)]. Between about 70 and 50 million years ago, India moved at among the highest plate speeds on record, up to about 16 centimetres a year. One source links that speed, only as a possibility, to activity of the Réunion plume [n14; VLIZ Marine World Heritage] [n23; VLIZ].

Even before the great floods of lava, the western margin was stirring. Offshore, in the Bombay High region, drilled basalts have given ages ranging from 69 to 61 million years, and one older basalt has given about 79 million years, a hint of earlier rifting off the west coast [n14; Goldschmidt 2023 abstract] [n23; Goldschmidt 2023]. The sea floor west of Vasai, in other words, is underlain by lava too.

As India raced north, its latitude changed. The research notes did not find a numeric palaeolatitude for Vasai’s site through time, only the statement that, by the time of the great lavas, India’s northward race had carried it into the tropics [n23; Inferences and Gaps]. The future Konkan was moving from the cold southern ocean toward the warm waters it borders today.

In general reconstructions, India in the mid-Cretaceous lay somewhere around thirty degrees or more south of the equator, roughly where Madagascar’s southern tip or South Africa’s coast lie today, and it crossed the equator around the start of the Eocene, about fifty million years ago [general knowledge; the notes did not retrieve a numeric palaeolatitude and recommend reading one off the northward-motion paper before print, n23; Inferences]. On that reckoning, the lava that became Vasai’s ground erupted somewhere in the southern tropics, perhaps around the latitude of Mauritius today, and the land has travelled more than two thousand kilometres north since it cooled. That figure is a rough, general one, and it is given only to make the scale of the journey concrete.

Two hotspots: Marion and Réunion

Two names recur in this part of the story, and they need explaining.

A hotspot, in the general account, is a place where an unusually hot column of rock, a mantle plume, rises from deep inside the Earth and melts as it nears the surface. Because the plume is thought to be fixed, or nearly so, relative to the deep mantle, while the plates above it move, a hotspot leaves a trail: a line of volcanoes that grows older the farther it lies from the present plume. The Hawaiian Islands are the textbook example. When a plume first arrives under a plate, its broad, mushroom-shaped head can melt on an enormous scale and produce a flood basalt province in a geologically short time; the long, narrow tail that follows makes the smaller chain of volcanoes [general knowledge].

The Marion hotspot today lies in the far southern Indian Ocean, near the Prince Edward Islands, on or beside the Southwest Indian Ridge [general knowledge]. The notes credit it, through the thick lavas of Volcan de l’Androy in southern Madagascar at about 88 million years ago, with helping to split Madagascar from India [n14; Storey et al.]. In general accounts, related lavas of about that age also occur on India’s south-western margin, around St Mary’s Islands off the Karnataka coast near Udupi, where columns of basalt and rhyolite of Late Cretaceous age stand out of the sea [general knowledge; not in the notes].

The Réunion hotspot today lies under the island of Réunion, east of Madagascar, whose volcano, Piton de la Fournaise, is one of the most active in the world [general knowledge]. The standard reconstruction, which the notes record as standard but unchecked, traces its trail backwards from Réunion through Mauritius and the Mascarene Plateau, across to the Chagos Bank, up the line of the Maldives and the Lakshadweep islands, and finally to the Deccan Traps, which it identifies as the outpouring of the plume’s head when it first reached the surface under western India [n23; Gaps] [general knowledge for the trail]. One source in the notes links India’s very fast motion between 70 and 50 million years ago to Réunion plume activity, but only as a possibility [n14; VLIZ] [n23; VLIZ].

The two names, then, mark two different phases: Marion for the splitting of Madagascar from India, about 88 million years ago; Réunion for the floods of lava that built the Deccan, about 66 to 61 million years ago. The research notes stress that their sources use the names in that way, for different phases, and that no source they retrieved reconciles the two into a single history [n23; Gaps]. Some geologists have argued that the west coast’s story involves both, in sequence, as India moved north over one plume and then the other; this book records that only as a general framing, not as a finding.

If the standard view is right, there is a strange and beautiful consequence. The fire that made Vasai’s ground is still burning. The same plume, now under Réunion, erupts every few years, some four thousand kilometres to the south-west, and the Maldives and Lakshadweep, the coral islands of the same Arabian Sea that the sailors of this coast would one day come to know, sit on the ridge it built as India slid north across it [general knowledge, conditional on the standard view].

Dinosaurs in India, but not here

India in the last part of the Cretaceous was dinosaur country. The evidence lies in a set of sediments called the Lameta Formation, which crops out in Gujarat, Madhya Pradesh and eastern Maharashtra, inland and north of the Konkan.

The most famous nesting ground is at Rahioli, near Balasinor in Gujarat. There, the story goes, quarry workers at the ACC cement quarry gave limestone “structures” to the palaeontologist Dhananjay Mohabey’s team, who recognised them as dinosaur eggs [n14; Rajasaurus (Wikipedia)]. Dinosaur clutches are also known from the Nand–Dongargaon Basin in Chandrapur district, Maharashtra, and eggs at Balasinor and Jabalpur occur mainly in the Lower Limestone [n14; PMC article (Lameta eggs)]. In 2003 Wilson and colleagues described Rajasaurus narmadensis, an abelisaurid, a big-headed predatory theropod; Wikipedia ties it to the Balasinor and Rahioli area, though the notes did not fetch a primary source for its exact type locality [n14; Rajasaurus (Wikipedia); Gaps].

At Pisdura, in Maharashtra, the Lameta beds yielded titanosaur material: tail vertebrae, teeth and limb pieces once assigned to “Titanosaurus indicus”, a genus now regarded as dubious. Eggs from Pisdura and Nand–Dongargaon have only a parataxonomic classification, a naming system for eggs that does not tie them to particular species of dinosaur [n14; Lameta Formation summary (Wikipedia mirror)]. In the Narmada valley, in Dhar district of Madhya Pradesh, titanosaur nests have produced eggs of six species, which suggests that the long-necked giants were more varied in India than once thought. The Natural History Museum in London reported these as fossilised nests of the world’s largest dinosaurs in 2023 [n14; Down To Earth; Natural History Museum 2023].

What the Lameta beds are

General knowledge in this subsection except where cited.

The Lameta Formation takes its name from Lameta Ghat on the Narmada near Jabalpur, where it was first described in the nineteenth century [general knowledge]. It is a thin sequence, often only a few tens of metres thick, of sandstones, clays and limestones laid down by rivers, in lakes and on floodplains, in the last few million years of the Cretaceous. Its key relationship, for this book, is its position: in most places it lies directly beneath the Deccan lavas, or beneath the lowest of them, which is why geologists sometimes call such beds “infratrappean”. The dinosaurs lived on the land surface just before the floods of fire buried it. Where the edge of the lava pile has been worn back by erosion, along the Narmada valley, in central Gujarat and in Vidarbha, that buried surface is exposed again, and its eggs and bones come to light [general knowledge for the stratigraphy; the nesting localities are from n14; PMC article (Lameta eggs)].

The picture the Lameta gives of India’s last dinosaur age is a rich one. Long-necked titanosaurs nested in colonies on warm, sandy floodplains, laying clutches of round eggs in shallow pits, apparently returning to the same grounds year after year; the nests in Dhar district show that several kinds of titanosaur were doing this [n14; Down To Earth; Natural History Museum 2023]. Predatory abelisaurids such as Rajasaurus, heavy-headed, short-armed hunters related to the abelisaurids of Madagascar and South America, preyed on them [n14; Rajasaurus (Wikipedia)] [general knowledge for the family resemblance]. That family resemblance is itself a trace of Gondwana: India’s late dinosaurs were most closely related to those of the other southern continents it had so recently left. In general accounts, a fossil from the Kheda district of Gujarat even preserves a snake, named Sanajeh indicus, coiled around a crushed titanosaur egg beside a hatchling, as if caught in the act of raiding a nest when the ground buried it [general knowledge; not in the notes].

The research notes keep two cautions on this material. The Pisdura and Nand–Dongargaon eggs can be classified only by the parataxonomic system for eggs, not tied to named species, and Titanosaurus, the name once given to the Pisdura bones, is now considered a doubtful genus [n14; Lameta Formation summary (Wikipedia mirror)]. And the exact type locality of Rajasaurus, the Balasinor–Rahioli area in the Wikipedia account, was not checked against a primary source [n14; Gaps].

It would be pleasing to put a dinosaur on the beach at Arnala. The evidence does not allow it. No dinosaur fossil is known from the Mumbai–Thane–Vasai area [n14; Takeaway] [n23; PMC, Lameta eggs]. Before the lavas of the coast poured out, the land here was part of the same Indian landmass that dinosaurs roamed, and they very probably walked it. But that surface, if it survives at all, lies buried under kilometres of basalt [n14; Inferences]. And, as the rest of this chapter shows, the rock that does lie at the surface here was born just after the dinosaurs had gone.

Why no dinosaur has been found near Mumbai

The absence is worth explaining properly, because it is not an accident of who has looked. It follows from the shape of the rock, and the explanation is the book’s inference from the sources, built step by step.

First, the dinosaurs’ land surface lies at the bottom of the Deccan pile. The Lameta beds, with their eggs and bones, sit beneath the lavas, so they can be seen only where the edge of the pile has been eroded back to expose its base [n14; PMC article (Lameta eggs)] [general knowledge for the infratrappean position]. In Gujarat, Madhya Pradesh and Vidarbha, the lava cover is thin or has been stripped at its margins, and the old surface reappears.

Second, the Konkan coast is at the opposite extreme. Here, west of the Panvel flexure, the lavas dip toward the sea, and the flows at the surface are among the youngest in the whole province: the Mumbai sequence at about 62.5 million years and the Uttan rhyolites at 62.5 to 61 million, both after the extinction [n14; Pande et al. 2017; IAVCEI-SA2025 abstract]. Any Cretaceous land surface under Vasai would lie beneath the entire thickness of those flows and of the older pile beneath them, buried, in the notes’ phrase, under several kilometres of basalt [n14; Inferences].

Third, the beds that do exist between the coastal flows, the intertrappeans of Worli and Andheri, are of the right kind to hold fossils, and they do hold them. But their age, Danian, places them in the world after the dinosaurs, so the animals they hold are frogs, turtles, crocodilians and ostracods, not dinosaurs [n14; Sheth & Pande 2014; Pande et al. 2017].

Put together, the reasoning is this: a dinosaur fossil near Mumbai would require a Cretaceous sedimentary layer at or near the surface, and on this coast the geometry of the lava pile puts every such layer far out of reach. The absence of dinosaurs here is a fact about depth, not about the animals. They very probably walked over the ground that now lies beneath Vasai. The ground they walked has simply been buried by younger fire, and then by the sea [n14; Takeaway; Inferences].

It is worth adding one regional observation from the notes. Even the intertrappean beds that might preserve life after the dinosaurs have not been found inside Vasai–Virar or Palghar, and their presence at Uttan, Gorai or Vasai has not been confirmed [n14; Gaps]. A palaeontologist walking the quarries and road cuttings of the taluka would be looking at the right rocks for frogs and turtles. No such search has been published.

The floods of fire

Somewhere beneath the moving Indian plate, a plume of hot mantle rock rose and began to melt. The standard account attributes the Deccan volcanism to the Réunion hotspot. The notes for this book flag that as the standard view but unchecked: their sources use “Marion” for the Madagascar breakup and “Réunion” for the Deccan, and no source retrieved reconciles the two phases [n23; Gaps].

What is not in doubt is the result. Around 66 to 65 million years ago, lava poured out across western and central India in one of the largest volcanic episodes in the planet’s history. It came not from tall cones but from fissures, cracks in the ground through which basalt welled up and spread in sheets, flow upon flow, building a plateau that later erosion would carve into the step-like hills of the Deccan. The Deccan flood basalt province formed in that interval, before the Seychelles broke away from India [n14; Pande et al. 2017].

The scale of it

General knowledge in this subsection; the research notes did not source these figures [n23; Gaps].

The numbers are hard to hold in the mind. The Deccan lavas today cover something like half a million square kilometres of western and central India, an area larger than the state of Maharashtra, and before erosion and the loss of the western margin to the sea they probably covered two or three times as much. Near the Western Ghats the pile is more than two kilometres thick. Estimates of the total volume of lava run to around a million cubic kilometres. Individual flows travelled astonishing distances; some lavas of Deccan type appear far to the east, near the mouth of the Godavari at Rajahmundry, close to the Bay of Bengal.

The word “trap” itself comes from the Swedish trappa, a staircase. When the flows cooled, each one formed a hard, resistant core with a softer, weathered, often bubbly top. Erosion strips the soft layers faster than the hard ones, so that a hillside cut into a pile of flows rises in steps. Anyone who has looked east from Vasai at the flat-topped, terraced shoulders of the Sahyadri, or driven up the Thal or Bhor ghats, has seen the staircase the word describes.

Most of the eruptions were not explosive. Basalt is runny when it is hot, and it welled up quietly through long fissures, spreading out in sheets that could cover thousands of square kilometres before they cooled. Feeding those fissures were swarms of dykes, near-vertical walls of once-molten rock that cut through the older flows; in general accounts, dyke swarms of this kind are well known along the west coast and in the Narmada–Tapi region, and the Thane–Vasai sequence described above is “cut through by gabbros and dykes” of just this kind [n01; x-mol listing] [general knowledge for the regional dyke swarms].

The named layers of the pile

Background knowledge; the research notes flag the named formations and their phases as not sourced [n23; Gaps].

Geologists who have walked the Western Ghats flow by flow, from the base of the pile near Igatpuri to the summits of Mahabaleshwar, have divided it into a stack of chemically distinct formations, grouped into three subgroups: from the bottom up, the Kalsubai, the Lonavala and the Wai. The names come from places on the Ghats: Kalsubai, the highest peak in Maharashtra, north-east of Vasai; Lonavala on the Bhor Ghat road; Wai in Satara district. Within them are formations named after Jawhar, Igatpuri, Neral, Thakurvadi, Bhimashankar, Khandala, Bushe, Poladpur, Ambenali, Mahabaleshwar and others. Jawhar, the lowest formation in some schemes, is named after the hill town in Palghar district, the same district as Vasai, and Igatpuri lies just over the Ghats from it. The oldest exposed Deccan flows of the Western Ghats sequence therefore crop out in the hills north-east of the Vasai plain, while the youngest lie toward the south and on the plateau.

The research notes record one point about all of this that does have a source: the lavas of Mumbai, and by inference of Vasai, are younger than the main pile that forms the Western Ghats [n23; Gaps]. In general accounts, that follows from the westward dip of the flows across the Panvel flexure: the Ghats sequence dips down toward the coast and disappears beneath it, and the coastal flows of Mumbai and Salsette lie on top, as a separate and later chapter of eruption [general knowledge; consistent with n14; Pande et al. 2017 and n23; Sheth and Pande 2014].

Its timing sits right across one of the great turning points in the history of life: the mass extinction at the end of the Cretaceous, about 66 million years ago, when the non-avian dinosaurs vanished along with much else. At about the same time, an asteroid struck the Earth at Chicxulub, in what is now Mexico. Whether the impact or the volcanism mattered more is still debated [n23; National Geographic].

Two studies published in Science in 2019 show how fine the argument has become. Schoene and colleagues, dating zircon crystals by uranium–lead methods, found that the Deccan erupted in four large pulses, each lasting about 100,000 years, and that the first began tens of thousands of years before the Chicxulub impact. Sprain and colleagues, using argon–argon dating, found that more than three-quarters of the lava erupted within about 600,000 years after the extinction. The two teams agree in broad strokes on the timing but disagree on the pulses, and on what caused what [n14; Science News; National Geographic; EurekAlert release]. Volcanic gases, carbon dioxide and sulphur, would have warmed and poisoned the climate; the impact would have darkened the sky. The debate is over the balance between them.

The end of the Cretaceous world

General knowledge in this subsection except where cited.

The case for the impact rests on a thin layer of clay found at the boundary between Cretaceous and Paleogene rocks around the world, enriched in iridium, a metal rare in the Earth’s crust but common in asteroids. Luis and Walter Alvarez and their colleagues proposed in 1980 that the iridium had come from a giant impact, and in the early 1990s the buried crater, some 180 kilometres across, was identified under the Yucatán peninsula of Mexico, at Chicxulub. Grains of shocked quartz and tiny glass spherules in the boundary layer, scattered across the globe, tell the same story. The case for the volcanoes rests on the Deccan itself: on the timing that the 2019 studies were refining, and on the sheer volume of gas that a million cubic kilometres of basalt would have released [n14; Science News; National Geographic] [general knowledge for the iridium and crater evidence].

Whatever the balance, the result is not in doubt. Roughly three-quarters of the plant and animal species on Earth disappeared. All the non-avian dinosaurs died, together with the pterosaurs, the great marine reptiles and the coiled ammonites of the seas. Among the survivors were small mammals, birds (the one lineage of dinosaurs that lived on), crocodilians, turtles, frogs, lizards, snakes and many fish and insects. The survivors inherited an emptied world. Within ten million years, mammals had radiated into most of the shapes that would fill the land, from bats to the ancestors of whales and elephants.

India’s position in this story is special. It was the one continent where the volcanic cause was erupting on the spot. The dinosaurs of the Lameta beds lived on the very plate that was splitting open, and their nesting grounds were being buried by the first flows while, on the other side of the planet, the impactor approached [n14; PMC article (Lameta eggs); Science News]. The frogs and turtles of Worli and Andheri, a few million years later, were among the survivors of that double catastrophe [n14; Sheth & Pande 2014; Pande et al. 2017].

To return to the named layers described above, which the notes flag as background knowledge not sourced in this research: what matters for Vasai is the one point the sources do make, that the flows of Mumbai, and by inference of Vasai, are younger than the main pile of lava that forms the Western Ghats [n23; Gaps]. The main Deccan phase, at about 66 to 65 million years ago, built the plateau. The coast had its own, later fire, which the next section describes.

The tearing of the coast

The lava under Vasai belongs to a later chapter of the Deccan story: the moment when the western edge of India was ripped away.

When India separated from Madagascar, the Seychelles block and a submerged ridge of continental crust called the Laxmi Ridge stayed attached to India’s west side. Then they, too, tore off. There is a conflict in the sources about when. Wikipedia gives the final severance of the Seychelles from India at about 66 million years ago, with the opening of the Carlsberg Ridge, a spreading centre in the Indian Ocean. Argon-dating work from IIT Bombay puts the India–Laxmi Ridge–Seychelles breakup at 62.5 million years ago, with Deccan magmatism continuing before, during and after the breakup, and lasting until about 61 million years ago in Mumbai and the Seychelles [n14; Seychelles microcontinent (Wikipedia); Pande et al. 2017]. This book follows the dated study, and gives the other figure for comparison. A primary source for a “63 million years” figure sometimes repeated was not found [n14; Gaps].

The pieces that left

General knowledge in this subsection except where cited.

The two fragments that tore away from this coast have had very different fates, and both are worth knowing, because in a real sense they are pieces of the same ground as Vasai.

The Laxmi Ridge never got far. It is a long, submerged ridge in the eastern Arabian Sea, lying a few hundred kilometres off the coast of Gujarat and Maharashtra, under two to three kilometres of water. Marine geophysicists have argued for decades about whether it is thinned continental crust, a sliver of old India, or something more volcanic; the notes treat it, following their sources, as part of the block that rifted from India together with the Seychelles at about 62.5 million years ago [n14; Pande et al. 2017]. Between the Laxmi Ridge and the Indian shelf lies a basin, the Laxmi Basin, and in general accounts a failed rift, the Gop Rift, lies west of the Saurashtra coast: places where the crust stretched and thinned without quite breaking through [general knowledge]. The coast of the north Konkan, in other words, does not end at the shoreline. Its broken edge continues far out under the sea, in steps and slivers.

The Seychelles went much farther. When the spreading ridge jumped, a new mid-ocean ridge, the Carlsberg Ridge, opened between India and the Seychelles–Laxmi block, and over the following tens of millions of years the Seychelles were carried away to the south-west, to their present position some three thousand kilometres from Mumbai [n14; Seychelles microcontinent (Wikipedia)] [general knowledge for the distance]. The granite islands of the Seychelles, Mahé and Praslin and their neighbours, are among the very few granite islands in the middle of an ocean anywhere. In general accounts their granites are about 750 million years old, far older than the Deccan, a fragment of the ancient continental crust that once lay beside and beneath western India. And on those islands, too, there are rocks of the same moment as Mumbai’s: the notes record Deccan-related magmatism continuing to about 61 million years ago “in Mumbai and the Seychelles” [n14; Pande et al. 2017].

So a person standing on the Uttan shore, looking out over the Arabian Sea, faces a scene with a hidden logic. Somewhere out there under the water lies the Laxmi Ridge; much farther, beyond the horizon and beyond the Carlsberg Ridge, the Seychelles. Both were once against this coast. The ocean between was made, crust and all, in the time since they left.

As the crust stretched and broke, the edge of India sagged toward the opening ocean. The lava pile bent down to the west along a great fold, a monocline, which geologists call the Panvel flexure, after the town of Panvel across the harbour from Mumbai. West of the flexure axis the basalts dip seaward. Vasai lies on that seaward-dipping coastal belt [n14; Inferences] [n23; Sheth and Pande 2014]. The tilt of those flows is the first great fact of Vasai’s landscape: the ground leans toward the sea, because the continent once broke here.

When the flexure formed is debated. Sheth and Pande, in 2014, argued that it had formed by 65 to 64 million years ago. A later study by the same group concluded that it formed, or kept forming, after 63 million years ago [n14; Sheth & Pande 2014; ICAR/KRISHI record] [n23; Sheth and Pande 2014; ICAR/KRISHI record]. For a broader view of how flood basalts, rifted margins and such flexures relate, the notes point to a 2020 review in the journal Geosciences, whose authorship was not checked [n14; MDPI Geosciences 2020].

What a flexure is, and where it runs

A monocline is the simplest kind of fold: a step in otherwise flat-lying layers, where the beds bend down from one level to another, like a rug draped over the edge of a stair [general knowledge]. The Panvel flexure is such a step on a continental scale. East of its axis, on the Deccan plateau, the lava flows lie nearly flat, as they were laid down. Along the axis they bend. West of it they dip toward the sea, more steeply near the axis and then more gently [n14; Inferences] [n23; Sheth and Pande 2014]. The axis runs roughly north–south, close to and parallel with the coast, through the Panvel area east of Mumbai harbour, which gives the structure its name. Mumbai and Vasai lie where the Deccan sequence exposes the western limb of this flexure, which is why the area is a key link for correlating the stratigraphy of the whole western Deccan [n01; Deposits Magazine, part 2].

Why did the land bend? The general explanation for flexures of this kind at rifted margins is that, as a continent stretches and breaks, its edge thins and sinks, and is further loaded by the weight of lava and, later, of sediment piled upon it; the edge sags toward the new ocean, and the layers bend down with it [general knowledge; the notes point to the 2020 Geosciences review for this context, n14; MDPI Geosciences 2020]. The 2021 Thane–Vasai study ties the region’s west-dipping basalts, volcaniclastic caps, gabbros and dykes directly to the Panvel flexure, the large structure of the western Indian volcanic rifted margin [n01; x-mol listing] [n23; x-mol listing].

The flexure explains more than one thing about the land of Vasai. It explains why the lava flows lean seaward. It explains, in general terms, why the youngest lavas lie on the coast: they were erupted into, and preserved in, the sagging edge, while older flows bent down beneath them. And, by inference, it helps explain why the coast here is low, with a broad shelf offshore: the edge of the continent has been let down toward the sea [n14; Inferences] [general knowledge for the last point].

The rocks of Mumbai record this moment closely. The whole Mumbai sequence of lavas is about 62.5 million years old, from the Danian stage, the first stage of the Paleocene, the epoch that followed the dinosaurs. That age comes both from fossils in the beds between lava flows and from argon–argon dates on the Mumbai tholeiites (a type of basalt) and on the Panvel flexure itself [n14; Pande et al. 2017]. Mumbai’s rhyolites have given ages of 62.6 ± 0.6 and 62.9 ± 0.2 million years [n14; Sheth & Pande 2014 (PDF)] [n23; Sheth and Pande 2014]. And at Dongri and Uttan, directly south of Vasai Creek, most rhyolite units, previously dated to between 62.5 and 61 million years ago, are the same age as or slightly younger than the breakup and the flexure [n14; IAVCEI-SA2025 abstract].

That leads to the central inference of this chapter, one the research notes state with appropriate care. Vasai–Virar lies on the coastal, seaward-dipping belt of the Deccan basalts west of the Panvel flexure axis. The rock under it is most likely the Bombay-type sequence, about 62.5 to 61 million years old, younger than the dinosaur extinction. That is an inference from the Mumbai and Uttan dates and from the Thane–Vasai geology abstract, not a date measured inside the taluka [n14; Inferences] [n23; Inferences]. A geological map of Palghar or Thane district from the Geological Survey of India would confirm the mapped formation; the research did not retrieve one [n14; Gaps].

Put plainly: the ground under Vasai was born as lava and ash at the moment the Seychelles tore away from India’s west coast. Loosely speaking, the Seychelles islands and the drowned Laxmi Ridge are Vasai’s oldest neighbours, pieces of the same broken edge, now carried hundreds of kilometres apart [n14; Inferences].

The rocks just south of the creek

Before going down beneath Vasai, it is worth crossing the creek once more, because the closest dated rocks deserve a closer look.

The hills of Uttan, Dongri and Gorai, at the northern end of the Bhayandar peninsula, face Vasai fort across the mouth of Vasai Creek. Their rhyolites are pale grey to buff, quite unlike the near-black basalt that makes most of the region. Rhyolite is the volcanic equivalent of granite, rich in silica and thick and sticky when molten, and in general accounts it erupts far more violently than basalt, often as ash flows and domes rather than as quiet sheets [general knowledge]. Its presence here, at the very edge of a basalt province, is itself a clue: silica-rich magmas of this kind are typical of the late stages of continental rifting, when crust is being melted and stretched [general knowledge]. The IAVCEI 2025 abstract concludes that most of the Dongri–Uttan rhyolite units, previously dated to between 62.5 and 61 million years ago, are of the same age as or slightly younger than the India–Seychelles breakup and the Panvel flexure [n14; IAVCEI-SA2025 abstract] [n23; IAVCEI-SA2025 abstract]. They are, in other words, the rocks of the rift itself, erupted while the Seychelles were pulling away.

On Salsette, the city’s own hills tell the same story at a slightly older moment. Mumbai’s rhyolites have given argon ages of 62.6 ± 0.6 and 62.9 ± 0.2 million years [n14; Sheth & Pande 2014 (PDF)]. The tholeiitic basalts of the Mumbai sequence and the beds between them, with their frogs and turtles, are about 62.5 million years old [n14; Pande et al. 2017]. Malabar Hill, Worli Hill and the quarry at Amboli, places that are now neighbourhoods of a megacity, are the localities named in the sources [n14; Records of the ZSI; Intertrappean Beds (Wikiwand)].

From Uttan, Vasai is a few kilometres away across water. No geological boundary is known to run along the creek; the creek is a much younger feature, a drowned river mouth of the last few thousand years, as the next chapters show. The rocks of Uttan and the rocks of Vasai were almost certainly continuous when they formed [inference, from n14; Inferences and n23; Inferences].

What lies under Vasai

Here, then, is the book’s best reconstruction of what lies beneath a garden in Vasai, from the surface down. Every layer below the first is inference, and the confidence falls the deeper it goes.

At the surface: a thin, young skin. Quaternary alluvium and tidal mud: river silt, clay, sand and the dark mud of former mangrove creeks, laid down in the last few hundred thousand years and mostly in the last few thousand. The official groundwater survey gives exactly this, “Quaternary alluvium”, as the top of the local sequence [n01; CGWB Thane district brochure]. On the low hills, in place of alluvium, there may be a crust of laterite, the red weathered rind of the basalt, which the next chapter describes [n01; Kale blog]. This skin is the only part of the column whose story is written in the next chapters rather than this one.

Beneath it: the coastal lavas, about 62.5 to 61 million years old. West-dipping basalt flows, several hundred metres thick, capped in places by volcaniclastic layers, the shattered debris of eruptions, and cut by dykes and by bodies of gabbro such as the one exposed at Chena. That is the Thane–Vasai sequence of the 2021 study [n01; x-mol listing] [n23; x-mol listing]. Their age is inferred from the dated Mumbai and Uttan rocks, which they are taken to match [n14; Inferences] [n23; Inferences]. Somewhere among these flows there may be thin intertrappean beds like those of Worli, with the remains of frogs and pond life; none has been found here [n14; Gaps]. The weathered and fractured top of this basalt is the region’s main aquifer, the rock that feeds every well [n01; CGWB Thane district brochure].

Beneath that: probably the older Deccan pile, about 66 to 65 million years old. If the Western Ghats sequence dips westward across the Panvel flexure and passes beneath the coastal flows, as the general geometry implies, then the main Deccan lavas, the ones that erupted across the dinosaur extinction, continue under Vasai at depth [inference; n23; Gaps on the named formations; n14; Pande et al. 2017 for the relative ages]. How thick the whole basalt column is under the taluka is not known; the notes speak only of “several hundred metres to kilometres” of basalt over the older basement [n23; Takeaway].

Possibly, near the base: earlier lavas and rift sediments. The offshore drilling in the Bombay High region found basalts with ages from 69 to 61 million years, and one older basalt of about 79 million years, a hint that the western margin was already rifting and erupting before the main Deccan floods [n14; Goldschmidt 2023 abstract] [n23; Goldschmidt 2023]. Whether anything of that kind lies beneath Vasai itself is unknown. So is whether any Cretaceous sediments, of the Lameta kind or another, lie on the old land surface beneath the lavas here. If they do, they would be the layer where the dinosaurs of this coast, if any fossilised, would rest [inference; n14; Inferences].

At the bottom: the ancient continent. Beneath all the lava lies the Precambrian crust of India, part of the cratons and basins that formed between three and one billion years ago. Which craton, or which mobile belt, lies directly below Vasai is not stated in any source the research found, and no deep borehole or geophysical interpretation was located [n23; Gaps]. The nearest exposed craton is the Dharwar, to the south-east, and it is tempting to suppose that Dharwar-type crust continues north-west under the coastal Deccan. The notes call that a guess, not a finding [n23; Inferences]. In the end, the rock beneath Vasai rests on the same deep foundation as the rest of the subcontinent, but no one has seen it.

That column, from mud to craton, is the true depth of Vasai’s history. The rest of this book, from the first stone tool to the last high-rise, is written on the top few metres of it.

It is worth pausing on what that birth must have looked like, in the generic terms that the rocks themselves make certain. Basalt does not explode the way the rhyolite of Uttan could; it spreads. Sheets of glowing rock moved across the land, cooled to black crusts, and were buried by the next flow. Where the lava met water it shattered into fragments. Volcanic ash and debris settled in layers, which is what the volcaniclastic caps of the Thane–Vasai sequence are [n01; x-mol listing]. Molten rock forced its way upward through cracks and froze there as dykes, and larger masses cooled slowly underground into coarse-grained gabbro, like the body exposed at Chena [n01; x-mol listing]. Then, as the continent stretched and the sea opened to the west, the whole pile bent toward it.

Life between the lavas

The lava floods did not come all at once. Between eruptions there were quiet intervals, years to thousands of years long, when the surface cooled, soils formed, rain collected in hollows, and ponds and lakes appeared on the black ground. Sediments laid down in those intervals were later buried by the next flow, and geologists call them intertrappean beds: the layers “between the traps”. In Mumbai, tuffs, clays and shales between the lavas have yielded fossil frogs, ostracods (tiny shelled crustaceans), turtle skulls and crocodilian eggshell [n14; Sheth & Pande 2014].

The most famous of these fossils come from Worli Hill. In 1847 Owen described a small fossil frog from there as Rana pusilla. In 1930 Noble placed it in a new genus, Indobatrachus, so it is now Indobatrachus pusillus. In 1940 the palaeontologist Chiplonkar described a second species, I. trivialis, in the Journal of the Bombay Natural History Society; that name, and another, I. malabaricus, are now treated as the same animal. The adult frogs were about 20 millimetres long [n14; Chiplonkar 1940, BioStor; Indobatrachus (Wikipedia)].

The age of the frog beds has been revised more than once, which says something about how hard the science of this coast is. They were first thought to be Early Eocene, about 50 million years old or more recently; then latest Cretaceous, from the very end of the dinosaurs’ time; and now Danian, about 62.5 million years old, from the first ages of the world after the extinction [n14; Indobatrachus (Wikipedia); Pande et al. 2017].

Turtles lived in the same waters. Carteremys leithii, from Worli Hill, is an early Paleocene turtle of uncertain relationships. Sankuchemys sethnai, a bothremydid (a family of side-necked turtles), comes from the Amboli Quarry at Andheri [n14; Intertrappean Beds (Wikiwand)]. Malabar Hill, too, is listed as a Bombay fossil-amphibian locality [n14; Records of the ZSI]. A fossil-locality database keeps an entry for Worli Hill [n14; Mindat paleo locality]. In the 1930s, Commander K. R. U. Todd, whose name will return in the next chapter as the first serious prehistorian of the Bombay coast, also reported fossils from Kasu Shoal (1932) and Khandivli (1939) [n14; Ruikar, Antiquity project gallery].

These are the earliest known living things near Vasai: pond animals, frogs the length of a thumbnail and turtles, living between floods of lava [n23; Chiplonkar 1940; Intertrappean Beds]. The research found no fossil locality inside Vasai–Virar or Palghar. Intertrappean beds at Uttan, Gorai or Vasai have not been confirmed [n14; Gaps]. But the beds at Worli and Andheri lie on the same lava sequence, a few tens of kilometres south, and they are the best picture available of what lived on this ground while it was still being made.

The picture they give is a striking one. The dinosaurs were gone. On a new black land at the edge of a tearing continent, small survivors carried on: frogs in rain-filled hollows, turtles in ponds, ostracods in the mud, and crocodilians laying eggs on the banks. The world that would follow, the world of mammals, birds and eventually people, was beginning in places exactly like this.

Reading a pond from its mud

It is worth asking what the intertrappean animals tell about the land itself, because that is the only evidence of what this ground was like while it was being made.

Frogs need fresh water to breed; their eggs and tadpoles cannot survive in salt. Ostracods of the kinds found in intertrappean beds are, in general, animals of lakes and ponds. Side-necked turtles of the bothremydid family, like Sankuchemys from Amboli, lived in fresh or brackish water in general accounts, and crocodilians lay their eggs on land near water [n14; Sheth & Pande 2014; Intertrappean Beds (Wikiwand)] [general knowledge for the habitats]. Taken together, the Worli and Andheri fossils describe a landscape of standing fresh water: ponds and shallow lakes on the cooled surfaces of lava flows, fed by rain, with mud at the bottom and plants, presumably, around the edges. The tuffs, clays and shales in which the fossils lie are the deposits of such water bodies, interleaved with volcanic ash [n14; Sheth & Pande 2014].

In general terms, the climate must have been warm. India was then in the southern tropics, moving north, and the Danian world, the first few million years after the extinction, was a warm one globally [general knowledge]. The intervals between flows were long enough for ponds to form, for frogs to colonise them, for generations to live and die, and for their bones to be buried in mud before the next lava arrived. Each intertrappean bed records one such interval, and each lava flow above it records its end.

The frog Indobatrachus has a scientific history of its own that touches this coast. Its first description, as Rana pusilla, came from Richard Owen in London in 1847, from Bombay material; its second species was named in 1940 by Chiplonkar in the Journal of the Bombay Natural History Society, the society founded in the city in the nineteenth century whose journal became one of the great records of Indian natural history [n14; Chiplonkar 1940, BioStor; Indobatrachus (Wikipedia)] [general knowledge for the society]. Its relationships to living frogs have been argued over; in general accounts it has at times been compared with frogs of Australia and South America, another faint echo of Gondwana, but the question is not settled [general knowledge; not in the notes].

The reason these fossils survive is the same reason dinosaur fossils near Mumbai do not: position in the pile. The intertrappean beds of Worli and Andheri lie between the young coastal flows, near the top of the column, where erosion and quarrying can reach them. Where the city cut into its hills for stone, building land and roads in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, these pond beds were exposed, and naturalists and geologists collected from them [inference; n14; Mindat paleo locality; Records of the ZSI]. On the Vasai side of the creek, no such collecting has been reported [n14; Gaps].

How the dates are known

Because so much of this chapter rests on numbers with millions of years attached, it is worth saying, in general terms, how such numbers are made, and why they so often disagree.

The methods named in the sources work on a common principle, which is general knowledge rather than something the notes for this book sourced in detail. Certain elements in a rock decay radioactively, at steady and known rates, into other elements. When a lava cools or a crystal grows, it locks in a starting amount of the parent element and, ideally, none of the daughter. Measure how much daughter has built up, and the time since cooling can be calculated. Argon–argon dating measures argon produced from the decay of potassium; it is the method behind the IIT Bombay dates for the Mumbai lavas and the Seychelles breakup, and behind Sprain and colleagues’ timeline for the Deccan [n14; Pande et al. 2017; Science News]. Uranium–lead dating measures lead produced from uranium inside tough little zircon crystals; it is the method behind Schoene and colleagues’ four pulses [n14; Science News].

Fossils give a second clock. The frogs, turtles and ostracods of the intertrappean beds can be compared with fossils elsewhere whose ages are known, and that is one of the two lines of evidence that put the whole Mumbai sequence at about 62.5 million years [n14; Pande et al. 2017]. Magnetism gives a third. When lava cools, magnetic minerals in it align with the Earth’s magnetic field of the moment, and that field has flipped direction many times. The pattern of flips recorded in rock and on the ocean floor can be matched to a master timeline, and it is the same kind of evidence that tracks India’s journey across the Indian Ocean [n23; Northward motion paper (typeset.io)] and supports the united Southern India Block at 1.79 billion years [n23; NSF PAR record].

The disagreements in this chapter come from the gaps between these clocks, from the error margins attached to each measurement (that “± 0.6” on a Mumbai rhyolite date is half a million years either way), and from the difficulty of knowing which rock records which event. The frogs of Worli moved from the Eocene to the Cretaceous to the Paleocene as the methods improved [n14; Indobatrachus (Wikipedia)]. The Seychelles’ departure is 66 million years in one account and 62.5 in another [n14]. The Panvel flexure is “by 65–64” in one paper and “after 63” in a later one by the same group [n14; Sheth & Pande 2014; ICAR/KRISHI record]. This book gives both sides in each case, because that is the honest state of the science.

The rock that people would use

The basalt laid down in this age is not only a geological fact. It is the material of nearly everything built on this coast before the twentieth century, and it is worth looking ahead, briefly, to see how far the consequences of these eruptions reached.

The fractured and weathered basalt is, as the groundwater survey says, the main aquifer of the old Thane district [n01; CGWB Thane district brochure]. The wells that would later water Vasai’s gardens drew on it. In Bhuigaon, a twenty-first-century account describes about forty large irrigation wells, bhowkals, maintained mostly by women, watering vegetables, bananas and flowers [n08; Mumbai Paused]. Their water comes from the cracks in lavas that cooled more than sixty million years ago.

The rock itself became architecture. When the traveller Pyrard de Laval wrote of this coast in 1607, he described Bassein as a great ship-building centre whose building stone was “as fine and hard as granite”, and said that it was used in the churches and palaces of Goa [n15; Thana Gaz. XIV]. The great fort of Baçaim, its churches and its walls, the subject of later chapters, were cut from the ground that this chapter describes. The edicts of Ashoka found at Sopara were carved on a basalt slab [n22; Sircar, Epigraphia Indica 32]. The hills of Tungareshwar and Kamandurg to the east, which frame every view inland from Vasai, are themselves part of these flows [n01; Takeaway].

Even the shape of the coast, its tilt toward the sea, began here. The seaward dip of the basalts west of the Panvel flexure set the grain of the land on which every later process would work [n14; Inferences] [n23; Sheth and Pande 2014].

What the rock remembers

By about 61 million years ago, the eruptions of the Mumbai coast were ending [n14; Pande et al. 2017]. The Seychelles and the Laxmi Ridge were moving away, the sea was widening to the west, and the edge of India had been bent down toward it along the Panvel flexure. Under a thin cover that would come much later, the ground of Vasai was in place: a pile of basalt flows, several hundred metres thick, tilted seaward, capped with volcanic debris and cut by dykes and gabbro [n01; x-mol listing].

It is worth being exact about what this chapter has and has not shown. It has set out, as general knowledge, how the Earth formed, how continents and life began, and how supercontinents gathered and broke. It has shown, from good regional evidence, how India assembled from ancient cratons, joined Columbia and Rodinia, rode in Gondwana, broke from Madagascar about 88 million years ago, and raced north. It has shown that dinosaurs lived in Gujarat, Madhya Pradesh and eastern Maharashtra until about 66 million years ago, and that the great Deccan floods straddled their extinction. It has shown that the rocks of Mumbai and the rhyolites just south of Vasai Creek are about 62.5 to 61 million years old, laid down as the Seychelles tore away. It has not shown, because nobody has yet measured it, the age of any rock inside Vasai taluka. It has not found a single fossil from the taluka. Everything said about Vasai’s own ground is inference from its neighbours [n14; Gaps] [n23; Gaps].

That honest uncertainty is the right note on which to leave the age of fire. The lava had made the land. Now the rain, the sea and time would begin to shape it. Over the next sixty million years, India would collide with Asia, the Himalaya would rise, the monsoon would begin to blow, and the coast would be carved into the plain, hills and creeks that people would one day call home. And at the very end of that long age, someone would pick up a stone from the ground of the Bombay coast and shape it into a tool.
