In this chapter. Thread three: how each settlement was born, from the island of Sopara to the housing colonies of today.

A doorstep at Bolinj

Some time before 1882, a surveyor working for the Gazetteer of the Bombay Presidency walked into the village of Bolinj, at the northern end of what the people of the coast still called the garden of Sopara. He was looking for old stones, and he found one in an unexpected place. In the house of a Christian villager, a block of stone about two feet two inches long, carved with letters in the Devanagari script, had been laid as a doorstep. The Gazetteer recorded the owner’s name exactly as the village said it, “Juju Manu Lop”, and then translated it for its English readers: “that is Joseph Manuel Lopez”. Carved stones were also said to lie at the bottom of the Devhara pond nearby. “Bolinj,” the surveyor concluded, “was formerly a large town and probably had rich temples.” [n40; Gaz. XIV pp. 341–342] [n11; Gaz. 1882 p. 342]

Half a mile to the south-west, at the hamlet of Koprad, another inscribed stone lay near the house of the village headman. He too was named: Bhiku Patil, a Samvedi Brahman. That stone carried a date, Shaka 1386, which is Hijri 868 and 1464 of the Common Era, and it spoke of Muslim officials, of Mahim and Thana, of Koprad itself, and of a place called “Bimbstan”. [n03; TG-XIV Koprad entry; TG-II p. 441 n. 4] [n40; Gaz. XIV p. 342]

These two stones, one under a Christian’s threshold and one beside a Hindu headman’s house, are a fair introduction to this thread of the book. Every village of Vasai taluka is built on the remains of something older, and almost none of them was “founded” in the way that a city in a textbook is founded, by a named person on a recorded day. Bolinj had been a town of temples before it was a village of Christian gardeners. Koprad was a place where a fifteenth-century grant was made under Muslim overlords, and in 1882 its headman was a Hindu while many of its gardeners were Christian. The stones moved from temple to doorstep, from church to temple, from Sopara to the walls of Bassein. So did the people.

Other chapters tell the story of this coast era by era. This one follows a single question through every era: how did each port, town, village and neighbourhood of Vasai taluka come to be where it is, and how did it change? It moves in the order in which places first appear in the record, from the ancient port of Sopara to the colonies of Nallasopara East and the industrial estates of Waliv. It will often have to say that the founding of a place is simply not known. Where that is so, it says so plainly, and tells what can be known instead: the first time a place is named, what stood there, and why that spot made sense to the people who lived on it.

How to read a place here

Before the story begins, a word on the evidence, because it is of very different kinds, and this chapter leans on each kind differently.

The oldest layer is legend and sacred text. The Puranas, the Mahabharata, the mahatmyas of Nirmal and Tungar, and the Buddhist and Jain stories about Sopara all describe this landscape, but none of them is a record of when a village was laid out. They tell us what people later believed about a place, and that is worth knowing; they do not tell us when it began.

The next layer is inscriptions: Ashoka’s edicts at Sopara, and above all the copper plates and stone grants of the Shilahara kings of the north Konkan, who ruled from about 800 to about 1260. These are the first documents that name villages of Vasai taluka, and they are hard evidence for a name existing at a given date. They do not usually say how old the village already was. [n03; TG-II pp. 421–424; CII-VI]

Then come travellers and geographers: the Greek Periplus of the Erythraean Sea, Ptolemy, the Arab geographers from al-Masudi in 915 to al-Idrisi in 1153, the friar Jordanus in the 1320s, and the Portuguese Duarte Barbosa in 1514. They describe towns and ports, never villages. [n02; Periplus; Shah 2020] [n03; TG-XIV pp. 321–322]

Then come Portuguese records, of which the most valuable for this chapter is the Tombo de Baçaim of 1727–1730, a register of the Crown’s leases of Bassein’s villages and gardens just before the Portuguese lost them. It names dozens of villages under their Portuguese spellings, with the names of the people who held them. [n40; Tombo, Teixeira and Pires 2007]

Then the British survey, above all the Gazetteer of the Bombay Presidency volumes on Thana, published in 1882 and compiled under James M. Campbell, with the inscriptions read by Pandit Bhagvanlal Indraji. It is the first source to describe Vasai village by village, with headmen, ponds, temples, landing places and populations. It is also a colonial compilation, and it makes mistakes. Where this chapter quotes it, it is quoting a careful but fallible survey, not a final authority. [n37; source note]

Then parish and community records: the dates the Catholic parishes give for their own foundation, the East Indian Calendar’s village pages, and the community’s own lists of Kupari villages. These are written from inside the community, usually without references, and their dates often conflict. They are the only source for most twentieth-century parishes. [n40; reliability tags]

Then the census, the municipal corporation and the press, which take the story from the 1980s to November 2026. For the newest neighbourhoods, which have grown up since 1988 on old salt waste and rice land, there is often nothing at all except a newspaper report of a flood or a demolition.

Every claim below carries its source. Where a date or a founder rests on tradition, the text says so.

The island and its water

To understand why any settlement here stands where it does, begin with the ground.

In 1882 the whole western strip of the taluka, between the Bassein creek in the south and the Vaitarna river in the north, was a band of garden land about three miles broad. It was not quite mainland. A backwater left the Bassein creek “a little above the railway bridge” and wound north until it reached the Vaitarna, cutting the strip off from the country to the east. People on the ground called this backwater by three names, according to the stretch they lived beside: the Manikpur creek in the south, the Sopara creek in the middle, and the Bolinj creek in the north. “Under the Portuguese the whole of this rich strip, with its patches of salt marsh, was known as the island of Bassein, and, before Portuguese times, as the island of Sopara.” [n11; Gaz. 1882 pp. 314–315] [n42; Gazetteer XIV pp. 314–315]

The island was divided, in local speech, into garden belts called agar. The agar of Agashi lay to the north, the agar of Bassein to the south, and between them the agar of Sopara: “a richly wooded well-watered and highly tilled tract, including Bolinj, Koprad and Umbrala in the north, Nirmal and Vagholi in the west, Gas in the south, and Sopara and Mardesh in the centre”. [n11; Gaz. 1882 p. 314] Those names, Bolinj, Koprad, Umrale, Nirmal, Vagholi, Gass, Sopara and Mardes, are the villages of this book’s heartland. The surveyor saw them as a single landscape: sugarcane under coconut and brab palms; mango, tamarind, banyan, pipal and karanj trees; lanes sunk “four or five feet below the level of the fields” between high hedges; old basalt-block footways; garden wells with their runnels; round ponds and winding lakes “fringed by high pile-supported Persian wheels”; and tiled houses with wooden walls set on high masonry plinths, often built from older carved stones. [n11; Gaz. 1882 p. 315]

To the east of the backwater the country was different. There lay salt waste and rice flats, the low hills of Tulinj, and beyond them the long forested ridge of Tungar, 2,200 feet high, with the Vaitarna washing its northern foot and the Bassein river its southern one. [n12; Gaz 1882 pp. 366–367] Along the Tansa valley, between Tungar and the Vaitarna, ran an old inland route to the Deccan. [n12; Gaz 1882 Sopara fn., information from W. W. Loch]

The backwater had once been much more than a ditch. The Gazetteer called it “once a considerable arm of the sea overflowing a large breadth of land on both banks of its present channel”. [n42; Gazetteer XIV pp. 314–315] By 1882 it was dying. “With the mud-laden Vaitarna on the north and the Bassein creek or Ulhas river on the south, silting has been rapid, especially since the long railway embankment has dammed the flow of water from the south.” Boats of ten to twelve tons still came up from the south as far as the landing at Gass, about a mile from Sopara, and from the north as far as Bolinj, about two miles off. Small boats came from the west as near as Vagholi. “At Sopara the creek is dry.” [n42; Gazetteer XIV p. 315] [n36; Gaz XIV pp. 314–315]

The silting was not new. In 1728 a Portuguese officer reporting on the defences of the island found the creek between Sopara and Bolinj fordable at low water for about 4,000 feet, and asked that it be dug out “as deep as the height of a man”, or it would soon “be filled to a level with the fields, because of the great amount of silt that was brought by the river … from its two mouths”. [n36; Gaz XIV p. 322] [n12; Gaz 1882] Six centuries earlier, al-Idrisi had placed Sopara a mile and a half from the sea. [n03; TG-XIV pp. 321, 355–356]

This one fact, a waterway slowly turning into land, explains more about where the settlements of Vasai taluka stand than any king or priest. The oldest town grew on a raised island guarded by water. Its landings, Gass, Vagholi and Bolinj, lived as long as boats could reach them. Bassein rose at the mouth of the deep creek to the south when the shallow one in the middle failed. The nineteenth-century railway, laid on an embankment across the old waterway, finished the creek and then drew new towns to its stations. And the twentieth-century city built its newest colonies on the very salt flats and backwater beds that the Gazetteer called “formerly flooded during the rains”. [n42; Gazetteer XIV, Sopara section] [n31; Gazetteer 1882; inferences]

Sopara: the town on the raised island

Why there

The 1882 Gazetteer gave a reason for Sopara’s site that still reads well. “Its height above the surrounding salt marsh, its rich soil and fresh water, and its nearness to the holy Vaitarna, Jivdhan, Tungar and Nirmal must early have made Sopara a chosen settlement. Its distance from the coast and its broad backwater guarded it from pirate raids or the forays of the wild hill and forest tribes, and its water connection with the Vaitarna on the north and the Ulhas or Bassein river on the south … made it an early centre of trade.” [n37; Gaz. XIV p. 316]

It is worth separating the parts of that sentence, because each points to a different kind of settlement that would later grow on the island. Raised dry ground and good water made a farming village possible. The holy rivers and hills made it a place of pilgrimage. The backwater made it defensible. And the water connection both ways made it a port, with the Tansa valley route behind it leading inland. [n12; Gaz 1882 Sopara fn.] Every later village of the agar inherited some of these advantages, and every later decline came from losing one of them.

What the legends say

The sacred literature remembers Sopara as Shurparaka, a place won from the sea. The Harivamsa makes it a city reclaimed by the arrow of Parashurama, and the Mahabharata names it as a very holy place where the Pandavas rested. The Gazetteer, which reported these stories, also warned that the Harivamsa is “probably modern”. [n37; Gaz. XIV pp. 316–317] Da Cunha in 1876 gave two meanings for the name: Lassen’s Sanskrit Surparaka, “fine shore”, or surpa, “winnow”, for the winnow-shaped coast of Parashurama’s land. [n37; da Cunha pp. 169–170] A nineteenth-century theory made Sopara the biblical Ophir, the land of gold to which King Solomon’s ships sailed; it rests only on the likeness of the Greek forms “Sophir” and “Soupara”, and no material from the tenth century BCE has ever been found at Sopara. The Gazetteer itself called the idea “not unlikely, though doubtful”. [n02; Wikipedia: Ophir; Jewish Encyclopedia] [n37; Gaz. XIII-II]

The Buddhist stories go further, and they are vivid, but they are stories. In the Divyavadana, Purna, son of a rich Sopara merchant by a slave woman, becomes a sea-captain, builds a sandalwood hall near Shurparaka and invites the Buddha, who arrives “flying through the sky” with five hundred monks. [n02; 84000 Knowledgebase; LiveHistoryIndia] In the Mahavamsa, Prince Vijaya, legendary first king of Sri Lanka, sails from Supparaka. [n02; Wikipedia: Sopara, citing Thapar] A local tradition recorded in 1882 told of a “Widows’ Stupa” raised by five hundred widows over nail and hair relics of the Buddha. [n02; Shah 2020, citing Indraji] None of these is evidence that a town stood at Sopara on a known date. They are evidence that, by the time they were written down, Sopara was famous enough to be the setting for them.

What the evidence says

The evidence begins in the third century BCE. In 1882 Bhagvanlal Indraji found a broken slab of basalt bearing part of Ashoka’s Rock Edict VIII near the Bhatela pond, east of Sopara. On New Year’s Day 1956, N. A. Gore, librarian of the Bombay Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society, found a fragment of Rock Edict IX at Bhuigaon, a village to the west. D. C. Sircar concluded that “a complete set of the fourteen Rock Edicts of Asoka must have originally existed near Sopara”. [n02; Sircar, Epigraphia Indica 32] An emperor does not carve his edicts at an empty place. Sopara in Ashoka’s time was a town that mattered, and the Gazetteer inferred that it was then the capital of Aparanta, the western coast. [n37; Gaz. XIV p. 319]

In April 1882 Indraji also opened a brick mound in the village of Mardes known as “Burud Rajacha Kot”, the Burud king’s fort. Inside was a brick chamber holding a stone coffer, and inside the coffer nested caskets of copper, silver, stone, crystal and gold, with gold flowers, fragments believed to be of the Buddha’s begging bowl, a Satavahana silver coin, and eight bronze figures of the Buddhas and Maitreya. The mound was a stupa, first built in the Mauryan age and enlarged over the following centuries; the bronzes are of the seventh to ninth century, which means the monument was being cared for a thousand years after it was first raised. [n02; Asiatic Society of Mumbai; LiveHistoryIndia; Shah 2020] So the village of Mardes, which the Gazetteer placed “in the centre” of the agar, sits beside the oldest standing structure on the island. [n40; A8; Gaz. XIV p. 314]

Donor inscriptions far away show Sopara’s merchants at work. At Karle a pillar was given by Satimita of Sopara, in about the first century BCE. At Nashik, in the first or second century CE, Ushavadata recorded building a rest-house with four doors and four verandahs at “Sorparaga”. At Kanheri a cistern was given by Samika, a merchant of Soparaka; at Naneghat another by Govindadasa “of Soparaya”. [n37; Gaz. XIV pp. 319–320] [n02; Shah 2020] The Periplus, written in the first century CE, named “Suppara” as the first market-town south of Barygaza (Broach), before Calliena (Kalyan), and Ptolemy called it “Soupara”. [n02; Periplus, Schoff translation] Excavations in 1993 by the Archaeological Survey of India with the British Academy found Satavahana and Kshatrapa coins, beads, Northern Black Polished Ware, pieces of Roman amphorae and an earthen and a stone wall. [n02; Shah 2020, citing Tripati and Gaur 1997]

So the first settlement of Vasai taluka about which anything firm can be said is a port town on the raised island, with a stupa, an Ashokan edict, a merchant class rich enough to endow monasteries across the Ghats, and trade reaching the Roman world. What it looked like on the ground, where its streets ran, where its landing lay at its height, is not known. “No traces of the ancient port remain,” one modern account says, “only a few protected mounds and a dried-up lake.” [n02; LiveHistoryIndia]

Where was the harbour?

Two modern finds point to the southern edge of the island. In 2018 a local researcher reported a stone anchor near Gass and Taki Pada, close to a small mosque that people call the Bandar Masjid, the “port mosque”. The paper that published it says the spot “coincides with the place where an earlier port would have been; which has now been filled up due to siltation”. [n42; Shah 2020, sections 2–3] The anchor has not been dated. The same paper reports a “Portuguese landing place to the east of Gaas”, credited to a survey by Sila Tripati and A. S. Gaur of 1997. [n42; Shah 2020] A Wikipedia account mentions remains of a Portuguese jetty and customs house near the Bhatela pond. [n02; Wikipedia: Sopara] Together they suggest that at more than one period the boats came in on the south and east side of the town, towards today’s Gass, but no excavation has yet fixed a harbour of any date.

The rise and fall of the ancient town

Tripati and Gaur’s survey found two peaks of activity at Sopara: from before Ashoka to about the third century CE, and again from the ninth to the thirteenth century, with “no cultural remains from 4th to 9th centuries”. [n02; Wikipedia: Sopara, citing Tripati and Gaur 1997] The first decline is not explained in any source this chapter can use, beyond the general fact of siltation and of rival ports: the Periplus already names Kalyan and Chaul as competing markets. [n36; Periplus] Whether the town shrank, moved or simply left no remains that have been dug is unknown.

When the Arab geographers wrote about Sopara between 915 and 1153, it was a living town again. Al-Masudi named “Subara” with Thana and Saymur (Chaul) as places where the Lariya language was spoken. Al-Biruni placed it correctly, forty-eight miles south of Sanjan and forty north of Thana. Al-Idrisi in 1153 described “Soubara” as a mile and a half from the sea, “very well peopled”, with a great trade, “one of the emporiums of India”, with pearl fisheries nearby and an island called Bara where coconut palms and costus grew. [n03; TG-XIV pp. 321, 355–356] That island is very probably the island of Sopara itself.

It is from this second life of Sopara that the earliest named villages of the taluka come, and they are the subject of the next part of this chapter. But it is worth noting first that Sopara’s townsmen were already a mixed population. Kosmas Indikopleustes in the sixth century reported a Christian bishop at Kalyan. [n03; TG-II p. 420 n. 4] In the 1320s Friar Jordanus found “many Nestorian Christians and a church dedicated to St Thomas” at Sopara, stayed fifteen days and buried four martyred companions there. [n37; Gaz. XIV p. 322] Jain tradition counted Sopara among the eighty-four great tirthas, with an image of Rishabhadeva, and one of the Jain gachchhas took its name from the town. [n37; Gaz. XIV p. 319] [n02; Shah 2020] Muslim traders, the Naitas, “descendants of Arab and Persian refugees and traders”, were part of the town by the time the Portuguese arrived. [n37; Gaz. XIII-I pp. 216–217; Gaz. XIV p. 315 note] The town had become a place many peoples had reasons to live in.

The holy places around the town

The Gazetteer’s phrase “nearness to the holy Vaitarna, Jivdhan, Tungar and Nirmal” names the other kind of early settlement on this coast: places that drew people because they were sacred, before anyone made them villages.

Nirmal

Nirmal is the clearest case. It stands on “a wooded mound rising from bare flats once under water”, six miles north of Bassein, with a great lake below it running towards the sea. [n40; A1; Gaz. XIV p. 292 n. 2] The Nirmal Mahatmya tells how the demon Vimal harassed Brahman sages “who lived near the sacred Vaitarni”; Parashurama cut off his hands and feet, which grew back; with Shiva’s help Vimal was overcome and pardoned, and Parashurama set up the linga Vimaleshvar where he fell. “From Vimal it came to be called Nirmal or stainless.” [n40; A1; Gaz. XIV p. 292 n. 2] The Gazetteer recorded a second explanation from a civil servant, A. Cumine: that the name was a Sanskrit dress for Dravidian nir, water, and mal, hill, “the sea hill”, “a true description of the place”. [n40; A1] The Skanda Purana calls Nirmal “the best of holy pools”. [n11; Gaz. 1882 p. 292 fn.]

Those are legends and an etymology. The first datable thing at Nirmal is a dressed stone “with letters which seem part of a Sanskrit inscription of the seventh century”, standing in front of a house about five hundred yards north of the chief temple in 1882. [n40; A1; Gaz. XIV p. 293] So Nirmal was a place where someone carved an inscription in about the seventh century; it was a pilgrimage centre before the Portuguese came, because the Portuguese “pulled down the temples and destroyed the linga”. [n37; Gaz. XIV pp. 292–293] Tradition makes it the burial place of a Shankaracharya; the Gazetteer noted that the great Shankaracharya died elsewhere and suggested that “this Nirmal Shankaracharya may have been one of his religious descendants”. [n03; TG-XIV p. 292] Modern temple traditions that give the samadhis dates in the fifth and fourth centuries BCE are, in the words of the research note, “chronologically impossible as given”. [n03; Wikipedia: Samvedi; Heritage Chronicles] [n40; A1, CRIT Vol III asset 82]

When Nirmal became a village, as distinct from a holy hill, no source says.

Tungar, Jivdhan and the Vaitarna

The other sacred places named in the Gazetteer’s sentence were not villages at all in their early life. Tungar was the demon Vimal’s hill in the Tungar Mahatmya, part of the Padma Purana: Parashurama drove the asuras into the sea, Vimal alone settled on Tungar, won Shiva’s favour by penance, and received “a holy pool and a lingam”. The Gazetteer suggested, without much confidence, that Tungar might be Ptolemy’s “Dunga”. [n12; Gaz 1882 p. 366 fn.] Jivdhan, the hill a mile east of today’s Virar station, carried in 1882 the ruins of fortifications and “very old-looking caves and cisterns said to be the work of the Pandavs”. [n12; Gaz 1882] The Vaitarna was the holy river of all these legends. None of these sacred places can be dated before the medieval period from the sources available, and the temples that stand on them now were mostly built or rebuilt in the eighteenth century, as later sections show. [n03; inferences]

What they did for settlement was to make the island and its rim a place people came to. Pilgrims, priests and the traders who sold to them are a reason for villages, and the November fair at Nirmal, as the nineteenth century would show, was a market as much as a pilgrimage.

Puri: the capital no one can find

Between the end of Sopara’s first life and the Shilahara grants lies the question of Puri.

In the sixth century a dynasty called the Konkan Mauryas ruled this coast from a capital named Puri. A stone from Vada in north Thana names one of their kings, Suketuvarman. [n03; TG-II p. 420] Under the Chalukya king Pulakeshin II, between about 610 and 640, a general named Chanda-danda “with hundreds of ships, attacked the Maurya capital Puri, the goddess of the fortunes of the western ocean”, in the words of the Aihole inscription. [n03; TG-II p. 420] The Shilahara kings who ruled the north Konkan from about 800 also called their capital Puri. [n03; TG-II pp. 421–424]

Where Puri was is not known. The great epigraphist V. V. Mirashi set aside Elephanta, a site north of Marol in Salsette and Rajapuri in Janjira, and concluded that “the identification of Puri will have to be left for further research”. [n03; CII-VI p. 2] The 1882 Gazetteer contradicted itself. Its volume on places of interest said that “the site of the Silhara capital seems to have been between Bassein and Agashi. Puri may therefore have been Sopara, to which a Bakhar of the eighteenth century seems to refer as Puri.” Its history volume preferred Elephanta. [n03; TG-XIV p. 355 n. 5; TG-II p. 423 n.]

One Vasai village was put forward as a candidate: a place called Puri, “said to be Burhanpur”, a few hundred yards south-west of the railway station at Manikpur, which is named in a Maratha chronicle of the 1738 siege of Bassein. The Gazetteer weighed it and found “no signs of old remains”. [n11; Gaz. 1882 p. 402] [n37; Gaz. XIV pp. 315, 402] The name Barhanpur appears again in 1882 among the villages of the Christian Vadval cartmen (“Chulna, Mánikpur, Gokhivra, Sándor, and Barhánpur”). [n40; GzXIII-1 p. 97] So a Puri or Burhanpur existed near Manikpur; whether it had anything to do with the lost capital is unknown, and the best modern authority leaves the question open.

The question matters to this chapter for one reason. If Puri was on the island, then the Shilahara heartland was here, and Sopara was a royal town as well as a port. If not, Sopara was a district headquarters and a port under a capital somewhere else. The grants that follow are consistent with either reading.

Villages named in copper: the Shilahara grants

The district of Sopara’s sixty-six

The Shilaharas of the north Konkan were, by their own counting, lords of “the Konkan 1400”, a realm of about fourteen hundred villages, and they held it for some four hundred and fifty years, from about 810 to about 1260. Their places of note were Hamjaman (Sanjan), Shristhanaka (Thana), Shurparaka (Sopara), Chemuli (Chaul) and Lavanatata (Lonad). [n03; TG-II pp. 421–424] They divided the land into districts, and the district around Sopara was called the Shurparaka-shatshashti, which means “the sixty-six” of Shurparaka. [n03; CII-VI p. 115] [n02; LiveHistoryIndia] Which sixty-six villages that unit held is not known; a popular account says the Shilahara-era city covered “present-day Sopara, Bolinj, Nirmal and Gaas”, but that comes from a popular history site and has not been checked against an inscription. [n42; LiveHistoryIndia] [n02]

What is known is that some of the villages of today’s Vasai taluka first appear by name in Shilahara documents. For most of them, this is the earliest record of their existence, and they appear already as villages, already with names, already being given away.

1049: Virar, Pelhar and Adane

The most important document is a set of copper plates issued by the king Mummuniraja and dated 20 February 1049 (Shaka 970). It records a grant of villages to fourteen Brahmans, some of them from Karahata, the modern Karad in the Deccan. Part of the grant lay in the Shurparaka-shatshashti vishaya, “the territory round Shurparaka, modern Sopara in the Bassein taluka”. The villages named there include Brihad-Adanika and Laghu-Adanika, Great and Little Adanika, which Mirashi identified with Adane, eight and a half miles east of Virar; Virara, which is Virar; Pedhala, which is Pelhar, about eight miles north-north-east of Bassein; Uppalapallika, a hamlet of Virar; and Khandivali and Khanuvada. [n03; CII-VI pp. 115–118]

This plate is the birth certificate, so far as one exists, of three places that are now large parts of the modern city. Virar in 1049 was a village with a hamlet of its own, worth granting to a Brahman. Pelhar was a village on the inland side. Adane, in two parts, great and small, lay further east towards the hills. The plate does not say who lived there, what they grew or how long the villages had existed; it says only that in 1049 they existed, that they lay in Sopara’s district, and that their revenue could be given to learned men from the Deccan.

Some later history of these names can be traced. Adane survives as the rural village of Adne, with 1,995 people in the 2011 census. [n12; census2011.co.in] Pelhar gave its name to a dam and a river: by the 2010s the “Pelhar (old scheme)” supplied about 14 million litres a day to the city, and the Pollution Control Board drew up an action plan for “the polluted Pelhar river stretch”. [n12; Surya project summary 2014; MPCB 2019] [n29; Surya EC summary] And Virar, the village of 1049, became the northern third of a city of more than a million people. The plate is also a reminder of who the grantees were: Brahmans brought from the Deccan. The Shilaharas settled learned families on the land, and one of their earlier plates, from Bhandup in 1026, granted land to a Brahman of the Chhandoga shakha of the Sama Veda, which is worth noting for the later history of the Samvedis of this coast, though it does not prove a link. [n03; TG-II p. 425 n.]

1094: Sopara as a toll port

A plate of the king Anantadeva dated 1094, found at Kharepatan, exempted the ships of two merchant-ministers from tolls at the ports of Shristhanaka, Nagapur, Shurparaka, Chemuli and others. [n03; TG-II pp. 425–426; TG-XIV p. 321] Sopara was, in other words, a customs point where ships paid duty. The grant also tells us something about the coast’s settlements: there were enough ports that a king could list them. Where Nagapur was is uncertain; the Gazetteer later thought it was probably Nagaon (Nagothna side), and this chapter does not identify it with Naigaon of Vasai. [n37; Gaz. XIV p. 402]

1150: Vatar and its four headmen

In the Collector’s garden at Thana in 1882 stood a stone that had been found at Agashi. It was dated Shaka 1072, which is 1150 of the Common Era, in the reign of the Shilahara king Haripaladeva, and it granted the village of Vattaraka “in Shurparaka”. The grant was witnessed by the headmen of Vattaraka: “Risi Mhatara, headman of Vattaraka village, Naguji Mhatara, Ananta Nayaka, and Changdev Mhatara”. The Gazetteer added in a footnote: “Vattaraka is the modern Vatargaon, two miles north-west of Sopara. Du Perron (1760) notices it as Outar.” [n40; A13; GzXIV p. 385 and n. 1]

This is the oldest dated record of any village of the Kupari garden belt, and it is the only one in which the villagers themselves appear. In 1150 Vatar had headmen, and they had names. Three of them bore the title Mhatara, which the research notes observe matches the Mhatre surname still found among the Samvedi and Vadval families of the coast; no source has tested the link, and it remains an inference. [n40; A13, inferences] The stone was found at Agashi, not at Vatar, so it does not by itself prove anything about Agashi’s age. [n40; A2]

Vatar’s later life can be followed through the documents. In 1727–30 the Portuguese leased the pacaria of “Vatar Rajoly” to Manuel Varjão da Fonseca for 700 pardaus a year, the highest rent among the Agashi leases. [n40; A13; Tombo fl. 45v] In 1882 it was one of the plantain-drying villages, with Hindu Samvedi, Hindu Pachkalshi and Christian families. [n40; GzXIII-1 pp. 292, 395] In 2016 its hamlets, Vatar-Barodi, Jeladi and Adlay, were community centres of the Nandakhal parish. [n40; A13]

1160 and 1203: stones near Bassein and at Mandvi

Two more Shilahara stones come from the Vasai side. One, dated Shaka 1082 (1160), comes from Bassein and styles the king “Shri-Silahara Mallikarjun”; it records a field given for restoring a temple. [n03; TG-II pp. 426, 436–437] This is the earliest dated object from Bassein, but it is a find-spot, not a mention: the stone does not name Bassein. The Gazetteer noted that a Kanheri inscription once read as “Vasai” had been shown by Bhagvanlal Indraji to be a misreading. [n03; TG-XIV p. 28 n. 4] So in the Shilahara age the place that would become the greatest town of the taluka has no name in the record at all.

The other stone, of the king Keshideva and dated 1203, was found near Mandvi in Bassein, and grants offerings to the god Lakshminarayana. Another undated stone of the king Aparaditya stood near Kalambhon (Kolambhom), an inland village on the Tansa eighteen miles east of Manikpur, which also had a carved memorial stone. [n03; TG-II pp. 426–427 nn.] [n11; Gaz. 1882 p. 112] These are the eastern villages along the Tansa valley route. The Gazetteer recorded, on the authority of W. W. Loch, that “along the Tansa valley Pelar and Mandvi were once places of importance, and at Bhatana, a landing station to the north of Tungar, are a reservoir of dressed stones and quantities of old bricks”. [n12; Gaz 1882 Sopara fn.] It gave that inland route as one reason Sopara had become a trade centre. [n12] The link rests on a single footnote, but it fits the stones: Pelhar named in 1049, Mandvi carrying a royal grant in 1203, Bhatane a landing with old brickwork. These were the villages through which the inland trade of Sopara reached the Deccan. Kalambhon had 1,081 people and Bhatane 4,786 in 2011, both still rural. [n12; census2011.co.in]

1255: a garden at Sandor

The last Shilahara grant that names a Vasai village is a stone found at the Relbav pond in Sandor, three miles north of Bassein. It is dated Shaka 1177 (1255) and records a gift by a king titled “Konkan chakravarti”, emperor of the Konkan, to an astrologer named Kheidev: “a garden in the part called Nivayi within the limits of Sandor village”. It names a headquarters station called Nagapur and a high road. [n37; Gaz. XIV p. 402] So Sandor in 1255 was a village with named parts, gardens and boundaries. It would become, three hundred years later, the “mother church of all the churches of South Bassein”. A second stone at Sandor, dated Hijra 966 (1558), mentions “Nayak Laro, lord of the western ocean”, a title whose bearer is not identified. [n37; Gaz. XIV p. 402]

The other medieval villages

Several villages of the agar have no grant naming them, but have medieval remains that show they were inhabited.

At Gass, in a field called Sonarbhat (“gold field”), the Gazetteer recorded a broken cross-legged female figure, perhaps the Jain goddess Ambika, and the foundations of a temple “said to have been dedicated to Parasnath”; a damaged stone, perhaps the Sun with a lotus in each hand, “probably of the eleventh or twelfth century”; and on the shore of the Gass lake, at a spot called Mala, the foundations of “probably a Brahmanic temple of the eleventh or twelfth century” with a richly carved stone lotus pendant. [n42; Gazetteer XIV p. 341] The research for this book found no Shilahara inscription naming Gass, and no Tombo entry for it. [n42; section 3] Gass was a place of temples in the Shilahara age; whether it was a separate village then is unknown. In 1882 “the people of the place” said that Gass was “the site of old Sopara”, a tradition the Gazetteer recorded and did not adopt. [n42; Gazetteer XIV p. 341]

At Chakreshwar, by the lake 550 yards north-east of the stupa, the Gazetteer saw a headless Nandi “probably of about the same age (AD 1060) as the Ambarnath temple” and a Parshvanatha image “probably of the tenth century or earlier”; plinths in Sopara town held “many old carved Hindu stones”. [n03; TG-XIV pp. 336–337] At Bolinj, as the opening of this chapter showed, there were carved stones and the memory of “rich temples”. [n40; A11] At Parol and Majavli, on the eastern foot of Tungar, there were four ruined Hindu temples with no worship in 1882; one Majavli temple’s stones had been built into a Portuguese granary. [n12; Gaz 1882]

The pattern, then, is this. By the twelfth century the island of Sopara and the Tansa valley behind it were dotted with villages and temples, some of them named in royal grants. Sopara was the district’s chief town and a toll port. Bassein, six miles south, has left a stone but no name. The Gazetteer put it plainly: “As Bassein lies only six miles south of Sopara it can hardly have been a place of consequence so long as Sopara (AD 1100) remained a centre of trade.” [n37; Gaz. XIV pp. 28, 322]

The Yadava viceroys and the first hint of Bassein

The Shilaharas fell around 1260, and the Yadavas of Devagiri took the north Konkan, governing it through viceroys. The Gazetteer’s history volume says that in the late thirteenth century the north Konkan was “ruled by viceroys of the Devgiri Yadavs, whose head-quarters were at Kalyan and Bassein”, and records stones of 1280 and 1288 found near Bhiwandi and Bassein. [n03; TG-II p. 437] Its places volume is more cautious: “A doubtful reference makes Bassein the head of a district under the Devgiri Yadavs. There is no certain notice till 1507.” [n37; Gaz. XIV p. 28] [n03; TG-XIV p. 28]

So the beginning of Bassein as a town lies somewhere in a fog between about 1280 and 1500. It may have been a Yadava administrative seat; the evidence is not strong enough to say.

A stone from this period does name Sopara. It was “brought from Nila about a mile north of Sopara” and set up in the compound of the double-storeyed rest-house by Manikpur station, and it names the Yadava king Ramchandradev, with a date read by the Gazetteer as Shaka 1202 or 1220 (1280 or 1298), and the name Shurparak twice. [n40; A15; GzXIV p. 387] This stone is also the first appearance in the record of a place called Nila, about a mile north of Sopara. It will matter later, when the railway halt of “Neela” appears and the town of Nallasopara gets its name.

In 1290, the Gazetteer says, “after their first conquest … the Muhammadans left to the local chief the right of making land grants”, and a sun-and-moon land-grant stone dated 1351 stood near Pada, a hamlet of Sondavli, in the Sopara area. [n42; Gazetteer XIV, Sopara pages] [n31; Gazetteer pp. 313–340]

Under the sultans: Koprad, Bimbstan and the Gujarat coast

The Delhi interlude and the Gujarat garrisons

From about 1312 to 1350, Delhi’s armies reached the coast and then lost interest. The friars Jordanus and Odoric found, in the 1320s, that the “Saracens” had lately seized the country and destroyed many temples and some churches, and that a military governor and a qazi sat at Thana. [n03; TG-II pp. 439–440] The Gazetteer also records that many of Sopara’s stone temples “are said to have been destroyed by the Musalmans in the beginning of the fourteenth century”, but the phrase carries “are said to” and the footnote cited does not obviously support it, so it is best treated as a tradition. [n37; Gaz. XIV p. 323] A Hindu chief of Bhiwandi was still making land grants in 1404: “Musalman supremacy can have been little more than a name.” [n03; TG-II pp. 440–441]

From about 1390 Gujarat held the coast through garrisons and tributary Hindu chiefs. [n03; TG-II p. 441] The documents that name Vasai villages in this period are few, and they are stones.

Koprad, 1464, and the name Bimbstan

The Koprad stone of 1464 is the best of them. It is written in Devanagari, carries both a Shaka and a Hijri date, mentions Muslim names, and names Mahim, Thana, Koprad and “Bimbstan”. It lay in 1882 near the house of the headman Bhiku Patil, a Samvedi Brahman. [n03; TG-XIV Koprad entry; TG-II p. 441 n. 4] So Koprad, half a mile south-west of Bolinj, was a named village in 1464, under Muslim overlordship, with a land grant worth carving.

“Bimbstan” connects this stone to one of the great founding stories of the coast: the tale of Raja Bimb. The Mahikavatichi Bakhar, a Marathi chronicle whose oldest chapters were written by Keshavacharya in about 1448 and which was finished in the seventeenth century, tells how a prince named Pratap Bimb came from the north in about 1138, made his capital at Mahikavati, which is Mahim near Kelve in Palghar, and brought settlers with him; in its later chapters a Yadava prince named Bimbdev appears. [n03; Wikipedia: Mahikavatichi Bakhar] Several of the coast’s communities, among them the Panchkalshis and Vadvals, trace their arrival to Bimb, and the Bhandaris also said they “came with Bimb”. [n03; section 3] [n08; Gazetteer 1882 p. 149–150] The Bakhar’s kings and dates are, in the words of the research, “legendary or uncorroborated”, and “archaeological, epigraphic, and numismatic evidence does not corroborate many smaller early dynasties”. But inscriptions of 1368 at Deonar and 1464 at Koprad both name “Bimbasthan”, so a real Bimb polity or place-name existed. [n03; section 3] The 1882 Gazetteer thought “Bimbsthan” was perhaps an old name of Bhiwandi. [n03; TG-II p. 437 n.]

For the history of settlement, the Bimb tradition matters because it is the only story that claims to explain how whole communities came to the villages of Vasai. It cannot be dated, and it should be told as what it is: a founding myth, written down in the fifteenth to seventeenth centuries, with a kernel of real place-names. The Bakhar also names one village of this chapter: Virar appears in it as “Vihar”. [n37; Wikipedia: Virar] [n40; B11]

Jains and Muslims in the late medieval villages

Two other kinds of late medieval evidence show who lived in the villages. A Surya image from Gass is inscribed with a date of 1412, and seven inscribed bronze images of Jain Tirthankaras dated between 1458 and 1555 are held by the Jain congregation of Chalpeth in Agashi. [n42; Shah 2020] [n02] Research in 2019 found thirteenth- to sixteenth-century inscriptions showing a Jain Modha-nyati clan at Sopara. [n03; Dalal 2019] So there was a Jain community around Sopara, Gass and Agashi right up to the arrival of the Portuguese.

The Muslim traders of the coast, the Naitas, had settled in the eighth to eleventh centuries; the Portuguese physician Garcia d’Orta mentions “Naitas of Bassein” who married local women and were rich traders when the Portuguese came. [n37; Gaz. XIII-I pp. 216–217] At Sopara the heritage survey of 2008 records a dargah of Rehmatullah Alleya dated 1555 and the dargah of Hazrat Diwan Shah, and at Gass the Gazetteer recorded the tomb of the saint Shaikh Akbar-moi-ud-din bordered with very large old bricks. [n40; A15; CRIT Vol I items 8–11] [n42; Gazetteer XIV p. 341] At Pali, south-east of Bassein, the survey noted a sixteenth-century dargah with a step well, and observed that “before the Portuguese domination” there was “significant Muslim population due to the traders and merchants who had settled here”. [n40; B5; CRIT Vol I items 107–109]

Bassein: born a port, made a fortress

The name

The town that the Gujarat sultans made important had a plain name. The Gazetteer explained it as “Vasai, that is the settlement”; the Muslims called it Basai and the Portuguese Baçaim. Da Cunha thought the “most plausible” root was Sanskrit vas, “to dwell”, and mentioned a conjecture “Vanavasai”, “forest residence”. [n37; Gaz. XIV p. 28 note; da Cunha p. 137] If the explanation is right, the name itself says that Vasai was born as a settlement, a place where people stayed, not a holy site or a royal seat.

The first certain notice, 1507

The Gujarat sultan Mahmud Begada (1459–1511) brought the town into the light. The Mirat-i-Ahmadi says that in 1503 he “effected his designs against Bassein and Bombay”. In about 1505–07 Bassein timber was sent to Egypt to build the Mamluk fleet against the Portuguese, and in 1507, after the Portuguese “apparently stirred up the Hindu chiefs”, Begada “settled disturbances at Bassein”. [n03; TG-II pp. 442–443, 449] The places volume of the Gazetteer gives 1507 as its first “certain notice” of the town. [n37; Gaz. XIV p. 28]

By 1514 it was a busy port. The Portuguese writer Duarte Barbosa described “Baxay” as “a town of Moors and Gentiles, a good seaport belonging to the king of Gujarat”, with much shipping, including boats from Malabar laden with areca, coconuts and spices. [n03; TG-XIV p. 28] Why there? The research notes give the answer: easy water access, a good landing and a custom house under the Gujarat sultans, at the mouth of the deep Bassein creek where the Ulhas meets the sea. [n37; section 6] When the Sopara creek was silting into a ford, the Bassein creek could still take sea-going ships. The town that rose here was a Gujarat customs port, and its population, as Barbosa saw, was mixed: Muslims (“Moors”) and Hindus (“Gentiles”).

Malik Tokan’s citadel, 1532–33

The Portuguese came first as raiders. They had a factory, a trading post, at Bassein by 1526. In 1528 or 1529 Heitor da Silveira, having destroyed a Gujarat fleet near Bandra, entered the difficult Bassein river by night, stormed its cannon-defended works, beat 3,500 men under Alishah, and “plundered and burnt Bassein”. [n03; TG-II pp. 450–451] To put a stop to these raids, in 1532 (early 1533 in Portuguese sources) Sultan Bahadur Shah ordered Malik Tokan, governor of Diu and son of the famous Malik Ayaz, to fortify Bassein. Tokan built a citadel with ramparts and a salt-water ditch and garrisoned it, the Gazetteer says, with 15,000 men (12,000 in the other volume). Nuno da Cunha came with 150 sail and about 4,000 men; Diogo da Silveira and Manuel de Macedo scaled the walls; the defenders fled; the Portuguese took 400 guns, razed the citadel and left. [n03; TG-XIV pp. 28–29; TG-II p. 451] Bocarro, writing in 1634, pointed out a small round bastion by the pillory yard inside the later Portuguese fort, then unmanned, and called it the Moors’ former residence. [n32; DC 217; BG 39]

So the first fortress at Bassein was Malik Tokan’s, and it lasted a year. What stands now is Portuguese work begun in 1536. [n03; inferences]

The cession, 1534

Squeezed between the Mughal emperor Humayun and the Portuguese, Bahadur Shah signed Bassein away. The standard date is 23 December 1534, aboard the galleon São Mateus; the Gazetteer gives 1533, and a further treaty of 25 October 1535 confirmed it. [n03; section 5] [n04; Wikipedia: Treaty of Bassein] Bahadur ceded “Bassein and its dependencies”, with its revenues, on terms that tell us what the town was for: every Gujarat ship bound for the Red Sea had to call at Bassein for a Portuguese pass and call again on the return to pay the customary dues; all horses from Arabia were to come to Bassein for three years after the fortress was finished; and the 5,000 larins formerly paid from Bassein’s revenues to the mosques were to continue. [n36; Danvers vol. I p. 416] The dependencies ceded included Salsette, Bombay, Parel, Wadala, Sion, Worli, Mazagaon, Thana, Bandra and Mahim. [n03; Wikipedia: Fort Vasai] For the next 127 years the seven islands of Bombay were a minor dependency of Bassein. [n04; inferences]

In 1534 the Portuguese “found the land guarded by stockades and fortified posts”. Revenue was taken in kind, with cash cesses on coconut oil, opium, cotton, palm spirit, vegetables, fish, sugarcane and betel, and on butchers, dyers, fishermen and shepherds. [n03; TG-II p. 451] In 1535 the first half-year’s revenue of the Bassein district came to 158,475 fedeas. [n36; Da Cunha pp. 158–159] That list of taxes is, in effect, a portrait of the villages the Portuguese took over: palm groves and toddy tappers, cotton and sugarcane fields, fishing hamlets and herders.

Agashi: the timber port

While Bassein was becoming a customs town, Agashi, ten miles to the north, was a port of a different kind.

The Gazetteer called Agashi “probably an old timber trade and ship-building centre”. It stands on a bay whose mouth “is closed by a reef except one channel”, “full of shoals and navigable only by boats and small vessels”, and in 1882 it was linked to Virar station by a metalled road. [n11; Gaz. 1882 p. 1] [n36; Gaz XIV p. 1] Its timber came from the forests inland; its shipwrights were good enough that a writer of about 1630 said it “built ships as good as Portuguese ships, able to make the voyage to Europe”. [n37; Gaz. XIV pp. 1–2] Between 1550 and 1600, the Gazetteer says elsewhere, “great ships built at Agashi and Bassein made many voyages to Europe”. [n36; Gaz XIII-II pp. 468–469] A modern geographer adds that Agashi built ships of up to 500 tons for both Marathas and Portuguese. [n02; Shah 2020, citing Arunachalam 2002]

When was Agashi founded? No source says. Its name has no recorded etymology, and the research for this book found none. [n40; A2] The oldest dated object linked to it is the 1150 Vatar stone, which was found there but names another village. [n40; A2] What can be said is that by 1530 it was a large enough shipbuilding town for the Portuguese to burn it with three hundred boats, and that Antonio de Saldanha burned it again on the way back from Diu in 1531. [n03; TG-II p. 451] [n11; Gaz. 1882 pp. 1–2]

Arnala: a fort on Cows’ Island

At the northern end of the island, guarding the southern mouth of the Vaitarna, “the most extensive inlet in the north Konkan”, lies a small island now called Arnala, or Janjira, “the Island”. [n11; Gaz. 1882 p. 10] “About 1530, the Portuguese found Arnala a strong fort in the hands of the Gujarat Musalmans with domes and Saracenic arches.” [n37; Gaz. XIV pp. 10–11] Who built it, the Gazetteer does not say. Wikipedia attributes the first fort to Mahmud Begada in 1516, a date this book cannot confirm from a primary source. [n11; Wikipedia: Arnala Fort] [n39; Incredible India] The safest statement is that a Gujarat fort stood on the island before 1530, built to watch the river mouth.

The Portuguese destroyed its Muslim buildings and “made over [the place] to a Portuguese gentleman of Bassein, who built an oblong square about 700 feet with a round tower”. They called the island Ilha das Vacas, “Cows’ Island”. [n37; Gaz. XIV pp. 10–11 and note] In 1727–30 the coloria of Agashi and the Ilha das Vacas, a lease of some revenue the source does not define, was held by Dona Domingas Pereira de Azevedo, who also held the pacaria of Agashi. [n40; A2; Tombo fl. 41–42] Arnala in the Portuguese age was a fort and a revenue holding; nothing in the sources speaks of a fishing village on its shore at that time.

Baçaim: a city built inside a wall, 1536–1554

Choosing the site

At the end of March 1536, the Portuguese governor Nuno da Cunha “went to Bassein”, the English historian Danvers wrote, “found all the materials ready … and having fixed upon a site” began the fortress. [n37; Danvers vol. 1; Gaz. XIV pp. 28–29] The oldest inscription on the walls, on a ruined bastion near the citadel, reads in Portuguese: “The first Captain who built this fortress was Garcia de Sá, by command of the Governor Nuno da Cunha, in the year 1536.” Da Cunha found it in the nineteenth century with the help of the resident lessee of the fort, J. H. Littlewood. [n32; DC 217; BG 39] Wikipedia adds that António Galvão laid the first cornerstone, which this chapter cannot confirm from a primary text. [n04; Vasai Fort, Wikipedia]

The site chosen was the shore at the mouth of the Bassein creek, beside the place where Malik Tokan’s citadel had stood. The research does not record why that exact spot was chosen beyond the obvious: deep water for the customs ships the treaty required to call, and the old landing and customs place of the sultans. [n37; section 6]

Building the city

What the Portuguese built over the next eighteen years was not a fort with a town beside it, but a town inside a fort. Hull, copying da Cunha, wrote that “the great fort was founded in 1535, and became the official residence of the General of the North. Within its walls the city contained many sumptuous edifices … five convents, a cathedral wrongly so-called, in reality a matriz … six other churches, and one asylum for orphans and maidens.” [n37; Hull vol. 1 pp. 4–5] A stone on the bastion of São Sebastião records that it was built on 22 February 1554, under King João III, Viceroy Dom Afonso de Noronha and Captain Francisco de Sá. Wikipedia gives the same date as the completion of “a new fortress … enclosing a whole town”. [n32; BG 42–43] [n04; Wikipedia]

In 1882 the wall ran about a mile and a half round, an irregular decagon of stone thirty to thirty-five feet high (up to forty-five on the west), with a four-sided bastion at each of its ten corners, two main double gateways and a postern. The Sea Gate, Porta do Mar, had massive teak doors cased in iron bars and spikes, one of them dated 20 November 1720; the Land Gate was the Porta do Campo. [n32; BG 38–39] Another passage in the same Gazetteer counts eleven bastions, in a late-sixteenth-century description, and says that fort-building began again in 1590 and was finished “about the close of the century”. [n32; BG 30, 34 n. 8] The walls the visitor sees are therefore the work of more than one campaign.

The stone for the walls came partly from Sopara. “During the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, the lake banks were stripped and the temples pulled down, and the stones used in building Portuguese churches, forts, and houses”, including “the great walls … of Bassein”. [n36; Gaz XIV p. 323] [n37; Gaz. XIV p. 323] In a literal sense, the old town of the island went into the foundations of the new one on the creek. Indraji wrote in 1882 that the Portuguese court at Bassein “took from Sopara its former importance”. [n36; Indraji 1882 p. 279]

Inside the walls

Inside, the seaward half of the walled space was built up and the landward third was left empty. Da Cunha offered three possible reasons: a free field of fire, a rice field to feed a garrison under siege, or depopulation after the late-seventeenth-century plague. [n32; DC 213; BG 38] The built part held a great square with a market and the State House, where in 1675 the English traveller Fryer saw the governor meet the nobles each morning; the citadel, with a portal dated 1606, beyond which lay the palaces of the General of the North and the Captain, the factory and a great rice granary; the matriz of St Joseph, rebuilt in 1601 on the site of a church of about 1546; the Jesuit college, whose foundation was laid in 1548; the Franciscan church of Santo António; the Dominican church of São Gonçalo of 1583; the Augustinian chapel; the Misericórdia church and hospital; and the church of Nossa Senhora da Vida, “one of the oldest churches in Bassein”. [n32; BG 38–43; DC 213–232] The St Joseph church of 1546 was paid for, according to King João III’s letter of March 1546, with the 3,000 pardaos that had once maintained the mosques. [n32; BG 38–39] [n37; da Cunha pp. 215–216]

The walled city had a population that can be counted once. In 1634, by a source cited on Wikipedia, about 400 Portuguese families, 200 Indian Christian families and 1,800 slaves lived there; Bassein had 63 friars that year, 30 Franciscans, 15 Jesuits, 10 Dominicans and 8 Augustinians. [n04; Vasai Fort; Bombay East Indians, Wikipedia] [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 462] It was, the Gazetteer said, home to “more than 100 families of the highest in India and proverbially rich”. [n36; Gaz XIV p. 29 n. 6] Hindus and Muslims were not part of the walled city’s recorded population in the sources; they lived outside.

Baçaim de Cima and the district

Outside the walls lay a second Bassein. Da Cunha and the Gazetteer both record that the Tanadar-mor of Baçaim, the officer in charge of the countryside, lived at “Baçaim de Cima”, Upper Bassein, outside the walls. [n32; DC 221–222; BG 31] Where exactly Baçaim de Cima stood, and what it looked like, the sources do not say. Its existence shows that the Portuguese town had two parts from the start: the walled city of the nobility, clergy and soldiers, and an upper town or suburb where the administration of the villages was carried on.

The district the fort governed was large. The Baçaim district stretched about ten miles north to the Agashi river, about twenty-four miles east to Asserim and Manora, and about forty miles south to Karanja. [n32; BG 30 n. 5] Da Cunha listed the units of the Province of the North around its capital: the Saibana de Baçaim, the Caçabe de Tana, the island of Salsette, the island of Karanja, the island of Bellaflor de Sambayo, the Pragana de Manora, the Pragana de Asserim and the island of Bombay. He explained the terms: caçabé, from Persian kasaba, a town district; pragana, the Indian pargana; mandovim, the customs house; hortas, the orchards. [n32; DC 157–158] There was, interestingly, no royal customs house at Bassein; a private house was rented for the purpose at 84 pardaos a year. [n32; DC 222–223]

Agashi under the Portuguese

Agashi was the first place of the Bassein district that the Franciscans visited, da Cunha wrote. [n37; da Cunha p. 159] In 1535 the Franciscan António do Porto founded an orphanage for forty boys there, Nossa Senhora da Luz. In 1540 a Gujarat force under a general the Portuguese called “Bramaluc” came through, and, by Portuguese accounts, burned the orphans who refused to become Muslims; da Cunha called them a “batch of Christian martyrs … consisting entirely of native converts”. [n40; A2; daCunha pp. 159–160] In 1554 “a great mosque became the commandant’s residence”. [n40; A2; GzXIV pp. 1–2] In 1570 “Agazim” was listed as a port trading with Gujarat. In 1618 a hurricane blew a boat from the sea into a house, killing a woman and a child. In 1634 Mughal raiders from Gujarat destroyed “a handsome Dominican monastery”. [n11; Gaz. 1882 pp. 1–2]

Agashi had a fortress with a customs house, whose captain was paid 30,000 reis and its customs clerk 20,000 reis a year. [n36; Da Cunha pp. 158–159] The customs and town revenue survive for a run of years: 281,249 fedeas in 1636, 313,874 in 1637, 162,822 in 1638, then 176,876, 199,400 and 216,400 in 1640–42, and only 38,800 in 1643. [n36; Gaz XIV p. 1 n. 4] Somewhere in those years the port’s fortunes broke, though the sources do not say exactly why.

The parish church of St James at Agashi is dated 1568 by the heritage survey of 2008, which calls it the first church outside the fort; the East Indian village page says that around 1568 two churches stood at Agashi, Our Lady of Light under the Franciscans and St James under the secular clergy. [n40; A2; CRIT Vol III asset 44; EI Agashi] The 1882 Gazetteer described the church then standing as “built after the destruction of Nossa Senhora da Luz”, seventy-five feet long. [n40; A2; GzXIV p. 2] The Agashi of the Portuguese, then, had a fort, a customs house, a converted mosque, a Franciscan orphanage, a Dominican monastery, two churches and shipyards, and a Hindu and Jain population that the records barely mention. In the Tombo of 1727–30 it was the head of its own caçabé, with twenty-eight titles. [n40; Tombo pp. 338–339]

The aldeias: villages as Crown property

The Tombo of 1727–1730

The Portuguese did not, on the whole, found villages. They took over the villages they found, renamed them in Portuguese spelling, and let them out. The clearest picture of how this worked comes at the end of the period, in the Tombo de Baçaim of 1727–1730, a register of the Crown’s leases published in 2007 by André Teixeira and Silvana Pires. It lists 362 titles: 36 in the caçabé of Baçaim, 28 in the caçabé of Agaçaim, and 4 in Sopará. [n40; Tombo pp. 338–339] The holders were Portuguese fidalgos, widows of fidalgos and the Jesuits, not the villagers. Grants were made, nominally, for three lives, and “no right in the land was conceded to the husbandmen or tenants”. [n39; section 1.B] [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 460]

The Tombo gives many of the villages of this chapter their first Portuguese-era record. The spellings are the Tombo’s; the matching to modern villages is the research note’s, made by sound and place, and is not certain in every case. [n40; Tombo note]

  • Pacaria “Gris”, held “in three lives” by Dona Margarida Teles de Meneses for 166 pardaus 4 larins, the first title of the Baçaim caçabé (fl. 23). This is Giriz. A “horta Vitoba of the wild palms which is in the pacaria Gris” was held by a man with an Indian name, “Jasaman Naique” (fl. 53), a rare trace of a local landholder. [n40; B9]
  • Pacaria “Sandor”, held in enfiteuse by Jorge da Silva Coelho for 435 pardaus (fl. 29v). [n40; B1]
  • Pacaria “Bolinja e terra Belvar”, held for lives by Dona Maria Henriques Coelho da Silva for 450 pardaus (fl. 54v). [n40; A11]
  • “Vatar Rajoly”, held by Manuel Varjão da Fonseca for 700 pardaus, the highest rent in its list (fl. 45v). [n40; A13]
  • “Mardes Sirgoly”, held by Lucas de Almeida de Andrade for 180 pardaus (fl. 47v). [n40; A8]
  • “Vagoly”, held by Manuel de Abreu de Lima (fl. 41v), and “Vastal Vagoly”, by Fernão da Silveira e Meneses for 60 pardaus (fl. 45). [n40; A7]
  • “Cholna Roba”, held by the Jesuit Procurador Geral for 130 pardaus (fl. 49). This is Chulne. [n40; B7]
  • “Agaçaim”, held by Dona Domingas Pereira de Azevedo (fl. 42), with the coloria of Agaçaim and the Ilha das Vacas (fl. 41). [n40; A2]
  • “Nala Magir”, held by Miguel Francisco Mira de Vasconcelos (fl. 43). This is the first appearance of Nala as a Portuguese holding. [n40; A15]
  • “Umella”, held by Francisco Pereira de Sousa for 157 pardaus (fl. 26v). This is Umela. [n40; B10]
  • Aldeia “Virar Naringuy”, held by Dom Pedro de Meneses for 2,000 pardaus, the highest rent among the aldeias (fl. 121). Naringi survives as a ward name in Virar. [n40; B11]
  • “Boroly Mulgão”, held by Fernão de Brito de Melo for 333 pardaus (fl. 24v), which may be Mulgaon. [n40; B10]
  • Aldeia “Palle”, held by Dona Maria Correia de Macedo (fl. 172). [n40; B5]
  • Aldeia “Sircar”, taken over on 7 May 1727 by the licenciado André Toscano (fl. 136v). [n39; section 1.B]
  • “Maroy” and “Naucer e Cascarida”, held by the Jesuits. [n40; section 2]

Two observations follow. First, Virar was the richest village of the northern aldeias by rent, and Vatar the richest pacaria of the Agashi group. Second, the people named are the landlords, not the founders or the tillers. Whether any of the Christian families of the Kupari and Vadval villages took their surnames from these landholders, as the 1931 Census said village tenants in this region sometimes did, is not documented for any family. [n39; inferences; Census 1931]

What the Tombo does not show

Gass does not appear in the Tombo under any spelling the research could find; neither does Bhuigaon, nor Umrale. [n42; section 3] [n40; A4, A9] That does not mean they did not exist; they may have been parts of larger holdings or hidden under spellings no one has matched. The Portuguese name of Gass is unknown. [n42; gaps]

Stockades and towers: the island defended

The Portuguese ringed their villages with small defences, and several modern places owe a ruin or a name to them.

At Sopara, in 1535, the old town had “four wooden stockades”; the 1728 report still called it “a fortified post with four wooden stockades”. [n36; Da Cunha pp. 158–159] [n37; Gaz. XIV p. 322] The town that had been the capital of a district was now an outpost of Bassein. A Portuguese tower and an old landing-place stood at the Bhatela pond, and the 1728 report complained that a hill near Sopara called the Nil hill had been fortified “without an engineer”, with bastions too small for a four-pounder gun. [n31; Gazetteer, citing O Chronista de Tissuary]

At Achole, a mile and a half north of Manikpur, there was a Portuguese fort that the 1882 Gazetteer sent visitors to see in the afternoon. [n12; Gaz 1882] At Virar, fifty yards south of the station in 1882, stood the remains of a Portuguese church and well, and on a knoll about a mile south “a notable Portuguese tower or fortified mansion”; on Jivdhan hill and on another rocky hill to the east were the ruins of fortifications. [n12; Gaz 1882 p. 382] At Gokhivare (Gokirva) there was a shrine of Mahadev, a pond, “and the remains of an old Portuguese stockade”, and on a hillock at the foot of Tungar the ruins of a Portuguese tower, “probably built as a defence against the attacks of the Jawhar chief and his Kolis, who were very formidable to the Portuguese, ‘leaping like monkeys from tree to tree’”. [n12; Gaz 1882 pp. 366–367]

The 1728 report described the whole system as it decayed: General Louis de Mello Pereira had built watch-towers of coconut-palm stems and a bridge across the creek at Gokirva; the bridge was unprotected, and the creek could also be crossed at Timli. [n12; Gaz 1882] The towers faced east, towards the hills. The island’s defences were built against the people of the forest and the Maratha uplands, and they mark the line where the Portuguese garden villages met the country of the Kolis and Varlis.

Kamandurg, the rounded peak at the south end of Tungar, is said by trekking sites to have been taken by the Portuguese from the Marathas on 12 September 1685 and abandoned for want of water; this rests on low-quality sources and Wikipedia says little is known. [n12; durgbharari; trekshitiz]

The parish villages: how churches shaped the villages

Mission and parish

The Portuguese did not found most of the villages of the island, but they gave them a new centre. Between the 1550s and 1606 the religious orders built parish churches outside the walls, and over the next four centuries the parish, rather than the old village boundary, became the unit by which Christian Vasai described itself. The story of how and why people converted belongs to Thread Four and to the Kupari chapters; here the question is the shape of settlement.

The Franciscans came first, from 1534–35, with António do Porto, who is credited with destroying two hundred temples, making over ten thousand converts and building twelve churches. The Jesuits came from 1542 and established their college at Bassein in 1548. Dominicans and Augustinians followed. [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 461] [n04; Catholic Encyclopedia] At many sacred tanks, the Gazetteer says, “temples were thrown down”; at one famous pool between Bassein and Agashi the pool was converted, a chapel to Our Lady of Healing was built, “and the pilgrimage and cure-working continued”. [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 461 note 1] That is one way a sacred Hindu site became a Christian village centre without the village moving.

The dates and their disagreements

A commonly repeated list gives the parishes outside the fort as: “Nirmal (1557), Remedi (1557), Sandor (1566), Agashi (1568), Nandakhal (1573), Papdi (1574), Pali (1595), Manickpur (1606), and Merces (1606)”. [n40; section 2; vasaiker.com] [n04; Vasai Fort, Wikipedia] It is a tidy list, and it is not reliable in its details. For Nirmal, the bell-tower plaque says 1580, the heritage survey says 1586, and the community and a parish directory say 1557 or 1558. [n40; A1] For Remedy the survey says the Dominicans founded the church in 1577. [n40; B3] For Sandor the dates in different sources run 1556, 1564, 1565, 1566, 1571 and 1573. [n40; B1] Hull’s list, published in the twentieth century from the Goa Annuario, gives Papdi as built in 1565, Remedy (“Douli”) in 1583, Sandor in 1575, and Nirmal’s church as rebuilt in 1856 on the ruins of a church of 1580. [n38; Hull I pp. 5–6] None of these is a contemporary document. The safe statement is that the nine parishes outside the fort were founded between the late 1550s and 1606, by the Franciscans, Jesuits, Dominicans and Augustinians, and that each date should be given as the parish’s own or the source’s.

Two belts

What the parishes did was to divide the Christian countryside into two belts that are still visible.

In the north and west, the old garden villages of the Agashi and Sopara agar, the converts were mainly cultivators of the Samvedi community, the people later called Kuparis. Their mother parishes were Nirmal (Holy Cross, Santa Cruz), Agashi (St James) and Nandakhal (Holy Spirit, Espirito Santo). The 1882 Gazetteer, which never uses the word Kupari, said simply that “the husbandmen of Sopara are Christians, many of them converted Samvedi Brahmans”. [n40; section 1; GzXIV pp. 314–315] The community’s own list of its villages names thirteen places: Nirmal, Agashi, Nandakhal, Gass, Bhuigaon, Umrale, Nanbhat, Shirlaiwadi, Bolinj, Mardes, Wagholi, Gomes Ali and Rajodi. [n40; section 1; Rajan D’Mello 2009] Independently, the Gazetteer in 1882 named nine villages where dried plantains were made, “Agáshi, Vagholi, Vatár, Bolinj, Koprád, Nala, Umrále, Rajodi, and Murdes”, which overlaps that list closely. [n40; section 1; GzXIII-1 p. 292]

Nandakhal’s church of the Holy Spirit, built “about 1573” by the Franciscans “primarily for the convenience of those parishioners of Holy Cross who lived closer to Nandakhal”, is a good example of a parish founded for distance. The heritage survey says that although the Franciscans had to give it up in 1739, “the church escaped destruction”; a parish legend says Maratha soldiers who stormed it retreated before a swarm of bees. [n40; A3; CRIT Vol III asset 60b; EI Nandkhal] Da Cunha in 1876 thought it “the oldest among the rural churches, and the only one standing in its primitive state”. [n40; A3; daCunha p. 161] Nandakhal itself has no recorded etymology, and the Franciscan founding document has not been found. [n40; A3]

In the south, around the fort, the parishes of Sandor, Papdi, Remedy, Merces, Pali and Manickpur served a different population: the people the Gazetteer called Vadvals, “Christian cartmen chiefly of Chulna, Mánikpur, Gokhivra, Sándor, and Barhánpur in Bassein”, “the best axemen in the district”. [n40; B7; GzXIII-1 pp. 97–98] Sandor’s St Thomas was “the Mother Church of all the churches of South Bassein”, from which, by parish tradition, Remedy, Papdi, Pali, Merces and Porim (Manickpur) were carved between 1574 and 1606. [n40; B1] Merces, the parish page says, was a village of mostly Koli fisher-folk “nestled between the villages of Sandor and Remedy”; the village “was named after the church, which in Portuguese is Nossa Senhora das Merces”, and before that it was Kaular. [n40; B4] That is one of the few cases in which a village’s present name can be shown to come from its church.

Manickpur and the five pacarias

The best-described parish foundation is Manickpur. “Till 1606 the Catholics of Porim were parishioners of St Thomas, Sandor. It was only at the insistence of the Jesuit Provincial that the Archbishop of Goa, Dom Aleixo de Menezes, decided to make Porim an independent parish.” The first church was “a simple mud and thatch structure”, and the Jesuits at the fort then built a better one “on the spacious grounds in the centre of the five pacarias of Wananapor, Devamany, Porim, Navgaon and Ullmemany”. Its jurisdiction ran to “Gocavarem (Gokhivare), Calmona, Balliam, Sattolli, Achola and Rajaoli”. [n40; B6; EI Manikpur]

Here the parish is explicitly a gathering of separate holdings, five pacarias, around a central church on open ground. The names in the list are recognisable: Gokhivare, Sativali (Sattolli), Achole (Achola), Rajavali (Rajaoli), and perhaps Umele (Ullmemany). The parish church stood in the village of Porim; “the name of this village was changed to Manickpur in the beginning of the 20th century”, the parish page says. [n40; B6] But the Gazetteer already called the railway station “Manikpur” in 1882, and da Cunha wrote “Purim or Manikpura” in 1876, so the new name was in use well before 1900. [n40; B6; daCunha pp. 157, 161] Where the name Manickpur came from, the Gazetteer gives no etymology. [n37; section 6]

Settlements outside the record

The parish records describe the converted villages. They say almost nothing of the Hindu villages that stayed Hindu, of the Muslim traders, or of the fishermen and forest people. The Gazetteer’s later observation that the Christian converts’ castes ran from Brahmans and Prabhus through Pachkalshis, Bhandaris, Kunbis and Kolis to Mhars, that “the bulk were Bhandaris, Kolis and Kunbis”, and that Mhar converts lived outside the villages and could not use the wells, shows that conversion did not erase the old pattern of hamlets by caste. [n40; section 2; GzXIII-1 pp. 199–201] [n37; Gaz. XIII-I pp. 201–202]

And there were losses. A Portuguese writer, Goez, of about 1630 (the Gazetteer gives both 1603 and 1630), said that “the persecution of the Portuguese had made many Hindus, Mussulmans and Parsees abandon their homes and live in the dominion of Shah Jehan … between Bassein and Damaun there are few natives and the greater part of the village lands being uncultivated”. The Gazetteer doubted it and added that “if this account is correct the districts soon recovered their prosperity”. [n37; Gaz. XIII-II pp. 463–464] Whatever its scale, it is a reminder that the Portuguese century emptied some villages as well as building churches in others.

The seventeenth century was hard. In 1618 plague, a cyclone on 15 May and famine struck. In 1634 the Mughal raiders burned Agashi’s Dominican monastery. In 1674 six hundred Muscat Arab raiders plundered the churches outside the walls, by one source flagged as unreliable, and More Pandit forced the Portuguese to pay a quarter of Baçaim’s revenues as chauth. In 1676 Shivaji’s forces advanced near Saiwan. [n04; Wikipedia] Around 1690 a plague the Gazetteer calls “carazzo” emptied cities, and the English traveller Hamilton wrote about 1700 that “Bassein was a place of small trade, its riches dead and buried in the churches”. [n36; Gaz XIII-II pp. 484–485] Yet Bassein’s revenue rose in the early eighteenth century, from £8,646 in 1686 to £45,706 in 1729, and da Cunha gives a population of 60,499 in 1720, of whom 58,131 were native Christians, though both figures come through damaged print. [n32; BG 34 n. 8] [n36; Da Cunha pp. 145–146]

1737–1739: the conquest and the re-founding

Arnala rebuilt

The Maratha conquest began at the northern end of the island. The first assault on Arnala, with Govindji Kasar and the navy of Manaji Angre, failed; the second, under Chimaji Appa, took it, on 28 March 1737 by the date Wikipedia gives. [n11; Wikipedia: Arnala Fort] [n36; Wikipedia] A Marathi inscription over the northern gate says the fort was rebuilt under Bajirao I in Shaka 1659 (1737) by Baji Tulaji (the name is read “Baji Tuklaji” in another note). [n11; Gaz. 1882 pp. 10–11] [n37; Gaz. XIV pp. 10–11] The research notes record that Bajirao had Shankaraji Keshav build the new fort, on Wikipedia’s authority. [n11; Wikipedia] The fort the visitor sees at Arnala today is therefore Maratha work of 1737 on a Gujarat and Portuguese site. In 1882 the Gazetteer ranked it “next to Bassein in size and strength” among the north Konkan’s coast forts. [n37; Gaz. XIV pp. 10–11]

Inside the walls the Marathas placed temples, and alongside them the Muslim shrine of the island survived: in 1882 there were temples of Trimbakeshvar (with a yearly government grant of Rs 45) and Bhavani (Rs 91), and the tomb of two Muslim saints, Shahali and Hajiali (Rs 80 a year), with a stone-lined eight-cornered pond and many wells. [n11; Gaz. 1882 p. 11] The heritage survey dates a Ram Mandir at Arnala to 1806. [n40; C1; CRIT asset 51] Arnala became a national protected monument on 26 May 1909. [n11; Wikipedia]

Bassein falls

The siege of Bassein began on 17 February 1739. After mines breached the wall beneath the bastion of São Sebastião, the garrison surrendered on terms signed on 16 May 1739 (one passage of da Cunha reads 19 May). [n32; DC 148; BG 34] The terms let the garrison march out with the honours of war and gave inhabitants who wished to leave eight days to take their moveable property. [n32; DC 148–149] The Christians were allowed liberty of worship, and the Portuguese, the Gazetteer says, secured only five churches: three in Bassein city, one in the district and one in Salsette. [n37; Hull vol. 1 pp. 75–76; Gaz. XIII-I p. 203]

The countryside’s churches suffered. Manickpur’s was “the first to be demolished by the conquering Maratha army in 1739”. [n40; B6] Nirmal’s Holy Cross was “destroyed during Portuguese-Maratha war in 1739”; its parishioners used their cemetery as their church from 1739 until they rebuilt in 1856. [n40; A1; EI Nirmal plaque] Merces was destroyed and its priest fled. [n40; B4] Agashi’s two churches were destroyed, and St James was “rebuilt soon after” (the heritage survey says 1760). [n40; A2] But Sandor, Papdi and Nandakhal, by their own parish histories, escaped. [n40; A3, B1, B2] The Christian village belt survived the conquest with its people in place, and Anquetil du Perron, passing through in 1757–60, found that “the ordinary people remained, a native priest taking charge of their souls with the approbation of the Mahrattas”. [n37; Hull vol. 1 p. 76; Gaz. XIV pp. 1–2]

Bajipur and the policy of resettlement

The Marathas renamed the town. Bassein became “Bajipur”, Baji Rao’s city, and the seat of a governor whose charge ran from the Bankot river to Daman. [n32; BG 34; DC 149] And they set out, deliberately, to re-found a Hindu population. “To restore a Hindu population grants of rent-free land were offered, and a tax was levied for the support of Brahmans who were brought to purify the Christians and make them fit to take their place in their old castes.” [n37; Gaz. XIV p. 34] This is one of the few documented settlement policies in the whole history of the taluka: land offered free to bring people in, and priests brought to restore them to caste.

The walled city did not revive. Du Perron and Tieffenthaler (1750) said Bassein “never counted more than 3,500 inhabitants” after the fall, though 30,000 lived in the country around. [n37; Hull vol. 1 p. 76] The life of the district moved outside the walls.

Papdi: a Brahman settlement beside the fort

The clearest example of the new settlement is Papdi. In 1876 da Cunha described “Papari, a village about a cannon-shot from Bajipura, chiefly inhabited by Brahmans of Chitpawan, Karada, and Deshastha divisions, as well as Palshas, Sonars, and other inferior castes of the Hindus”, with an annual revenue of Rs 1,80,630. [n40; B2; daCunha p. 157] [n32; DC 156–157] These are Deccan and Konkan Brahman communities of the kind the Peshwa’s government brought to administer and sanctify its conquest. The heritage survey adds that “although Papdy was known as a famous shipbuilding centre, the present fabric of Papdy shows palimpsests from the 18th century when it was given as a jagir to the community which transported goods, supplies and food to the Maratha army”, and records a Ram Mandir whose original “marked Maratha dominance 1739–1818”. [n40; B2; CRIT Vol II assets 36–42] The research note suggests that the carting community may have been the Vadval carters of the Gazetteer, but the survey does not name it. [n40; B2, inference]

Yet Papdi was also the seat of the Jesuit parish of Our Lady of Grace, founded for “poor farmers” from Davly (Remedy), part of Sandor and a place called Maferegua, and its church was not destroyed in 1739. [n40; B2; EI Papdy; CRIT sheet 40] So eighteenth-century Papdi held, side by side, a Brahman settlement of the Peshwa’s era and a Christian farming parish. In 1882 its parish had 1,294 people and a church rebuilt in 1865; in 1998 that church became the cathedral of the new Diocese of Vasai. [n40; B2]

The temple sites re-founded

The Marathas’ other great settlement work was the rebuilding of the old sacred places, and the man most associated with it is Shankarji Keshav (Phadke), the Peshwa’s Sarsubhedar of Bassein.

At Nirmal, “the Portuguese had pulled down the temples and destroyed the ling. On the fall of Bassein (1739) Nirmal was purified, prints of the feet of Shri Dattatraya took the place of the ling, and a reservoir was built.” Eight temples were built about 1750 by Shankarji Keshav: the Shankaracharya Svami temple, with a government grant of Rs 421 a year in 1882; Mahavishnu (Rs 118); Vimaleshvar Mahadev (Rs 100); Bhuteshvar (Rs 60); Ganpati, Durga and Hanuman together (Rs 35); and Siddheshvar (Rs 25), with grants to a Brahman annachhatra (Rs 454) and a Bairagi alms-house (Rs 148). The Gazetteer thought stones the Portuguese had built into churches “were probably used to raise the temples of the older gods”. [n40; A1; GzXIV pp. 292–293] A blog of 2019 credits Chimaji Appa with the rebuilding, contradicting the Gazetteer; the heritage survey’s “early 17th century … patron: Peshwa” cannot be right as stated. [n40; A1] So Nirmal, by 1750, was two settlements on one hill and its flats: a Christian parish whose church lay in ruins and whose people worshipped in their cemetery, and a Hindu temple town of eight shrines and two alms-houses. “Under the Marathas,” the Gazetteer said of the whole district, “Brahmanism revived, and except those that are more modern, most of the present Hindu temples date from the eighteenth century.” [n37; Gaz. XIII-I p. 66]

At Tungar, the temple of Tungareshvar, a group of four square buildings in a small valley, was “rebuilt about a hundred and thirty years ago [about 1750] by Shankarji Keshav, the celebrated Maratha Sarsubhedar of Bassein”. The Peshwa granted the shrine a plot of about three square miles, and the British continued the grant. [n12; Gaz 1882 p. 367] Stones carved with the sun, the moon and human figures lay in a nearby stream bed. The Gazetteer recorded a path that rose from Sativali “through the Varli hamlet of Dhondvira to the temple of Shri-Tungareshvar, about five miles from the foot of the hill”. [n12; Gaz 1882 p. 367] So the shrine sat in the forest among Varli hamlets, endowed with land by a government in Poona.

At Agashi, the large temple of Bhavani Shankar is said by the Gazetteer to have been built “in AD 1691 (Shak 1613) by Shankarji Keshav Phadke”, with a holy bathing place supposed to cure skin diseases, a Hanuman temple by the same man, and smaller Vishnu and Ganpati temples. [n40; A2; GzXIV p. 2] The date is a problem: Shankarji Keshav is documented as the Maratha commander of about 1737–1750, and the heritage survey labels these temples “18th century, patron Peshwa”. The 1691 date may be a slip. [n40; A2] A local site says the temple was built between 1739 and 1750 on the site of a Bimb-era fort named in the Mahikavati Bakhar, and that large stones and old idols were found when the lake was dug; it is a single local source. [n11; Durg Bharari] Around the Agashi tank the survey recorded a Khanolkar House, said to be a Peshwa guest house, and Phadkewada, built by Subhedar Phadke and “lost to high-rise construction” by 2019. [n40; A2; CRIT] Agashi became a Maratha base after 1737, and its Hindu quarter around the tank took the shape it kept into the twentieth century. [n40; A2]

At Bassein itself, inside the empty fort, the Marathas left a Hanuman temple just inside the Sea Gate, which da Cunha thought “mean architecture” and read as a memento of conquest, and a Trivikram temple, which in 1882 still enjoyed a yearly government allowance of Rs 1,061. [n32; BG 37–39; DC 214]

Beyond the taluka, the temple of Vajreshwari at Vadavli on the Tansa “is said to have been built about the beginning of the last century by … Shankarji Keshav … to commemorate a victory over the Portuguese”; popular accounts tie it to Chimaji Appa’s vow before the siege. [n03; TG-XIV Vajrabai entry]

These temples did not create new villages so much as re-centre old ones. The Hindu sacred geography that the Portuguese had broken was rebuilt within about fifteen years of the conquest, on the same hills and beside the same tanks, sometimes with the same stones.

Traders from Cambay

The other new settlers of the Maratha years were merchants. In about 1760, Gujarat Vanis of the Lad subdivision came from Cambay, “when its traders fled from the exactions of Momin Khan II”, and with them several Gujarat Brahmans; they settled in Sopara, Bassein and other coast towns. [n37; Gaz. XIV p. 315] [n36; Gaz XIII-I p. 63] In 1882 Lad Vani families lived at Nala, Agashi and Papdi, and in 1837 Vaupell found Bassein’s shops mostly held by Gujarati Vanis and a few Muslims. [n05; Gazetteer snippets] [n28; G-XIV] Agashi’s Jain temple of Parasnath was built “about sixty years ago”, that is, around the 1820s, “by Motilal a Vani of Bombay”. [n40; A2; GzXIV p. 2] A Parsi rest-house was built at Bassein in 1780 by Lavji Sorabji Lakdawala, and another in 1836 by Dadabhai and Mancherji Pestanji Wadia. [n11; Gaz. 1882 pp. 36–37] The trading quarters of Sopara, Agashi and Bassein in the nineteenth century were partly the creation of this Gujarati migration.

Du Perron’s island, 1760

The best eyewitness of the island in the Maratha years is the French scholar Anquetil du Perron. He reached Agashi on the church feast day, 16 December 1760, “par les plus beaux vergers du monde”, through the finest orchards in the world, and found “the lanes full of people going to church as freely as in a Christian country”. [n40; A2; da Cunha p. 164; GzXIV p. 2] He noticed Vagholi “with a creek of its own”, and he noted Vatar under the name “Outar”. [n40; A7, A13] His island was a landscape of orchards, Christian lanes and creeks, under a Maratha governor, with Hindu temples newly raised on the old sites. That is roughly the pattern of settlement that the British would find.

Kolis, bandars and padas: the settlements without founders

Before following the British, it is necessary to say something about the settlements this chapter can least explain: the fishing landings of the Kolis and the hamlets of the forest peoples. The honest summary of the research is that “founding story of specific Koli bandars (Pachu, Arnala fishing village, Bhuigaon jetty) and individual adivasi padas: no reliable source found”. [n37; section 6]

The fishing people and their landings

What can be said is this. The 1882 Gazetteer described the sea-fishing mass of the coast south of the Vaitarna as the Son Kolis, about 8,000 in the district, “probably a tribe of Kolis who have mixed with foreign settlers from beyond the sea”, who said their tribe had come originally from Alibag, where their chief, the Vagh Patil, lived. In Bassein, it added, “the Uraps and other Christian Kolis … are professional fishermen”. The Vaitis, 4,596 strong, lived “in north Bassein and south Mahim”; they were fishers, cultivators, sailors and a few timber and hay dealers who “chiefly worship Ram and the sea”. The Machhis of Bassein spoke Gujarati at home and hired out boats. The Mangelas, 10,718, lived in Mahim, Dahanu and Salsette; in 1881 the Bassein list counted 2,375 Mangelas and 77 Machhis. [n08; Gazetteer 1882 pp. 147–149, 182] [n21; Gaz XIII-II p. 680] Before 1844 Kolis, Mangelas and Vaitis paid a fishing poll tax. In 1851 the district had 129 salt-water fisheries, 22 of them in Bassein. [n36; section 7]

The three Bassein ports in 1881–82 were Agashi, Bassein and Navghar, with local vessels of fifty to seventy tons “built locally by Marathas and Native Christians” and visiting craft anchoring “twenty-five to 200 feet from the landing at Bassein, and 100 to 200 feet at Agashi”. [n36; Gaz XIII-I pp. 358–359] Navghar, which would later give half its name to the Navghar-Manikpur municipal council, was in 1882 a port of the Bassein group; when it began, no source says. [n21; Gaz XIII-I] Sasunavghar, “about six miles east of Bassein”, was “a small port” on the creek. [n12; Gaz 1882]

For the modern fishing settlements the record is thinner still. Killa Bandar, the fort landing at Vasai, appears in the notes only as the bus-route name “Killa Bunder” and as a village beside the fort; no reliable source on its fishing life was reached. [n32; section 5] [n11; Wikipedia pointer] Pachu Bandar appears in a modern list of Mangela Koli villages, with Vasai and Arnala, and on property listings as “Pachu Bandar Road, Vasai West”. [n08; Wikipedia: Mangela Kolis] [n11; NoBroker] A Koliwada of Vasai West is listed among the region’s Koli settlements. [n11; Wikipedia: Koliwada] Naigaon’s fishing and salt-pan past is likewise unrecorded in the sources: the research found nothing on its name’s origin or its early history, and it has no entry in the 1882 Gazetteer. [n12; section 5] [n37; section 6] The Agris, who worked the salt pans, lived in villages the Gazetteer named only in part: the Dasagri villages of Bassein were “Kofar, Chandve, Navsai, Khanivde”, and in 1881 Dublas worked the salt-works near Bassein Road station in the fair season. [n08; Gazetteer 1882 p. 116] [n28; G-I, Salt]

Arnala’s fishing village is a case where the modern settlement has eclipsed the fort. In 2011 the mainland village of Arnala had 15,331 people and the fort island, Arnala Killa, 2,566; a study describes Arnala as “one of the major dol net landing centres in Thane district”. [n11; census2011.co.in] [n36; ICSF] The Koli congregation of Arnala, once attached to Our Lady of Light and then St James at Agashi, got its own chapel only in the twentieth century: Fr Ismail Da Costa built a hut chapel near the beach in 1919, a tin shed dedicated to St Peter followed in 1921 “with the Kolis’ help”, and Archbishop Joaquim Lima blessed the church on 27 December 1931; a Hindu community association gave Rs 3,000, and “Kolis consider Fr Da Costa as the true Apostle of Arnala”. [n40; C1; EI Arnala] Arnala was also the centre of the Salt and Forest Satyagraha of 1929–30, marked by a Gandhi memorial. [n40; C1; CRIT Vol I p. 9] But when Arnala’s mainland village first grew up beside the landing, the sources do not say.

The forest hamlets

The Adivasi hamlets, the padas, are as old as anything on this coast and as undocumented. The Varlis, “probably originally Varalis or uplanders”, numbered 6,099 in Bassein in 1872; the section called Nihiris belonged to south Mahim, Bassein, Jawhar and Vada, and “their love for the forests is so great that … they cannot be induced to go so far from their woods”. [n08; Gazetteer 1882 pp. 182–183] Kathkaris and Thakurs also lived in the eastern hills. [n08; section 5] The Gazetteer names one Varli hamlet, Dhondvira, on the path from Sativali up Tungar. [n12; Gaz 1882 p. 367] It names the Saturday market at Virar, “thronged by Kolis and Varlis, selling fish, garden stuff, and cloth”. [n12; Gaz 1882 p. 382] And it records, through the Portuguese tower at Gokhivare, the Jawhar chief “and his Kolis” in the hills. [n12; Gaz 1882 pp. 366–367] The heritage survey of 2008 notes a talav at Gokhivare “near an adivasi pada”. [n40; B8; CRIT item 122] The research found “no verified sources on specific padas (Kaman, Pelhar, Bhatane, Chandip)”. [n08; section 5, gaps]

So the forest and fishing settlements of Vasai have no founding dates, no founders and almost no written history before the twentieth century. They are present in the record mainly at its edges: in a tower built against them, a market where they sold fish, a hamlet passed on a path to a temple. Their later history, of moneylender bondage already hinted at in 1882 and of the forest sanctuary and the encroaching city, belongs to the end of this chapter. [n08; inferences]

The Company’s island: ruins and markets, 1780–1860

The fort abandoned

Colonel Goddard took Bassein for the British on 11 December 1780 after a twelve-day siege, and in 1781 the British prepared to bombard Arnala “from Agashi” before its commandant yielded. [n05; Wikipedia: Vasai] [n11; Gaz. 1882 p. 11] Under the Treaty of Salbai both went back to the Marathas. On 17 December 1802 Peshwa Baji Rao II, fleeing Holkar, reached Bassein, and on 31 December he signed the Treaty of Bassein; a palm-tree stockade was built “at the Sopara bridge” to protect the passage to the island. [n28; G-XIV; G-II] By 1817–18 the whole north Konkan was British. [n28; G-XIV]

The walled city was already dead. In 1818 there was “scarcely a house habitable”; in 1824 the fort was uninhabited and locked, with a small garrison under a conductor of ordnance; in 1825 Bishop Heber found it “perfectly uninhabited”, ruined houses and churches under brushwood; in 1838 Mrs Postans found only a few fishermen and hunters. [n28; G-XIV] The town that the Portuguese had built inside a wall had moved out of it entirely.

Malonde: the new Bassein

Where did it go? By 1882 the modern town of Bassein stood “half a mile inland” to the north of the fort walls, in the village of Malonde. [n11; Gaz. 1882 pp. 36–37] [n32; BG 28] The Gazetteer does not say when the town settled there, but the institutions it lists show the town growing in that village through the nineteenth century: the first Government vernacular school in the Thana district, opened at Bassein in 1827; a travellers’ bungalow of 1834; a road cut in 1856 “through the town to the landing place”; a mamlatdar’s office built in 1869 for Rs 35,530; the Balvantrao Hari Naik dispensary of 1872, in a house given by Yashvantrao Balvant Naik; a school-house of 1878 for 150 pupils; a public garden granted by Government in 1877; and the Robertson vegetable and fruit markets, named after the Collector James Walker Robertson, with the municipal office upstairs. [n28; G-II, G-XIV] [n32; BG 35–36] In 1837 Vaupell called Bassein “the chief market town of a petty division”. [n28; G-XIV] In 1872 the census counted 9,356 people in the municipality, and in 1881, 10,356: 6,850 Hindus, 2,623 Christians, 885 Muslims, 29 of the “wild tribes”, 14 Parsis and 5 Jews. [n21; Census 1872; Gaz XIV p. 28]

The municipality was established by Government Resolution 621 of 15 March 1864. It took in “besides the old fort and most of the modern town which stand in the village of Malonde, the survey villages of Dhauli, Sandore and Mulgaon, and many gardens and fields between Bassein and Pápdi, about a mile and a half to the east”. [n32; BG 36] Dhauli is Remedy (Davly), whose church of 1821 held the largest congregation of the Bassein parishes in 1881, 3,238 people. [n40; B3; GzXIV pp. 36–37] So the first civic Bassein was a union of a market town at Malonde with three Christian parish villages and their gardens, around an empty fort.

Sugar in the ruins

The empty fort and its esplanade became a laboratory for failed industries. In 1829 a Mr Lingard was given a forty-year rent-free lease of about 83 acres on the esplanade and an advance of Rs 23,000 to grow Mauritius cane and build a sugar mill; he died in 1832 and the scheme halted. In the same year the land inside the fort was leased to a Mr Cardoza. The estate passed through Narayan Krishna, McGregor Brownrigg and Co., a Mr Joseph, Dosabhai Jahangir and J. H. Littlewood. In 1852 Mrs Xavier, a daughter of Cardoza, was allowed to turn the ruined church of Nossa Senhora da Vida into a sugar factory, which closed in 1874; digging its foundations, workmen found the bones of a man and a horse with a rusty rapier. In 1860 the fort’s interior was leased for thirty years to Macfarlane and Littlewood, and in 1882 Littlewood’s widow held it, with her son farming the fields inside the walls. [n28; G-I, Sugar] [n32; BG 36–40; DC 227] The Collector wrote in 1882 that the lease made “the proper preservation of the ruins almost impossible”. [n32; BG 37–38]

Sopara, kasba

Sopara survived as a country town. In 1826 Clunes’s gazetteer called it a kasba, a market town, with 400 houses and a sugar factory, and a post-runner’s station. [n37; Gaz. XIV pp. 315, 323] [n31; Gazetteer] In 1882 it was “still a rich country town with a crowded weekly market and a population of about 1700”, with a broad market place of one- and two-storey houses “with rich wood carving”. Its husbandmen were Christians, many of them converted Samvedi Brahmans, Naita Muslims, and Hindus, chiefly Samvedi Brahmans and Bhandaris; its traders were Naitas and the Lad Vanis of Cambay. [n11; Gaz. 1882 pp. 314–315] The Gazetteer’s local informant for the town was Ramdas Kasidas Modi, “a native of Sopara”. [n11; Gaz. 1882 p. 314 fn.] Its landings at Gass, Bolinj and Vagholi sent molasses, rice and betel leaf by sea to Surat, Broach, Jambusar, Bhavnagar and Gogha. [n42; Gazetteer XIV pp. 315–316]

Agashi, chief town of the island

In 1837 Agashi was “the chief town on Bassein island with a large Christian and Hindu population, and a brisk trade to Gujarat and Bombay in grain, garden produce, and salt. Very good fishing boats and coasting craft were built.” [n40; A2; GzXIV p. 2] In 1881 it had 6,823 people (5,168 Hindus, 1,500 Christians and 155 Muslims) and drove “a great trade with Bombay in plantains and betel leaves, its dried plantains being the best in the district”. [n11; Gaz. 1882 p. 2] It was, in population, more than half the size of Bassein town. Its parish school of seventeen pupils taught Portuguese reading, arithmetic, singing and music, with a master who played the violin in church. [n40; A2]

The railway draws new towns, 1858–1900

An embankment across the island

Construction of the Bombay, Baroda and Central India Railway began in May 1858. On 28 November 1864 the line opened for traffic: ninety-five miles from Bombay to the Surat border, with sixteen stations. Two of them lay in Bassein: Bassein Road, at 33¾ miles, and Virar, at 38¾. [n28; G-I, Communications] [n21; Gaz XIII-I pp. 328–329] The line crossed the Bassein creek on two bridges divided by Panju island, of sixty-nine and twenty-five spans, and then ran north along the eastern edge of the island, on a long embankment laid across the old backwater. [n05; Wikipedia: Vasai Creek Railway Bridge] [n21]

The embankment changed the island’s settlements twice over. It finished the dying creek: the Gazetteer said silting had been rapid “especially since the long railway embankment has dammed the flow of water from the south”. [n42; Gazetteer XIV p. 315] A modern geographer says it “dismembered the channel”. [n02; Shah 2020, citing Arunachalam 2002] And its stone came partly from the east: on Tulinj hill, the Rakshi Dongar, a dyke of basalt pillars six to twenty-two feet long glistened with mica, “and when struck rings like an anvil”; “many of the pillars were used when the railway was made”, and people were still carrying them away in 1882. [n12; Gaz 1882] [n31; Gazetteer]

The other change was in where trade went. “Along the coast, the opening of the Baroda railway has destroyed the old cart traffic, and has reduced the sea-trade with Gujarat and Bombay.” [n36; Gaz XIII-I pp. 341–342] By 1882, “by rail, chiefly from the Virar station, molasses, rice, and betel-leaves pass north to Ankleshvar, Nadiad, Viramgam, and Patan, and large quantities of plantains and betel-leaves are sent south to Bombay”. [n12; Gaz 1882] The landings at Gass, Bolinj and Vagholi still worked, but the future of the garden villages now ran through the stations. The research notes call this the reorientation of settlement “from creek landings (Gass, Bolinj, Vagholi) to stations (Virar, Bassein Road/Manickpur, later Nallasopara)”. [n37; section 6, inferences]

Bassein Road and Manickpur

Bassein Road station was not at Bassein. It stood four and a half miles away, in the parish of Porim, which the Gazetteer already called Manikpur; a metalled road ran from the town to the station. [n32; BG 28; DC 157] On 1 November 1865 the railway began a local service between Grant Road and Bassein Road, one train each way, with Bassein Road as the northern terminus; in December a second roofed platform was sanctioned because two passenger trains were often at the station at once. [n05; Wikipedia: Vasai Road railway station] Passenger numbers at the station rose from 86,478 in 1873 to 140,837 in 1880. [n21; Gaz XIII-I p. 340] In 1868 “a Portuguese opened a hotel with refreshment rooms at Bassein Road station”. [n28; G-XIV, Tungar] By 1882 there was a double-storeyed rest-house by the station, in whose compound inscribed stones from Nila and Nandui had been set up, and a bungalow that served as the base for visitors to Sopara. [n40; B6; GzXIV p. 387] [n12; Gaz 1882 p. 366]

So the station gave the Christian parish village of Manickpur a second life as a railway place. St Michael’s church there had been rebuilt in 1851 and had 800 parishioners in 1881. [n40; B6] In 1881 Dublas worked salt pans near the station in the fair season. [n28; G-I, Salt] Over the next century the area around the station, Navghar on one side and Manickpur on the other, would grow into the town of “Vasai Road”, and in the twentieth century into the Navghar-Manikpur municipal council. When that council was formed, the sources do not say. [n28; gaps] [n12; VVCMC]

Virar: a station village

Virar, the village granted away in 1049 and leased for 2,000 pardaus in 1727, was in 1882 “a rich village well-wooded and with many patches of sugarcane”, seven miles north of Bassein, with a station that had refreshment and waiting rooms. Its passengers rose from 48,294 in 1878 to 83,176 in 1880, and goods from 1,730 to 3,349 tons (one reading of the table gives the first year as 1873). A metalled road ran four miles west to Agashi, making Virar the station for Agashi and Sopara. “To the east of the Virar station, across the railway, is the market place, on the west bank of a large pond under big banyan and mango trees. A large market is held every Saturday, thronged by Kolis and Varlis, selling fish, garden stuff, and cloth.” [n12; Gaz 1882 p. 382] [n40; B11]

That Saturday market is the first description of a settlement created by the railway on this coast: a market on the east side of the line, where the forest and fishing people met the garden villages and the train. The Gazetteer also recorded the village’s older layers around the station: the Portuguese church and well fifty yards south, a land-grant stone carved with a cow and calf a hundred yards east, and on a knoll beyond it two cow’s feet cut in the rock. These were explained by a legend: the Mhar herdsman of Virar followed a cow whose owner never paid him to the top of Jivdhan hill, where a woman appeared and agreed to pay; as she reached out he said, “Do not touch me, I am a Mhar”; the goddess vanished, “and the cow leapt from the hill-top and lighted on this rock”. [n12; Gaz 1882 pp. 382–383] On Jivdhan, barren women made offerings in the fair season to an invisible deity said to have fled her niche “at the touch of a Mhar”. [n12; Gaz 1882] The hilltop temple of Jivdani that now draws crowds up its steps is often said to be about 150 years old; popular sources link Virar’s name to the goddess Ekvira, which no scholarly source confirms. [n12; Wikipedia: Jivdani Mata; gaps]

On 12 April 1867 the railway began its first regular suburban service between Back Bay and Virar. [n28; Wikipedia: Western line; Virar station] The research flags a conflict with the 1865 Bassein Road date, but both may be right: Bassein Road was the first terminus, Virar the later and longer-lasting one. [n28; inferences] From then until 2013, Virar was the end of the Bombay local train, and this fact, more than any other, made it a town. [n29; 2013 entry]

The hill station that never was

The railway also inspired a settlement that was planned and never built. Between 1865 and 1874, T. C. Hope, Sir Bartle Frere and others tried to make the top of Tungar a health resort. The summit was surveyed in 1869, “fifty-three house sites were marked off”, and about thirteen miles of foot and bridle paths were cleared. Hope built a road from Bassein Road station by way of Gokirva “on a gradient better than the Matheran ascent”, the Gokirva-to-top section in 1869–71 for Rs 8,555. Readings in May 1876 gave 74°F at sunrise and 82°F at 4 pm. The Gazetteer thought Tungar “seems to possess peculiar advantages as a sanitarium for poor families, who cannot avail themselves of the expensive accommodation of Matheran; and for the large colony of Europeans employed by the Baroda and Peninsula Railway Companies”. It named viewpoints: Vaitarna point, Kaman point, Bellevue and Panorama. [n12; Gaz 1882 pp. 366–368] By 1874 the scheme had failed. [n28; G-XIV, Tungar]

The hill station left one mark on settlement: the government road made Gokhivare the gateway to Tungar. In 1882 the road ran “station to Gokirva, road bridged, drained, embanked, and metalled, 1 mile 766 feet”, and a five-mile feeder ran from Gokirva to Papdi. [n12; Gaz 1882 pp. 366–367] [n40; B8; GzXIII-1 p. 319] Gokhivare, a Vadval cart village of 1882, became a census town of 19,772 people by 2001. [n12; Wikipedia: Gokhivare] The hilltop itself became, in 2003, the core of the Tungareshwar Wildlife Sanctuary, 85.7 square kilometres of forest linking Sanjay Gandhi National Park and Tansa. [n12; Wikipedia]

Sasunavghar’s paper mill

A second failed venture stood on the creek at Sasunavghar, “about six miles east of Bassein … a small port”, which exported an average of Rs 46,610 a year in the five years to 1878–79, swinging wildly from year to year. “A paper-mill was started here in 1871 by Messrs. Johnson and Littlewood.” Its buildings, English machinery and a dam across the nearby stream cost Rs 80,000. It began working in 1877, making paper from grass and rice straw, “but none was ever sold although many dealers had approved of the samples”. The manager died in an accident in 1880 and the mill closed; “the project failed through want of funds to buy new machinery”. [n12; Gaz 1882] Whether anything of it survives was not checked. [n12; gaps] It was the taluka’s first factory settlement, and it lasted three years.

The taluka in 1881

The Gazetteer’s figures give a snapshot of the settled land at the end of this period. The Bassein sub-division covered 221 square miles with 68,967 people in 1881, up from 61,089 in 1872; about a fifth were Christians. The revenue survey of 1858–62 had counted 104 villages. In 1881–82 the sub-division had 191 ponds and 2,624 wells, 2,997 carts and 5,308 ploughs. [n21; Gaz XIII-II pp. 678–680] [n28; G-II p. 610] By 1911 the taluka had 79,978 people in two towns and ninety villages. [n21] Almost all of it was still villages of gardens, rice and salt, with two towns, Bassein and Agashi, and a string of station settlements growing beside the line.

Nallasopara: a station gives a town its name

No station in 1882

The most famous name of the modern city is also the one whose origin is least certain. In 1882 the Gazetteer still called the place only “Sopara”. It complained that Sopara, though “not more than three-quarters of a mile from the line of railway”, was “somewhat badly off for communications”, reached from Manikpur across a salt waste that flooded and became impassable in the rains. “A station at Sopara or a made road to the town from the Agashi high road would be a boon to its people.” [n31; Gazetteer p. 316] The Gazetteer’s list of stations, Bandra to Bhilad, had no Nallasopara and no Naigaon. [n28; G-I] So in 1882 there was no station at Sopara, and the author knew of none.

Wikipedia gives the station’s opening as 1865, citing a Western Railway heritage book of 2009. That date conflicts with the Gazetteer, and the research leaves it unresolved. [n31; Wikipedia: Nallasopara railway station] One other clue survives. A newspaper article of 2022, drawing on a 1923 railway magazine, says an early Back Bay–Virar service halted at “Neela or (Nalasopara)”, then Bassein, Panjo and Berewla (Borivali). [n31; Free Press Journal, 12 Apr 2022] The article does not tie that list to a specific date. It is the only evidence of the station’s earliest name.

Nala, Nila, Neela

Was there a village called Nala? The answer turns out to be yes, though for a time the research thought not. A stone “brought from Nila about a mile north of Sopara” named the Yadava king Ramchandradev. [n40; A15; GzXIV p. 387] The Tombo of 1727–30 lists a pacaria “Nala Magir”. [n40; A15] And the 1882 Gazetteer’s population volume names “Nala” as one of the nine plantain-drying villages, one of four Bassein villages (with Agashi, Sopara and Navghar) where bills of exchange were cashed up to £1,000, and probably the “Nela” of its list of ten trade centres; Lad Vani families lived at Nala. [n40; A15; GzXIII-1 pp. 292, 300, 304] [n05; Gazetteer snippets] So a village of Nala or Nila, near Sopara, existed from at least the late thirteenth century and was a trading place in 1882.

A popular account says the name arose when two villages, Nala east of the railway and Sopara west of it, came to be treated as one; it gives no dates and no source. [n31; Lokshahi] It sits awkwardly with Wikipedia’s list of localities, which places “Nale Gaon” with “Sopara Gaon” in Nallasopara West and “Achole Gaon” and “More Gaon” in the East. [n31; Wikipedia mirror] There is also a Nale-Rajodi gram panchayat and a Nale Talav on the Arnala–Vasai road in the north-west, which may be another place again. [n40; A14; CRIT item 67] What can be said plainly is this: an old village of Nala or Nila lay near Sopara; a railway halt opened at some date after 1882 under a name recorded as “Neela”; and the station’s name, joined to Sopara’s, became the name of the town. The year and the reason are not documented. The official-sounding “Shurparak Nagar” appears on Wikipedia without a source. [n31; Wikipedia]

Naigaon

Naigaon, the southernmost station of the taluka on the creek, also opened after 1882; its date is unknown, and so is the origin of its name. [n28; inferences] [n12; gaps] A “Nagão” appears in a Tombo group of the Thana caçabé, “Sedacer, Choly, Calamba, Nagão e Varchory”, which may or may not be Naigaon, Holi and Kalamb. [n40; B10] The Vadvali dialect is said to be spoken by “the Vadvals of the Naigaon, Vasai to Dahanu region”. [n08; Marathi-Konkani languages] The Pali church of the Mother of God stands in Naigaon West, and a chapel of the Immaculate Conception was built at Umela (Naigaon) in 1974 under Manickpur. [n40; B10] Naigaon was a gram panchayat in 2008, with a Naigaon Bazaar. [n40; B10; CRIT] Its modern history begins with the housing colonies of the 1990s and the creek bridges: a road bridge over Vasai creek on the Mumbai–Ahmedabad highway built in 1968 and a second, 918-metre bridge opened on 27 March 2023. [n12; Mumbai Live; FPJ]

Rails into the twentieth century

The railway’s twentieth-century milestones are the only well-dated events of the period for the taluka. Virar station was remodelled with an island platform in or after 1913. The creek bridges were rebuilt between 1920 and the later 1920s. Electric trains reached Borivali in 1928 and Virar in 1936. The BB&CI became the Western Railway on 5 November 1951. [n28; Wikipedia: Western line; Virar station; Vasai Creek bridge] By 1951 Bassein taluka had 133,523 people, of whom 41,130 lived in Bassein town. [n21] The railway had settled Virar as the end of Bombay’s commuting world seventy years before the housing boom. [n28; inferences]

New parishes, new villages: 1900–1980

Parishes for distance and for vows

The twentieth century saw the Christian villages reorganise themselves, and the founding dates here are, for once, reasonably firm, because they come from the parishes’ own recent records. Most new parishes were split from the old mother churches because people lived too far away.

  • Giriz: part of Sandor parish since its foundation. Its people petitioned the Archbishop of Damaun; the foundation stone of St Francis Xavier’s was laid on 14 February 1917 and the church was finished in 1918 “without foreign aid”. The parishioners had walked to distant churches, hard in the monsoon. [n40; B9; CRIT Vol III asset 90; EI Giriz]
  • Gokhivare: “founded as a result of a promise made to St Roque by the people of Gokhivare” after a severe cholera epidemic in 1914; two hundred villagers built a forty-by-eighteen-foot chapel, blessed on 30 June 1930; an independent parish in 1943. [n40; B8; EI Gokhivare]
  • Gass: the villagers petitioned in 1941; the parish of St Gonsalo Garcia was formed in 1942, split from Nirmal, with Fr Thomas as first parish priest; the church was built in 1942, expanded in 1957, and blessed on 8 March 1962 by Cardinal Gracias. Why Gass got a parish in 1942 is not recorded; distance from Nirmal is the probable reason. [n42; section 5] [n40; A6]
  • Mulgaon: St Mary Magdalene, 1945, from Pali. [n40; B5]
  • Bhuigaon: “out of the thousand Catholics of the village only a handful attended services at Nirmal”; a simple church on land donated by the villagers, an independent parish on 2 June 1946, the present church completed in 1984. [n40; A4; EI Bhuigaon]
  • Chulne: Sunday Mass from 1951, Our Lady of Fatima parish established 1 June 1963. [n40; B7]
  • Umrale became independent in 1970 (St Joseph) and Nanbhat on 1 June 1975 (St Paul), both from Nandakhal. [n40; A9, A10]
  • Gomes Ali, with Chowkala, Vanda, Rambai and Darseng, had its first Mass in 1979 in the local board school at Chowkala and became the parish of the Most Holy Trinity on 5 June 2004. [n40; A5]
  • Rajodi’s church was inaugurated on 1 May 1999 and Bolinj became a parish on 10 June 2001. [n40; A11, A14]

These splits did not create villages, but they gave hamlets that had been outlying wards of a mother parish their own church, school and feast, and so their own identity. Bhuigaon, a village of forty large irrigation wells kept by its women in 2011, with a Madikar Cross that a blogger called “the local equivalent of the Gaondevi”, is an example. [n40; A4; Mumbai Paused 2011]

Village institutions

Other dated foundations show the villages acquiring public buildings: Zilla Parishad schools at Juchandra (1872), Bolinj (1879) and Agashi (1886); the Agashi public library, founded in 1914 by Seth Shah Prabhudas Bhagvandas, with its building of 1939; St Joseph’s Marathi school at Nandakhal (1939); the Nirmal Bazaar of 1957 in the Vagholi gram panchayat. [n40; A2, A11, B10, A7; CRIT] [n28; 1939] A gram panchayat list of 2008 gives the units into which the plantation zone had been organised before the corporation: Agashe, Arnala, Arnala Fort, Vatar, Satphale, Kofrad, Bolinj, Umbrale, Nale-Rajodi, Vagholi, Madres, Navale, Ghas, Nirmal, Bhuigaon Khurd and Budruk, Girij, Kaular Khurd and Budruk, Saloli, Sandor, Mulgaon, Kiravali, Vadavali, Umele, Naigaon, Juchandra, Sasunavghar, Kaman, Rajavali, Gokhivare, Waliv, Sativali, Pelhar and Dhaniv. [n40; section 2; CRIT Vol I]

Still a garden country

Until the late 1980s the taluka remained mostly villages. In 1981 the whole Vasai–Virar region had fewer than 250,000 people; Vasai town had 34,900 and Virar 23,303, “and only Agashi passed 10,000”; many towns had lost population between 1971 and 1981. [n05; TIFR] The Kuparis depended mainly on farming until the late 1960s and early 1970s. [n11; Rajan D’Mello] Vasai’s farmer-vendors, the kawadis, took the afternoon train to Bombay to sell bananas and dried bananas at Bora Bazaar and Dadar. [n05; India Food Network 2015] In the late 1980s Moreshwar Bhoir of Arnala introduced the sonchafa flower from a Kudal nursery, beginning the flower farming of the coastal villages. [n11; 101Reporters 2023] The 1970s regional plan treated the area as a green zone apart from the station areas. [n37; section 6] A student project from the Tata Institute of Social Sciences reports that 1970s industrial zoning led entrepreneurs to build chawls for workers, and that many people in Makarandnagar were displaced from Sanjay Gandhi National Park, but the page could not be opened and the claim rests on a search summary. [n31; TISS]

After 1988: the colonies

The decision

In August 1988 the Maharashtra government reclassified much of the Vasai–Virar green belt as residential land, against the advice of the metropolitan planning authority and without matching infrastructure. [n29; Sabrang] [n05; TIFR] On 14 May 1990 CIDCO was appointed planning authority for the sub-region. [n29; VVCMC DCR] The church-led Harit Vasai movement, convened by Fr Francis D’Britto, rallied tens of thousands against the plans, won a ban on tankers drawing groundwater from the green belt, and, by the account of a lawyer at D’Britto’s funeral in 2024, kept the state to limiting development “to 1.5 km on either side of the railway line”. [n29; Sabrang; FPJ 25 Jul 2024]

That line along the railway is the key to the geography of the new city. By 1991, a Sabrang account says, housing colonies had spread near Vasai, Naigaon, Nalasopara and Virar stations. [n29; Sabrang] The population of the region rose by about 150 per cent between 1981 and 1991, and Vasai, Virar, Sopara and Sandor grew by 290 per cent. [n05; TIFR] The census series for the city area runs 219,868 (1981), 365,480 (1991), 693,350 (2001) and 1,343,402 (2011). [n06; Wikipedia: VVCMC] Almost all of the growth came from people who worked in Mumbai and could not afford to live there. [n37; TIFR; Manickpur.com]

The station towns fill the flats

The new colonies were built where the garden villages were not: on the east side of the line, on the old salt waste and rice flats that the 1882 Gazetteer had described as “formerly flooded during the rains”. [n12; inferences] [n31; inferences] Nallasopara had 184,664 people in 2001; a student project puts it at about 564,664 in 2011 on 43 square kilometres, with low-rise baithi chawls being replaced by four-storey blocks in the East. [n31; Wikipedia; TU Delft] The TISS project says that after 2000 landowners converted farmland into chawls for migrants, many from Mumbai, and that low-lying flood-prone land overlaps the urbanisable zones. [n31; TISS, search summary]

For the named neighbourhoods of Nallasopara East and its neighbours the sources are almost silent. Achole, a village of Urap Agris in 1882 with a Portuguese fort, lent its name to Achole Road and Achole Gaon. Tulinj, the basalt hill whose pillars built the railway, lent its name to Tulinj Road and a police station. [n40; B10] [n31; section 2] But for Santosh Bhuvan, Shirdi Nagar, Pragati Nagar and Nilemore the research found “nothing sourced … (origins, dates, communities)”. [n12; section 3, gaps] [n31; gaps] Their founding is unknown. They appear in the record only when the water comes: in July 2018 residents of Achole Road and Chheda Nagar were stranded for five days, and Vishal Nagar, Tulinj Road and Achole went under. [n31; NewsBytes; Lokmat Times]

Agarwal Nagari: a colony founded and unfounded

One colony of this period has a founding date, because it was demolished by court order. On survey numbers 22 to 30 at Agarwal Nagar, Achole, across about thirty acres of land reserved in the plan for a sewage treatment plant and a dumping ground, private builders put up forty-one buildings from 2006 onward. The Bombay High Court declared them “wholly illegal and unauthorised”; residents received eviction notices in October 2024; seven buildings came down from 28 November 2024 and the remaining thirty-four from 23 January 2025, after the Supreme Court dismissed the residents’ appeal. Estimates of those displaced range from about 2,000 to about 8,000. [n06; Land Conflict Watch; Lokmat Times; PropNewsTime] A former corporator had been arrested in 2022 for land-grabbing and building them, and in August 2025 the Enforcement Directorate arrested the former municipal commissioner in a related case, an arrest the Bombay High Court later held illegal. [n06; sections 4] The colony had lived for about eighteen years. In March 2025 the corporation proposed moving the dumping-ground reservation to a plot in Gass; the villagers filed more than 2,500 objections, and in October 2025 the corporation withdrew its application. [n42; section 6]

Seven months after the last Agarwal Nagari building fell, the rear wing of Ramabai Apartment in Virar East, an allegedly unauthorised building put up in 2011–12, collapsed shortly after midnight on 27 August 2025, killing seventeen people. [n06; The Week/PTI; FPJ]

Evershine and the townships

Not every new settlement was unauthorised. Evershine in Vasai East is a planned residential township, but the research found no sourced history of its developer, launch year or scale. [n12; section 4, gaps] At Virar, Wikipedia names a “Global City” of Rustomjee and Evershine over about 200 acres. [n40; B11; Wikipedia: Virar] Their founding dates are not in the notes, and this chapter does not supply them.

Waliv and Sativali: the industrial estates

East of Vasai Road, the old Vadval and Agri villages of the St Michael parish’s jurisdiction, Gokhivare, Sativali and Waliv, became a dense belt of small industrial estates: Tara, Samruddhi, Pride on Sativali Main Road, Pinnacle, Pushpraj and Anmol, mostly sold or let as galas, small units; Nova Industrial Estate in Gokhivare was registered on 24 January 2023. [n12; section 4] When Waliv became an industrial zone, the research could not establish; it is “likely 1970s–90s, unconfirmed”. [n12; gaps] Whether any are MIDC estates was not researched. [n06; gaps] Sativali, the starting point of the 1882 path up Tungar through the Varli hamlet of Dhondvira, now begins the climb with industrial lanes. [n12; Gaz 1882 p. 367]

One corporation

On 3 July 2009 the state constituted the Vasai-Virar City Municipal Corporation from four municipal councils, Vasai, Virar, Nallasopara and Navghar-Manikpur, and fifty-three villages. (Some sources give 2010.) [n29; VVCMC] Forty-nine of the villages opposed inclusion, and in 2011 the state moved to exclude twenty-nine of them, a decision that went to court and was still in litigation in 2025. [n29; Wikipedia: Hitendra Thakur; vasairoad blog] [n40; section 2] The 2011 census therefore counted the corporation as a single urban unit of 1,222,390 people, with Chandrapada as a census town and fifty rural villages outside. [n11; census2011.co.in] Most of the old garden villages, Agashi, Nirmal, Gass, Bolinj, Sopara, Papdi, Sandor and the rest, have since then had no separate census figure. The villages that kept one are the north-west fishing and coast cluster around Arnala and Kalamb, a south-west group around Rangaon and Tarkhad, and the eastern hill villages: Arnala 15,331, Arnala Killa 2,566, Kalamb 5,329, Rangaon 4,166, Kopri 10,180, Parol 2,153, Bhatane 4,786, Adne 1,995. [n11; Q1] [n12; table] Palghar district was carved from Thane on 1 August 2014, with Vasai as one of its talukas. [n29; Wikipedia: Palghar district]

The city’s edges are now fixed less by creeks than by forest and law. Activists estimated in 2024 at least 20,000 illegal constructions between Kaman and Virar East along the Tungareshwar sanctuary. [n08; HT 2024] The MMRDA’s draft regional plan of 2016–36 proposed opening about 1,560 acres of former salt pans as a “growth centre”; about 63,000 residents objected. [n11; ICSF] Gass, described by a newspaper in 2025 as “a predominantly Christian village often described as ‘Mini Goa’ for the ancestral mansions that dot the surrounding farmland”, sits inside the corporation, beside its lake, with the creek long gone. [n42; section 6]

The lake at Gass

The 1882 Gazetteer described the lake at Gass as “a winding hollow, 120 yards broad and 800 long with two cross dams”, fringed by gardens and Persian wheels, with dressed stone steps at its south end and the foundations of an eleventh- or twelfth-century temple on its shore. [n42; Gazetteer XIV p. 341] No source says whether it is a remnant of the old creek or an excavated tank. [n42; gaps] A mile to the north, the stupa of Mardes still stands as a mound. A few hundred yards east, near the place called the Bandar Masjid, a stone anchor lay under ground that is now lanes and house plots. [n42; Shah 2020]

Seen from here, the whole history of settlement on this coast is a single movement. People settled first on the raised ground of the island because the water around it protected and connected them. Villages grew on its gardens and were named in the grants of kings. A port rose at the deep creek when the shallow one failed, and a fortress city was built of the old town’s stones. Churches and then temples re-centred the old villages without moving them. The railway laid its embankment across the dying backwater and called new towns to its stations. And the city of the last forty years built its colonies on the very flats the sea had once covered, where the monsoon still finds its old channels every July. Almost none of these places has a founding date. Each has a reason to be where it is, and the reason is nearly always water. What the people who lived in these villages carried with them through all of it, in their kitchens and their feasts, is the subject of the book’s last chapter.
