In this chapter. The peoples of Virar and its villages, from the hill peoples to the newest arrivals, and how each came.

The Saturday market

East of Virar station, across the railway line, there was in the early 1880s a market place on the west bank of a large pond, under big banyan and mango trees. Every Saturday a market was held there, and the Gazetteer’s compilers, who walked the district for the Thana volumes printed in 1882, wrote down who came to it. It was “thronged by Kolis and Varlis, selling fish, garden stuff, and cloth.” [n12; n45; Gaz XIV p. 382]

Two peoples are named in that sentence, and they came from opposite directions. The Kolis came up from the shore and the creeks to the west and north, from Arnala at the mouth of the Vaitarna, from the fishing hamlets along the beaches, carrying the catch. The Varlis, the people the twenty-first century spells Warli, came down from the east, from the wooded slopes behind Jivdhan hill and the ridges beyond it, carrying whatever the forest and their small upland fields gave. The garden stuff on the mats between them came from the third world of the Virar side: the plantain and betel gardens of Agashi, Bolinj, Vatar and Koprad to the west, worked by Samvedi Brahmans, Pachkalshis and Christians, and the sugarcane patches that made the Gazetteer call Virar itself “a rich village well-wooded and with many patches of sugarcane.” [n45; Gaz XIV p. 382] The same Gazetteer counted four weekly markets in the Bassein sub-division, at Agashi, Sopara, Dhovh and Virar, held on Monday, Wednesday, Friday and Saturday, each attended by five or six hundred people. [n49; Gaz XIII-1 pp. ~319, 329–332] Virar’s was the Saturday.

This chapter is about everyone who might have stood in that market, and everyone who has come since. It tells the communities of Virar and its northern villages roughly in the order in which they reached this ground, as far as the record lets that order be known: the hill peoples first, then the fishers, then the Brahman and gardening castes whose traditions place their coming in the age of the Shilaharas and of King Bimb, then the Agris, Bhandaris and Kunbis of the salt pans, palm groves and rice fields, then the Muslims, Jains, Gujaratis and Marwaris who came by sea and road for trade, and last the hundreds of thousands who came by train after 1988. For each it asks the same questions: where they came from and when, and how sure we can be; which villages they lived in; what work they did; what they spoke; what they worshipped, and whether and why some of them changed their faith; what customs the record holds; and how they live now. The second part of the chapter walks through the sacred places of the Virar side, and the third goes village by village through Virar East and West, saying plainly where the record is empty.

A word on how much can be known. The deepest single source for nearly every community here is one book: the Gazetteer of the Bombay Presidency, Volume XIII, Thana, Part I, published in 1882 under James M. Campbell, whose population chapter describes each caste of the district in turn, and its companion Volume XIV, Places of Interest. [n49; n08] It was compiled by British officers from reports by Indian and European informants, and it carries their judgements, some of them crude. Behind it, for many groups, stands the 1872 census, whose figures the Gazetteer quotes. The next layer is R. E. Enthoven’s The Tribes and Castes of Bombay (1920–22), much of which was compiled from the communities’ own informants, so that it is excellent evidence of what each community said about itself and much weaker evidence of what actually happened. [n35] George Grierson’s Linguistic Survey of India, Volume VII (1905), recorded how several of these communities spoke. [n22] After that the record thins to parish histories, community websites, newspapers and Wikipedia pages, several of which are tagged by Wikipedia’s own editors as needing citations. Where a claim rests on one of these weaker sources, this chapter says so. Where the record is silent, it says that too.

Some of this ground is already covered elsewhere in the book. Chapter 27 tells the history of Virar town, the Jivdani legend and the Urap Agris at length; Chapter 38 gives the full village histories of the Kupari and Vadval villages, including those on the Virar side; Chapter 32 tells Arnala, its fort, its Koliwada and its beaches; and Chapters 33 to K5 tell the Kuparis from their origins to today. This chapter points to those chapters rather than repeating them, and adds what they leave out: the communities that are not Kupari or Vadval, the customs of each, and the villages east of the railway. The Kuparis are the one exception to that rule of pointing elsewhere. They are the largest Christian community of the Virar side, and a great part of their country, Agashi, Nandakhal, Bolinj, Umrale, Nanbhat, Shirlai, Vatar and Rajodi, lies inside what is now Virar West; so Section IV gives the Kuparis of Virar and the northern villages a full account of their own, and every village in Part Three carries, after the account of all its peoples, a separate note on whether Kuparis live there and what the record holds of them. Where the notes say nothing of Kuparis in a village, that note says so.

Part One: The Peoples

I. The people of the hills: Warli, Katkari and Thakur

Before the record

Of all the communities of the Virar side, the Adivasi peoples of the eastern hills are the ones with the strongest claim to having been here first, and the weakest written record of when they came. The plain statement of the best colonial source is that nobody knows. Enthoven, writing of the Varlis in 1922, put it in five words: “Regarding their origin nothing is known.” [n35; Enthoven III pp. 445–447]

What the sources offer instead is a name and a geography. Dr John Wilson, the nineteenth-century missionary scholar whom both the Gazetteer and Enthoven quote, derived “Varli” from varal, a small cultivated patch, and read the name as “uplander”. The 1882 Gazetteer put it the same way: the Varlis were “probably originally Varalis or uplanders”, and they gave the name Varalat to the sixth of the seven ancient divisions of the Konkan. [n08; n35; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 182–183; Enthoven III pp. 445–447] The Gazetteer also suggested a ladder of such names among the farming peoples of Thana: Talheri, the name of the local Kunbis, “seems to mean a lowlander, perhaps as opposed to Varli an uplander and Malhari a highlander.” [n35; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 124–125] Whether or not that reading is right, it places the Varlis where the 1882 market sentence places them: on the slopes above the plain.

Enthoven called the Varlis “an aboriginal tribe only slightly influenced by Brahmanism”. [n35; Enthoven III pp. 445–447] Grierson, recording their speech about 1905, judged it “originally a Bhil dialect”, now heavily shaped by Marathi except in the extreme north, where it took a Gujarati form. [n22; LSI vol. VII] That linguistic hint, Bhil roots under a later Marathi layer, is the nearest the record comes to an origin, and it is a hint about language, not a dated migration. No archaeological site in the notes is tied to the ancestors of the Varlis, Katkaris or Thakurs on the Virar side. Chapter 03 of this book tells what is known of the first people of the coast in general; nothing in the evidence links those Stone Age toolmakers to any named tribe of today, and this chapter does not draw that line.

The Varlis in 1872 and 1882

The 1872 census counted 70,015 Varlis in the Thana district. Their heartland was Dahanu, with 45,330; Mahim had 6,804; Bassein, the sub-division that included Virar, had 6,099; Shahapur 4,277; Vada 2,142. [n08; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 182–183] Among the hill tribes of the district in 1872 the Gazetteer listed, in round figures, Varlis 70,000, Thakurs 55,500, Kathkaris 34,000, Dublas 8,600, Vaitis 4,500, Konkanis 4,500 and Dhodias 3,000. [n08; Gaz XIII-1, Population introduction]

The Gazetteer divided the Varlis into three sections that did not intermarry: Murdes, Davars and Nihiris. The Nihiris, it said, belonged to “South Mahim, Bassein, Jawhar and Vada”, and Enthoven, forty years later, repeated that the Nihir division “belong to South Mahim, Bassein, Jawhar and Vada”. [n08; n49; Gaz XIII-1 p. 183; Enthoven III, Varli] The Varlis of the Virar side were therefore, by both accounts, Nihiris. The Gazetteer lists Varli clan names: Bhavar, Sankar, Pileyana, Ravatia, Bantria, Bhangara, Meria, Vangad, Thakaria, Jadav, Karbat, Bhendar, Kondaria. [n08; Gaz XIII-1 p. 183] Which of these clans lived on the slopes behind Virar is not recorded.

Their speech, the Gazetteer said, differed little from Kunbi Marathi but clipped words, kot jás for kothe jātos, “where are you going?”, and used nāngne for “to see”. [n08; Gaz XIII-1 p. 183] Enthoven added other non-Marathi words: nange (see), tal (here), davar (old man), jhinguor (blanket), yayki or tup (ghee). [n35; Enthoven III pp. 445–447] Their huts had walls of karvi reed on floors smeared with cowdung. In the dry season they wandered in search of labour. [n35; Enthoven III]

The Gazetteer recorded one more thing about them, in a sentence that is half observation and half a colonial officer’s sentiment: “Their love for the forests is so great that… they cannot be induced to go so far from their woods.” [n08; Gaz XIII-1 p. 183] And it recorded, without comment, a sign of what was to come. Varlis who had pledged their land were employed in tillage during the rains. [n08; Gaz XIII-1 p. 183] That is the earliest written trace on this coast of debt bondage to moneylenders, the problem that would define Adivasi politics in the district a century later.

Katkaris and Thakurs

The Katkaris (Kathkaris) took their name, Enthoven said, from kath, catechu, the astringent extract boiled from the heartwood of the khair tree, and karne, to make. They were “probably of Bhil origin… believed to have come from the north, and to have been originally settled in the Gujarat Athavisi, the present district of Surat”, and they still had a division called Athavar. [n35; Enthoven II pp. 171–173] Their own story was different: they descended from Rama’s monkey army, blessed into human form. [n35; Enthoven II] Enthoven recorded five endogamous divisions: Athavar; Dhed or Dhor; Sidhi, whose African origin he thought “doubtless”; Son or Maratha; and Varap, made up of people who had returned from Christianity or Islam. [n35; Enthoven II pp. 171–173] The 1882 Gazetteer also noted an African (Sidi) strain among some Katkaris and Thakurs, as among Thana’s Christians and Konkani Muslims. [n35; Gaz XIII-1 p. 64] So even the forest people of this coast were not sealed off from the sea’s traffic.

The Katkaris made catechu and charcoal and cut sugarcane, as the Varlis also did, and the Kunbis feared them for their reputed spirit powers. [n08; Gaz XIII-1, Kathkari section] Their dialect was “a slightly disguised Marathi with many Gujarati and Hindustani words”, with words of its own: suna dog, him snake, narak bear, akii fire, vadis wife. [n35; Enthoven II pp. 171–173] They admitted strangers of higher castes, Varli, Koli, Thakur or Kunbi, on payment of five rupees, an unusual openness that Enthoven noted. [n35; Enthoven II p. 172] The Gazetteer also mentions “Urap Kolis and Kathkaris”, some of whom “seem to be Musalman reverts”. [n49; Gaz XIII-1 p. 208 n.4]

The Thakurs, 122,019 in 1901 across Thana, Kolaba and Nasik, carried a tradition that a Rajput element entered the tribe when “certain fugitives from Gujarat” took refuge among them “during the reign of Mahmud Begada (1459–1511)”. Wilson had said the same: the Thakurs “are the descendants of the Thakurs of Gujarat, who fled to the hills in the time of Mahmud Begada’s persecutions.” [n35; Enthoven III pp. 376–377; Enthoven II p. 245] The only artefact offered in support is a copper plate found in the possession of a Thakur at Nasik, which shows the word Thakur in use since about AD 650; it says nothing about refugees. [n35; Enthoven III] Their home tongue was Marathi “spoken with a long drawl”. [n35; Enthoven III]

The pattern is the same for all three peoples: Bhil-related speech, a tradition of movement from the north, Gujarat in particular, and a Marathi language laid over the older tongue. The Gujarat-refugee story of the Thakurs is a tradition, not a documented event. [n35]

On the Virar side

How many Varlis, Katkaris and Thakurs lived on the eastern side of Virar in any year, and in which hamlets, no source in the notes says. The research for this chapter looked specifically for a record naming a Warli, Katkari or Thakur pada (hamlet) on Virar’s eastern side, and found none. [n49] What can be said is this.

In 1882 Varlis came to Virar’s Saturday market. [n45; Gaz XIV p. 382] In the same years the people of Tungar hill, the ridge south-east of Virar, were “Kolis and Varlis”, and the path up to the Tungareshwar temple passed the Varli hamlet of Dhondvira. Stones in the stream near that temple were carved with the sun, the moon and human figures. [n10; Gaz XIV pp. 367–368] A twenty-first-century search summary of a Down To Earth report says Warli Adivasis live on Jivdani hill itself. [n10; Down To Earth (search summary)] Several of the place-names on the eastern slopes between the railway and Jivdani end in -pada, “hamlet”: Phulpada, Manvelpada, Chandrapada, Dongarpada. In the Konkan such names are often attached to Adivasi or low-caste hamlets, and their position fits the 1882 geography of the hill peoples, but no village-level record links any named pada of Virar to a specific tribe. This chapter does not state it as fact. [n49]

The most important religious practice the record ties to the Warlis of this region is the worship of Waghoba, the tiger or leopard god. A peer-reviewed study published in 2021 by Nair, Patil, Surve, Andheria, Linnell and Athreya recorded 150 Waghoba shrines across Mumbai Suburban, Thane and Palghar, clustered in Palghar, and found that every Warli village its researchers visited had at least one. Vasai is named among the Warli areas surveyed. A typical shrine shows a feline under a sun and moon, carved in stone or teak and smeared with vermilion, often at the village entrance; wooden images are renewed every fifteen to twenty years. The festival is Waghbaras, on Vasu Baras, the first day of Diwali: a two-day ritual led by a shaman, with offerings of coconut, toddy, chickens and goats. [n10; Frontiers in Conservation Science, 2021] The sun-and-moon stones near Tungareshwar that the Gazetteer saw in 1882 match that iconography, though no source makes the identification. [n10] No named Waghoba shrine or sacred grove on the Virar side appears in any source reached. [n10]

Conversion, bondage and the movement

No document in the notes records the conversion of Virar’s Adivasis to any faith. The Varap division of the Katkaris, made of people returned from Christianity or Islam, shows that some Katkaris somewhere had passed through those faiths, but not where or when. [n35; Enthoven II] A 2016 AsiaNews report carried the headline that a new church for tribal Catholics had been inaugurated in the Vasai diocese; the page itself could not be opened, so its place and details are unknown. [n49; AsiaNews (headline only)]

The modern history of these peoples in the taluka is a history of labour and land. In 1982 Vivek Pandit, born in Dahanu in 1957, and his wife Vidyullata, who had first worked in Dahisar village in Vasai taluka, founded the Shramjeevi Sanghatana, which became the main Adivasi movement in the district against bonded labour, above all among the Katkaris. Pandit won the International Anti-Slavery Award in 1999 and was independent MLA for Vasai from 2009 to 2014 with Shiv Sena support; he later chaired the state’s Tribal Area Review Committee. [n08; Wikipedia: Vivek Raghunath Pandit] In 2026 the Sanghatana, led by its president Rambhau Varna, staged silent fasts for the Katkaris, who face bonded labour, child trafficking and the collapse of schooling under seasonal migration to brick kilns. [n08; Free Press Journal 2026] At one Palghar brick kiln, Katkari families refused bayana, the advance that binds a family to a season’s labour, and the owner signed an affidavit acknowledging that it was illegal under the Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act of 1976. [n08; Newsdrum] The thread from the Varlis “who had pledged their land” in 1882 to the bayana of the 2020s runs through a century and a half of the same economy.

The forests themselves have shrunk. The Tungareshwar Wildlife Sanctuary, about 85 square kilometres east of Vasai and Virar, is a corridor between the Sanjay Gandhi National Park and Tansa; activists estimate at least 20,000 illegal constructions between Kaman and Virar East. [n08; HT via PressReader, 28 Apr 2024] A proposed water dam for Vasai-Virar would submerge eleven Adivasi hamlets. [n08; India Together]

In the 2011 census, Scheduled Tribes made up 7.32 per cent of Vasai taluka and 4.70 per cent of the population inside the Vasai-Virar City Municipal Corporation. [n49; CensusIndia2011.com; census2011.co.in] Some of the villages around Virar returned noticeable ST populations: Arnala 1,473, Kalamb 604, Khochivade 452. [n40] Warli as a language had about 390,000 native speakers in 2011 across its whole range, and UNESCO’s atlas lists it as potentially vulnerable. [n22; Wikipedia: Varli language] How many of those live on the Virar side is not known.

So the oldest people of the Virar side have the thinnest record in it. They appear in 1882 as sellers in a market and as the people of a hill path, and in the twenty-first century as plaintiffs, protesters and the people whose hamlets lie in the way of dams and towers. Everything between is silence.

II. The people of the shore: Son Kolis, Mangelas, Vaitis and Christian Kolis

“A term of vague meaning”

The word Koli covers more people than any other on this coast, and means less precisely. Enthoven, whose volume on the Kolis was largely written by C. Rothfeld of the Indian Civil Service, called it “a term of vague meaning covering a number of tribes of inferior status”, and added: “Probably the term Koli has been used for centuries to describe the greater part of the original inhabitants of the Presidency.” There were 1,714,107 Kolis in the Presidency in 1901. A Koli who settled to farming in the Deccan, he said, “frequently becomes a Kunbi”. Wilson had called the Kolis the aborigines of the plains as the Bhils were of the hills, and said “they do not differ in race from the Kulambis or Kunbis”. [n35; Enthoven II pp. 243–245] Wilson also counted “the Son Koli as the original inhabitant of Bombay Island”. [n35; Enthoven II pp. 245–246]

That is the first thing to understand about the fishing peoples of Virar’s coast: their own records and their observers agree that they are old, and agree on little else. The second thing is that “Koli” on this coast was never one community. The 1882 Gazetteer distinguished the sea-fishing Son Kolis, the Mangelas, the Vaitis, the Christian Kolis of Bassein, and the “Urap Kolis” who had returned from Christianity, as well as a dozen inland Koli tribes, the Mahadev and Malhari Kolis among them. [n08; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 148, 160 ff.] Of the fishermen of the district it said: “the Uraps and other Christian Kolis in Bassein… are professional fishermen, but the mass of the sea-fishing population are Son Kolis.” [n08; n49; Gaz XIII-1 p. 55]

The Son Kolis

The Gazetteer estimated 8,000 Son Kolis, “chiefly along the coast south of the Vaitarna”, which is to say from Arnala southward along the whole Virar and Vasai shore. It thought them “probably a tribe of Kolis who have mixed with foreign settlers from beyond the sea”. Their name might mean “the younger or later-come Kolis”, or come from son, red, or sonay, stranger. [n08; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 148–149] Enthoven added a Dravidian possibility, Caldwell’s sonag, and left the question undecided. [n35; Enthoven II]

Their arrival on the coast is not dated. Enthoven’s author wrote that “history is not clear as to their arrival on the coast, but a probable inference from tradition is that a majority of the present Kolis is descended from immigrants from the hills who arrived some four hundred years ago”, and then threw doubt on his own inference: “doubt is thrown upon the tradition by the difficulty of understanding the sudden conversion of hillmen into fishers.” [n35; n49; Enthoven II p. 259] Four hundred years before 1920 would be the early sixteenth century. That date sits uneasily with Wilson’s view of the Son Kolis as the original people of Bombay, and with the fact that boats and fishermen are recorded on this coast since Sopara’s port days (Chapter 04). The plain conclusion is that the tradition records a movement of some Koli families, not the beginning of fishing here.

The Thana Son Kolis looked south for their origin and their authority. Their tribal head, the Gazetteer said, was the Vagh Patil at Alibag in Kolaba, “whence the Thana Son Kolis say they originally came”, and village headmen (patils) were his shishyas, disciples. [n08; Gaz XIII-1 p. 149] Enthoven called the hereditary head the Sarpatil at Alibag, and recorded a religious tradition that the Kolis were taught “the gods properly” by Kalu Bhagat, a Koli of Varsoli near Alibag, “in the times of the Peshwas some two centuries ago”, who directed them to Khandoba of Jejuri; before that, the Kolis said of themselves, there had been “ignorance (adnyani)”. Enthoven’s author read this as “the first Hinduising of the Koli tribe”. [n35; Enthoven II p. 260] It is a tradition, recorded about 1920, about events placed around 1720. It shows at least that the Kolis themselves remembered their Khandoba worship as something that came to them, not something they had always had.

The 1882 description is one of the most vivid in the Gazetteer. The Son Kolis were short and sturdy with powerful shoulders; the men wore a top-knot and a moustache. They were “hardworking, hospitable and honest” but “much given to drink”. They were fishers, sailors, husbandmen and labourers. Their Marathi had “many strange words and so curious an accent” that it was hard to follow; they said r for d and n for l. [n08; n49; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 148–149] Enthoven called their home tongue “a patois of Marathi gradually obsolescent under the grinding of the Local Board school and the newspaper”, which may be the earliest written observation on this coast of a dialect giving way to the standard language; it gave the forms tavade for tikde (there) and avade for ikade (here). [n35; Enthoven II pp. 259–260]

Most of them refused to eat porpoise (gáda), crocodile, kend, whale (devmása, “god-fish”) and a few others, and ate all other fish, fowl, goats and sheep; their daily food was rice, nachni bread, pulse and fish. [n08; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 148–149] The men wore a loincloth, waistcloth, woollen jacket and red broadcloth cap. The women wore a loose long-sleeved bodice and a robe wound tight and ending above the knee, with glass bangles on the left hand only. “At their weddings the bangles intended for the right hand are consecrated and thrown into the sea, the ocean being invoked to take care of the husband.” [n08; Gaz XIII-1 p. 149] Enthoven, writing later, recorded instead that the women wore a silver bracelet on the right hand and nothing else there, a sign of the sea-god’s covenant protecting the fishermen; the two accounts describe the same right hand from two ends of a custom. [n35; Enthoven II p. 259] Girls married after eighteen and boys after twenty-two; they burned their dead, allowed widow marriage and practised polygamy; they made pilgrimages to Benares, Nasik, Pandharpur and Jejuri. Their family gods were Khanderao, Bhavani, Bhairav, Babdev, Vir, Kalkai, Cheda and Marubai, kept in the houses of older men, where the rest came daily to pray and take bhandar (turmeric) or vibhut (ash). Koli women sold the fish and, Enthoven said, “wield great power, manage household affairs”. [n08; n35; Gaz XIII-1 p. 149; Enthoven II p. 260] “Most of them have a good market for their fish, and on the whole are well-to-do.” [n08; Gaz XIII-1 p. 149]

Before Act XIX of 1844, Kolis, Mangelas and Vaitis paid a fishing poll-tax, dāy-dene, of one rupee four annas. [n08; Gaz XIII-1, fisheries footnote] In June and July, when the boats could not go out, the fishermen made new ropes, nets and sails, and some took to tillage. [n08; n49; Gaz XIII-1 p. 55]

The Mangelas, and Narangi

The Mangelas are the fishing community most often named in connection with a particular Virar village, and the record on them is tangled.

The 1882 Gazetteer counted 10,718 Mangelas in Mahim, Dahanu and Salsette, with the surnames Nijak, Dhanu, Kinhi, Mare, Somte, Pagdhar, Naik and Chodhre. They did not shave the top of the head. Their Marathi was “indistinct… with many Gujarati words”: kain gelā hotā? for kothe gelā hotā?, “where had you gone?”; shāḍi for shāḷā; mana bhuk nāngli for malā bhuk lāgli, “I am hungry”. “The child is a Mangela” was a common phrase for an abusive boy. They were fishermen, coasting traders and labourers; their women sold the fish; the men wore the red broadcloth cap; their marriages took place at sunset with a Palshe Brahman as priest; they were Smartas with no images at home; a patil settled disputes. In Dahanu they were also called Divars. [n08; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 147–148] Grierson in 1905 called the Mangelas “a tribe of fishermen in Thana”, with the greatest numbers returned from Mahim, Bassein and Dahanu and 11,538 at the 1901 census, and described their speech as “a dialect of the same kind as Vadval, Samvedi, etc., with a strong admixture of Gujarati”. [n22; LSI vol. VII] The Gazetteer also listed Mangelas among the groups of Gujarat origin who “dress in Gujarat fashion and speak Gujarati at home”. [n35; Gaz XIII-1 p. 64]

A second strand of the Gazetteer complicates this: elsewhere it treats the Mitna Machhis, a Dahanu fishing class of 2,957 “found only in Dahanu”, and says the Mangelas or Machhis are a Dahanu class. Read that way, the Mangelas were not a Bassein group at all in 1882. [n49; Gaz XIII-1 p. 148] Grierson’s 1901 figure, with Bassein among the places of greatest numbers, suggests that by the turn of the century the Mangelas were counted in Bassein too. The two readings cannot be fully reconciled from the notes.

What is clear is the modern association. Narangi (also spelled Naringi), a village in Virar, inside Ward 5 of the Vasai-Virar corporation, is described in its Wikipedia stub as a place where “the people living in Narangi are mostly the Mangela community”. The same stub glosses Mangela as mang, net, and ela, people, and names the corporator at the time of writing as Dr Vasant Mangela of the Bahujan Vikas Aghadi, chairman of a sand-lifters’ cooperative. The stub has no references. [n49; Wikipedia: Narangi] Marathi Wikipedia calls Narangi “a well-known village in Virar”. [n49; Marathi Wikipedia: Virar] A Wikipedia-derived list of Mangela Koli villages, seen only through a search summary, includes Kalamb, Navapur (Vatar), Arnala, Arnala Killa, Virar and Pachu Bandar; the Mangela Kolis article itself, when fetched, carried no village list, so this is unverified. [n49; n08] Narangi appears in the 1727–30 Tombo de Baçaim, the Portuguese register of crown estates, as the second half of “Virar Naringuy”, the aldeia held by D. Pedro de Meneses at 2,000 pardaus, the highest rent of all the aldeias in the register (Chapter 27 tells that entry). [n40; Tombo fl. 121] Whether Mangelas lived there then is not recorded.

The sand-lifters’ cooperative in that stub is a small clue to how the fishing communities of the Vaitarna mouth have earned a living in recent decades. Sand mining near Arnala is blamed by local people for the depressions in the sea floor that have drowned bathers (Chapter 32). [n45] No source in the notes ties those two facts together, and this chapter does not.

The Vaitis

The Vaitis were, in 1882, 4,596 persons “found in North Bassein and South Mahim”, which is precisely the Virar coast and the Vaitarna creek country north of it. They ranked lower than the Son Kolis “but apparently belong to the same tribe”. Their founder was “one Valhya Koli”, an echo of the Valmiki legend of Valya Koli, the robber who became the poet of the Ramayana, and their headman lived at Chaul in Kolaba. They had no sub-divisions and no surnames. They were cultivators, fishers, sailors and day labourers, and a few dealt in timber and hay. They chiefly worshipped Ram and the sea, and kept only a coconut at home as an object of worship. Widow marriage was allowed. [n08; n49; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 181–182]

No later source in the notes mentions the Vaitis by name on the Virar side. Whether the community still identifies itself as Vaiti, or has merged into the wider Koli and Mangela identities of the coast, is unknown.

Christian Kolis and the church at Arnala

The Christian Kolis of Bassein were, in the Gazetteer’s account, the professional fishermen of the sub-division, and among the castes from which the Portuguese-era converts came, “the bulk of them are Bhandaris, Kolis, and Kunbis”. [n49; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 55, 200–201] Christian Kolis are recorded today in Mumbai, Bandra, Gorai, Bassein and Agashi; they share the shakarpuda engagement and other customs with Hindu Kolis, and some revere Ekvira. [n08; Wikipedia: Koli Christians] The modern scholar Sidh Mendiratta wrote that the Indian Christians who stayed in Salsette after 1739 were “overwhelmingly either fisherfolk or farmers, mostly of Koli, Kunbi or Bhandari castes”. [n34; Mendiratta 2017 p. 218]

The Christian history of the fishermen of Arnala is told in Chapter 32: the hut chapel built in 1919 by Fr Ismail Da Costa, assistant priest at Agashi; the tin shed dedicated to St Peter in 1921, built with the Kolis’ help; the church blessed by Archbishop Joaquim Lima on 27 December 1931, to which a Hindu community association gave Rs 3,000; and the line in the community’s own history that “Kolis consider Fr Da Costa as the true Apostle of Arnala”. [n40; EI Arnala] The Rs 3,000 from a Hindu association is the detail to keep: at Arnala, Hindu and Christian fishermen paid for each other’s buildings.

Not all Christian Kolis stayed Christian. The story of those who returned to Hinduism after the cholera of 1818–1828, the Urap Kolis and the Thalkars, belongs with the other returners in Section VIII below.

Arnala and the shore today

Arnala, at the northern tip of the coast, is the Koli village of the Virar side above all others. A Marathi fort website says three to four thousand people, mostly Koli fishermen, live around the fort. [n45; n49; Durg Bharari; Marathi Wikipedia: Arnala] The 2011 census, as compiled by two secondary websites, gave Arnala village 15,331 people in 3,005 households with 1,473 Scheduled Tribe members, and Arnala Killa, the fort island, 2,566 people. The two compilations disagree on literacy (70.87 per cent in one, 80.10 per cent in the other for Arnala village), a conflict this chapter cannot resolve. [n49; n40; CensusIndia2011.com; census2011.co.in] Arnala kept its own gram panchayat, whose sarpanch is quoted in 2026 reports. [n45]

The festivals of the fishing year belong to Chapter 32, but their outline is this. Narali Purnima, the full moon of Shravan, marks the end of the monsoon closure and the start of the new season, with coconut offerings to the sea; fishing communities across Mumbai, Thane and Palghar keep it. [n08; Jio Institute; News on AIR] Koli Holi, Shimga, can run fifteen days from the day after Mahashivratri, and in Vasai, as in Worli and Colaba, the Holi pyre is built of areca-nut branches. [n08; FPJ (search summary)] Jivdani, the goddess on the hill above Virar, is called by devotional websites the “Kuldevi of Mangayle, Koli and Bhandari community”. [n08; apnisanskriti (search summary)]

The fishing economy itself is under strain. Studies and reports summarised in the notes describe Vasai as having become “largely a one-fish town” after decades in which fishermen caught many species in their traditional dol bag-nets; purse seines are the most immediate threat, creek pollution chokes the breeding grounds, and educated young people are leaving fishing. A 2017 Maharashtra government resolution banned LED lights, purse seining and gill netting by mechanised boats in certain waters, and traditional fishermen have demanded a special investigation into illegal purse-seine and LED fishing. [n08; ICSF; Outlook; FPJ] No source gives boat counts, landings or the Hindu–Christian proportion of the fishing families of Arnala or any other Virar-side village. [n08]

At Arnala the fishing families also took up the newest crop of the coast. Moreshwar Bhoir of Arnala brought the sonchafa (Magnolia champaca, the “Velankar chafa” from Uday Velankar’s nursery at Kudal) to the village in the late 1980s, and by 2023 the Arnala Farmers’ Cooperative Society, chaired by his grandson Kiran Patil, had 1,100 members. [n17; 101Reporters, Jan 2023] Which community Bhoir’s family belongs to the source does not say; Bhoir is a surname shared by Sudagris and Malhar Kolis in the colonial lists, so the name alone settles nothing. [n08; n35]

III. The Samvedi Brahmans

The oldest anchor

Of the Brahman communities of the Virar side, the Samvedis are the ones most rooted in these particular villages. In 1872, the census counted 2,563 of them in the whole Thana district, 1,323 men and 1,240 women, and the Gazetteer added that they were “found only in Bassein and Mahim”. [n49; n08; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 74, 82] Enthoven’s entire entry on them, forty years later, was one line: “Samavedis are found only in the Thana district, where they chiefly grow garden crops.” [n35; Enthoven I pp. 241–242, 249]

Their name means followers of the Sama Veda, the Veda of chants. The only documentary anchor for a Samaveda Brahman presence on this coast before the Portuguese is a land grant of 1026 in Salsette to a Brahman of the Chhandoga shakha, the Samaveda school (Chapter 05 tells the Shilahara grants). [n35; n03] That grant does not use the word Samvedi, and it is on Salsette, not on the Virar side. It shows that Samaveda Brahmans were known to the Shilahara kings; it does not show that the community of 1882 descended from them.

A second early record, much closer to Virar, is worth setting beside it. A stone found at Agashi, now in the Collector’s garden at Thane, dated Shak 1072 (AD 1150) in the reign of the Shilahara king Haripaldev, grants the village of Vattéraka in Shurparaka, and its witnesses include “Risi Mhatara, headman of Vattéraka village, Naguji Mhatara, Ananta Nayaka, and Changdev Mhatara”. The Gazetteer identifies Vattéraka as “the modern Vatargaon, two miles north-west of Sopara”. [n40; Gaz XIV p. 385 and n.1] Vatar is one of the three villages where, seven hundred and thirty years later, the Gazetteer found eighty-five families of Samvedi Brahmans. The name Mhatara resembles the surname Mhatre, which occurs among Samvedi, Vadval and Agri families today; but no source tests whether the headmen of 1150 were ancestors of any of them, and the resemblance is an inference only. [n40]

The tradition of Odisha and the musicians

The Samvedis’ own account of their origin is quite different. In a community blog post of 2008 and the Wikipedia articles derived from it, the Samvedis came to Shurparaka, that is Sopara and the country round it, from Utkala or Kalinga, modern Odisha, as temple musicians and dancers. The name Samvedi, or “Shamedi” as it is said locally, is explained as “musicians of the samadhi temple”, the shrine of the Shankaracharya at Nirmal. Their family deities are given as Bhuvaneshwari and Vimala. Their Vedic practice, the story goes, lapsed under Buddhism and was restored by Adi Shankaracharya at Nirmal, whom they regard as their chief preacher, and their musicians served the dawn and night worship with sanai, choughada, mridanga, bheri and veena at the Nirmal Shankaracharya Samadhi Mandir, the Sureshwar (Suleshwar) Mandir, and the Bhavani Shankar Mandir at Agashi. The community deity is Nirmaleshwar on the Nirmal hillock. [n49; n08; nirajpatilsblog 2008; Wikipedia: Shamedi; Wikipedia: Samvedi]

The same sources give folk derivations for the surnames: Naik from nayak, poet-playwright; Vartak from vartakah, players of brass and bronze; Mhatre from mahapatra, lead singer; Joshi from jyoshih, music director; Vaze from vache, commentator; Patil from pattolika, make-up artist. The gotras are given as Bharadwaj, Kashyap, Atri, Kaushik, Vashishtha and Angirasa. [n49; nirajpatilsblog 2008]

The community history continues: under Raja Pratap Bimba their work shifted to sugarcane and nagvel (betel vine) cultivation; paddy was added in the late seventeenth century; flower gardens and wadis came in the eighteenth century as Bombay grew; and a visit by the Shankaracharya in 1926 was commemorated by a large yajna. [n49; n08; nirajpatilsblog 2008]

All of this is tradition, and the notes are firm on its weaknesses. The Wikipedia page that carries it is tagged for original research and lacking sources; it has a single reference; its dates contradict each other, giving “1500 BC” for the coming from Odisha and 497 BC for Shankara. [n08; n49] No scholarly source supports the Odisha origin. [n35] The folk etymologies of the surnames run into a plain difficulty: Vartak and Mhatre, Naik and Patil are also Pachkalshi, Agri and Koli surnames in the 1882 Gazetteer’s own lists, so a surname alone does not identify the community, still less its ancient trade. [n49; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 116, 130] The oldest written stratum, the 1882 Gazetteer and the Bimbakhyan as it reports it, ties the Palshe Brahmans, not the Samvedis, to King Bimb; the Samvedi–Bimba link exists only in modern community tellings. [n08] And the one linguistic observation from a century ago points the other way: Grierson, in 1905, judged the Samvedi dialect “a mechanical mixture of Konkan Marathi and Gujarati”, which suggests a western, Gujarat-linked contact zone rather than Odisha. That last point is the research notes’ inference, not a published finding. [n35; n22]

The fair way to tell it is the way the notes recommend: the Odisha musicians are the community’s own story of itself, held with pride, and the record cannot confirm it. Where the record can speak, it describes the Samvedis of 1882 as farmers and gardeners, not musicians, and the community history itself agrees that by the time of the Portuguese they were growing sugarcane and betel.

The Samvedis in 1882

The Gazetteer’s portrait is detailed, and it is the single best description of any community of the Virar side before the twentieth century.

The Samvedis were “strong, tall and fair, with regular features… clean, neat, sober, thrifty and orderly. Husbandmen and gardeners by craft, they live in one-storied houses with walls of wood planking and tiled roofs. Their daily food is rice, pulse and vegetables.” [n08; Gaz XIII-1 p. 82] The men wore short waistcloths, a shoulder cloth wrapped round the body, and “red broadcloth caps like Telugu Brahmans”; on high days they wore white turbans, coats and silk-bordered waistcloths. The women wore the Maratha bodice and robe. The Gazetteer named the women’s gold ornaments: mudaga khudi for the head; lavanga bali, mugdya and gáthe for the ear; putlyáchi mál, vajratik, and flint, coral and gold bead necklaces for the neck; tode, vále and phule for the feet. Boys wore silver. [n08; Gaz XIII-1 p. 82]

Their life-cycle rites were set down step by step. On the fifth day after a birth, Sati was worshipped, and on the twelfth the child was named with a caste dinner. The thread ceremony came before the age of ten. The boy stood on a raised earthen seat, the bahule, with a cloth held between him and his father; when the chanting ended the cloth was pulled away and the drums beat. Then came the mock pilgrimage to Benares: the boy set off and walked about fifty paces, and his maternal uncle asked him not to go and promised him his daughter in marriage. The boy came back and begged alms, and each guest gave him a pulse ball and two annas to a rupee. [n08; Gaz XIII-1 p. 82]

Girls were married before ten. A bride-price of two hundred to a thousand rupees was paid to the girl’s father, which the Gazetteer records without comment, and which marks the Samvedis off from the dowry-paying Brahmans of the Deccan. The date was fixed at an astrologer’s house, the tithinischay. The day before the wedding, twenty to thirty earthen pots were brought from a potter and worshipped. Lamps made of rice flour were lit in a bamboo basket, the zad, and held over the heads of the elders, and the priest called “Vidávida ála ho”, “the time for sandal and betel has come”. The food was pápads, vadás and shev. The groom rode to the bride’s house on horseback. The couple stood on stools facing each other with a cloth between them, and when the cloth was pulled aside they garlanded each other. A procession, the varghoda, followed. Widow marriage was not allowed. [n08; n49; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 82–83]

The dead were burned, except children under three. On the third day the ashes were gathered and food on a leaf plate was given to the village Mhar. Mourning lasted ten days, with rice balls offered on the eleventh. If the dead man was married but childless, a caste-fellow, the vánzkuli, was taken outside the village, paid two rupees and fed rice, ámti and vadás, and feasted again on the thirteenth day; food cooked that day could not stay in the house. [n08; Gaz XIII-1 p. 83]

They had no headman. Disputes were settled by majority vote of the caste, and fines were spent on feeding Brahmans. “They send their boys to school and are on the whole prosperous.” [n08; Gaz XIII-1 p. 83]

The Gazetteer’s statement that they had no headman needs one qualification from the same set of volumes. In the hamlet of Koprad, half a mile south-west of Bolinj, the Gazetteer’s antiquarians found “an inscribed stone (3’5” x 1’2” x 7”) of the fifteenth century… near the house of the headman Bhiku Patil, a Samvedi Brahman”. [n49; Gaz XIV p. 342] Bhiku Patil was a village headman, a patil, not a caste headman; the two statements describe different offices. His is one of the very few names of a Hindu villager of the Virar side that the nineteenth-century record preserves, and it comes down to us because a carved stone happened to lie near his door.

Language

The Samvedis, the Gazetteer said, “speak incorrect Marathi”, and a footnote gave three examples: áde for kothe, “where”; hád for kā, “why”; geltu for gelā hotā, “had gone”. [n08; n49; Gaz XIII-1 p. 82 n.] Grierson, about 1905, estimated 2,700 Samvedi speakers in Bassein and a place his OCR renders as “Alabim”, probably Alibag, and described the dialect as “a mixed dialect, the chief components of which are the current Marathi of Thana and vulgar Gujarati”. Among its features he listed ch and chh becoming s and h (hakra, “son”; hit, “field”); s becoming h; strong masculine nominatives in -o, as in Gujarati and Konkani; and a dative in -do (mansa-do). [n22; LSI vol. VII] He grouped Samvedi with Vadval and Phudgi as three small dialects of Thana “which still have preserved many of the characteristic features of Gujarati Bhili”. [n22; LSI vol. VII]

The same speech, under the names Samvedi, Kadodi or Kupari, is spoken today by both the Hindu Samvedis and the Christian Kuparis. Modern estimates of its speakers range from about 50,000 to 60,000; the notes warn that Grierson’s 2,700 probably counted only part of the community, so the numbers cannot be read as growth. [n22] The s-to-h shift Grierson noticed in 1905 is still the dialect’s best-known feature. [n22] Wikipedia’s Virar page names “Samvedi Brahmins, Kupari, Vadwals, Bhandhari, Panmali, and Aagri” as local communities who “speak local Konkani dialects”; the “Konkani” label is disputed by the linguistic evidence and should be read as a loose description. [n49; Wikipedia: Virar]

The villages, and the parting of ways

In the 1882 record the Samvedi Brahmans live in a tight ring of garden villages on the western side of what is now Virar. At Agashi, Vatar and Koprad, the Gazetteer said, “about eighty-five families of Samvedi Brahmans, fifteen families of Pachkalshis, and about seventy-five families of Native Christians originally Samvedis and Pachkalshis” dried ripe plantains, the craft for which Agashi’s produce was “the best in the district”. [n49; n34; Gaz XIII-1 p. 395] At Sopara, a little to the south, the Hindus were “chiefly Samvedi Brahmans and Bhandaris”. [n34; Gaz XIV p. 315] A modern list of villages around the Nirmal lake associated with the Samvedis names Agashi, Vatar, Kofrad (Koprad), Bolinj, Nala and Umrale. [n08; Wikipedia: Samvedi (search summary)] Gass, in the south, had a Samvedi Brahman householder named Bab Naik in 1882 (Chapter 26). [n42; Gaz XIV p. 341]

That sentence about the plantain driers is the hinge of the whole story of the Samvedis and the Kuparis. In the same three villages, in the same craft, lived Hindu Samvedi families and Christian families whom the Gazetteer called “originally Samvedis and Pachkalshis”. The two were, in the notes’ own words, “separate religious groups of one husbandman-gardener stock”. [n49] How the Christian half came to be Christian, between the Franciscan mission at Agashi in 1535 and the end of Portuguese rule in 1739, is the subject of Chapter 34. Why the Hindu half did not convert is a question no document answers. Mendiratta’s work suggests some of the reasons the pressure was uneven: the Portuguese Provisions in favour of Christianity of 1549–1557 “were never enforced in the Northern Province’s subdivisions closest to the mainland”, and whole villages tended to convert “following the conversion of the respective matará or headman”. [n34; Mendiratta 2017 pp. 210–211] A village whose headman stayed Hindu may have stayed largely Hindu; the presence of a Hindu Samvedi patil at Koprad in 1882, in a hamlet that also held Christian families, fits that picture but does not prove it. [n49; inference]

The Hindu Samvedis of the Agashi side lived through the Maratha conquest of 1737–39 on the winning side. The temples that the Maratha subhedar Shankarji Keshav Phadke built at Agashi and Nirmal (see Part Two) were the temples their musicians, by tradition, served. The Gazetteer’s 1882 verdict, “on the whole prosperous”, and its note that they sent their boys to school, describe a community comfortable under British rule. [n08]

The Samvedis today

The twentieth-century record of the Hindu Samvedis is almost empty in the notes. No academic study of the Samvedi community, its dialect as spoken by Hindus, its present population or its institutions was found. [n08] Wikipedia lists among notable Samvedis Rajan Naik, the BJP politician who won the Nalasopara assembly seat (which covers Virar) in 2024, and the cricketer Ayush Mhatre of Virar, who commuted about eighty kilometres each way by train to Churchgate for cricket and went on to captain India’s Under-19 side on an England tour; both community attributions are tagged “citation needed”, so this chapter records them as Wikipedia’s claim only. [n08; n47; ESPNcricinfo; Wisden] A 2019 opinion piece in Swarajya by Shrish Vaze described Hindu Vadvals, affluent Catholics and Samavedi Brahmins as the groups that “dominate the villages”, and the villages as “highly segregated socially and communally”; it is a polemical piece and is cited here only as one voice. [n41; Swarajya, 5 Mar 2019]

The bride-price, the bahule, the mock pilgrimage to Benares and the rice-flour lamps in the zad are vivid in the 1882 text. Whether any of them survive in Samvedi weddings in Agashi or Vatar in 2026 is a question for oral history; no source in the notes says. [n08]

IV. The Kuparis of Virar and the northern villages

In brief

The Kuparis, the Samvedi Christians, are the community to which this book gives a whole Part (Chapters 33 to K7). This section tells their story as it was lived on the Virar side, the northern half of their country, from the Franciscans at Agashi to the parishes of 2026, and leaves the community-wide themes to those chapters.

On the Virar side the Kupari villages are the northern half of the old “garden or agar of Sopara”: Agashi, Bolinj, Umrale, Nanbhat, Wagholi, Vatar with its hamlets of Jeladi, Barodi and Adlay, Koprad and Nandakhal, Rajodi on the coast, and Shirlai within Agashi parish. The community’s own list of thirteen Kupari places includes Agashi, Nandakhal, Umraala (Umrale), Naanbhat, Shrilaiwadi, Bolinj, Wagholi and Rajodi; the 1882 list of plantain-drying villages, “Agáshi, Vagholi, Vatár, Bolinj, Koprád, Nala, Umrále, Rajodi, and Murdes”, independently names most of them. [n40; Rajan D’Mello blog 2009; Gaz XIII-1 p. 292]

In 1881 Agashi had 1,500 Christians among its 6,823 people, about 22 per cent; it is the only Virar-side village for which the Gazetteer gives a Christian count (the congregation of 2,415 it gives for the Holy Spirit church at Koprad is a parish figure, not a village count). [n49; n40; Gaz XIV p. 2] In 1876 Gerson da Cunha counted 900 Native Christians in the parish of Agasi and 2,000 in Espirito Santo, the Holy Spirit parish of Nandakhal. [n40; da Cunha p. 161] By December 2016 Nandakhal parish alone counted 2,150 families and 7,339 people in twenty-nine hamlet groups, and had, by its own count, sent sixty-eight priests and many nuns into the Church. [n40; Wikipedia: Holy Spirit Church Nandakhal]

The word Kupari is absent from every primary text searched, from 1876 to 1931. Its most repeated derivation is the Portuguese compadre, co-father or godfather, which fits the Portuguese godparent words, padrinho and madrinha, that the Christians used at baptism in 1882 and the padron and madron that Grierson recorded in 1905, but no scholarly source states it. A second, unsourced folk etymology, “those who drank from the well (kup)”, is regarded by many as an insult and belongs to folklore. [n34] In the 1880s the upper-class Christians called themselves Firgi, and Hindus called them Firangis or, more often, Kiristans. [n34; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 199–200]

For their conversion and why it happened, see Chapter 34; for their life under Maratha and British rule, K3; for their language, dress, food and songs, K4; for their migrations, faith and revival since 1947, K5; for their family names and villages, K6. What matters for this chapter is that on the Virar side the Kuparis were never a separate world. They shared villages, crops, a dialect and, in 1882, a single sentence of the Gazetteer with the Hindu Samvedis and Pachkalshis beside whom they worked.

“Virar has many Kuparis”: what the record shows

It is often said in the community that Virar has many Kuparis, and the record bears that out in one sense and is silent in another. It bears it out in the map. Of the thirteen places in the community’s own list of Kupari villages (Nirmal, Agashi, Nandakhal, Gass, Bhuigaon, Umrale, Nanbhat, Shirlaiwadi, Bolinj, Mardes, Wagholi, Gomes Ali and Rajodi), eight, Agashi, Nandakhal, Umrale, Nanbhat, Shirlaiwadi, Bolinj, Wagholi and Rajodi, lie on the Virar side or on its southern edge by Nirmal. [n39; n40; Sachin Mendes blog 2007; Rajan D’Mello blog 2009] Two more villages that the 1882 Gazetteer names as homes of Christian plantain driers, Vatar and Koprad, are also on the Virar side. [n40; Gaz XIII-1 p. 395] The Holy Spirit church at Nandakhal is described in the notes as a Virar West church; Our Lady of Lourdes at Shirlai is listed as a Virar West church with an Agashi post office; and the Catholic centres of Virar East and Virar West were carved out of Nandakhal parish. [n49; Wikipedia: Holy Spirit Church Nandakhal; UCAN directory (search summary)] In the municipal sense of the word, then, much of the Kupari heartland is now Virar.

The record is silent on numbers. No census has ever counted Kuparis: caste was dropped from the census after 1931, and the word Kupari does not appear in any primary text from 1876 to 1931. [n34] Community bloggers estimate the whole community at “about 40,000 to 45,000” (2007) or “around 60,000+” (2015), figures the notes treat as unsourced. [n34; Sachin Mendes blog 2007; Post in Mumbai blog 2015] The only hard numbers for the Virar side are parish counts: 900 Native Christians in the parish of Agasi and 2,000 in Espirito Santo (Nandakhal) in 1876; 1,500 Christians in Agashi village in 1881; a congregation of 2,415 at the Holy Spirit church of Koprad in 1882; and 2,150 families and 7,339 people in Nandakhal parish in December 2016. [n40; n46; da Cunha p. 161; Gaz XIV pp. 2, 210; Wikipedia: Holy Spirit Church Nandakhal] The 2011 census gives the Vasai-Virar corporation as 8.28 per cent Christian and the whole taluka as 8.25 per cent, but those figures include every Christian group, Kupari, Vadval, Koli, Goan, Keralite and Protestant alike. [n34; n49] How many Kuparis live in Virar, in the old villages or in the towers that now surround them, no source says.

Before the conversion

The Kuparis’ ancestors on the Virar side were, by the best period evidence, the same garden people as the Hindu Samvedis and Pachkalshis of 1882: the Gazetteer called the Christian plantain driers of Agashi, Vatar and Koprad “originally Samvedis and Pachkalshis”, and the husbandmen of Sopara “Christians, many of them converted Sámvedi Bráhmans”. [n34; n40; Gaz XIII-1 p. 395; Gaz XIV pp. 314–315] Rita Rodricks, writing for Sahapedia, puts the first converts among “Samvedi Brahmins who had settled near the Vaitarna between Agashi and Nalasopara”, which is the Virar side and the Sopara garden; her article cites no sources and the notes treat it as an informed popular synthesis. [n34; Sahapedia] The deeper history of that stock, the Shilahara headmen of Vatar in 1150, the fifteenth-century stone at Koprad and the community’s traditions of Odisha and of King Bimb, is told in Section III above and in Chapter 33. One sentence of the 1882 Gazetteer belongs here too: “One recent report mentions a Christian village in Bassein that claims to be older than the Portuguese. No confirmation of this statement has been received.” The village is not named. [n34; n49; Gaz XIII-1 p. 200 n.7]

The conversion on the Virar side, 1535–1739

The Christian history of the Kuparis begins on the Virar side. Agashi, in Gerson da Cunha’s words, “was the first place of this district visited by Franciscan missionaries”. In 1535 the Franciscan Antonio do Porto founded an orphanage there for forty boys under the invocation of Nossa Senhora da Luz, Our Lady of Light, and in April 1540 a Gujarat army under the general da Cunha calls “Bramaluc”, retreating after it was repelled at Bassein by Captain Lourenço de Tavora, burnt it; five people who had stayed in the orphanage and refused to accept Islam were burnt alive, “a batch of Christian martyrs… consisting entirely of native converts”. [n40; n34; da Cunha pp. 159–160; Gaz XIV pp. 1–2] Those five unnamed converts of Agashi are the earliest Christians of the Kupari country whom any record mentions, and the Agashi parish’s English school, Holy Martyrs, founded in 1938, carries their memory in its name. [n40; EI Agashi] In 1554 a great mosque at Agashi became the commandant’s residence, a sign of how the new power took over the old port. [n40; Gaz XIV pp. 1–2]

A church was built at Agashi in 1568, by community and heritage-survey dating, and around that year, by the parish’s own history, two churches stood there, Our Lady of Light under the Franciscans and St James under the secular clergy. [n40; CRIT Vol III; EI Agashi] About 1573 the Franciscans built the Holy Spirit church at Nandakhal “primarily for the convenience of those parishioners of Holy Cross who lived closer to Nandakhal”; before that the priest of Nirmal had served them, and the first Franciscan priests of Nandakhal were appointed in 1585. [n40; EI Nandkhal; Wikipedia: Holy Spirit Church Nandakhal] In 1634 Agashi had, da Cunha says, “a rich monastery of the Dominicans”, which Mughal raiders from Gujarat destroyed. [n40; da Cunha p. 160; Gaz XIV p. 2] Chapter 34 gives the full story; on the Virar side the dates conflict between sources, and no contemporary Portuguese document for either church was found. [n34; n40]

Why did the Samvedi and Pachkalshi gardeners of these villages become Christian, when their neighbours, sometimes in the same hamlet, did not? The notes hold several answers, and none is complete. The 1882 Gazetteer judged that “force may have been used in some cases, but the Portuguese accounts seem to show that persuasion and interest were the chief means of conversion”. [n34; Gaz XIII-1 p. 201 n.2] The modern scholar Sidh Mendiratta wrote that between about 1570 and 1620 the Jesuits and Franciscans converted “a majority of the population” of Salsette and of the Bassein and Agashi subdivisions; that “whole villages were converted, normally following the conversion of the respective matará or headman”; and that “Christian converts who were farmers or fishermen were meted out particular benefits, while Hindu and Muslim communities that did not convert had to revert to low-key and mostly private religious practices”. He also noted that the harsher Portuguese Provisions in favour of Christianity “were never enforced in the Northern Province’s subdivisions closest to the mainland”, which takes in the Agaçaim subdivision of the Virar side. [n34; Mendiratta 2017 pp. 210–211] The Pachkalshis’ own tradition, recorded by Enthoven, speaks of persecution and forced conversion. [n41; Enthoven III p. 161] And the presence in 1882 of a Hindu Samvedi headman, Bhiku Patil, at Koprad, a hamlet that also held Christian families, fits Mendiratta’s headman pattern without proving it. [n49; Gaz XIV p. 342; inference]

The most famous son of that first Christian century may also have been a son of Agashi. St Gonsalo Garcia, born in 1556 or 1557 to a Portuguese soldier and a local “Canarim” mother, crucified at Nagasaki on 5 February 1597 and canonised in 1862 as one of the twenty-six Martyrs of Japan, is described in Lisbon archive (ANTT) documents as “natural de Agaçaim”, a native of Agashi. Tradition ties him instead to Bassein fort, where he studied; the dispute is between the fort and Agashi, and no source disputes the Bassein country itself. [n07; n38; Indian Catholic Matters 2023; DNA 2014] The patron of the Diocese of Vasai is therefore, by the archival reading, a man of the Virar side.

The landholders of the Kupari villages, 1727–1730

Twelve years before the Portuguese fell, the Tombo de Baçaim recorded who held the Crown’s leases on the lands of these villages. Every holder was Portuguese, a fidalgo, a widow or a religious order; none was a convert. In the caçabé of Agaçaim, D. Domingas Pereira de Azevedo held the pacaria of Agaçaim, the coloria of Agaçaim and of the “Ilha das Vacas” (Arnala island), and the rent on Agaçaim’s wild palms; Manuel Varjão da Fonseca held “Vatar Rajoly” for lives at 700 pardaus a year, the highest rent in that list; Manuel de Abreu de Lima held “Vagoly” and Fernão da Silveira e Meneses “Vastal Vagoly” at 60 pardaus; Miguel Francisco Mira de Vasconcelos held “Nala Magir”. In the caçabé of Baçaim, D. Maria Henriques Coelho da Silva held “Bolinja e terra Belvar” at 450 pardaus. Lourenço Dias held “Rajoly e Caraly” at 90 pardaus and António de Lisboa the aldeia “Rajoly Marelá” at 240, and inland D. Pedro de Meneses held the aldeia “Virar Naringuy” at 2,000 pardaus. The Agaçaim caçabé alone had twenty-eight titles in the register. [n39; n40; n46; Tombo fls. 37, 41–45v, 54v, 121, 149; Teixeira and Pires 2007] The matching of these spellings to modern villages is by sound and context, and which “Rajoly” is Rajodi cannot be fixed. [n40]

The register matters to Kupari families for one reason. The 1931 census recorded that village tenants were baptised “and given the surname of the owner of the village, e.g., Demonte, Pereira, Ferreira”, and the Tombo’s commonest surnames, Pereira, Melo, Meneses, Coutinho, Almeida and Brito, are the commonest of the Vasai Christian surname stock. A Kupari family named Pereira or Meneses may therefore descend from tenants on such an estate. But no document in the notes ties any Kupari family of the Virar side to any one landholder or godparent, and the same surnames are common among Goan, Mangalorean and Bombay East Indian Catholics. [n39; Census 1931 Vol VIII Part I pp. 363–364] Chapter 38 tells the surnames in full.

After 1739: the Marathas, the cholera and the British

The war of 1737–1739 broke the churches of the Kupari country but not its congregations. The treaty secured only five churches in the whole former Northern Province; the Portuguese and their descendants “fled or were expelled… leaving only the Indian converted population behind”; and Goan “Canarim” priests, organised from Kurla from 1740, kept the parishes going. [n34; Mendiratta 2017 pp. 216–218; Gaz XIII-1 p. 203] On the Virar side Agashi’s churches were destroyed, and St James was rebuilt about 1760. Nandakhal’s church survived, by the parish legend because the Maratha soldiers who stormed it fled from a swarm of bees out of the surrounding woods; da Cunha, in 1876, called Espirito Santo “the oldest among the rural churches, and the only one standing in its primitive state”. [n40; da Cunha p. 161; EI Nandkhal] “When their possession of the Portuguese territory was secure,” the Gazetteer wrote, “the Marathas did not interfere with the practice of the Christian religion.” [n46; Gaz XIV p. 2] On 16 December 1760, the feast day of the Agashi church, Anquetil du Perron rode in “through the finest orchards in the world” and found the lanes “full of people going to church as freely as in a Christian country”, with the priest entertaining the Canarim priests who had helped at the service. [n40; da Cunha p. 164; Gaz XIV p. 2]

The pressure to return was real all the same. Madhavrao Peshwa, who ruled from 1761 to 1772, instituted, da Cunha says, a tax to pay Brahmans to purify the native Christians before receiving them back into their castes. [n34; da Cunha p. 149] The Kirpal Bhandaris went back after 1739, and in the cholera years of 1820 and 1828 the Christian Agris of a list of Bassein villages that includes Agashi, Virar and Navapur were taken back into Hinduism by Palshe priests; a British report blamed the cholera returns of 1820–21, about 3,000 Christians in all, on the lack of “respectable ministers”. [n34; n46; n49; Gaz XIII-1 p. 117 n.1; Mendiratta 2017 p. 220] Those were Christian Agris and Kolis, not Christian Samvedis. No source records a return of Kupari families to Hinduism on the Virar side, and the parish figures, 900 Christians at Agashi in 1876 and 1,500 in 1881, 2,000 at Espirito Santo in 1876 and a congregation of 2,415 at the Koprad Holy Spirit church in 1882, suggest that the garden congregations held and grew. [n40; n46] In 1837 Agashi was “the chief town on Bassein island with a large Christian and Hindu population”. [n40; Gaz XIV p. 2] In 1881 the Bassein sub-division as a whole had 14,070 Christians, 20.40 per cent of its 68,967 people. [n46; Gaz XIII-2 pp. 678–680]

Work in 1882: driers, gardeners and sugar-makers

The 1882 Gazetteer is the one source that describes the Kuparis’ ancestors at work on the Virar side, and it describes them as the best gardeners of the district. The most famous sentence is the one already quoted: at Agashi, Vatar and Koprad “about eighty-five families of Samvedi Brahmans, fifteen families of Pachkalshis, and about seventy-five families of Native Christians originally Samvedis and Pachkalshis” dried ripe plantains, the Christians being “gardeners who grow the fruit”. The season ran from October to January; the dried fruit was sold “to Thana, Bombay, Surat, Poona, and Sholapur” at about nine rupees a Bengal maund of eighty pounds; Agashi alone produced about 160 tons a year worth about Rs 27,000, and its dried plantains were “the best in the district”. [n34; n40; n48; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 292, 395; Gaz XIV p. 2] The plantain-drying villages named on another page, “Agáshi, Vagholi, Vatár, Bolinj, Koprád, Nala, Umrále, Rajodi, and Murdes”, are almost exactly the Kupari villages of today. [n40; Gaz XIII-1 p. 292]

The Christians made sugar too: sugar in Bassein was “chiefly made by Pachkalshis, Malis, Native Christians and Samvedi Brahmans”. [n34; n41; Gaz XIII-1 p. 395] Betel-nut growers were “partly Christians, Malis converted by the Portuguese, and partly Hindus of the Chavkalshi or Pachkalshi class”. [n41; Gaz XIII-1 ch. IV] In the Gazetteer’s ranking of the district’s farmers, “the most hardworking and skilful husbandmen are the Christians of Bassein, originally Brahmans, Bhandaris, Chavkalshis, Pachkalshis, Kharpatils, and Kolis, who grow sugarcane and plantains, and have turned the light sandy country about Bassein into an evergreen garden”. [n49; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 301–302] Even the word for the oil-cake manure that came by boat from the Gujarat ports differed by faith: Christians called it phod, others khap. [n48; Gaz XIII-1 p. 291] “Pigs are found in most Christian villages,” the Gazetteer added. [n34; Gaz XIII-1 p. 55]

Two Christian trades of the taluka were not the Kuparis’. The cartmen and axemen were the Christian Vadvals of Chulna, Manikpur, Gokhivra, Sandor and Barhanpur in the south, and the Christian fishermen were the Uraps and Christian Kolis; the Christians of the Virar side’s garden villages appear in the record as gardeners, plantain driers and sugar-makers. [n40; n49; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 55, 97] Of the Thana Christians generally the Gazetteer wrote that wealthy families who were village headmen and owners of rich garden land “often bring up one or more of their sons for the Church”, that English-speaking men worked in Bombay as clerks, and that Christians “pride themselves on never taking household service with Europeans”; those sentences are written mainly of Salsette, and no Virar-side family is named in them. [n34; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 205–206] Sahapedia’s synthesis adds that the Samvedi Christians were once forest contractors and “the only Bassein group to receive Jesuit education”, both uncited. [n34; Sahapedia]

Schooling on the Virar side in 1882 was small: Agashi’s church school taught seventeen pupils Portuguese reading, arithmetic, singing, doctrine and music, and its master played the violin in church; the Koprad church had no parish school. [n40; n46; Gaz XIV pp. 2, 210]

Customs and festivals

There is no period ethnography of the Kuparis by name. The Gazetteer’s 1882 account of the Thana Christians, read beside its account of the Hindu Samvedis, gives the baseline, and a few of its sentences are about Bassein in particular. “In Salsette the parents of the girl propose; in Bassein the proposal comes from the boy’s side.” Children were baptised between the eighth and fifteenth day, carried by a godfather and godmother, padrinho and madrinha, and “the mother never goes to the christening”; on the third or sixth night the household guarded against the spirit Sathi by strewing gram at the door, the same spirit, Sati, that the Hindu Samvedis worshipped on the fifth day after a birth, which the notes read as a sign of customs shared across the line of faith. The groom of the lower classes was married in “the full European dress of bygone days, a scarlet or black military coat with cocked hat, epaulets, knee-breeches”, hired for five to ten rupees, while the bride went in a palanquin wearing the Hindu robe; the feast had two pork dishes with vinegar and spices. The dead were buried within a day, brotherhoods attending with candles, with prayers and a meal on the seventh day. The first fruits of the rice harvest were blessed in church. [n34; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 82, 207–215] The priests disapproved of secret offerings to Ganpati and Gauri and vows to Shitladevi in outlying Bassein villages, which were “said occasionally” to continue. [n34; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 207–210]

In dress, cart-drivers and husbandmen wore a long cylindrical white cotton hat or a woollen night-cap, and fishermen red broadcloth caps; the women wore the local Hindu robe, married women drawing its end over the right shoulder, in dark purple, green or black, and covered themselves for church with a white sheet-like robe. [n34; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 204–205] The community’s own description of Kupari dress is a red cap, dhoti, white shirt, black sleeveless jacket and an earring in the left ear for men, and a red blouse with a red checked kashta sari for women. The Hindu Samvedi men of 1882 also wore “red broadcloth caps like Telugu Brahmans”, so the notes read the red cap as a shared regional cap, not a convert’s mark, against a polemical claim that the Portuguese imposed it. A 2026 local article says the red cap and the red lugda have “largely disappeared from everyday life” and remain part of community identity. [n07; n34; Rajan D’Mello blog 2009; Gaz XIII-1 p. 82; All About Vasai Virar, 29 Jun 2026]

The community’s life-cycle words are Portuguese in root: Baltim (baptism), Komsar (confession), Cominyao (first communion), Chrisma (confirmation), Hamdao, Lagin and Varad for the stages of a wedding and Saudio for the celebration after it, Yentar for a funeral and Saatvoh for the seventh-day Mass and meal, Paya bharni for a house’s foundation and Bejimat for its blessing, Pirsao or miravnuk for the candlelit Lenten procession through the villages, and Upas for the Lenten fast. The foods are fish curries, bombil above all, indel, vade, dhapti, hingoli, fogey, dodal, fuddi and sanna. These come from a community blog of 2009, unverified, and nothing in the primary texts contradicts or confirms them. [n07; n34; Rajan D’Mello blog 2009] The “Kupari songs” of Nandakhal, about the bride leaving her father, women fetching fodder, the boy-groom’s first shave and a daughter coming home after the rice planting, “are rarely heard as lifestyles have changed”; the same source calls the Kuparis “more conservative” than the “more fun-loving” Vadvals of the south. [n07; Vasaiker, “Music of Vasai”]

The festivals of the Virar-side Kuparis that the notes name are their parish feasts and their crosses. Agashi’s church feast fell on 16 December in 1760. [n40; da Cunha p. 164] Shirlai’s Our Lady of Lourdes keeps its feast in February. [n49; UCAN directory (search summary)] A Community Cross stands at Cross Naka in Agashi, one of the wayside crosses that stand in nearly every gaothan of Vasai, where villagers gather for an annual cross feast and the rosary. [n09; Vasai Catholics blog 2015; VasaiCity] A lakeside statue of Christ the King has stood at Nandakhal since the 1960s. [n09] In 2026 the grounds of St James, Agashi, held the silver jubilee of the Yuvajallosh youth festival for the “SAJ family” of Shirlai, Agashi and Jyoti, and on 7 June 2026 the diocese made St Joseph’s Church at Umrale a pilgrim centre for the Year of St Francis, with a plenary indulgence. [n38; n49; Catholic Connect]

Language

The Kuparis of the Virar side speak the dialect their Hindu Samvedi neighbours speak, Samvedi, Kadodi or Kupari, with its s-to-h shift, and a community observer notes that Kupari Kadodi differs from Vadval speech. [n22; n07; Mumbai Paused 2011] The 1882 Gazetteer described the home tongue of the Thana Christians as Marathi “very little different from the Marathi spoken by the Kunbis and Kolis”, with a few speaking ungrammatical Portuguese and Latin as the Church language; Grierson in 1905 recorded padron and madron, godfather and godmother. [n34; n49; Gaz XIII-1 p. 204] The Diocese of Vasai lists “Samvedi, Kupari, Wadval” among its languages. [n49] Chapter 36 tells the dialect in full.

Names on record

Very few Kuparis of the Virar side are named in the record before the twentieth century. One is: in 1882 a Devanagari inscribed stone served as a doorstep in Bolinj in the house of a Christian, “Juju Manu Lop, that is Joseph Manuel Lopez”, the Portuguese name worn down by village speech. [n39; n40; Gaz XIV p. 342] The twentieth-century parish history adds others: Rev. Frank Lobo, the first local priest of Nandakhal, ordained on 5 January 1943 “after 370 years”; Fr Francis D’Britto of Jeladi; and the donor of Nandakhal’s 1908 bell, recorded as Dinda Kurel, whose community the source does not give. [n40; n46; Wikipedia: Holy Spirit Church Nandakhal] One of Nandakhal’s hamlet groups in 2016 was Rumao Ali, 369 people; Rumao is a Vasai Christian surname spelling and ali is Marathi for a lane, so the hamlet may be named for a family, as Gomes Ali near Nirmal seems to be; that reading is an inference. [n39; n40] No source places families of the author’s own surname, Tuscano, in any Virar-side village, though a licenciado André Toscano held the aldeia “Sircar” in the Tombo of 1727. [n44; Tombo fl. 136v] Chapter 38 lists every name that reached the record.

The twentieth century: schools, parishes and priests

The parish history of Nandakhal gives the clearest twentieth-century picture of a Kupari community on the Virar side. In 1907 the Collector granted forest timber free; in 1908 a big bell was given; in 1922 Mgr Francesco de Silva cleared forest, built a compound and planted coconuts, and in 1923 opened an English school that soon closed for lack of pupils, with a remark the parish remembers: “we get bread and butter from farming and not by studying”. Marathi schools followed from 1935, and in 1939 Fr Raymond Mendes founded St Joseph’s Marathi school. The first local priest was ordained in 1943, the Queen of the Apostles sisters came in 1952, and by 2016 the parish counted that it had sent sixty-eight priests “and many nuns” into the Church. [n40; n46; Wikipedia: Holy Spirit Church Nandakhal] Agashi opened its English primary school, Holy Martyrs, in 1938, the St James Marathi School in 1968 and St James High School in 1977. [n40; EI Agashi]

The congregation’s growth shows in the parishes cut from the two old churches: Umrale and Nanbhat, where Mass was said from 1949, became parishes in 1970 and on 1 June 1975; Bolinj became a centre in 1988 and a parish in 2001; Jeladi a prayer centre in 1990; Rajodi’s church opened in 1999; Virar East and Virar West became centres; and from Agashi came Jyoti and Shirlai, the latter listed by 1987. [n40; n49] Mother Teresa visited Nandakhal on 6 April 1986, two months after Pope John Paul II’s visit to Vasai on 9 February 1986. [n40]

By community accounts, the Kuparis depended mostly on farming until the late 1960s and early 1970s, and then moved into engineering, medicine, banking and information technology, and abroad to the United States, Europe, the Gulf and Australia. [n07; n34; Rajan D’Mello blog 2009; Sachin Mendes blog 2007] The notes treat this as community testimony; no figure for emigration from any Virar-side village was found. In Bombay’s terms the Kuparis became, after 1887, one of the groups under the “East Indian” name, “Samavedi Christians commonly called Kuparis” in Elsie Baptista’s classification; a Kupari family today obtains a state backward-class certificate as an East Indian, a status the community has sought on the central list too. [n34; Mendiratta 2017 pp. 234–236; east-indians.com; FPJ Oct 2026]

Kupari life today

The Kuparis of the Virar side have been, since 22 May 1998, Catholics of the Diocese of Vasai, which counted 117,026 Catholics in forty-nine parishes and four missions in 2022. [n49; GCatholic] Their most famous modern figure came from Jeladi in Vatar: Fr Francis D’Britto (1943–2024), editor of the diocesan Marathi monthly Suvarta, translator of the Subodh Bible, which won the Sahitya Akademi Award in 2014, the first Christian elected president of the Akhil Bharatiya Marathi Sahitya Sammelan (Osmanabad, January 2020), and leader of the Harit Vasai movement against the building boom that was swallowing the garden villages; about 3,000 people came to his funeral at Nandakhal in July 2024. [n07; n38; n40; FPJ; Sabrang] The movement’s advocates claimed that “the development plan was changed and villages were excluded from the urbanisation plans”; in 2011 the state moved to exclude twenty-nine villages from the new corporation after protests by the Vasai Janandolan Samiti, an exclusion still in litigation in 2025. [n38; n40]

A 2019 opinion piece described the villages of Vasai as “highly segregated socially and communally”, with affluent Catholics among the groups that dominate them; it is a polemical piece and is cited only as one voice. [n07; n41; Swarajya 2019] What the notes record directly is change: the Kupari songs rarely sung, the red cap and lugda worn for occasions, old houses in Agashi lost to towers, and at Bolinj the garden belt meeting the high-rises of Virar West. [n07; n34; n40] What they do not record is as important: no Kupari-specific organisation with a verifiable founding date, no study of a Kupari village of the Virar side, and no oral history of the plantain driers, whose craft ended at a date no source gives. [n34; n48] Those are questions for the families of Agashi, Nandakhal and Bolinj themselves, and Chapter 37 and Chapter 38 end with the same invitation.

V. The Pachkalshis and the Vadvals

One community, many names

The second great gardening community of the Virar side goes by so many names that the names themselves are a history. The 1882 Gazetteer counted 15,367 Pachkalshis in the district, “in strength along the coast”, and said they were also called Sutars, Malis, Vadvals, Chaukalshis, Somvanshi Kshatris and Pathares; all of these, the Chaukalshis excepted, ate together and intermarried. [n49; n08; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 129–130] Enthoven, in 1922, used the spelling Panchkalshi and treated the Vadvals as an endogamous division of the community, found particularly in the Bassein, Mahim and Dahanu talukas, “formerly known as the Feringana”, the land of the Franks, that is of the Portuguese. [n49; n35; Enthoven III p. 160] On the Virar side the community is known today mostly as Vadval or Wadval, and in its own formal usage as Somvanshi Kshatriya Pathare; Wikipedia’s Virar page adds “Panmali”, a name tied to the betel (pan) gardens. [n49; n41; Wikipedia: Virar]

The names explain themselves, or are said to. Pachkalshi or Panchkalshi comes, by the community’s account, from the five earthen pots (kalash) used in their marriage rite, and Chaukalshi from four. [n49; n08; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 129–130] Vadval, in Enthoven’s gloss, “literally means the managers of wadis or orchards, viz., of cocoa-nut and palm groves”. [n49; Enthoven III p. 160] That is an occupational name, and the notes stress that Vadval was both a work label and the name of a distinct endogamous division. [n35]

Where they came from: three stories and three dates

The Pachkalshis’ own tradition, recorded by both the Gazetteer and Enthoven, is that they claim descent from the sun god Surya-Narayan and came to these coasts “with Raja Bimba or Bhima, about the end of the thirteenth century, from Paithan on the Godavari south of Devgiri. Hence their Kshatriya origin.” [n35; n49; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 129–130; Enthoven III p. 161] Enthoven also recorded an alternative: that “King Bimba, though descended from the royal family of Devgiri, had a principality at Anhilvada Patan in Gujarat proper, whence he migrated on the overthrow of the Hindu principalities by the Muhammadans… during the regime of the Khiljis”, and made Mahim, Mahikavati, his capital. [n35; Enthoven III pp. 161–162]

The 1882 Gazetteer’s own editors argued against the Paithan story and for Gujarat. They noted that there is no record of Paithan being sacked by Muslims before 1318; that Prabhu and Palshe records agree that the first settlement was on the coast, at Kelve-Mahim, Bassein and Salsette; that the Prabhus use Gujarati words for household things; and that a grant to a Palshe priest says he came from Pattan and that Bimb was of the Anhilvada family. Ibn al-Athir, they pointed out, names a Bimb, nephew of the Anhilvada king, at Somnath in 1025. [n35; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 61–62] Grierson in 1905 wrote that the Vadvals were “supposed to have immigrated from Gujarat”, and his description of their dialect, with Gujarati words and sound changes, points the same way. [n35; n22; LSI vol. VII]

The dates do not agree. The Mahikavatichi Bakhar, the Marathi chronicle of the north Konkan whose oldest part is attributed to Keshavacharya about 1448, places Pratap Bimb’s arrival around 1138; the community tradition says 1297; Ibn al-Athir’s Bimb is at Somnath in 1025. [n35; Wikipedia: Mahikavatichi Bakhar] The notes advise setting the three side by side and saying plainly that the chronology cannot be reconciled. That is the honest position: the Pachkalshis almost certainly came to this coast from somewhere to the north, very probably by way of Gujarat, at some time in the centuries before the Portuguese, and the record cannot fix when. Chapter 05 and Chapter 40 tell the Bimb story in full.

The split, and the claim to rank

Enthoven’s informants, among them the secretary of the S. K. Hitechhu Mandali, the “Somvanshi Kshatriya well-wishers’ society”, told him that the Vadvals “are said to have separated some time in the fourteenth century”, and that the split “is reputed to be due to the admission of a Koli into the caste”, each side accusing the other. [n35; n41; Enthoven III p. 162] The Vadvals’ right to the Panchkalshi name, he said, rested on “letters from the Shankaracharya in 1836 and 1852 A.D. and a resolution of the Palshikar Brahmans of the Bassein taluka in 1855”. [n35; Enthoven III p. 162] Enthoven’s first volume, in its discussion of caste-precedence claims, also mentions the demand for “Vadvals to be admitted to rank as Panchkalshis, or Somvanshi Kshatriyas”. [n49; Enthoven I, Introduction]

The community’s organisation had a long history before that. In 1779, according to the Bombay City Gazetteer of 1909, “the Mankaris and leading members of the caste met together and passed stringent resolutions on the prevention of costly litigation, the re-marriage of widows, the sale of girls, and temperance”, and in 1884 the community founded the Kshattriya Union Club for education and for relief of the aged, widowed and orphaned. [n41; Bombay City Gazetteer I pp. 238–239] The name “Somvanshi Kshatriya Pathare” itself, the descendants of the moon, of the warrior order, was a claim to rank that Brahmans disputed; the notes call it “a modern claim to the warrior order”. [n35]

Under the Portuguese: flight and conversion

What the Portuguese century did to the Pachkalshis is told in the community’s own words through Enthoven: “Under the Portuguese, who succeeded the Muhammadans, there was much persecution, and this is said to have forced many members of the caste to fly to the outlying parts of the Southern Konkan and join those who were already settled in the Ashtagar. Many were then converted to Roman Catholicism by force and their descendants may still be traced at Bassein and Salsette.” [n41; Enthoven III p. 161] The 1909 Bombay City Gazetteer said much the same of the Bombay-island Panchkalshis, some of whom “were forcibly converted to Christianity and became the ancestors of some native Christian families now resident in Bombay, Bandora and Marole”. [n41; Bombay City Gazetteer I p. 238]

Those are community and colonial-era narratives. The notes found no Portuguese document, no foral or Jesuit letter, that names the Vadvals. [n41] The Gazetteer of 1882, describing the Christians of Bassein as a whole, gave a more measured judgement: “Force may have been used in some cases, but the Portuguese accounts seem to show that persuasion and interest were the chief means of conversion.” [n34; Gaz XIII-1 p. 201 n.2] And it listed Pachkalshis and Chaukalshis among the castes from which Bassein’s Christians came: “the most hardworking and skilful husbandmen are the Christians of Bassein, originally Brahmans, Bhandaris, Chavkalshis, Pachkalshis, Kharpatils, and Kolis, who grow sugarcane and plantains, and have turned the light sandy country about Bassein into an evergreen garden.” [n49; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 301–302] At Agashi, Vatar and Koprad the Christian plantain driers were, as we have seen, “originally Samvedis and Pachkalshis”. [n49; Gaz XIII-1 p. 395] So the Christian community of the Virar-side garden villages was, by the Gazetteer’s account, partly Pachkalshi in origin, and the Samvedi and Vadval Christian groups were not always sharply separate in the Gazetteer’s eyes. [n34]

The siege of Bassein and the Peshwa’s reward

In the war of 1737–1739 the Hindu Pachkalshis took the Maratha side, and were rewarded. Three government texts agree on it. Enthoven: “They rendered material help to the Marathas during the siege of Bassein and the acquisition of adjoining country in 1739–40. In consequence of this help the Naik family of Anjore, among others, obtained the title of Sarpatil and received inam lands from the Peshwa’s Court. To the siege of Bassein and other events of the great Maratha-Portuguese struggle are traced back the patelships of many families of the Vadval division.” [n41; Enthoven III p. 161] The Bombay City Gazetteer named them: “two Panchkalshis, named Bugaji and Gangaji Naik, were granted the village of Anjur in inam with the title of Naik Sarpatel by the Peshwa.” [n41; Bombay City Gazetteer I p. 238] The 1882 Thana Gazetteer, quoting a civil servant, W. B. Mulock, said the Peshwa made “one family chief Patils of Salsette, another family proprietors of Angaon in Bhiwandi, and a third family proprietors of Anjar in Bhiwandi”, but printed the year of the siege as 1743, which is almost certainly a slip for 1739. [n41; n49; Gaz XIII-1 p. 130 n.1]

Anjur is in Bhiwandi taluka, not on the Virar side. [n40] But a Marathi study of 1935, Gajanan Govind Naik’s edition of the Sashtichi Bakhar, lists the helpers in the Vasai campaign by caste, and among them names Somvanshi Kshatriya Chaudharis of Umele (Kuker alias Kuk Chaudhari, Lakham Chaudhari) and Vasai men of the Raut family: Dhaga, Gan, Laxman and Mayaji Raut. [n41; Naik 1935, pp. 15, 320 ff.] Raut and Chaudhari are two of the three Pachkalshi surnames the 1882 Gazetteer names. The landed, patil-holding status of Hindu Vadval families on this coast traces, in their own history and in these texts, to that war.

The Hindu Pachkalshi belt of Virar

The 1882 Gazetteer gave the Pachkalshi surnames as “Raut, Vartak, and Chodhri”. [n49; Gaz XIII-1 p. 130] It found fifteen Pachkalshi families among the plantain driers of Agashi, Vatar and Koprad. [n49; Gaz XIII-1 p. 395] Virar town has a ward called Vartakwada, with its own talav listed in the 2008 heritage survey by the Collective Research Initiatives Trust, alongside Naringi talav, Dongarpada, Totale talav, Manvelpada talav and Ranele talav. [n40; CRIT Vol I] Vartakwada, “the Vartaks’ ward”, and Naringi are candidates for old Hindu Pachkalshi wards inside the Virar gaothan; no source confirms the community of either. [n40] A Jain derasar listed at Doghar Pada, Vartak Ward, Virar West, shows that the ward name has entered the city’s map. [n49; jainmandir.org listing]

Wikipedia’s article on the Vartak surname places Vartaks of the Somvanshi Kshatriya Pathare community in Vasai, Virar, Agashi, Saphale, Kelve, Palghar, Mahim, Boisar, Tarapur, Chinchani, Vangaon, Bordi, Gholvad and Dahanu. [n08; Wikipedia: Vartak] The Vartak name is prominent in the modern history of the whole taluka: Hari Govindrao “Bhausaheb” Vartak (1914–1998), Congress MLA for Bassein from 1962, minister under Vasantrao Naik, Padma Shri in 1991, and founder of the Vidyavardhini institutions (founded 1970); and Tarabai Vartak (1926–2008), born at Bordi, president of the Thane Zilla Parishad in 1972 and later a state minister, founder of schools and colleges in Vasai-Virar. Neither person’s own Wikipedia page states their community, though Wikipedia’s Pachkalshi page lists Bhausaheb Vartak among the community’s notables. [n41; n08] The Vartak surname is also shared by a Chitpavan Brahman clan from Kelshi, so the name alone does not settle anyone’s community. [n41] Wikipedia’s Pachkalshi page also lists Hitendra Thakur of Virar, the founder of the Bahujan Vikas Aghadi, as a community member; his own page does not say so, and the notes decline to confirm it. [n41]

Work

The Gazetteer’s Pachkalshis earned their living “as husbandmen, gardeners and carpenters, and a few as writers and day labourers… The wives of husbandmen and gardeners help their husbands by selling vegetables, butter, and milk.” Their proverb was “Pachkalshi áni kon mhanel álshi?”, “Who can call a Pachkalshi idle?” [n41; n49; Gaz XIII-1 p. 130] Betel-nut growers in the district were “partly Christians, Malis converted by the Portuguese, and partly Hindus of the Chavkalshi or Pachkalshi class”, and sugar in Bassein was “chiefly made by Pachkalshis, Malis, Native Christians and Samvedi Brahmans”. [n41; Gaz XIII-1 Ch. IV] Enthoven added that “under the Portuguese many of them became skilled mechanics and carpenters (house and boat builders)”, that the majority of the Vadvals were still farmers, and that they worked other members’ land on the khand system, a fixed percentage of the produce, supplying their own cattle. [n35; n41; Enthoven III pp. 163, 166]

The best-documented Vadval trade of the nineteenth century belongs to the Christian Vadvals of the south of the taluka, but it touches the edge of Virar. “The traders, Christian cartmen chiefly of Chulna, Manikpur, Gokhivra, Sandor, and Barhanpur in Bassein, are known as Vadvals or gardeners. They purchase fallen timber… The Vadvals are the best axemen in the district, and their carts are larger and their cattle stronger than those found in other sub-divisions.” They travelled in gangs of five to thirty carts, by night. [n41; n49; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 97–98] Gokhivare, one of those cart villages, lies in Vasai East near the Virar boundary; its village story is told in the tour below and in Chapter 38.

Speech, faith and custom

The Pachkalshis of 1882 spoke “incorrect Marathi”, using l for ḷ and n for ṇ. [n08; Gaz XIII-1 p. 130] Grierson’s Vadval dialect turned s and sh into a sound “probably like the sound of ch in loch”, so that sona, gold, became hona; gh became s or h; and some words were plainly Gujarati. His Specimen No. 36, the parable of the prodigal son in Vadval with an interlinear translation (Eka don por hote, “One man-to two sons were”), is the earliest recorded connected text in the dialect. [n22; LSI vol. VII] Today the dialect is called Vadvali or Phudagi (ISO 639-3 phd); Ethnologue’s figure of about 2,500 speakers comes from a count of 2000, and the dialect is described as endangered as young people stop using it. [n08; Wikipedia: Phudagi language] The Vasai Kolis’ speech is said to resemble Vadvali closely, “with a heavier accent”. [n08]

Their gods, in 1882, sat side by side in the house: “images of Cheda and other demon-gods… along with brass and stone images of Ganpati, Shiv, and Krishna.” [n41; Gaz XIII-1 p. 130] Cheda, a local spirit, was shared with the Agris, the Bhandaris and the Son Kolis, a cross-caste deity of the whole coast. [n08] Enthoven, describing the Panchkalshis as a whole, called them “both Shaivas and Vaishnavas”, named their household goddesses as Mahalakshmi, Ekvira, Vajreshwari and Jogeshwari, and said they “believe in astrology and witchcraft, but cannot be described as demon-worshippers”, without mentioning Cheda at all. [n41; Enthoven III pp. 164–165] The poorer Vadvals, the Bombay City Gazetteer said, paid special veneration to Ekvira, to Mahalakshmi of Kolvan near Dahanu, and to Vajreshwari of Bhiwandi, kept the new moon of Chaitra as an important holiday, and kept a tulsi plant before the house. [n41; Bombay City Gazetteer I p. 240] Their priests were Palshe Brahmans. Some always wore the sacred thread and forbade widow marriage; the Chaukalshis wore it only at marriage and allowed widows to remarry. [n49; Gaz XIII-1 p. 130]

Enthoven recorded the wedding rites in detail. Among the Vadvals, unlike other Panchkalshis, the groom’s side approached the bride’s and asked for her horoscope; there was no compulsory cash dowry; the bride’s family gave a nose-ring, an ear-ring, five copper pots and a ring or two for the groom; and during the antarpat rite a sword or dagger was held aloft, piercing the canopy over the couple, which Enthoven called “a survival… of the old Kshatriya custom”. Widow remarriage by pat, at night under a mango tree, was permitted among the Vadvals. Their gotras, Bharadwaj, Kashyap, Vasishtha and others, were “not descent from a common ancestor but discipleship to a common guru”. [n49; n41; Enthoven III pp. 162–165]

In 1882 the men near Bombay dressed like Brahmans, in Bombay like Prabhus, and in outlying parts like Marathas, in loincloth, coarse blanket and cap; on festive days in silk-bordered waistcloths, turbans and Gujarati shoes; the women in the full Maratha robe. Their houses were one or two storeys, with mud or brick walls, thatched or tiled. Their food was rice, rice bread, pulse, vegetables and fish, with mutton, chicken and liquor on holidays. [n41; Gaz XIII-1 p. 130] The 1909 Gazetteer described the Vadvals of Bombay island in a short waistcloth and a bara bandi waistcoat fastened with twelve ties under the arms, with the Koli cap or a large red turban, the women’s cloth reaching to the knee and tucked up behind like a Koli woman’s, a silver belt (kargota), and yellow saris at weddings. [n41; Bombay City Gazetteer I pp. 240–241] The red head-cloth, which the Christian Vadvals and the Kuparis also wore, was in this record a general trait of the coast before conversion-era identities fixed it to one community or another. [n41]

The Vadvals today

There is no reliable count. The census stopped recording caste after 1931; the last figures in the notes are the 1872 Pachkalshi total and a community census of 1901 that returned 7,916 Vadvals, “about 8,000” at Enthoven’s time of writing. [n41; Enthoven III p. 160] A Joshua Project profile of unknown method gives 47,000 Vadvals and 528,000 “Somvanshi”; the notes judge it unreliable. [n41] The community is organised: the Somvanshi Kshatriya Samaj Sangh holds an annual sports festival, and a Vadval Samaj website offers directories, matrimony and blood-donor services. [n41; vadvalsamaj.com] Hindu Vadvals on the Virar side, by the notes’ reading, are a landed intermediate caste whose patilships go back to 1739; Christian Vadvals are part of the East Indian Catholic community of the south of the taluka. [n41]

VI. The priests: Palshe, Chitpavan and Deshastha Brahmans

The Palshes, the old priesthood of the coast

Before the Marathas came, the priests of this coast were the Palshe Brahmans. They called themselves Vajasaneyi Brahmans, followers of the White Yajurveda of the Madhyandin branch, tracing their line to the sage Yajnavalkya. Their name, the Gazetteer said, probably came from Palsavli, a village in Kalyan which, according to the Bimbakhyan, King Bimb gave to his family priest. [n49; n08; Gaz XIII-1 p. 81] They were “generally believed to have come in 1297 from Mungi Paithan… with Bimb”, but the Gazetteer thought they more likely came from Gujarat, with the Prabhus and Pachkalshis, as part of “the large settlement which from Gujarat supplied the foreign element to the Thana district”; Enthoven repeated both the tradition and the doubt. [n08; n35; Gaz XIII-1 p. 81; Enthoven I pp. 248–249]

The 1882 text says the Palshes were “returned as numbering 9,311 souls”, found in Panvel, Bassein, Mahim, Dahanu, Bhiwandi, Salsette and Kalyan; the district Brahman table on page 74 of the same volume prints 2,311 (1,238 men and 1,073 women, by the OCR). One of the two figures is a typesetting or OCR error, and the notes cannot say which. [n49; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 74, 81] Enthoven’s 1901 figure of 478 Palshikars was a narrow census count. [n35]

Their surnames were Kavle, Joshi, Phatak, Pandit, Chhatre, Mogre, Kirtane, Purandare, Devdhar, Paranjpe, Upadhye, Kshirsagar, Javaje, Parashare, Trivedi and Shesne. Their speech used mad for madhe and kai for kothe. They worked as priests, physicians and astrologers. [n08; Gaz XIII-1 p. 81] And they held a privilege that made them indispensable to every other community on this chapter’s list: “they have the privilege of fixing the time for marriage and thread ceremonies for all classes in Salsette, Bassein, Mahim and Dahanu.” [n49; Gaz XIII-1 p. 81]

The record shows them serving almost everyone. They were the priests of the Pathare Prabhus and of the Pachkalshis; they married the Mangelas at sunset; and, as Section VII tells, it was Palshe priests who took the Christian Agris back into Hinduism in 1820 and 1828. [n35; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 81, 117, 147] Their resolution of 1855, supporting the Vadvals’ claim to Panchkalshi rank, shows them also as arbiters of caste status. [n35; Enthoven III p. 162]

1740: the newcomers

The Maratha conquest of 1739–40 brought a new Brahman elite. “Of Maratha power”, the Gazetteer wrote, “the chief relics are priestly Brahmans of the Konkanasth and Deshasth classes”, and after the conquest priestly Chitpavan and Deshasth Brahmans “settled as priests, and to a great extent ousted the Palshis and other older classes of priests”. [n35; Gaz XIII-1 p. 63] It gave an example from just before the war: two Kudale Brahmans migrated to the north Konkan about 1728; one settled at Thana and was made a Sardar; the other, Yeshwantrao, settled in Bassein, and his son became Atrekar (karkun) of Bassein fort. A class of privileged land-holding Brahmans and Prabhus, the Pandharpeshas, were, the Gazetteer said, relics of Maratha rule. [n35; Gaz XIII-1 p. 63 n.2]

The Palshes did not take this quietly, and the record preserves the bitterness. The derisive reading of their name as pal-ashi, “flesh-eater”, was, in the Gazetteer’s judgement, “probably the work of their rivals the Deccan and Chitpávan Bráhmans at whose hands the Palshes have suffered much since the Maratha conquest of western Thana (1740)”. [n08; n49; Gaz XIII-1 p. 81 n.] Chitpavans refused to dine with Palshes, and the Palshes defended their rank with Peshwa-era sanads and, by Enthoven’s time, a recent recognition from the Smarta Shankaracharya. [n35; Enthoven I pp. 248–249] Their stress on Paithan and Yajnavalkya in their own accounts, the notes suggest, is best read against this injury: an old priesthood insisting on its standing against a new one. [n35]

The newcomers had their own origin stories. The Chitpavans told of fourteen shipwrecked foreigners burned on a pyre (chita) by Parashuram and restored to life, “pure from the pyre”; scholars derive the name from Chiplun, and the community began calling itself Konkanasth about 1715, as Peshwa Balaji Vishwanath rose. The Deshasths “appear to be the earliest Brahman settlers who migrated to the south of the Vindhyas”, in Enthoven’s words. [n35; Enthoven I pp. 243–245] In 1872 the district Brahman table counted 21,317 Brahmans in thirty classes, among them 8,722 Chitpavans, 1,006 Deshasths, 899 Devrukhas, 629 Shenvis, 585 Karhadas, 357 Yajurvedi Madhyandins and the Palshes and Samvedis already given. [n49; n08; Gaz XIII-1 p. 74] Those figures are for the whole district; no village figures exist for the Virar side.

The Brahman landscape of Agashi

Where the post-1739 Brahman presence can be seen on the Virar side is in stone and street-names at Agashi. The Maratha subhedar Shankarji Keshav Phadke built the Bhavani Shankar and Hanuman temples there (Part Two discusses their disputed date). A 2019 heritage walk, written up on a blog and to be read as one visitor’s observations, lists the Khanolkar, Joshi, Shakuntala and Phadke wadas (mansions), the Garde family’s private Ram temple built in 1885, a Peshwa-style Vishnu temple, and Chalpeth Road, “laid in the Maratha period”, where “Marathi Hindus and Jain Bania traders lived”. [n49; Heritage Chronicles, 3 Dec 2019] The 2008 heritage survey lists Khanolkar House as a Peshwa guest house and Phadkewada as built by Subhedar Phadke; by 2019 Phadkewada had been “lost to high-rise construction”. [n40; CRIT Vol I] Phadke, Joshi and Garde are surnames of the Deccan and Konkan Brahman families; whether the Agashi families were Chitpavan, Deshastha or Karhada the sources do not say.

For Virar town itself the notes found no village data at all for Chitpavan or Deshastha families. The research concluded that a Chitpavan and Deshastha presence in Virar proper probably came later, with the railway and the offices, but marked that conclusion as an inference with no source. [n49] It is recorded here as such.

VII. The Agris, the Dasagris of Khanivade and the Urap Agris of Virar and Navapur

Three Agris who did not marry each other

The Agris were the salt-makers, toddy-drawers, drummers and rice farmers of the creeks. The 1882 Gazetteer divided them into three groups that neither ate together nor intermarried: Sudagris, Dasagris and Urap Agris. The Sudagris had their own sub-divisions by work: Mithagris, salt-makers; Jasagris, toddy-drawers; Dholagris, drummers. [n49; n08; Gaz XIII-1 p. 116] Their surnames, as the notes read the Gazetteer’s list, included Bhoir, Chaudhari, Chavan, Gharat, Gulvi, Jadhav, Kini, Mali, Mandre, Mhatre, Mukul, Navraye, Naik, Pawar, Shelar, Shelke, Vaze and Yadav, and their family “badges” were trees and fruits: pineapple, hirda, mango, fig. [n08; Gaz XIII-1 p. 116] Their Marathi was “rough”; their women wore the robe tight round the waist and thighs; their staple food was nagli or coarse rice with fish; their favourite gods were “Cheda and other local spirits or devs”; and the Gazetteer, in the manner of its time, called them “excessively fond of liquor”. [n08; Gaz XIII-1 p. 116] In 1901 Enthoven counted 211,176 Agris in the Presidency, 88,733 of them in Thana. [n35; Enthoven I pp. 9–14]

No source dates the Agris’ arrival. The Gazetteer noted that “among some Bhandaris and Agris the remembrance of a hurried flight from the south and some traces of Lingayat customs remain”, and that the upper coastal castes, “and partly the Agris and Bhandaris”, said by their own accounts that they came from one settlement under Bimb. [n35; n08; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 61–63] Both are traditions. The Agris were on this coast when the record begins.

In the husbandry ranking of the 1882 Gazetteer, after the Christians of Bassein and the Kunbis, “next come the coast Agris and Son Kolis, who own salt land… the Agri his salt-making and the Koli his fishing”. [n49; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 301–302] Vasai taluka still has about 2,000 acres of salt pans, by one modern estimate, beside about 5,300 in Mumbai and about 2,000 in Palghar; salt-pan land is under pressure from development and from attempted fraudulent sales. [n08; Outlook; Asian Age] No modern source in the notes describes the Agri salt-workers of the Virar side in particular. [n08]

The Dasagris of Khanivade

The Dasagris lived along the Tansa and Vaitarna rivers. A footnote to the Gazetteer’s Agri account names their villages in Bassein: Kofar, Chandve, Navsai and Khanivde. [n49; n08; Gaz XIII-1 p. 116 n.] Khanivde is Khanivade (Khaniwade), a village of the Vaitarna tidal belt north-east of Virar; Navsai is the Navasai of the 2011 census; Kofar may be one of the Kopar or Kopri names of the taluka, though that identification rests on spelling alone. [n49] The Dasagri family badges included Naddi, river; Marichimbori, crab; and Mordi, peacock. Their origin story, as the Gazetteer told it, was that an Agri’s mistress had vowed herself to the Mhar’s god, carried a cow’s bone, and was excommunicated, and that the Dasagris descended from that breach. [n08; Gaz XIII-1 p. 116 and n.] It is a story of caste boundaries told by the higher division about the lower, and should be read that way.

In 2011 Khanivade had 2,386 people in 541 households, with a literacy rate of 68.32 per cent and a sex ratio of 870. [n49; CensusIndia2011.com] Whether its people still identify as Dasagri is not recorded.

The Urap Agris: the returners

The third group is the one with a dated turning point, and it is the community most directly tied to Virar town in the nineteenth-century record. The Gazetteer’s account, closely followed by Enthoven, says the Urap (or Varap) Agris of Salsette and Bassein were “said to be Christian Agris who reverted to Hinduism some in 1820 and others in 1828”. [n49; Gaz XIII-1 p. 117; Enthoven I, “Urap Agris”]

Their name had three rival explanations: Persian urf, “alias”; “Europe”; or Marathi orapne, “to sear” with a hot iron, after the purification ordeal by which they were taken back. The Gazetteer preferred the third. They were also called Nave, “new”, Marathas. They ranked below the Sud and Das Agris, who neither married nor ate with them, and had separate priests and a separate headman. “The only trace of their former Christianity”, the Gazetteer said, was their surnames: Gomes (Gomas), Soz, Fernan, Frutad, Minez, that is, Gomes, Sousa, Fernandes, Furtado and Menezes. [n49; n08; Gaz XIII-1 p. 117]

The Gazetteer names the priests who readmitted them, and all were Palshe Brahmans. In 1820 Ramchandra Baba Joshi readmitted the Christian Agris of Bassein for a fee the notes read as Rs 1,200 (the OCR prints “1290”, which is uncertain), and was himself temporarily outcasted for it. Bhai Makund Joshi is named with him. On 12 November 1828 Vithal Hari Naik Vaidya, “a Palshe Brahman of Bassein”, admitted a second group. [n49; n08; n35; Gaz XIII-1 p. 117] The outcasting of Ramchandra Baba Joshi is a telling detail: taking Christians back into caste was controversial among the Brahmans themselves, and the priest who did it paid a price.

Where did they live? The footnote listing the Urap villages is read in two ways in the notes, because the OCR of the 1882 text is rough. One reading gives the Bassein Urap villages as Navapur, Umela, Juchandra, Achole, Virar and Kolwad. [n40; P1; Gaz XIII-1 p. 117 n.1] A fuller reading of the same footnote gives: “Umelman (Umela), Manikpur, Agashi, Mukam, Padrichiwadi, Jot, Virar, Kolowda (Kolhavade?), Navapur, Achole and Juchandra”, alongside the Salsette villages of Balkham, Ralodi, Turbhe, Kavesar, Vadavali, Uthalsar, Mulund, Kopri, Pavai, Kalva, Sanghar, Ovale, Gavhan and Bhandup. [n49; Gaz XIII-1 p. 117 n.1] Both readings include Virar and Navapur. The fuller one adds Agashi and Mukam, the Mukkam of the 2011 census near Arnala, which would put Urap families in four Virar-side villages. The notes ask for the print edition to be checked before either list is relied on. [n49]

Why they returned

The Gazetteer’s chapter on the Christians gives the cause, part evidence and part tradition. In the great cholera of 1818–1820, and again in 1828, “some of the Christian Kolis… took to propitiating the goddess of cholera, and either left or were driven from the Church”. They reverted “with the help of a Palshi Brahman”. Others who were expelled did not become Hindu, but “still attend their parish churches at festivals”. [n49; Gaz XIII-1 p. 208 and n.3] Vaupell, an earlier British writer quoted in the footnote, blamed the 1828 reversion on “an attempt to extend the priestly prerogative”, which suggests a quarrel between the converts and their priests. And the Gazetteer quotes Gerson da Cunha for a third element: the Maratha rulers had levied a tax to support Brahmans who purified Christians before readmitting them. [n49; Gaz XIII-1 p. 208 n.3; da Cunha, Bassein, p. 149] The Urap Agris are not the Christian Kolis of that passage, but the same footnote and the same years govern both, and the notes read the Agri returns as part of the same movement. [n49]

The returns of 1820 and 1828 were not the first. According to “the local story” the Gazetteer recorded, Shivaji in 1665–70 “is said to have taken much care that the people should be purified by Brahmans and brought back to Hinduism”, and after 1737–43 “numbers of the Christians were, according to the local story, purified by Brahmans and admitted into Hinduism”. [n49; Gaz XIII-1, Christians chapter] The Urap families also kept a darker memory of how their ancestors had become Christian in the first place: “According to the story of the Uraps and other classes their forefathers were forced to become Christians by having pieces of flesh thrown into their wells.” [n34; Gaz XIII-1 p. 201 n.2] That is a family tradition, attested from the 1850s, recorded alongside the editor’s own judgement that persuasion and interest were the chief means of conversion; Chapter 34 weighs the two.

Putting it together, the notes conclude that the reconversions were “a response to epidemic and church-caste friction, not a single event”: a fear of the cholera goddess in the worst epidemic years, a quarrel over priestly authority, a Hindu priesthood willing (for a fee, and at some risk) to receive the returners, and behind it all the end of the Portuguese state that had rewarded conversion. [n49] Why the Kuparis of the neighbouring garden villages did not return, while the Uraps of Virar and Navapur did, is one of the open questions of this book (Chapter 27).

The Agris today

After 1882 the Urap families of Virar leave no trace in the notes: no surname list, no ward, no temple. [P1; n49] The 1882 village list supports the idea that the Hindu Agri population of old Virar village included Urap families, and it is often said that their surnames may still mirror Portuguese-era Christian names; but no source in the notes shows such a family in Virar today, and it is not a documented present-day fact. [n49] Wikipedia’s Virar page lists the “Aagri” among the town’s local communities. [n49; Wikipedia: Virar] Agri surnames such as Mhatre, Naik, Patil and Bhoir are common across Virar, and so are the same surnames among other communities, which is why surname is no guide. [n49]

VIII. The tappers and the returners: Bhandaris, Thalkars and Phudgis

The Bhandaris

Before the railway brought paraffin and the shop, the palm was this coast’s second harvest, and the Bhandaris were its harvesters. Their name, the Gazetteer said, came from Sanskrit mandhārak, distiller. There were 13,088 in the district, found “over the whole district except Murbad and Bhiwandi”. [n08; n49; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 149–150] They said they came with Bimb; others claimed the Portuguese had brought them from Goa, which the Gazetteer thought unlikely. [n08; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 149–150] Both are traditions; the “remembrance of a hurried flight from the south and some traces of Lingayat customs” that the Gazetteer noticed among some Bhandaris and Agris hints at a southern origin for at least part of the community, without a date. [n35; Gaz XIII-1 p. 63]

They had four divisions. The Kirtes tapped the coconut palm and ranked highest; the Gavads tapped the brab palm; then came the Sindes; and lowest, the Kirpals, who “were once Christians, and perhaps get their name from kriyapal, meaning allowed to make use of Hindu rites”. [n49; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 149–150] Their surnames included Surve, Jadhav and Kadam. [n08]

The Gazetteer’s picture of a Bhandari at work is one of the best images in the volume. He was “often seen with a hollow gourd full of palm-juice on [his] head” and carried “on [his] left thigh a heavy broad-bladed tapping knife”; and “they sing while they tap the trees”. At work they wore a scarlet waistcoat and skull-cap; their women decked their hair with flowers. They worshipped Cheda with offerings of goats and fowl, fasted on the fourth of Bhadrapad, Ganesh Chaturthi, and had a headman called the mukadam. [n08; Gaz XIII-1 p. 150] The palm spirit of the coast, ful, was about three to five per cent alcohol, and the Christians drank it too. [n49; Gaz XIII-1 p. 204] Virar’s own potters supplied the trade: the Gazetteer records Virar-made earthen pots, kondya, sold in the palm-juice business. [n49; Gaz XIII-1 pp. ~319, 329–332]

The trade was already declining in 1882. “Since (1877) the recent rise in the palm tree cess, many have become husbandmen and labourers.” [n08; Gaz XIII-1 p. 150] A tax on trees, levied by a government that wanted revenue from liquor, pushed the tappers off the palms and into the fields.

The Bhandaris stand on both sides of this coast’s religious line. Among the Christians of Bassein the Gazetteer listed Bhandaris among the castes of origin, and judged that “the bulk of them are Bhandaris, Kolis and Kunbis”. [n49; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 200–201] The Kirpals were Bhandaris who had gone back: after 1737–43, “numbers of the Christians were, according to the local story, purified by Brahmans and admitted into Hinduism”, and the Gazetteer thought these “probably” included the Bhandaris known as Kirpals. [n49; n35; Gaz XIII-1 p. 203] At Sopara in 1882 the Hindus were “chiefly Samvedi Brahmans and Bhandaris”. [n34; Gaz XIV p. 315] Jivdani is called the kuldevi of the Bhandaris, as of the Kolis and Mangelas. [n08] A Wikipedia talk-page comment calls Holi, a locality of the taluka, a Bhandari residential area, which is too weak a source to rely on. [n40] Wikipedia’s Virar page lists the “Bhandhari” among Virar’s local communities. [n49] No source in the notes describes present-day toddy tapping on the Virar side. [n08]

The Thalkars

A second group of returners came from the Christian Kolis. The Gazetteer called them Thalkars (Thankars): “found in small numbers in Bassein, Thana and Bhiwandi… descendants of Christian Kolis, who in the great cholera year (1820–21) sought the protection of Devi, Khandoba and Vithoba and left the Catholic Church”. They had “taken to wear the top-knot”, the visible sign of a Hindu man. [n49; Gaz XIII-1, Christians chapter, about pp. 205–208] The same Gazetteer, in its fisheries passage, also lists “Thalkars in Salsette” among the professional fishermen of the district; whether these are the same people or a different use of the name is not clear from the notes. [n49; Gaz XIII-1 p. 55]

The Thalkars and the Urap Agris are the same story in two castes: in the years when cholera killed most, Christian families of the coast turned to the goddesses and gods who were believed to govern epidemics, and some of them found their way, or were pushed, out of the Church and back into a Hindu caste. No source names a Thalkar village on the Virar side.

The Phudgis

The Phudgis are the most elusive community on this list. Enthoven described them as wanderers, descended by their own account from Hanuman, who had turned cowherds, found “only in the Mahim and Bassein talukas”, 1,035 in 1901. Among them the Kirpal Phudgis were “descendants of those Phudgis who were forced by the Portuguese to embrace Christianity, and who on the conquest of Mahim and Bassein by the Marathas reverted to Hinduism”. [n49; Enthoven III, Phudgi] Grierson, in 1905, recorded Phudgi as the dialect of “a wandering tribe in Thana”, with about 1,000 speakers, “almost identical with Vadval”, though he could not say whether they were the same. [n22; LSI vol. VII] Today the name Phudagi is used for the Vadvali dialect as a whole. [n08; Wikipedia: Phudagi language] Where the Phudgis of 1901 lived on the Virar side, and what became of them as a separate people, no source says.

Three layers

Taken together, these returners give the Hindu population of north Vasai in 1882 a structure that the notes describe in three layers. There were the never-converted Hindus: Samvedi Brahmans, Pachkalshis, Sudagris and Dasagris. There were the Christians-turned-Hindus: Urap Agris, Urap Kolis, Thalkars, Kirpal Bhandaris and Kirpal Phudgis. And there were the Christians: Kuparis, Christian Vadvals and Christian Kolis. [n49] Each returning group came back by a different road and at a different time: the Kirpal Bhandaris and Phudgis by tradition after the Maratha conquest of 1739, the Urap Agris in 1820 and 1828, the Thalkars in 1820–21. A village like Virar, which held Urap Agris in 1882, or Agashi, which held Hindus, Christians and (by one reading) Urap families, contained all three layers within walking distance of the market.

A smaller farming group, the Baris, also appears on the same Gazetteer page: 2,458 people “found in Bassein, Mahim and Dahanu”, said to have come from Gujarat, some of whose women kept Gujarati dress; they were husbandmen, cartmen and labourers. [n49; Gaz XIII-1 p. 117] No Virar village is named for them.

IX. The Kunbis, the Mhars, and the ladder of the land

The Kunbis were the largest Hindu group in the Thana district, 183,144 in 1872, and “form the bulk of the agricultural class”, with embanked rice fields. [n08; n49; Gaz XIII-1 pp. ~127, 301–302] But on the Virar coast they were a small presence. The Talheri Kunbis numbered only 1,050 in Bassein in 1872, against 33,000 in Shahapur; they were a central and eastern people of the district, not a coast group. [n35; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 124–125] The Gazetteer described them as made of “two main elements, a local apparently little different from the Son Koli, and a foreign”, the foreign element belonging to two periods, before and after the Muslims and Portuguese; early surnames such as More, Salunkhe, Jadhav, Yadav, Povar, Chohan and Shelar, and later Maratha ones such as Bhosle, Kadam, Shirke and Samble. The Maratha Kunbis, it said, “represent the conquerors, who, during the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, passing north from Satara and Ratnagiri, settled across the whole south of the district”. [n35; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 124–125]

The Kunbis also stand among the Christians. “The bulk” of Bassein’s Christians, in the Gazetteer’s words, were “Bhandaris, Kolis, and Kunbis”. [n49; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 200–201] And the colonial texts show how porous the line between Koli and Kunbi was: Malhar Kolis “in the Thana district frequently become Maratha Kunbis”, and the Talheri caste recruited from the children of Pardeshi and Marwari men and local women. [n35; Enthoven II p. 257; Gaz XIII-1 p. 125]

The Gazetteer’s ranking of the district’s husbandmen is the clearest statement of how all these peoples stood on the land: “The most hardworking and skilful husbandmen are the Christians of Bassein, originally Brahmans, Bhandaris, Chavkalshis, Pachkalshis, Kharpatils, and Kolis, who grow sugarcane and plantains, and have turned the light sandy country about Bassein into an evergreen garden”; the Kunbis “form the bulk of the agricultural class”; “next come the coast Agris and Son Kolis, who own salt land”; and “Brahmans, Musalmans, Vanis and rich Kunbis and Agris almost always have tenants”. [n49; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 301–302] No village record names a Kunbi family on the Virar side.

One more community stands at the edge of every Virar story in the record and is never described in it: the Mhars (Mahars), the village servants and herdsmen. It is the village Mhar of Virar, following an unpaid-for cow, who meets the goddess on Jivdhan hill in the 1882 legend; it is “the touch of a Mhar” from which the goddess flees; it is to the village Mhar that the Samvedis gave food on a leaf plate on the third day after a cremation. [n45; n08; Gaz XIV p. 382; Gaz XIII-1 p. 83] Among the Christians, the Gazetteer recorded, Mhars and Chambhars did not eat with the other sub-divisions, and “until lately the feeling of the impurity of the Mhars remained so strong that in some places they were not allowed to draw water from the village well or to enter the church”. [n49; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 200–201] The modern retelling of the Jivdani legend, in which the Mahar herdsman is granted moksha, softens the 1882 ending in which the goddess vanishes at his touch; that shift, the notes observe, is a documented change in the story between two texts. [n45] In 2011 Scheduled Castes made up 4.17 per cent of the Vasai-Virar corporation and Buddhists 2.65 per cent; no source in the notes links those figures to any village or tells the community’s twentieth-century history on the Virar side. [n49]

X. The Muslims

Old Muslims of the coast

The Muslims of this coast are older than the Portuguese, though the notes hold no document of a Muslim community on the Virar side before the Gujarat sultanate. The Gazetteer’s “Konkani” Muslims were, it said, “the largest and most prosperous class of Thana Musalmans”, who “owe their origin to the Arab and Persian refugees, merchants, and adventurers, who settled along the coast in the eighth, ninth, and tenth centuries”; their home speech was a dialect of Marathi. [n08; n35; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 216–217] Enthoven recorded the community’s tradition that the bulk of them fled from the Euphrates valley about the year 700 to escape the governor Hajjaj bin Yusuf, and that further waves came with the demand for Arab and Persian soldiers under the Bahmani and Bijapur kings in the fourteenth to sixteenth centuries; their speech was “Konkani Marathi with a slightly peculiar pronunciation and a mixture of Urdu, Persian and Arab words”, they followed the Shafi’i school, and they were landholders, traders in rice, timber and salt, and sailors. [n35; Enthoven III, Musalmans] Arabs were settled at Sopara, “Sofala”, in very early times, according to Reinaud’s reading of Abulfeda. [n08; Gaz XIII-1 p. 61 n.]

The Gujarat sultanate left its mark on the Virar side in two places. At Agashi, in 1554, under the Portuguese, “a great mosque” became the commandant’s residence; the mosque must therefore have stood there before the Portuguese came. [n40; Gaz XIV pp. 1–2] And at Arnala, about 1530, the Portuguese found “a strong fort in the hands of the Gujarat Musalmans with domes and Saracenic arches”, which they destroyed. [n45; Gaz XIV p. 10] Both are evidence of Muslim rule and building, not of a resident Muslim population; but rule of that kind came with soldiers, officials and traders.

1872 and 1881

In 1872 the Bassein sub-division had 2,025 Muslims, 1,866 Sunni and 159 Shia, among eleven Muslim communities in the district: Bohoras, Deccanis, Hajams, Julahas, Khojas, Konkanis, Memans, Sipahis, Syeds, Tais and Wajhas. [n08; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 216–217] In 1881 Agashi had 155 Muslims among its 6,823 people, the only Virar-side village with a Muslim count in the record. [n49; Gaz XIV p. 2] At Sopara the Gazetteer found “Musalmans many of them Naitas”, the Navayat trading Muslims of the coast. [n40; Gaz XIV p. 315] The Bohoras, Khojas and Memons were Gujarati trading communities whose presence in Thana came “from Gujarat through Bombay” in the nineteenth century; Momin weavers came from Upper India. [n35; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 64, 216]

The Gazetteer also notes that some Urap Kolis and Kathkaris “seem to be Musalman reverts”, people who had been Muslim and returned, a reminder that conversion and return on this coast ran through Islam as well as through Christianity. [n49; Gaz XIII-1 p. 208 n.4]

The saints of Arnala

The one dated Muslim sacred site on the Virar side is in Arnala fort. In 1882 the fort held “a tomb of two Musalman saints, Shahali and Hajiali, with a yearly endowment of £8 (Rs. 80)”. [n49; n10; Gaz XIV p. 11] The endowment was nearly as large as that of Arnala’s Bhavani temple (Rs 91) and larger than that of its Trimbakeshvar temple (Rs 45), so the British government that inherited the Maratha grants was paying more to the Muslim saints of the fort than to one of its two Hindu temples. A Marathi fort website today describes a dargah inside the walls with two tombs, of Shah Ali and Hajji Ali. [n45; Durg Bharari] Who the saints were, when they lived, and when their urs is held, no source in the notes says. [n10; n49]

Muslims of Virar today

The rest of the Muslim record of the Virar side is thin. Virar East has a dargah of Hazrat Sayyed Ali Mira Datar, which holds an urs; the only source is a user-compiled listing of low reliability. [n10] At Bolinj the 2008 heritage survey lists the Karmale Soneri talav with a small dargah. [n40; CRIT Vol I] No history of a mosque in Virar town was found. [n49] In 2011 Muslims made up 9.03 per cent of the population of the Vasai-Virar corporation, and Urdu was the first language of 2.72 per cent; the notes found no breakdown of where in the city Muslim families live, when they came, or from where. [n49; n08]

XI. Jains, Gujaratis and Marwaris

Sopara’s Jain memory

The Jains of the Virar side inherit a long memory from Sopara. The fourteenth-century Jain teacher Jinaprabhasuri listed Sopara among the eighty-four sacred places of the Jains, and noticed that in his own lifetime Sopara had an image of Rishabhadev, the first Tirthankara; one of the eighty-four Jain gachchhas was called after Sopara; and the legend of King Shripal marrying the daughter of King Mahasena of Soparaka belongs to the same tradition. [n10; Gaz XIV, Sopara entry] Jain images survive at Chakreshvar. [n10] Chapter 05 tells that medieval story. What concerns this chapter is the modern Jain community, and that begins with traders.

The Lad Vanis and the Cambay migration

The best-documented trading community of the coast in the eighteenth century is the Lad Vanis. The Gazetteer counted about 120 Lad Vani families in Thana, “about forty… in Thana, thirty-five in Supara, and the rest in Bassein, Agashi, Nala, Papdi, and Dahanu”. Masudi, writing in 915, said the Lar language was spoken in the coast towns as far south as Chaul, which made it “probable that from very early times Lad Vanis had settled along the Thana coast for purposes of trade”; but “most of the present families are late settlers, who about the middle of the eighteenth century fled from Cambay to escape the tyranny of Momin Khan II”. The Gazetteer dated that movement to 1760–1766, and said several Gujarat Brahmans came with them. They spoke Gujarati among themselves and Marathi with others; they were shopkeepers, moneylenders, superior landholders and merchants; and they were Vallabhacharya Vaishnavs who, because of their “close connection with Maratha Brahmans”, also kept Shaiva fasts and feasts. [n35; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 63, 112–113] Agashi is named among their towns. The notes call these families “the best documentary precedent for the later Jain and Gujarati presence in Nallasopara and Virar”: they came by the Cambay–Bassein sea route, 120 years before the railway. [n35]

The Agashi tirth

The Jain temple at Agashi is the oldest dated Jain building on the Virar side. The 1882 Gazetteer, in its Agashi entry, says: “The Jain temple, which is dedicated to Parasnath, was built about sixty years ago by Motilal a Vani of Bombay.” [n49; n10; Gaz XIV pp. 2–3] Sixty years before 1882 is about the early 1820s. Motilal is the only named Jain of the Virar side in the nineteenth-century record.

Today the temple is the Agashi Maha Tirth Jain Derasar at Chalpeth, and its main deity is Munisuvrat Swami, the twentieth Tirthankara, with Suparshvanath and Neminath. It is managed by the Shri Agashi Jain Derasar and Charitable Trust. [n49; n10; jainmandir.org] Its legends are told in several forms. The idol is said to have risen from a pond at Chakreshwar in Sopara, or from “the lake of Nalasopara”, about two hundred years ago, and to have been carted about nine kilometres to Chalpeth; in one version rival congregations disputed the image until a bullock cart with no bullocks carried it to Chalpeth by itself. The temple’s patron is named as Moti Shah or Motisha Seth, a sea trader who vowed a temple where his lost ships returned, and the ships reappeared at Sopara. Dharamshalas serve pilgrims, and a restoration (jirnoddhar) was carried out in 2019. [n10; jainmandir.org; Indianetzone; Inditales]

The 1882 record and today’s account differ on the deity, Parasnath then, Munisuvrat now. Either the temple was re-dedicated, or the Munisuvrat image was installed later, or there are two temples: a 2019 blog still describes a Parasnath temple “about 400 years old” built by Motilal and rebuilt in marble, with “another large Jain temple” nearby. The “400 years” conflicts with the Gazetteer’s “about sixty years ago” and is unverified. The notes recommend checking on site. [n10; n49; Heritage Chronicles] The Motilal of the Gazetteer and the Moti Shah of the legend may be the same man remembered two ways; no source says so.

The same Agashi heritage walk records Chalpeth Road as the street where “Marathi Hindus and Jain Bania traders lived”, and Agashi’s public library, the Sarvajanik Vachnalaya, was founded in 1914 by Seth Prabhudas Bhagvandas (the heritage survey gives Seth Shah Prabhudas Bhagvandas) and rebuilt in 1939. [n49; n40; Heritage Chronicles; CRIT Vol I] The Seth and Shah in that name mark a Gujarati merchant family; its community and faith are not stated.

In Virar West, a Shwetambar derasar of Vimalnath Swami stands at Doghar Pada, Vartak Ward; only a directory listing was seen, and its founding is unknown. [n49; jainmandir.org listing]

The Marwaris, and a colonial judgement

The Marwari Vanis came later and by land. The Gazetteer said they were “believed to have come to Thana from Rajputana or Marwar, almost entirely since the British conquest”, by way of Gujarat and Bombay, and “since the railway has been opened, they have come in great numbers”. Their first movement followed “the very liberal and general reduction of rent… between 1835 and 1838”, which “left a large margin of profit to the landholder and the Marvadis came, advanced money at from 100 to 200 per cent to the husbandmen, and sold them up”. In 1846 the Collector, Mr Law, noticed that “the thrifty avaricious Marvadi had begun to settle even in the remotest villages”. [n35; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 64, 113–114] Those words are the colonial author’s judgement and are quoted as such; the facts beneath them are a migration of Jain moneylenders following a fall in land rents and, later, the railway. The Gazetteer also recorded Lohanas and Bhatias from Cutch and north Gujarat as grain and cloth merchants in most of the leading towns. [n35; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 113–114]

The whole Thana district counted only 1,823 Jains (Shravaks) in 1872. [n08; Gaz XIII-1 p. ~60] By 2011 Jains were 1.90 per cent of the Vasai-Virar corporation, about 23,000 people by one aggregator’s figure for the wider taluka. [n49; census2011.co.in] Marwari was the first language of 1.64 per cent of the taluka. [n35] Gujarati is a larger presence: Wikipedia’s Virar page, without a source, gives Gujarati as the language of 28.30 per cent of Virar’s people and Hindi 15.31 per cent, with roughly half speaking Marathi; the census figures compiled for the whole corporation give Gujarati 9.26 per cent and Hindi 26.58 per cent. The two cannot both describe the same unit, and the Virar-page figure may refer to a part of the city; the notes treat it as unsourced. [n49; n08; n45] The notes found no source on when particular Gujarati or Marwari families reached Virar town, or on the history of its bazaar. [n49; n08]

XII. The Parsis, and an absence

The plan of this book asks for every community of the coast, the Parsis among them, to be traced through all three places. On the Virar side the record is simply empty. The Parsis were “very numerous” in Salsette until the Portuguese conquest of about 1533, when, by their tradition, they left Thana for Kalyan, returning under British rule about 1774; in 1882 the Gazetteer described “Gujarat Parsi liquor-contractors and Government servants, found all over the district”. [n35; Enthoven III pp. 192–195; Gaz XIII-1 p. 65] Given the palm-liquor trade of the Bhandaris and the Virar-made kondya pots, a Parsi liquor contractor at Virar in the 1880s would not be surprising, but no source names one. No Parsi household, fire temple or tower of silence on the Virar side appears in any source reached. [n35; n08]

XIII. The newcomers: migrants and today’s mix

Before the boom

The Gazetteer of 1882 already listed the “recent settlers” of the district, and the list reads like a preview of modern Virar: Upper India Pardeshis working as messengers and servants, Kachis as market gardeners, halwais from Sindh, Gujarat Brahmans and traders, Marwar Vanis and Lingayat Vanis. [n35; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 64–65] The railway, which reached Virar in 1867 (Chapter 27), carried some of them. But until the late 1980s the Virar side remained, in the words of a planning history, “mainly agricultural”. [n47; TIFR]

In 1981 Virar had 23,303 people and Vasai 34,900, and in the whole region “only Agashe had over 10,000 inhabitants”. [n49; TIFR] Agashi, the old port with its Samvedi, Kupari, Jain and Muslim households, was in 1981 the largest settlement of the Virar side, as it had been in 1881. That is the measure of what came next.

The flood

In 1988 the state government removed the agricultural reservation on the Vasai-Virar region, against the advice of the Mumbai Metropolitan Region Development Authority, and builders moved in; CIDCO took over planning in 1991 (another note dates its appointment to 1990). At that time electricity was available two days a week, there was no piped water and no central drainage, and less than ten per cent of the area was on motorable roads; by 1996 residents could get as little as ten litres of water a person a day. [n49; n47; TIFR] Between 1981 and 1991 the region grew by 150 per cent, and Vasai, Virar, Sopara and Sandor together by 290 per cent, growth attributed to people moving out from Mumbai’s mainland because of property prices and commuting in. [n49; n35; TIFR] By 2001 Virar alone had 118,945 people, about five times its 1981 figure if the boundaries are comparable, which the notes could not verify. [n47; MoHUA appraisal]

The corporation formed in 2009 (or 2010, by a conflicting source) from the Virar, Nallasopara, Vasai and Navghar-Manikpur councils and fifty-three villages had, in 2011, 1,222,390 people: 648,172 men and 574,218 women, a sex ratio of 886 women to 1,000 men, in 291,229 houses. [n49; n47; census2011.co.in] The sex ratio itself tells of migration: young men come first. Wikipedia says, without a source, that about seventy per cent of Virar’s residents are under thirty. [n49; Wikipedia: Virar]

The mix in 2011

The 2011 census, as compiled for the Vasai-Virar City Municipal Corporation, gives the religions of the city: Hindu 77.16 per cent, Muslim 9.03, Christian 8.28, Buddhist 2.65, Jain 1.90, Sikh 0.21, other 0.17, and no religion stated 0.60. [n49; census2011.co.in] For the whole Vasai taluka, a different unit, the figures are Hindu 78.05, Muslim 8.45, Christian 8.25, Buddhist 2.53, Jain 1.75; the notes warn not to mix the two. [n35] The city’s literacy is given as 88.57 per cent by one compilation and 77.91 per cent by another, a conflict the notes could not resolve. [n49]

First languages tell more. For the corporation area, a compilation of the 2011 census gives Marathi 46.49 per cent, Hindi 26.58, Gujarati 9.26, Bhojpuri 3.10 and Urdu 2.72. For the taluka, Marathi 49.97, Hindi 24.78, Gujarati 8.60, Bhojpuri 2.92, Urdu 2.52 and Marwari 1.64. [n08; n35] Hindi and Bhojpuri are the languages of the migrants from Uttar Pradesh and Bihar, Gujarati and Marwari of the trading families. No source in the notes gives the number of migrants from Uttar Pradesh or Bihar in Virar, or from any other state, and no study separates the city’s people by origin; the census migration tables were not opened. [n49; n35] The nearest documented example is in Nallasopara, not Virar: a settlement where the scrap dealers and recyclers all come from one village in eastern Uttar Pradesh. [n08; TISS]

Some migrant communities have left a dated mark on Virar. The clearest is the Syro-Malabar Catholics from Kerala. In 1989 Fr Vargehese Nadackal, with a Bolinj resident named Joseph, visited the Syro-Malabar families of the Virar area; the first Qurbana, the Syro-Malabar Mass, was said on 23 July 1989 at the house of C. K. Paul, for twenty-six families; a hall was inaugurated on 17 March 1991 by Bishop Mar Paul Chittilapilly; the church of St Thomas at Yashwant Nagar, Virar West, was consecrated on 19 January 2013; and it was erected as a parish of the Archeparchy of Kalyan on 13 April 2014. [n49; n47; Archeparchy of Kalyan] This is a migrant Christian layer quite separate from the Kupari parishes, and its first contact came through a man of Bolinj, one of the old garden villages.

The newcomers also include the families who made Virar’s best-known modern names. The cricketer Prithvi Shaw grew up in Virar, where his father took him to the six o’clock Virar local; sources disagree on whether he was born in Thane or Virar. [n47; Sportskeeda; Wikipedia] The politics of the city since 1990 have been dominated by the Thakur family of Virar and the Bahujan Vikas Aghadi; Chapter 27 tells that story.

What a migrant city is made of

The notes do not allow a portrait of Virar’s migrants by origin, caste or trade. What they allow is a shape. On the old ground of the Virar side, which in 1882 held perhaps a dozen communities sorted into villages by caste and faith, there now live well over a million people, a majority of whom, by the language figures, did not grow up speaking the Marathi of the coast, let alone its Samvedi, Vadvali or Koli dialects. The 1882 Gazetteer’s line that Gujarati was superseding Marathi in the extreme north of the district, and Enthoven’s of 1920 that the Son Koli patois was “gradually obsolescent under the grinding of the Local Board school and the newspaper”, describe early pressures on the old dialects; the train has added a far greater one. [n35]

Part Two: The Sacred Places

The sacred places of the Virar side are best dated where the 1882 Gazetteer saw them and the British government paid for them. When the British took over the Maratha territories in 1817–18 they continued many of the yearly grants that the Peshwas had made to temples and shrines, and the Gazetteer recorded the amounts. Those grants are the best single measure of which shrines mattered to the old regime. On the Virar side they were: Arnala’s Bhavani temple, Rs 91 a year; the tomb of the saints Shahali and Hajiali at Arnala, Rs 80; Arnala’s Trimbakeshvar temple, Rs 45; Agashi’s Bhavani Shankar temple, Rs 50; and Agashi’s Hanuman temple, Rs 8. For comparison, the Trivikram temple in Vasai fort received Rs 1,061 and the Nirmal temples about Rs 1,330 with their alms-houses. [n10; Gaz XIV] The Virar side was, in the Maratha scheme, a modest place of worship beside Nirmal and Vasai.

Jivdani

The goddess on Jivdhan hill is told in full in Chapter 27, and only her place among the peoples of Virar is set out here.

In 1882 there was no temple on the hill. The Gazetteer described ruined fortifications, “some very old-looking caves and cisterns said to be the work of the Pandavs”, and a cult: “Throughout the fair season people, especially barren women from the surrounding villages, go to the caves to make offerings to an invisible deity, who is believed to have fled from a niche in one of the caves at the touch of a Mhar. Offerings of betelnuts are thrown into a hole in the niche.” [n49; n10; Gaz XIV, Jivdhan entry] The cave cult is the evidenced older layer; the Pandava origin and the Mhar story are tradition. [n49] The heritage writer of Inditales thought the cave “originally part of a set of Buddhist caves”; no archaeological source confirms it. [n10]

Whose goddess was she? The women who came in 1882 were from “the surrounding villages”, which the Gazetteer does not name. Devotional sites today call Jivdani the kuldevi of the Mangela, Koli and Bhandari communities, and say she is worshipped by Dhiwar, Agri, Mangela, Koli and Bhandari people: the fishing, salt, toddy and farming peoples of the shore and creeks. [n08; apnisanskriti; Durg Bharari] Warli Adivasis live on the hill. [n10] The Pachkalshis’ household goddess Ekvira lends Virar its name in one popular derivation, which the notes treat as tradition, not etymology. [n45; n49] So the goddess of the hill is, in the record, the goddess of almost every Hindu community in this chapter except the Brahmans, whose temples were at Agashi and Nirmal.

The Palghar district’s website says the temple is about 150 years old; the Gazetteer saw none in 1882; the built temple is most likely a later development. [n10; n45] Today it is reached by about 1,375 to 1,500 steps, by different counts, and by a funicular whose environmental clearance was argued before the National Green Tribunal in 2020–21; a glass skywalk costing Rs 84 lakh was announced in April 2026. [n45; n10] The great festivals are Navratri and a Dussehra fair; Chaitra Navratri 2026 ran from 19 to 27 March. [n47; n10] The trust that manages the temple is variously named the Shree Jivdani Devi Mandir Trust or Sansthan. [n49]

Near Jivdani, on the Baronda hill, Marathi Wikipedia lists a Baronda Devi temple among Virar’s shrines; and at Phulpada, at the foot of the hill, the Papdkhind (Papadkhandi) lake was, the district says, “once a major source of fresh water”, with a botanical garden nearby. [n49; Marathi Wikipedia: Virar; Palghar district] No history of the Baronda Devi temple was found.

Agashi’s temples and their grants

Agashi has the richest group of Hindu temples on the Virar side, and the best-documented. The Gazetteer of 1882, verbatim:

“a large temple of Bhavanishankar which was built in A.D. 1691 (Shak 1613) by Shankarji Keshav Phadke, and enjoys a yearly Government grant of £5 (Rs. 50). Close to the temple is a holy bathing place, or tirth, supposed to cure skin diseases. A second temple dedicated to Hanuman was built by the same Shankarji Keshav and enjoys a yearly Government grant of 16s. (Rs. 8). There are two smaller temples, one of Vishnu and the other of Ganpati.” [n49; Gaz XIV pp. 2–3]

The date is the problem. Shankarji Keshav Phadke is documented as the Maratha commander and subhedar of the Agashi–Arnala country after the conquest of 1737–39; the Gazetteer itself credits him with the Nirmal temples about 1750 and with Tungareshwar; and in 1691 Agashi was Portuguese, where no new Hindu temple could be built. A temple website dates the Bhavani Shankar temple to 1739–1750, after Chimaji Appa’s conquest; the 2008 heritage survey labels the Bhavani Shankar and Hanuman temples “18th century, patron Peshwa”. The notes suggest that “1691 (Shak 1613)” is probably a slip for Shak 1673, AD 1751, but this has not been checked against the printed page. [n49; n10; n40; durgbharari.in; CRIT Vol III] A heritage blog repeats 1691 and ties it to the Agashi tank, “built in 1691 by a relation of the ruler of Miraj”; another legend says a Patwardhan of Sangli was cured after a dream told him to build the tank and bathe in it for six months. Both may repeat the same slip. [n49; n10; Heritage Chronicles; Inditales] Whatever the year, the temples are the stone record of the Maratha victory on the Virar side: a Brahman subhedar raising a Shiva temple and a tank of healing water in a town where, until a few years before, only churches could be built.

The 2019 heritage walk adds the modern state of the group: the Hanuman temple beside the talav, rebuilt in stone in 2015 (or 2012–13 by another account), with a four-foot image and Shani at his feet, which Inditales says was originally a Peshwa-period Shiva temple; the Bhavani Shankar temple with a four-faced marble linga and a Nandi, its stone sanctum original; a Sitla Devi shrine; five square samadhi platforms with paduka, the graves of monks; the Garde family’s private Ram temple of 1885, open to the public on the last Thursday of each month and for ten days at Ram Navami; a Peshwa-style Vishnu temple; and Dev Ali, a lane of five temples ending at Bhati Bunder. [n49; n10; Heritage Chronicles; Inditales] The 2008 survey grades the Bhavani Shankar temple as a fair, grade II A heritage structure within the “Agashe Talav Precinct”. [n40; CRIT Vol I]

The Samvedi tradition places Samvedi musicians in the dawn and night worship of the Bhavani Shankar temple. [n08] That is the one link the record (or rather, the community’s account) makes between a particular Virar-side temple and a particular community.

The Jain tirth at Chalpeth

The Agashi Jain temple is described above, in Section XI: built about 1820 by Motilal, a Vani of Bombay, dedicated to Parasnath in the 1882 record and to Munisuvrat Swami today, with the legends of the idol from the Chakreshwar pond and the merchant’s returning ships, and a restoration in 2019. [n49; n10] It is the only Jain shrine on the Virar side with a nineteenth-century record.

Arnala: temples, a dargah and a pond

Arnala’s sacred places are inside and outside the island fort. In 1882, the Gazetteer said, Arnala had “two temples, Trimbakeshvar’s receiving a yearly grant from Government of £4 10s. (Rs. 45), and Bhavani’s £9 2s. (Rs. 91); a tomb of two Musalman saints, Shahali and Hajiali, with a yearly endowment of £8 (Rs. 80) and a stone-lined eight-cornered pond and many wells”. [n49; n45; Gaz XIV pp. 10–11] Today’s sources add a Kalika Mata temple outside the main gate, with a hero stone and a cannon; the paduka of “Nityananda”; a Vetal Dev temple; a recent Datta temple; and the octagonal tank beside the Trimbakeshwar Mahadev temple. The fort’s bastions are named in Marathi sources for Yashwant, Bhavani, Ganesh, Vetal and Hanumant. [n45; n49; Durg Bharari; Marathi Wikipedia: Arnala] The 2008 heritage survey lists a Ram temple of 1806 at Arnala. [n40; CRIT Vol III]

The fort was declared a protected monument on 26 May 1909. [n45; n49] The fort and its history belong to Chapter 32. For this chapter Arnala’s sacred landscape is the clearest picture on the Virar side of the coast’s mixed devotion: a Shiva temple, a goddess temple and a Muslim saints’ tomb within one set of Maratha walls, each paid for by the same government, and outside the walls a church built by Koli fishermen with a gift from a Hindu association. [n49; n40]

Gaskopari’s Ganesh

In the village of Gaskopari in Virar, Marathi Wikipedia records a Ganesh temple whose image is “said to be centuries old”, where a large fair is held on Ganesh Jayanti in the month of Magh. The temple was renovated in a three-day programme in 2015. The same source adds, as tradition, that the installation of household Ganpati images in the village began only thirty or forty years ago. [n49; Marathi Wikipedia: Virar] The age of the image and the fair are not documented beyond this. Gaskopari must not be confused with Gass, the Kupari village south of Nirmal that has its own chapter (K7).

The Magh fair and the late arrival of the household Ganpati are a small but telling contrast. By the village’s own account, the Bhadrapad custom of bringing Ganpati into each home came only in the late twentieth century; its older devotion was the winter fair at a single temple. In 1882 the Bhandaris already fasted on Ganesh Chaturthi, and Agashi already had a small Ganpati temple, so the god himself was not new to the Virar side; what was new, if the tradition is right, was the household form of his festival in this one village. [n49; n08; Gaz XIV p. 2]

Other Hindu shrines and the gods of the village

At Bolinj, the 2008 heritage survey lists a seventeenth-century talav “used by devotees visiting the temple”, and the Manukarnika Devi talav with a new temple beside it. [n40; CRIT Vol I] A Shani temple stands at Wagholi. [n10] Umrale’s two talavs are used for Ganesh immersions. [n40; CRIT Vol I] At Narangi, Totale, Manvelpada and Ranele in Virar there are talavs listed by the same survey, without temples named. [n40]

The village goddesses of the Virar side, the gaondevi of each village, are absent from the written record. No itemised list of gaondevi shrines at Wagholi, Umrale, Navapur, Kalamb or any other Virar-side village was found, and no jatra calendar. [n49; n10] What the 1882 Gazetteer does record are the household gods of each caste: Cheda and other local spirits for the Agris; Cheda beside Ganpati, Shiv and Krishna for the Pachkalshis; Cheda for the Bhandaris; Khanderao, Bhavani, Bhairav, Babdev, Vir, Kalkai, Cheda and Marubai for the Son Kolis; Ram and the sea for the Vaitis. [n08; n49] Cheda, the spirit honoured by Agri, Bhandari, Pachkalshi and Son Koli households alike, is the nearest the record comes to a shared village god of the Hindu coast. [n08] Whether Cheda is still worshipped in Virar’s houses is not documented. [n41]

The churches

Agashi

The church of the Virar side with the longest history is Agashi’s, and its story is told fully in Chapter 38. The facts and disputes are these. The Franciscan Antonio do Porto founded an orphanage at Agashi in 1535 under the invocation of Nossa Senhora da Luz, Our Lady of Light; Agashi was “the first place of this district visited by Franciscan missionaries”; in April 1540 a retreating Gujarat force burnt the orphanage, and five people who stayed there and refused to accept Islam were burnt alive, “a batch of Christian martyrs… consisting entirely of native converts”, in Gerson da Cunha’s words. [n40; n34; da Cunha pp. 159–160; Gaz XIV pp. 1–2] A church was built at Agashi in 1568; whether it was dedicated to Our Lady of Light (as Wikipedia’s Nandakhal article says) or to St James (as a 2019 heritage blog, the 2008 heritage survey and a 2026 Catholic news report say) is disputed, and the community’s own history says that around 1568 two churches stood there, Our Lady of Light under the Franciscans and St James under the secular clergy. [n49; n40; EI Agashi] Both were destroyed in 1739; St James was rebuilt about 1760 and extended in 1900 by Fr Jovito V. Dias, and again in 1975. [n40; CRIT Vol III; EI Agashi] Anquetil du Perron reached Agashi on the church’s feast day, 16 December 1760, under Maratha rule, and found the lanes “full of people going to church as freely as in a Christian country”. [n40; da Cunha p. 164; Gaz XIV p. 2]

In 1882 the church, “built after the destruction of Nossa Senhora da Luz”, measured seventy-five feet by twenty-two by sixteen, had a vicarage, and its vicar was paid a monthly salary by the British government; its school of seventeen pupils was taught Portuguese reading, arithmetic, singing and music, and the master played the violin in church. [n49; n40; Gaz XIV p. 2] In the 1881 list of nine Bassein churches, Agashi appears as St James (“St. Iagos”). [n34; Gaz XIII-1 p. 206 n.3] The parish later opened an English primary school, Holy Martyrs, in 1938, the St James Marathi School in 1968 and St James High School in 1977, and today covers seven villages or wards: Mangala, Jyoti, Kumbar Wada, Bhandar Wada, Cross Naka, Dhobi Wada and Shirlai. [n40; EI Agashi] The names of those wards seem to preserve the caste geography of the old town within the parish itself: Kumbar Wada reads as the potters’ quarter, Dhobi Wada the washermen’s, and Bhandar Wada perhaps the Bhandaris’, though no source glosses them. A Community Cross stands at Cross Naka. [n09] In 2026 the grounds of St James hosted the silver jubilee of the Yuvajallosh youth festival, organised by Yuvadarshan with the “SAJ family” of Shirlai, Agashi and Jyoti parishes. [n49; Catholic Connect]

Two parishes have been cut from Agashi: Jyoti, Our Lady of Light, which revives the old Franciscan dedication; and Shirlai, Our Lady of Lourdes, listed in a diocesan directory from 1987, with its feast in February. [n40; n49; UCAN directory (search summary)]

Nandakhal and its daughters

The mother church of the Virar belt is the Holy Spirit church at Nandakhal, in Koprad (Kofrad) gram panchayat, dated to about 1573 and built by the Franciscans; it is the only church dedicated to the Holy Spirit in the diocese, and it escaped the destruction of 1739, by a parish legend because the Maratha soldiers who stormed it retreated before a swarm of bees from the surrounding woods. [n40; CRIT Vol III; EI Nandkhal] The 1881 list of Bassein churches gives a Holy Spirit church at Koprad; the notes think it probably the same church. [n34] The parish’s own page gives other dates, a mission from 1557, an official establishment in 1858 and parish status in 1886, and a directory gives 1572; these conflict. [n49] Its story belongs to Chapter 38.

From Nandakhal the twentieth-century parishes of the Virar side were carved out, in an order the parish history gives as “firstly Umrale, then Nanbhat, Bolinj, Virar East, Virar West centers”. [n49; Wikipedia: Holy Spirit Church Nandakhal] The dates, from that parish history (Wikipedia’s article, tagged as needing more citations, and drawn in part from a parish policy document), are:

  • Umrale, St Joseph: Mass said there from 1949; an independent parish in 1970. [n40; n49]
  • Nanbhat, St Paul: Mass from 1949; “On 1 June 1975, due to the efforts of Monsignor Philip Tavares, the Nanbhat parish became independent”. [n40; n49]
  • Bolinj, Christ the King: a centre from 10 May 1988; a separate parish from 10 June 2001; a diocesan directory dates the church to 2000, which probably refers to the building rather than the parish. [n40; n49]
  • Rajodi: church inaugurated on 1 May 1999; the dedication is given in a mirror list as Our Lady of Velankanni, which the same list also gives for Wagholi, so at least one entry is wrong. [n40]
  • Jeladi (in Vatar): a prayer centre from 1 January 1990, the centre inaugurated 1 January 2006. [n49]
  • Vatar: Our Lady of Nazareth community centre under the Nandakhal vicar. [n40]
  • Virar East and Virar West: centres of Nandakhal, without dates. [n49; P1]

The parish history adds the earlier milestones of the mother church that touched its villages: free forest timber granted by the Collector in 1907; a big bell given in 1908; an English school started in 1923 and closed for lack of pupils, with the remembered remark “we get bread and butter from farming and not by studying”; Marathi schools from 1935; St Joseph’s school in 1939; the first local priest, Rev. Frank Lobo, ordained on 5 January 1943, “after 370 years”; the Queen of the Apostles nuns in 1952; and Mother Teresa’s visit on 6 April 1986. [n40] Fr Francis D’Britto of Jeladi, elected president of the 93rd Akhil Bharatiya Marathi Sahitya Sammelan in 2019 and the leader of the Harit Vasai movement, came from this parish. [n40]

The notes record no founding dates for the church buildings of Virar East and West, for Wagholi, for Narangi, for Shirgaon or for the parishes of Global City. [n49]

Arnala, Our Lady of Lourdes, and the diocese

St Peter’s at Arnala, built by the fishermen between 1919 and 1931, is described in Section II. Our Lady of Lourdes at Shirlai, Virar West, is called by one listing a church with “strong Portuguese roots”; no founding year beyond the directory’s 1987 was found. [n49; prayersandpetitions.org]

All these parishes belong since 22 May 1998 to the Diocese of Vasai, erected from the Archdiocese of Bombay, whose first bishop, Thomas Dabre, was born at Bhuigaon in 1945. In 2022 the diocese counted 117,026 Catholics, 2.6 per cent of its population, in forty-nine parishes and four missions. Wikipedia lists “Samvedi, Kupari, Wadval, Marathi, Konkani” among its languages. [n49; GCatholic; Wikipedia: Roman Catholic Diocese of Vasai]

St Thomas, Yashwant Nagar

The Syro-Malabar church of St Thomas in Virar West, consecrated in 2013 and made a parish in 2014, is described in Section XIII. It belongs to a different church, the Syro-Malabar Archeparchy of Kalyan, and to a different people, the Kerala Christians who came with the city. [n49]

Shared ground

The record holds a few moments when the sacred places of one community were used by another. At Umrale, a Kupari village, Hindu families immerse Ganesh images in the village talav. [n40] At Arnala, a Hindu association gave Rs 3,000 to build the Koli Catholics’ church. [n40] Among the Urap families of 1882, some who had been expelled from the Church but had not become Hindu still went to their old parish churches at festivals. [n49] The Nirmal fair of 1882, a few miles south of the Virar side, drew six to seven thousand pilgrims of every faith, Hindu, Muslim and Christian. [n10] And Christian Kolis of the coast are said to revere Ekvira, the goddess of the Hindu Kolis and Pachkalshis. [n08] None of this undoes the hard lines the record also shows, the Mhars kept from the well and the church, the castes that would not eat together, the Samvedis and Kuparis living side by side in separate faiths. But it shows that the lines were crossed.

Part Three: Village by Village

What follows is a walk through the villages of the Virar side, west of the railway and then east of it, with what the record holds for each. A village can be “in Virar” in three ways: within the old Bassein sub-division of 1882, to which all of them belonged; by postal address, Virar West (401303) or Virar East (401305, also 401309); or within the municipal corporation’s urban boundary. [n45; P1] Most of the villages that fell inside the corporation in 2009 have no separate 2011 census figure, because the census counted them inside the city; those that stayed outside, Arnala, Kalamb, Kopri, Saiwan, Khanivade and others, have village rows. [n49; n40] Two secondary websites compile those rows, and they sometimes disagree, particularly on literacy; where they do, both figures are given.

West of the railway

Arnala and Arnala Killa

At the northern tip of the coast, at the mouth of the Vaitarna, Arnala is the Koli village of the Virar side (Section II and Chapter 32). Its island was known to the Portuguese as the “Ilha de Vaccas”, Cows’ Island, and the Tombo of 1727–30 records its coloria held by D. Domingas Pereira de Azevedo. [n45; n40; Gaz XIV p. 10 n.; Tombo fl. 41] Its fort was a Gujarat Muslim fort before about 1530, an unfinished Portuguese one after, and a Maratha fort rebuilt in 1737 by the architect Baji Tulaji, according to the Marathi inscription the Gazetteer read over the northern gateway. [n45; Gaz XIV p. 10] The Salt and Forest Satyagraha of 1929–30 centred on Arnala, and a Gandhi memorial stands there. [n40; CRIT Vol I] Its people: Koli fishermen, three to four thousand of them living round the fort by a Marathi website’s count; a Catholic Koli congregation at St Peter’s since 1919–31; flower growers in a cooperative of 1,100 members by 2023. [n45; n40; n17] The 2011 census: Arnala village 15,331 people in 3,005 households, 1,473 Scheduled Tribe and 117 Scheduled Caste members, sex ratio 985, literacy given as 70.87 per cent by one compilation and 80.10 per cent by another; Arnala Killa 2,566 people in 467 households, no SC or ST members recorded, literacy 69.8 or 80.64 per cent. [n49; n40] Its sacred places are described in Part Two. Arnala kept its own gram panchayat and rural status in 2011, and is better described as Virar’s coastal village than as a Virar neighbourhood. [n45]

Kuparis. No Kupari presence at Arnala is recorded in the notes. Arnala is not in the community’s list of Kupari villages, and its Catholics are Koli fishermen, not Samvedi Christians. [n39; n40] Its Christian history nonetheless runs through a Kupari parish: the hut chapel of 1919 was built by Fr Ismail Da Costa, assistant priest at Agashi, so the Koli Catholics of Arnala were first served from St James, Agashi, before St Peter’s was blessed in 1931. [n40; EI Arnala] And in the Tombo of 1727–30 the coloria of Arnala island, the “Ilha das Vacas”, was held together with the lands of Agaçaim by D. Domingas Pereira de Azevedo, tying the island’s revenue to the Kupari port. [n40; Tombo fl. 41] Whether any Kupari families have since settled at Arnala, for instance among the flower growers of its cooperative, no source says.

Near it, the census village of Mukkam had 2,490 people in 2011, with literacy of 71.57 per cent. [n49] If the fuller reading of the 1882 Urap footnote is right, “Mukam” was one of the Bassein villages with Urap Agri families; nothing else is recorded. [n49] Kuparis: none recorded; the Urap Agris of 1882 were Christian Agris who had returned to Hinduism, not Samvedi Christians. [n49]

Navapur

Navapur lies on the coast between Arnala and Rajodi, and is known today mainly for its beach. [n45; vasaivirar.org] In 1882 it was one of the Bassein villages listed with Urap Agris, in both readings of the footnote. [n49; n40; Gaz XIII-1 p. 117 n.1] A Wikipedia-derived list, seen only through a search summary, names “Navapur (Vatar)” among Mangela Koli villages. [n49] No temple, church or later history of the village was found; the Navapur in the 1882 Places index is a different place near Navli in Palghar. [n40; n49] That is everything the notes hold.

Kuparis. No Kupari presence at Navapur is recorded. Note 40, which set out to verify the Kupari and Vadval villages, lists Navapur among the places with “no Kupari or Vadval evidence in any source reached”. [n40] Its one tie to the Christian past is the 1882 Urap list: some of its Agri families had been Christians and returned to Hinduism in 1820 or 1828, a different community from the Kuparis. The “Navapur (Vatar)” of the unverified Mangela list places it beside Vatar, a village with Kupari families, but no source describes Kuparis in Navapur itself. [n49]

Kalamb

Kalamb, south of Rajodi near Nirmal, had 5,329 people in 2011 in 1,169 households, with 604 Scheduled Tribe and 46 Scheduled Caste members, sex ratio 1,016, and literacy given as 79.64 or 87.20 per cent. [n49; n40] It appears in a Wikipedia-derived list of Mangela Koli villages, unverified. [n49] Its beach has its own small modern history in Chapter 32, including the car swept in by the tide in September 2025. [n45] Kalamb must not be confused with Kalambhon, an inland village on the Tansa with a Shilahara inscription. [n11] No community history of the village was found. [n40]

Kuparis. No Kupari presence at Kalamb is recorded. Note 40 lists Kalamb among the villages whose community membership is “not established”, checked and nothing found. [n40] It lies between Kupari villages, Rajodi to the north and Nirmal and Wagholi to the south, and the Nirmal Bazaar of 1957 stands on the Nirmal–Kalamb road in Wagholi gram panchayat; a Tombo group “Sedacer, Choly, Calamba, Nagao e Varchory” may include Kalamb, but that identification is unverified. [n40] No church, parish or Christian family of Kalamb appears in the notes.

Rajodi

Rajodi, on the coast road, is a Kupari village by the community’s own list and one of the nine plantain-drying villages of 1882. [n40; Gaz XIII-1 p. 292] The Tombo lists several “Rajoly” estates, among them “Vatar Rajoly”, the highest rent of its list at 700 pardaus; which of them is Rajodi cannot be fixed. [n40] Its church was inaugurated on 1 May 1999 under Nandakhal. [n40] Its Nale talav lies on the Arnala–Vasai road, where the gram panchayat found old ghats submerged below the water. [n40; CRIT Vol I] Four teenagers drowned off Rajodi beach in August 2016. [n45] For its village story, see Chapter 38.

Kuparis. Rajodi is a Kupari village in both community lists, of 2007 and 2009, and the coastal northern end of the Kupari country. [n39; n40] In 1882 it was one of the nine villages of the dried-plantain trade, which the Gazetteer says was carried on by Samvedi, Pachkalshi and Christian families; it does not say which families dried plantains at Rajodi. [n40; Gaz XIII-1 p. 292] Its lands in 1727–30 were held by Portuguese lessees, Manuel Varjão da Fonseca (“Vatar Rajoly”, 700 pardaus), Lourenço Dias (“Rajoly e Caraly”, 90 pardaus) or António de Lisboa (“Rajoly Marelá”, 240 pardaus), depending on which Rajoly is meant. [n40; Tombo fls. 37, 45v, 149] For most of its Christian history Rajodi’s Catholics belonged to Nandakhal parish; their own church was inaugurated on 1 May 1999 as one of Nandakhal’s daughters, its dedication given as Our Lady of Velankanni in a list that gives the same dedication to Wagholi. [n40; Wikipedia: Holy Spirit Church Nandakhal] No Kupari family, custom, notable person or population figure for Rajodi is recorded, and nothing describes Kupari life there today beyond the church and the beach.

Agashi

Agashi is the old port and the largest village of the Virar side through most of its history: 6,823 people in 1881, of whom 5,168 were Hindus, 1,500 Christians and 155 Muslims; still the only settlement of the region with more than 10,000 people in 1981. [n49; Gaz XIV p. 2; TIFR] In this chapter’s terms it is the most mixed place of all: Hindu Samvedi Brahmans and Pachkalshis, Christian families “originally Samvedis and Pachkalshis”, Maratha-era Brahman families in their wadas, Jain traders on Chalpeth Road, a Muslim community large enough to be counted in 1881, Lad Vani families in 1882, and possibly Urap Agris. [n49; n35; n40] Its trade in 1881 was plantains and betel leaf, its dried plantains “the best in the district”, and its sea exports averaged Rs 3,18,470 a year in the five years to 1879. [n49; Gaz XIV p. 2] It held one of the four Bassein weekly markets and was one of the four Bassein places where hundis (bills of exchange) were cashed up to £1,000. [n49; n40] The parish wards of Kumbar Wada, Bhandar Wada and Dhobi Wada appear, by their names, to preserve old caste quarters. [n40] Its Zilla Parishad school dates from 1886 and its library from 1914. [n40] Its temples, its Jain tirth and its church are described above; its full history is in Chapter 38. Many old structures, “some 100–150 years old”, were lost to urbanisation in the ten or fifteen years before 2019. [n40; Heritage Chronicles] Agashi has no separate 2011 census figure; it lies inside the corporation. [n40]

Kuparis. Agashi is where the Kuparis’ Christian history begins, and the Kupari village with the longest record. It heads the community’s list of Kupari places after Nirmal. [n39] It was “the first place of this district visited by Franciscan missionaries”: the orphanage of Our Lady of Light, founded in 1535 for forty boys, was burnt in April 1540 with five native converts who refused Islam, the first Christians of the Kupari country in any record; Lisbon archive documents call St Gonsalo Garcia, India’s first canonised saint, “natural de Agaçaim”, a native of Agashi, though tradition ties him to Bassein fort. [n34; n38; n40; da Cunha pp. 159–160] The churches of Our Lady of Light and St James were built about 1568, destroyed in 1739, and St James rebuilt about 1760; on its feast day, 16 December 1760, Anquetil du Perron found the lanes full of people going to church. [n40; da Cunha p. 164] In 1727–30 the lands, the coloria and the wild-palm rent of Agaçaim were held by D. Domingas Pereira de Azevedo. [n40; Tombo fls. 41–44v]

The nineteenth-century numbers are the best of any Kupari village: 900 Native Christians in the parish in 1876 and 1,500 Christians among 6,823 people in 1881, about 22 per cent. [n40; da Cunha p. 161; Gaz XIV p. 2] Most of the seventy-five Christian families “originally Samvedis and Pachkalshis” who dried plantains at Agashi, Vatar and Koprad in 1882 may have lived here, the largest of the three, though the Gazetteer does not divide them by village; they were “gardeners who grow the fruit”, and Agashi’s dried plantains were “the best in the district”. [n34; n40; Gaz XIII-1 p. 395] The church school of 1882 taught seventeen pupils Portuguese, arithmetic, singing, doctrine and music, with a violin-playing master. [n40; Gaz XIV p. 2] Agashi is also on the 1882 list of villages whose Christian Agris returned to Hinduism in 1820 and 1828, which means that some non-Samvedi Christian families of Agashi left the Church in the cholera years; no source records a Kupari return. [n46; n49; Gaz XIII-1 p. 117 n.1]

Today the parish of St James covers Mangala, Jyoti, Kumbar Wada, Bhandar Wada, Cross Naka, Dhobi Wada and Shirlai; it has given birth to the parishes of Jyoti and Shirlai; its schools date from 1938 (Holy Martyrs), 1968 and 1977; a Community Cross stands at Cross Naka; and in 2026 it hosted the silver jubilee of the Yuvajallosh youth festival for the Shirlai–Agashi–Jyoti “SAJ family”. [n09; n40; n49; Catholic Connect] Fr Ismail Da Costa, assistant priest here, founded the Arnala chapel in 1919. [n40] No Kupari surname of Agashi, no Kupari population figure after 1881 and no account of Kupari household life in the town today appear in the notes.

Shirlai (Shirlaiwadi) and Jyoti

These two parishes of the Agashi side, cut from St James, are named in the record mostly by their churches, and nothing in the notes describes their non-Christian neighbours, their Hindu shrines or their census figures. Shirlai’s church of Our Lady of Lourdes is listed in a diocesan directory from 1987, with its feast in February, at an Agashi post office in Virar West, and a church listing says it has “strong Portuguese roots”; Jyoti’s church of Our Lady of Light revives the dedication of the Franciscan orphanage-church of 1535. [n40; n49; UCAN directory (search summary); prayersandpetitions.org] Whether “Jot”, one of the Bassein villages with Urap Agris in the 1882 list, is Jyoti, the notes do not establish; the likeness of the names is all there is. [n49]

Kuparis. Shirlaiwadi (“Shrilaiwadi”) is a Kupari village in the community’s list, and both parishes are part of the Kupari belt round Agashi; in 2026 they joined Agashi as the “SAJ family” for the Yuvajallosh festival. [n39; n49; Catholic Connect] Their Kupari history before the twentieth century is Agashi’s. No family, notable person, custom specific to either village, or population count is recorded.

Vatar, with Jeladi, Barodi and Adlay

Vatar holds the oldest dated mention of any village of the Virar side: Vattéraka, granted in AD 1150 by the Shilahara king Haripaldev, with its headmen Risi Mhatara and Changdev Mhatara among the witnesses. [n40; Gaz XIV p. 385] Anquetil du Perron noticed it in 1760 as “Outar”. [n40] It was one of the three villages where in 1882 Samvedi, Pachkalshi and Christian families dried plantains together. [n49; Gaz XIII-1 p. 395] Its hamlets, Jeladi, Barodi and Adlay, are a Nandakhal centre of Our Lady of Nazareth; in December 2016 the parish counted 401 people in Jeladi, 187 in Vatar-Barodi and 37 families in Adlay. [n40] The 2008 survey lists Vatar gram panchayat with the Don talav (two talavs on the Arnala–Nirmal road) and the Bhongale talav. [n40] Vatar is listed as urban in a Vasai tehsil village table. [n45] Jeladi was the home of Fr Francis D’Britto. [n40]

Kuparis. Vatar is not on the community’s list of thirteen Kupari places, but the record shows Christian families of Samvedi and Pachkalshi origin there since at least 1882, when it was one of the three villages, with Agashi and Koprad, where “about seventy-five families of Native Christians originally Samvedis and Pachkalshis” dried plantains beside Hindu Samvedi and Pachkalshi families; a parish table in the Wikipedia article on the Kuparis, seen in a mirror copy, adds Vatar as a Kupari parish centre. [n40; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 292, 395; Bharatpedia mirror of Wikipedia “Kupari”] Its lands in 1727–30 were the pacaria “Vatar Rajoly”, held for lives by Manuel Varjão da Fonseca at 700 pardaus, the highest rent in its list. [n40; Tombo fl. 45v] Vatar’s Catholics belong to Nandakhal parish, through the Our Lady of Nazareth community centre; in December 2016 the parish counted 108 families and 401 people in Jeladi, 53 families and 187 people in Vatar-Barodi, and 37 families in Adlay, and Jeladi had a prayer centre from 1990 and a centre building from 2006. [n40; n49] Its notable Kupari son is Fr Francis D’Britto of Jeladi (1943–2024), translator of the Sahitya Akademi-winning Subodh Bible, president of the 93rd Marathi Sahitya Sammelan and leader of the Harit Vasai movement. [n07; n38; n40] The Shilahara headmen of 1150, Risi and Changdev Mhatara, belong to the Samvedi past of both faiths; no source tests whether any family of Vatar descends from them. [n40]

Koprad (Kofrad)

Koprad, half a mile south-west of Bolinj, was in 1882 the hamlet of Bhiku Patil, “a Samvedi Brahman”, near whose house lay a fifteenth-century inscribed stone; it was also one of the three villages of the mixed plantain driers. [n49; Gaz XIV p. 342; Gaz XIII-1 p. 395] The Holy Spirit church of Nandakhal stands in Kofrad gram panchayat, and the 1881 list of Bassein churches names a Holy Spirit church at Koprad. [n40; n34] The “Kofrad Talav Precinct”, with the open space before the church, is in the 2008 survey “the chief public place in the area”. [n40] Koprad must not be confused with Kopri, the census village east of Virar (below). [n40] See Chapter 38.

Kuparis. Koprad is the clearest picture the record gives of Kuparis living beside the Hindu Samvedis from whom they came. In 1882 its headman, Bhiku Patil, was a Hindu Samvedi Brahman, and it was one of the three villages where Christian families “originally Samvedis and Pachkalshis” dried plantains with Hindu Samvedi and Pachkalshi families. [n40; n49; Gaz XIV p. 342; Gaz XIII-1 p. 395] The 1881 list of the nine Bassein churches names a Holy Spirit church at Koprad, and the 1882 Gazetteer describes it at “Kopra’d village, ten miles north of Bassein”: dedicated to the Holy Ghost, sixty feet by twenty-two by twenty-seven, with a vicarage, a vicar paid about Rs 14-8 a month by the British government, no parish school, and a congregation of 2,415, the largest Christian congregation recorded on the Virar side in 1882. [n34; n46; Gaz XIII-1 p. 206 n.3; Gaz XIV p. 210] The notes judge it very probably the same church as Nandakhal’s, which stands in Kofrad gram panchayat; its Kupari story continues under Nandakhal below. [n40; n46] Koprad is not in the community’s list by name, presumably because its people count themselves of Nandakhal, but that is an inference. [n40]

Nandakhal

Nandakhal, on the Nandakhal road in Kofrad gram panchayat, is today described in the notes as part of Virar West. [n40; n49] Its history in the record is almost entirely the history of its church, and the notes record nothing of a Hindu, Muslim or other community living in the hamlets of the parish itself, though Hindu Samvedi families were its neighbours at Koprad in 1882 (above). Its talav precinct, the open space before the church at the Kofrad talav, was in 2008 “the chief public place in the area”. [n40; CRIT Vol I] The hamlet names of the parish, Unganbhat, Ranebhat, Torbhat, Sakharbhat, Medhe-Borbhat and Banbhat, contain bhat, the Marathi word for a rice field, and carry the farming past in them. [n40; inference] No 2011 census figure exists for Nandakhal. [n40]

Kuparis. Nandakhal is the largest Kupari parish of the Virar side and the mother church of the belt. Its Holy Spirit church, the only church of that dedication in the diocese, was built by the Franciscans about 1573 for parishioners of Holy Cross, Nirmal, who lived nearer; its first Franciscan priests came in 1585; it escaped the destruction of 1739, by the parish legend because a swarm of bees drove off the Maratha soldiers; after 1739 its priests came from Goa; and da Cunha in 1876 counted 2,000 Native Christians in its parish of Espirito Santo and called the church “the only one standing in its primitive state”. [n40; da Cunha p. 161; EI Nandkhal] The parish’s own page gives other dates, a mission from 1557, official establishment in 1858 and parish status in 1886, which conflict. [n49]

The twentieth century is told in the parish history: free forest timber in 1907; the big bell of 1908, given by Dinda Kurel; four metal pillars and an upper floor in 1910 and the “Alpari” front in 1920; Mgr Francesco de Silva’s coconut planting in 1922 and the short-lived English school of 1923 (“we get bread and butter from farming and not by studying”); Marathi schools from 1935 and St Joseph’s school in 1939; Rev. Frank Lobo, the first local priest, ordained on 5 January 1943 “after 370 years”; the Queen of the Apostles sisters in 1952; a new sacristy and residence blessed by Cardinal Simon Pimenta on 27 November 1983; Mother Teresa’s visit on 6 April 1986; the 425th anniversary in 1996–97; statues of Mary in 2006 and of Pope John Paul II in 2011. [n09; n40; n46; Wikipedia: Holy Spirit Church Nandakhal] From it were cut Umrale (1970), Nanbhat (1975), the Bolinj centre (1988; parish 2001), Jeladi (1990), Rajodi (1999) and the Virar East and Virar West centres. [n40; n49]

In December 2016 the parish counted 2,150 families and 7,339 people in twenty-nine hamlet groups, the largest Ghosali (767), Nandan (585), Persav (552), Kolbhag (485) and Jeladi (401), with Rumao Ali (369), Ranebhat (330) and Vatar-Barodi (187) among the rest, and said it had sent sixty-eight priests and many nuns into the Church. [n40; Wikipedia: Holy Spirit Church Nandakhal] Its “Kupari songs”, about brides, fodder, the groom’s first shave and the return from rice planting, are “rarely heard” now. [n07; Vasaiker] Fr Francis D’Britto’s funeral was held here in July 2024, with about 3,000 mourners. [n38] By community account the families of the parish farmed until the late 1960s and 1970s and are now largely professionals and emigrants; no figure confirms it. [n40; n34]

Bolinj

Bolinj, on the old Virar–Agashi road, was in 1882 “formerly a large town” that “probably had rich temples, but no remains were seen”; a Devanagari inscribed stone served as a doorstep in the house of a Christian, Joseph Manuel Lopez, “Juju Manu Lop” in the village’s speech, and carved stones were said to lie in the Devhara pond. [n49; Gaz XIV pp. 341–342] Boats could not pass beyond Bolinj on the silting creek. [n40] The Tombo names it “Bolinja e terra Belvar”. [n40] It was a plantain-drying village in 1882, a Kupari village in the community’s list, and the home of the Joseph who in 1989 accompanied the first priest to visit Virar’s Syro-Malabar families. [n40; n49] Christ the King parish was a centre from 1988 and a parish from 2001. [n40] Its talavs include a seventeenth-century one used by visitors to the temple, the Manukarnika Devi talav, and the Karmale Soneri talav with its small dargah; its Zilla Parishad school dates from 1879. [n40; CRIT Vol I] Bolinj is where the old garden belt now meets the towers of Virar West (Chapter 27). See Chapter 38.

Kuparis. Bolinj is a Kupari village in the community’s list, and in 1882 it was both a plantain-drying village and the home of the only named Christian of the Virar side in the nineteenth-century record: Joseph Manuel Lopez, “Juju Manu Lop” in the village’s speech, at whose door lay the Devanagari stone. His name, a Portuguese baptismal name reshaped by Marathi speech, is the earliest Kupari name of the Virar side on record. [n39; n40; Gaz XIV p. 342; Gaz XIII-1 p. 292] In 1727–30 the pacaria “Bolinja e terra Belvar” was leased for lives to D. Maria Henriques Coelho da Silva at 450 pardaus. [n40; Tombo fl. 54v] For most of their history Bolinj’s Catholics went to Nandakhal; a centre was opened at Bolinj on 10 May 1988, the Christ the King church is dated 2000 in a directory, and the parish became independent on 10 June 2001. [n40; n49] The “Joseph of Bolinj” who in 1989 guided the first priest to Virar’s Syro-Malabar families from Kerala is recorded without a community, so the notes cannot say whether he was Kupari. [n49] Bolinj’s Kupari families today live where the garden belt meets the towers; no source describes their life, numbers or customs there.

Umrale, Nanbhat and Wagholi

These three Kupari villages lie on the southern and western edge of the Virar side, where it meets Nirmal and Nallasopara; they are told in Chapter 38 and only placed here. Umrale (Umbrala) was in the north of the 1882 Sopara garden, a plantain village, a parish from 1970, and the village where Ganesh images are immersed in the talavs. [n40] Nanbhat is a hamlet group of the Nandakhal side whose name may contain bhat, paddy field, a parish from 1975; it appears in no Gazetteer, Tombo or heritage survey entry. [n40] Wagholi (Vagholi) was a creek landing from which molasses, rice and betel went by sea to Surat, Broach, Jambusar and Gogha in 1882, one of the nine plantain villages, and the site of an illegible fourteen-line inscribed stone; it has a Shani temple. [n40; n10] In 2013 a survey counted fifty-four family irrigation ponds (bavkhal) at Wagholi where the records showed sixteen. [P1; n17]

Kuparis. All three are Kupari villages in the community’s list, and all three were plantain villages or garden villages in the 1882 record, though the Gazetteer does not say which families of each dried the fruit. [n39; n40; Gaz XIII-1 p. 292; Gaz XIV p. 314] Umrale’s Catholics belonged to Nandakhal until Mass was said in the village from 1949 under Mgr Mendes; a prayer house foundation is dated “1935” by a source that also places its founder’s term in 1952, so the date is doubtful; St Joseph’s became an independent parish in 1970. On 7 June 2026 the Diocese of Vasai made St Joseph’s, Umrale, a pilgrim centre for the Year of St Francis, with a plenary indulgence, the newest honour of any Kupari church of the Virar side. [n38; n40] Umrale’s talavs, where Hindu families immerse their Ganesh images, are the one recorded place in the Kupari villages of Virar where the two faiths share a sacred use of the same water (see “Shared ground” above). [n40] Nanbhat shared Umrale’s history until “on 1 June 1975, due to the efforts of Monsignor Philip Tavares, the Nanbhat parish became independent”, dedicated to St Paul; nothing else of its Kupari families is recorded. [n40] Wagholi’s lands were held in 1727–30 in two leases, “Vagoly” by Manuel de Abreu de Lima and “Vastal Vagoly” by Fernão da Silveira e Meneses at 60 pardaus; its parish, by an unverified mirror list, is Our Lady of Velankanni, shared with Mardes and cut from Nirmal, with no founding date. [n40; Tombo fls. 41v, 45] Who dug the fifty-four bavkhal of 2013 the source does not say. No Kupari family, notable person or population figure of any of the three villages is recorded.

Narangi

Narangi (Naringi) is the second half of the Tombo’s “Virar Naringuy”, the aldeia of the highest rent in 1727–30. [n40] The 2008 survey lists Naringi talav and the Naringi Zilla Parishad school among Virar’s items. [n40] Its people are described in an unreferenced Wikipedia stub as “mostly the Mangela community”, and it lies in Ward 5 of the corporation. [n49; Wikipedia: Narangi] No 1882 entry for Narangi was found. [n49] Its modern landmark is the Narangi (Naringi) flyover at railway crossing No. 41, built by the Public Works Department over six or seven years and opened on 8 May (the year is not printed in the report, but is consistent with 2026) by the guardian minister Ganesh Naik, expected to save at least thirty minutes between Virar East and West. [n47; FPJ; Mumbai Live] Rustomjee Global City, a township of about two hundred acres, lies at Chikhal Dongari on the Narangi Bypass Road in Virar West. [n47]

Kuparis. No Kupari presence at Narangi is recorded. Its one Portuguese-era record is the aldeia “Virar Naringuy”, leased to D. Pedro de Meneses at 2,000 pardaus, a Crown estate whose tenants are not named; the notes find no church, parish or Christian family there, and no founding date for any church at Narangi. [n40; n49; Tombo fl. 121]

Virar gaothan: the town itself

The old village of Virar, round the station, the pond and the lake, is told in Chapter 27. For this chapter its peoples are: the Urap Agris named in the 1882 footnote; the Pachkalshis of Vartakwada, if the ward’s name means what it seems to; the Kolis and Varlis of the Saturday market; and from the 1980s the migrants of the dormitory town. [n49; n40; n45] The 2008 survey lists, besides Vartakwada and Naringi, Dongarpada, Totale talav and Ranele talav. [n40] In 1882 the Gazetteer saw the remains of a Portuguese church and well fifty yards south of the station, a carved stone with female figures on the west bank of the Virar lake, and the cow-and-calf land-grant stone east of the station. [n45; Gaz XIV p. 382] The church remains are the only trace of a Christian congregation in Virar town before the twentieth century; who worshipped there, and when it fell, is not recorded.

Kuparis. The old Virar gaothan is not in the community’s list of Kupari villages, and no source records Kupari families in it before the city. [n39; n40] Its Christian past in the record belongs to others: the Portuguese chapel of the Crown aldeia “Virar Naringuy”, whose congregation is unknown and which the notes judge a village chapel, not a parish church; and the Christian Agris of Virar, who returned to Hinduism in 1820 or 1828 and whose only trace in 1882 was surnames such as Gomes, Soz, Fernan, Frutad and Minez. [n46; n49; Gaz XIII-1 p. 117 n.1] Before the 1970s the Catholics of Virar were served from Nandakhal, and the Catholic centres of Virar East and Virar West were carved out of that Kupari parish after Umrale and Nanbhat, without recorded dates. [n46; n49] Who makes up those town congregations, Kuparis from the western villages, Vadvals, Koli Christians, or the migrant Catholics who came with the railway city, no source says. The statement that Virar has many Kuparis is true of the Virar West villages round Nandakhal and Agashi; for the station town itself the notes hold no figure.

Tirupati Nagar, Yashwant Nagar and the new West

The layouts of modern Virar West, Tirupati Nagar among them, have no recorded history beyond their names. [n49] Yashwant Nagar holds the Syro-Malabar church of St Thomas. [n49] The Viva colleges, run by the Late Shri Vishnu Waman Thakur Charitable Trust, stand on Viva College Road: Utkarsha Junior College dates from 1991 by one account, and the Viva College of Arts, Science and Commerce from 2000 or 2001, depending on the source. [n49; n47] They are part of the private education that grew with the city in the 1990s and 2000s.

Kuparis. No Kupari presence in Tirupati Nagar or Yashwant Nagar is recorded. The Catholic church of Yashwant Nagar, St Thomas, belongs to the Syro-Malabar Archeparchy of Kalyan and to the Kerala Christians, a different church and people from the Latin-rite Kuparis of the Diocese of Vasai; its story touches the Kupari villages only through the Bolinj resident, Joseph, who helped begin it in 1989. [n49]

East of the railway

East of the line the record is thinner still. The 1882 Gazetteer has no entry under the modern names of Chandansar, Phulpada, Manvelpada, Kaner, Saiwan, Kharwai or Tirupati Nagar, and online histories of these places are mostly property listings. [n49; n12; n45] Marathi searches returned only Wikipedia mirrors. [n45] What follows is therefore short, and says so.

Phulpada and Papdkhind

Phulpada (Phoolpada) lies at the foot of Jivdani in Virar East. The Papdkhind (Papadkhandi) lake at Phulpada is, by Marathi Wikipedia, the city’s largest reservoir; the Palghar district page calls the dam at the foot of Jivdani “once a major source of fresh water”. A botanical garden and the Baronda Devi temple are nearby. [n49; Marathi Wikipedia: Virar; Palghar district] The name Papdkhind should not be confused with Papdi, the old fort-and-church village of south Bassein. [n49] No community history, first mention or census figure for Phulpada was found; it is inside the corporation. [n12; n49]

Kuparis: no Kupari presence recorded in the notes. [n49]

Manvelpada

Manvelpada is a Virar East road and residential zone, associated in one listing with the Ekvira Darshan locality; the 2008 heritage survey lists a Manvelpada talav. [n49; n40] The pada of the name means hamlet; manvel is unexplained. [n49] No history was found, and nothing about its people before the towers. [n12; n49]

Kuparis: no Kupari presence recorded in the notes. [n49]

Chandansar and Shirgaon

Chandansar is a residential locality of Virar East. A listing places “Shirgaon Road in Chandansar, Virar East, near a municipal office and a Zilla Parishad school”. [n49; villageinfo.in (search summary)] Chandansar Hospital in Virar East appears in a news report of 20 November 2026. [n47] Some sources group Chandansar with Gas (Gaskopari) and Kopri as a cluster of gram panchayats. [n40] No history was found.

Shirgaon, in this sense, is a road and locality name in Chandansar, and the Viva Institute of Technology (2009 by one aggregator) is placed at Shirgaon, Virar East. [n49; n47] It must not be confused with the Shirgaon (Sirgaon) of Palghar taluka, which has a Portuguese-era coastal fort described in the 1882 Gazetteer. [n49] The 1882 Virar entry also mentions a “Shrigaon”, two miles east of Jivdhan, with an old Portuguese fort to its south and, about a mile north, Pandav Dungri, “so called from a set of small caves which were cut away at the time of making the railway”. [n45; Gaz XIV p. 383] Whether that Shrigaon is today’s Shirgaon of Virar East the notes do not establish; if it is, it is the only east-side locality with an 1882 mention.

Kuparis. No Kupari presence in Chandansar or Shirgaon is recorded. The Tombo of 1727–30 has an aldeia “Chandory/Sandory”, leased to Manuel Pereira de Castro, which note 40 thinks “probably Chandansar or another northern aldeia”; if so, its lands were a Portuguese Crown lease like those of the Kupari villages, but no Christian community there is recorded, then or since. [n40; Tombo fl. 111v] The notes give no founding date for any church at Shirgaon. [n49]

Kaner

Kaner appears as an urban entry in the Vasai tehsil list. No history was found. [n49]

Kuparis: no Kupari presence recorded in the notes. [n49]

Gaskopari and Kopri

Gaskopari is the village of the Ganesh temple and its Magh fair (Part Two). [n49] Kopri is the census village of 10,180 people in 2011, in 2,507 households, with a sex ratio of 904 and literacy given as 76.39 per cent by one compilation and 90.02 by another. [n49; n11] Which Kopri this is has been disputed within the notes: one note places it on the Virar side in the Chandansar–Gas–Kopri group, citing the 2008 heritage survey; another warns that Vasai has more than one Kopri and that the census row may be a different one; a third wonders whether the 1882 Dasagri village “Kofar” is a Kopri or Kopar. [n40; n12; n49] What is certain is that it is not Koprad, the Kupari hamlet of Bolinj. No history of Kopri or its people was found. [n45]

Kuparis. No Kupari presence in Gaskopari or Kopri is recorded. Two confusions need guarding against. Gaskopari, the Ganesh-temple village of Virar East, is not Gass, the Kupari village south of Nirmal that is the subject of Chapter 26; and Kopri is not Koprad, the hamlet of the 1882 Christian plantain driers. [n40; n49] The Kopri in the 1882 list of Salsette villages with Urap Agris is a different Kopri again, outside Bassein. [n49]

Saiwan

Saiwan had 2,647 people in 2011 (1,329 men and 1,318 women), with literacy of 50.62 per cent, low for the taluka. [n49] The notes read that low figure as suggesting a farm or Adivasi village, but that is an inference; no source describes its people. The “Saivan” mentioned in the 1882 Gazetteer is in Dahanu, a different place. [n49] No history was found.

Kuparis: no Kupari presence recorded in the notes. [n49]

Kharwai

For Kharwai no source of any kind was found. The Kharvis of the 1882 Gazetteer were a Gujarati salt-boat caste and have nothing to do with the place-name. [n49]

Kuparis: no Kupari presence recorded in the notes. [n49]

Khanivade

Khanivade (Khaniwade), in the Vaitarna tidal belt, was one of the four Bassein Dasagri villages named in 1882, with Kofar, Chandve and Navsai. [n49; Gaz XIII-1 p. 116 n.] In 2011 it had 2,386 people in 541 households, literacy 68.32 per cent, sex ratio 870. [n49] Navasai, another of the four, had 1,131 people. [n49] Nothing else is recorded of either.

Kuparis: no Kupari presence recorded in either village; the Dasagris of 1882 were a Hindu Agri division that never converted. [n49]

Gokhivare, on the edge

Gokhivare lies in Vasai East, near the Virar boundary, and is a Vadval village rather than a Virar one. In 1882 it was one of the five villages of the Christian Vadval cartmen, “the best axemen in the district”. [n49; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 97–98] In 1914 cholera struck it; the villagers vowed a chapel to St Roque, two hundred of them built one forty feet by eighteen, it was blessed on 30 June 1930, and it became an independent parish in 1943. [n40; EI Gokhivare] It had a bridge across the creek built by General Louis de Mello Pereira in the Portuguese period, according to a 1728 report, and a Gokhivare talav near an Adivasi pada is listed in the 2008 survey. [n40] In 2001 it was a census town of 19,772 people. [n49] The cholera vow of 1914 at Gokhivare is the Christian mirror of the Urap returns of 1820 and 1828: in one century Christian families turned from the Church to the cholera goddess, in the next a Christian village turned to a plague saint. Chapter 38 tells it.

Kuparis. Gokhivare is a Christian village, but a Vadval one, not Kupari: its Christians of 1882 were the Vadval cartmen and axemen of the south, and it is in neither community list of Kupari villages. [n40; n49] The contrast is the point. The Christian Vadvals of the cart villages and the Kuparis of the Agashi–Nandakhal gardens were both Portuguese-era converts of gardening stock, but by 1882 they lived in different belts, did different work, and today speak different forms of the coast’s speech. [n07; n40]

What the eastern map holds

The -pada names of the eastern slopes, the 1882 market of Kolis and Varlis, the Varli hamlet of Dhondvira on Tungar and the Warlis of Jivdani hill, the Adivasi pada beside Gokhivare’s talav, and the 20,000 illegal constructions activists count between Kaman and Virar East together suggest that the eastern side of Virar was the country of the hill peoples until the city climbed its slopes. [n49; n10; n08; n40] Not one record in the notes says so of any named place. That is the eastern side’s history as the written sources leave it: a few pointers, and a great deal of silence. For the Kuparis the division of the map is plain: every village where the record places them lies west of the railway, in the old garden belt; east of the line the notes record no Kupari village, and the Virar East Catholic centre of Nandakhal parish is the only Kupari-linked institution named there, without a date or a count. [n39; n40; n49]

What the record does not hold

The list of what is unknown about the peoples of Virar is long, and it is worth setting out, because each item is a question that someone in Virar may be able to answer.

  • No source names a Warli, Katkari or Thakur hamlet on Virar’s eastern side, or gives their numbers there at any date after 1872, when Bassein as a whole had 6,099 Varlis. [n49; n08]
  • No Waghoba shrine, sacred grove or village gaondevi of the Virar side is named in any source, and no jatra calendar is recorded. [n10; n49]
  • The Mangelas’ place in the 1882 record is unclear, and Narangi’s Mangela identity rests on an unreferenced Wikipedia stub. The Vaitis are not mentioned after 1882. [n49; n08]
  • The boat counts, catches and Hindu–Christian proportion of Arnala’s fishing families are not recorded. [n08]
  • The Hindu Samvedi community of the twentieth century has no study, no population figure and no institutional history in the notes; whether the 1882 customs survive is unknown. [n08]
  • No census of Vadvals exists after 1901. [n41]

From the market to the rice pot

Go back to the Saturday market east of the station in the early 1880s, under the banyans by the pond. The Kolis are there with fish from Arnala and the shore; the Varlis with what the hills gave; garden stuff from Agashi, Bolinj and Vatar, grown by Samvedi Brahmans whose musicians, by their own story, played at the Bhavani Shankar temple, and by Christian families whom the Gazetteer called “originally Samvedis and Pachkalshis”; cloth that may have passed through the hands of a Lad Vani or a Marwari moneylender; pots made in Virar for the palm-juice of the Bhandaris; and, in the town itself, the Urap Agri families whose grandparents had been taken back into caste by Palshe priests in 1820 and 1828, and whose surnames, the Gazetteer noted, were still Gomes, Soz, Fernan, Frutad and Minez. Each of them had a different story of where they came from, a different god in the house, a different way of speaking the language of the coast. They were sorted, in 1882, into villages and castes and faiths that did not marry each other and often did not eat together. And they came to the same market every Saturday.

The market has long since been swallowed by the city, and the city has added a million people to the people of that crowd. But the peoples of the 1882 market are still there, in Narangi and Arnala, in Agashi and Vatar, in the wards of the old gaothan and on the slopes of Jivdani, speaking, some of them, the dialects that Grierson wrote down in 1905. The last chapter of this book, the Epilogue, asks what this coast’s long history has left in the rice pot of each of its peoples. On the Virar side, the answer begins with that crowd under the banyans.
