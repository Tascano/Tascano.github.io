Book One

The Coast Through Time

From the lava that made the ground to the megacity that has reached it: the story of the coast in the order it happened.

The ground came first. Before there were people, there was basalt, poured out in a few hundred thousand years about sixty-two million years ago, then worn, drowned and uncovered again as the sea rose and fell. People came to a shore that was not yet the shore we know, and a port grew on an island in the creeks that Ashoka’s engravers and Greek sailors both knew by name.

For more than a thousand years after that the coast’s history is told by stones in fields, copper plates and the passing notes of foreign travellers. Then the written record thickens: sultans, a Portuguese treaty signed on a galleon, and a walled city that became, for two centuries, the capital of the Portuguese North. Its fall in 1739 opens a different story, of Marathas, the Company, the railway, the plantain trade and the long road to the republic.

Read in order, these chapters are a single argument: that this strip of land has always lived by the sea, by its water and by the people who came to it, and that each age built on what the last left behind.

Parts and chapters in this book

Part One — Before People

The coast begins in fire and ends this part under ice-age seas. These two chapters cover almost all of time, and almost none of it has a person in it. They are here because the lava, the monsoon and the rising sea explain why a port could stand on this island and why the land looks as it does.

  1. 1Fire and Drift
  2. 2Rock, Monsoon and Ice

Part Two — The First Coast

Now people arrive, thinly and long before any written word. The evidence is shells, stone tools and the gaps between finds, and the chapters are careful about what they can and cannot say. They end at the threshold of history, with farmers and sailors beginning to move along a coast that would soon have a name.

  1. 3The Ice Age Shore and the First People
  2. 4Farmers, Seafarers and the Road South

Part Three — Shurparaka

With Ashoka’s engraved slab the coast enters the written record, and for the first time we know a name: Shurparaka, Suppara, Sopara. These two chapters follow the port at its height, its Buddhist and Jain world and its Greek and Roman contacts, and then the long, quieter centuries of the Shilahara kings, when the villages first step out of the dark in copper-plate grants.

  1. 5Shurparaka: Port of the Western Sea
  2. 6Quiet Centuries and the Shilahara Kings

Part Four — Sultans and the Portuguese

The sultans of Delhi and Gujarat reach the sea, a king called Bimba is remembered in a chronicle, and the Portuguese arrive. These three chapters take the coast from 1300 to 1739: the port of Basai and its fortress, the treaty of 1534 that handed it to Portugal, the rise of Baçaim as a walled city and a mission field, and its life and fall as the Court of the North. This is the best-documented stretch of the whole book, and the longest.

  1. 7Sultans, Raja Bimba and the Sea
  2. 8Baçaim Rising
  3. 9The Court of the North and the Siege

Part Five — Marathas and the Company

After 1739 the walls became Bajipur, the Peshwas’ town, and in 1818 the Company took the coast. These four chapters follow the Maratha years, the British district, the sugar and plantain economy, the railway that made Virar and the plague that came with it, and the long movement toward independence.

  1. 10Maratha Vasai
  2. 11The Island of Bassein under the Company
  3. 12Rails, Plantains and Plague
  4. 13Toward Freedom

Part Six — The Republic

India is independent and the coast is a quiet place of gardens and salt pans, until 1988, when a green belt opens to the city. These three chapters follow the republic’s years from the quiet decades to the megacity’s edge, and end in November 2026.

  1. 14The Quiet Republic Years
  2. 15From Green Belt to City
  3. 16On the Edge of the Megacity
