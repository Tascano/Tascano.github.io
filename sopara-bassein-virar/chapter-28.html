Period covered: 28 November 1864 to 15 August 1947

In this chapter. The line arrives in 1864 and a town grows round the station, on plantains, a goddess in a cave and the sub-division counted by the 1881 census.

At a quarter to seven on the morning of 12 April 1867, a train of four carriages pulled out of a station that the timetable spelled “Viraur” and headed south for Bombay. It stopped at Neela, which was Nalasopara; at Bassein; at Panjo; at Berewla, which was Borivali; at Pahadee (Goregaon), Andaru (Andheri), Santa Cruz, Bandora (Bandra), Mahim, Dadure (Dadar) and Grant Road; and it ended at Back Bay. That evening, at half past five, the same train left Back Bay for the return journey to Virar. It was, by the account of the railway company’s own magazine, the first regular suburban service of the Bombay, Baroda and Central India Railway: a train run not for the long-distance traveller to Gujarat but for people who lived outside the city and went into it and came home again. There was one such train each way a day until 1870, and five each way after that. A second-class ticket cost seven paise a mile from Back Bay, and a third-class ticket three. [n46 §9; n45 §2; Wikipedia: Virar railway station, citing BB&CI Magazine, Dec 1923; Free Press Journal, 12 Apr 2022]

The details come at second hand: from a company magazine of 1923, quoted by Wikipedia, and from the railway historian A. K. Shrivastava, quoted by the Free Press Journal on the service’s 155th anniversary; the fares are the newspaper’s own figures, and the notes treat them as such. [n46 §9; n45 §2] But the core fact is not in doubt. From its first year as a commuter railway, Bombay’s local train ran as far as Virar and turned back. Virar was the end of the line. For the next eighty years, and long after, that fact would decide what kind of place it became.

This chapter follows Virar from the opening of the railway in 1864 to Indian independence in 1947: the station and its traffic, the first full description of the village in 1882, the Saturday market where the forest and the sea met, the goddess in the cave on the hill, the plantains that went north and south by rail, the plague and influenza years, the electric wires of the 1930s and the one great political crowd that the police recorded at Virar. It is a thin record in places. The census never counted Virar separately in these decades, no school or temple at Virar itself has a dated founding before 1947, and the notes hold almost no individual Virarkar’s name. Where the record is silent, the chapter says so, and turns to what the neighbours and the district were doing.

The line arrives, 1864

Ninety-five miles and sixteen stations

The Gazetteer of 1882 gave the railway a dry paragraph. “The Baroda railway runs for ninety-five miles along the coast from Bombay to the border of Surat. In these ninety-five miles are sixteen stations.” Among them were Bassein Road at 33¾ miles from Bombay, Virar at 38¾ and Saphala at 43¾ (the OCR of the fractions is uncertain, and another reading gives Saphala at 48¼; the Gazetteer’s own Virar entry rounds Virar to “about thirty-eight north of Bombay”). “The railway was begun in May 1858 and the line was opened for traffic on the 28th November 1864. The chief difficulty was the number of creeks and streams.” Two bridges of sixty-foot spans crossed the Bassein channel, twenty-nine and thirty miles north of Bombay, and two more the Vaitarna channel, forty-four and forty-five miles out. [n46 §9; n21; Gaz XIII-1 p. 329] The line ran between the garden island and the hills, close under Jivdhan, and Virar’s station stood on the plain about a mile west of the hill. [n46 §1]

The 1882 station list has no Nallasopara and no Naigaon. Bassein Road, at Manikpur, was the station for the old fort-town; it had a waiting room and a traveller’s bungalow. [n21; n37 §6; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 328–329] Virar, five miles further north, was the next stop, and the nearest station for the old town of Sopara. [n46 §8; Gaz XIV p. 382]

What the line destroyed

The railway took something from the land as it went. Two miles east of Jivdhan, south of Shirgaon, the Gazetteer recorded “an old Portuguese fort”, and “about a mile north of it is Pandav Dungri, so called from a set of small caves which were cut away at the time of making the railway”. [n46 §3; n45 §1; Gaz XIV pp. 382–383] Nothing more is known of them: no description, no inscription, no date beyond “the time of making the railway”, that is, between 1858 and 1864. [n46 §3] The one sentence explains why the “Pandava caves” of Virar’s legend survive today only on Jivdhan itself: the line that made the modern town quarried away another of its oldest sites. Basalt was the railway’s material; the Gazetteer noted that the trap of the district “has been largely used on the Bombay and Baroda railway”. [n48 §2; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 48–49] Whether the stone of Pandav Dungri went into the embankment the record does not say.

The embankment itself changed the water. The Gazetteer’s account of the Sopara creek, the old backwater that cut the garden island from the mainland, says that silting had been rapid, “especially since the long railway embankment has dammed the flow of water from the south”. [n46 §1; n48 §1; Gaz XIV pp. 314–315] By 1882 the creek was dry at Sopara, and boats from the north could reach only as far as Bolinj. [n48 §1] The railway thus finished a process the Portuguese had warned about in 1728: the island of Sopara stopped being an island. In 2026, a century and a half later, Western Railway would order a survey into raising the track between Vasai and Virar because the line and the low plain flood together; the embankment that dammed the creek in the 1860s is part of that story. [n48 §2]

From creek to station

The deepest change was in the direction of trade. Before the railway, the produce of the Virar side went out by water: by sea from Agashi, and from the creek landings at Gas, Bolinj and Vagholi in small boats to Surat, Broach, Jambusar, Dholera, Bhavnagar and Gogha. [n48 §1; Gaz XIV pp. 315–316] After it, the produce went increasingly by rail. “By rail, chiefly from the Virar station, molasses, rice, and betel-leaves pass north to Ankleshvar, Nadiad, Viramgam, and Patan, and large quantities of plantains and betel-leaves are sent south to Bombay.” [n46 §8; Gaz XIV p. 316] The notes sum it up: the railway reoriented settlement and trade “from creek landings (Gass, Bolinj, Vagholi) to stations (Virar, Bassein Road/Manikpur, later Nallasopara)”. [n37 §6 Inferences; n46 §8 Inferences] The old landings did not die at once; in 1882 boats still loaded at Gas and Bolinj. But the future had moved to the line.

Viraur, 12 April 1867

The first suburban train

The 1867 service is the single most important fact in Virar’s modern history, and it is worth looking at its evidence closely. The Wikipedia article on Virar station cites “The Bombay Local & Suburban Train Service”, an article in the BB&CI Magazine of December 1923, for the statement that the first regular suburban service of the railway ran from Bombay Backbay to “Viraur” on 12 April 1867, and that Virar “was the northernmost station”. [n46 §9] The Free Press Journal of 12 April 2022 adds the four cars, the times (6:45 a.m. up from Viraur, 5:30 p.m. down from Back Bay), the frequency (one each way from 1867 to 1870, five each way after 1870), the stop list and the fares, crediting the 1923 magazine and A. K. Shrivastava. [n46 §9; n45 §2]

Two cautions belong beside the date. Another source on the history of Vasai Road station says that from 1 November 1865 Bassein Road was the northern end of Bombay’s first suburban service; the two claims conflict, and the notes cannot settle which was first. [P1; n05; n28] And the stop list includes “Neela (Nalasopara)”, although the Gazetteer’s list of 1882 has no Nallasopara station. Either Neela was a halt the Gazetteer did not count, or the magazine of 1923 projected a later stop back onto the first timetable. [P1; n46 §9] The book’s plan leaves the date of Nallasopara’s station, and of its name, as an open question.

What is not in doubt is that Virar was a terminus. The morning train began there, and the evening train ended there. The timing tells its own story: a train leaving the country at a quarter to seven and returning from the city at half past five is a train for people who work in Bombay and sleep outside it. Who those first passengers were, whether Bombay clerks with homes on the coast, traders, or farmers taking produce to the city markets, the sources do not say. In the twentieth century the “Vasaiwale” farmers of the garden belt would carry their produce in kawad baskets on the afternoon train to Victoria Terminus and sell it near Dadar’s Kabutarkhana and Bora Bazaar; that custom is described only in a modern food-history article and is not dated. [n48 §4; India Food Network]

Traffic

The station grew quickly. The Gazetteer’s Virar entry gives its traffic returns: passengers rose “from 48,294 in 1878 to 83,176 in 1880, and in goods from 1730 to 3349 tons”. [n46 §8; n45 §1; Gaz XIV p. 382] (One later note reads the first year as 1873; the Gazetteer’s Virar entry, read directly by two researchers, gives 1878. [n37 §6; n45 §1]) A rise of about seventy-two per cent in passengers and ninety-four per cent in goods in two years is the mark of a station whose district was learning to use it.

The figures can be set beside the neighbours’. Bassein Road, the station for the fort-town and its municipality, carried 86,478 passengers in 1873 and 140,837 in 1880; its goods fell, from 5,292 tons in 1873 to 3,278 in 1880. [n21; Gaz XIII-1 p. 340; Gaz XIV p. 36] Bhayandar’s goods rose from 2,627 tons in 1873 to 19,770 in 1880, almost all of it salt from the Rai Murdha pans. [n21; Gaz XIV p. 45] The whole Baroda line carried 865,990 passengers in 1873 and 1,485,391 in 1880, and its goods rose from 20,075 to 45,438 tons. [n21; Gaz XIII-1 p. 340] So in 1880 Virar, a village, handled more than half the passengers of Bassein Road, a town of ten thousand, and slightly more goods. [inference from n21 and n46] The goods were the produce of the garden villages west of the line: dried plantains, molasses, betel leaf, rice.

The station had facilities to match. In 1882 it had “refreshment and waiting-rooms”. [n46 §8; Gaz XIV p. 382] A refreshment room at a village station is a telling detail: it means travellers waited there, which fits a terminus where the local train turned and long-distance trains stopped. [P1] There was a government rest-house at Virar, one of four in the sub-division with Manikpur, Agashi and Nirmal, and a post office. [n46 §8; n49 §3a; Gaz XIII-1 p. 330] A four-mile metalled feeder road ran west from the station to Agashi, one of the new feeder roads built for the Baroda railway. [n46 §8; n48 §1; Gaz XIII-1 p. 319]

The Gazetteer’s Virar, 1882

The first description

Fifteen years after the first local train, the compilers of the Gazetteer of the Bombay Presidency, Volume XIV, Thana: Places of Interest, gave Virar a full entry of its own on pages 382 and 383 (the archive.org OCR spells it “Virdr” or “Vira’r”). [n46 §8; n45 §1] It is the first description of the place ever printed, and nearly every later account of Virar’s history repeats it, often without saying so. It deserves to be walked through in the order its compilers wrote it, because it is a map.

“Virar, a rich village well-wooded and with many patches of sugarcane, lies in the Bassein sub-division, about seven miles north of Bassein and about thirty-eight north of Bombay. It has a railway station with refreshment and waiting-rooms.” Then the traffic returns. Then: “To the west of the station is the village and a metalled road, which runs four miles west to Agashi. Virar is the nearest station for visitors to the old town of Sopara,” reached “along the Agashi road for about a mile, and then about two and a half miles along a winding lane”. [n46 §8, §3; Gaz XIV p. 382]

So in 1882 the village lay west of the line, on the Agashi road, and the station stood at its eastern edge. The Gazetteer elsewhere complained that the lane from Bolinj to Sopara was “a narrow low lane passable for wheels only in fair weather”, and that “a station at Sopara or a made road to the town … would be a boon to its people”. [n40 A11; n21; Gaz XIV p. 316] Virar had the road and the station that Sopara wanted.

The market by the pond

“To the east of the Virar station, across the railway, is the market place, on the west bank of a large pond under big banyan and mango trees. A large market is held every Saturday, thronged by Kolis and Varlis, selling fish, garden stuff, and cloth.” [n46 §8; n48 §1; Gaz XIV p. 382]

That sentence is a social map of the Virar side in one line. The Kolis came up from the creeks and the coast at Arnala, Navapur and Narangi with fish. The Varlis came down from the forest and hill hamlets to the east. The garden stuff came from the plantations of the Samvedi, Pachkalshi and Christian villages to the west and south. The cloth came, presumably, by rail. [P1] The market stood east of the line, between the station and the hill, under banyans and mangoes on the bank of a pond: the earliest description of the place that became the crowded heart of Virar East. [P1]

The Gazetteer’s district volume adds the network the market belonged to. There were four weekly markets in the Bassein sub-division, at Agashi, Sopara, a place the OCR spells “Dhovh”, and Virar, held on Monday, Wednesday, Friday and Saturday, each attended by five or six hundred people. [n46 §8; n49 §3a; Gaz XIII-1 p. 333] Sopara’s market, the Gazetteer said elsewhere, was “crowded”. [n40 A15; Gaz XIV p. 314] Virar’s Saturday was one stop in a weekly round. Its list of the ten trade centres of the sub-division (Bassein, Nela, Sopara, Agashi, Kaman, Bolinj, Manikpur, Virar, Bhatane and Kharbhav) puts Virar beside the old ports for the first time, though the OCR of that page is uncertain. [n40 A15; P1; Gaz XIII-1 p. 300]

Virar also made things for the market. The Gazetteer’s account of sugar-making mentions Virar earthen kondyas, pots for boiling cane juice, sold to the sugar-makers at eight annas each. [n46 §8; n49 §3a; Gaz XIII-1 p. 392] There must have been potters at Virar to make them, though the Gazetteer names none.

The antiquities

The compilers then listed what a walker would meet around the station. “About fifty yards south of the station, are the remains of a Portuguese church and a Portuguese well, and on a knoll about a mile to the south, stands a notable Portuguese tower or fortified mansion.” On the west bank of the Virar lake, a carved stone with a group of female figures. “About 100 yards east of the station, on the dam of a rice field, stands a stone 2’ 10” x 1’ 4”, with a roughly-cut cow and calf, a symbol of eternity or perpetuity, which, in some land-grant stones, takes the place of the ass-curse.” About two hundred yards further, near the foot of a rocky knoll, two cow’s feet cut in the rock. Further east, “on another rocky hill, a little further east, are the ruins of a Portuguese fort. On Jivdhan hill are small plain caves and a favourite shrine of Devi, and some fortifications.” [n46 §3, §6, §8; n45 §1; Gaz XIV pp. 382–383] The previous chapter discussed what these remains mean. Here what matters is their arrangement: the station had been laid down in the middle of a landscape of medieval grant stones, Portuguese ruins and a sacred hill, and the compilers walked out from the platform to see them.

None of these objects is described in any later source in the notes. Whether the church ruin, the well, the tower, the cow-and-calf stone, the carved figures and the cow’s feet survive in 2026 is unknown. [P1; n46]

The Mhar and the cow

Between the cow’s feet and the fort the Gazetteer told a story, the only story it recorded at Virar. The Mhar of Virar, the village servant (the text also calls him by the Marathi word mirasi) who took the village cattle out to graze, always found among them a cow whose owner never paid him. One day he followed her. She climbed Jivdhan. At the top a woman appeared and agreed to pay what was owed. As she was about to put the money in his hand, he said: “Do not touch me, I am a Mhar.” At that “the goddess Devi, for she was the owner of the cow, disappeared, and the cow leapt from the hill-top and lighted on this rock”, the rock with the two cow’s feet near the station. [n46 §8; n45 §1; Gaz XIV p. 382]

The compilers printed the legend and then, being who they were, explained the stone nearby as a grant-stone symbol. [n46 §8] But the story itself is the most revealing document of Virar’s nineteenth-century society. A goddess appears to a herdsman of the caste whose touch high-caste Hindus of the time held polluting. He warns her, honestly, of what he is. She vanishes. The place where she vanished becomes her shrine. The caste order of the Konkan was strong enough, in the story people told at Virar in the 1880s, to make a goddess flee. [P1; n45 §3]

It was not a story only about Hindus. The Gazetteer’s account of the Christians of the district says that among them too “until lately the feeling of the impurity of the Mhars remained so strong that in some places they were not allowed to draw water from the village well or to enter the church”, and that in 1859 Mhar converts lived outside the villages. [n49 §1g; n37 §3; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 200–202] In the Bassein sub-division in 1881 there were 1,482 Mhars, by the OCR of the census table. [n21; Gaz XIII-2 p. 680] One of them, in Virar’s own tradition, had met the goddess.

The goddess in the cave

Jivdhan in 1882

The Gazetteer gave Jivdhan hill an entry of its own. “Jivdhan Hill, in Bassein, about a mile east of the Virar station on the Baroda railway, has on its top ruins of fortifications visible from the railway … and within the fortifications some very old-looking caves and cisterns said to be the work of the Pandavs. Throughout the fair season people, especially barren women from the surrounding villages, go to the caves to make offerings to an invisible deity, who is believed to have fled from a niche in one of the caves at the touch of a Mhar. Offerings of betelnuts are thrown into a hole in the niche in which the offended deity is believed to live.” [n46 §1, §8; n49 §2c; Gaz XIV p. 116] (The printed page is inferred from the OCR’s running heads and should be checked against the scan; the printed index gives page 110. [n46 §1; n45 §1])

There is no temple in this description. There is a ruined fort, caves, cisterns, a niche with a hole in it, betel nuts thrown into the hole, and women praying for children. The goddess has no name in the entry; she is “an invisible deity”, “the offended deity”. The Virar entry calls the place “a favourite shrine of Devi”. [n46 §8] The 1882 record is of a cave cult, not a temple complex. [n45 §3; P1] The two passages, read together, make one story: the cow and the Mhar below, the vanished goddess in the niche above, and the women who came in the dry months to throw betel nuts into the dark.

When the temple came

Modern sources date the hilltop temple loosely. The Palghar district’s website says it is “about 150 years old”; popular devotional sites claim more than 1,500 years. [n46 §12; n49 §2c; P1] The temple is run by a trust, the Shree Jivdani Devi Mandir Trust (also called the Sansthan). [n46 §12] Wikipedia notes that “the present idol is a very recent one” and that the original sanctum is “the hole in the niche of the cave”. [n49 §2c] If the district’s “about 150 years” is counted back from the 2010s or 2020s, it would put the first temple in the 1870s; but in 1882 the Gazetteer, which describes the hilltop in detail, saw none. [n45 §1, §3] The likeliest reading is that a built temple came after 1882, within the period of this chapter; it fits a loose “about 150 years”, but no source proves it. [n45 §1 Inferences] A Marathi search for the founding of the temple, the building of the steps or the first trust deed returned nothing. [n45 §3] So the transformation of a cave cult into the great pilgrimage of the coast, which Chapter 29 follows, began somewhere in these decades, unrecorded.

The legend changed too, though when is not known. In the version told today, a Mahar herdsman, told by Shankaracharya to graze the village cattle, follows the unpaid-for cow to the hilltop, recognises the goddess as his family deity, Jivdani, and asks not for money but for moksha, release; a barren woman present asks for children, which is why women still offer betel nuts at the hole in the niche. In one version the cow leaps not onto a rock by the village but across the Vaitarna. [n45 §3; n49 §2c] In 1882 the goddess fled from the Mhar’s touch; in the modern story she grants him the highest reward. That is a documented shift between two dated texts. [n45 §3] Why it shifted is not recorded. It is a fair inference, and only an inference, that the change reflects the twentieth century’s rejection of untouchability and the dignity movements of the Mahar community, and that a pilgrimage drawing people of every caste needed a story in which the goddess rejected none of them. [n45 §3 Inferences; P1]

Whose goddess

Who climbed Jivdhan in these decades? The Gazetteer says “people, especially barren women from the surrounding villages”, without naming castes. [n46 §8] Modern devotional and trekking sites call Jivdani the kuldevi (family goddess) of the Mangela, Koli and Bhandari communities and say she is worshipped by Dhiwar, Agri, Mangela, Koli and Bhandari people. [P1; n08] Those are the fishing, salt, toddy and farming peoples of the shore and the creeks: the same people who came to the Saturday market in 1882. [P1] Warli families live on the hill itself. [P1; n10] The cave cult of 1882 may already have been theirs; the sources do not say.

Nearby, the established pilgrimage of the coast in 1882 was not Jivdhan but Nirmal, where the Shankaracharya samadhi drew six to seven thousand pilgrims of every faith to its November fair. [n40 A1; Gaz XIV p. 292] Earlier research concluded that Jivdani’s rise to the dominant mass pilgrimage of the region was a twentieth-century change; that rests on the contrast between the 1882 cave and today’s crowds, not on any series of numbers. [P1; n10]

The neighbours in 1882

The Gazetteer’s entries for the villages round Virar show the country the station served.

Agashi

Agashi, four miles west at the end of the metalled road, was “a town and port”. In 1881 it had 6,823 people: 5,168 Hindus, 1,500 Christians and 155 Muslims. [n46 §8; n49 §2a; Gaz XIV pp. 1–2] It drove “a great trade with Bombay in plantains and betel leaves, its dried plantains being the best in the district”. Its sea trade in the five years to 1879 averaged exports of Rs 3,18,470 and imports of Rs 85,650 a year, rising from Rs 2,46,430 of exports in 1875 to Rs 4,81,810 in 1879. [n48 §1; Gaz XIV pp. 1–2] So, fifteen years after the railway came, Agashi was still a working port, and a growing one; the railway at Virar and the sea at Agashi were partners, not rivals, in the 1870s. [n48 §1 Inferences] Except for one channel, the mouth of its bay was closed by a reef. [n45 §1; Gaz XIV p. 2] At Agashi alone the yearly yield of dried plantains was about 160 tons, 3,000 Bengal maunds, worth about Rs 27,000. [n48 §1; n21; Gaz XIII-1 p. 292]

Its church, “built after the destruction of Nossa Senhora da Luz”, measured seventy-five feet by twenty-two by sixteen, with a vicarage and a vicar paid about Rs 14-8 a month by the British government; its school of seventeen pupils taught Portuguese reading and writing, arithmetic, singing, doctrine and music, and the master played the violin in church. [n46 §8; n49 §2b; Gaz XIV p. 2] Its temples were Shankarji Keshav’s Bhavanishankar, with a government grant of Rs 50 a year and a healing tank, and his Hanuman, with Rs 8; two smaller temples of Vishnu and Ganpati; and the Jain temple of Parasnath built about sixty years before by Motilal, a Vani of Bombay. [n49 §2a; Gaz XIV pp. 2–3] Christians, Hindus, Jains and Muslims, the port, the church school in Portuguese and the Peshwa-era temples: Agashi in 1882 held the whole layered history of the coast in one town.

Arnala

Arnala fort, “also called Janjira or The Island”, was “an oblong square of about 700 feet defended by round towers”, next to Bassein in size and strength among the forts of the north Konkan coast. In 1882 its two temples received government grants, Trimbakeshvar’s Rs 45 a year and Bhavani’s Rs 91; a tomb of two Muslim saints, Shahali and Hajiali, had an endowment of Rs 80; and there was “a stone-lined eight-cornered pond and many wells”. [n46 §8; n45 §1; Gaz XIV pp. 10–11] The Portuguese had called the island the Cows’ Island. On 26 May 1909 the fort was declared a protected monument. [n49 §2c; n45 §4] Around it lived Koli fishing families, whose twentieth-century chapel is described below. [n40 C1]

Koprad and Bolinj

At “Kopra’d village, ten miles north of Bassein”, the Gazetteer recorded a church “dedicated to the Espirito Sancto or Holy Ghost with a congregation of 2415”, sixty feet by twenty-two by twenty-seven, with a vicarage and a vicar paid Rs 14-8 a month by the British government, and no parish school. [n46 §6; Gaz XIV p. 210] The Holy Spirit church at Nandakhal stands in Kofrad (Koprad) gram panchayat and is “the only church dedicated to the Holy Spirit in the Vasai diocese”, so the notes infer that the 1882 “Koprad” church and the Nandakhal church are the same building; neither source says so directly. [n46 §6 Inferences; n40 A3] If so, the parish that served Virar’s Catholics had 2,415 members in 1882, against 2,000 in da Cunha’s count of 1876. [n40 §2]

At Koprad’s hamlet, half a mile south-west of Bolinj, the fifteenth-century stone lay by the house of the Samvedi headman Bhiku Patil; at Bolinj, “formerly a large town”, the Devanagari stone was Joseph Manuel Lopez’s doorstep. [n46 §8; n49 §1a] Bolinj was the northern landing of the creek, about two miles from the old Sopara landing at Dedar. [n40 A11] The heritage survey of 2008 dates a Zilla Parishad school at Bolinj to 1879; if that date is right, it is the oldest school recorded on the Virar side. [n40 A11; CRIT Vol I]

Sopara

Sopara, three and a half miles south-west of the station, was “still a rich country town with a crowded weekly market and a population of about 1700”. Its husbandmen were “Christians, many of them converted Samvedi Brahmans; Musalmans many of them Naitas … and Hindus, chiefly Samvedi Brahmans and Bhandaris”; Gujarat Vanis of the Lad division had come from Cambay about 1760. [n40 A15; n48 §1; Gaz XIV pp. 314–316] The ancient port of the Konkan was now a village visited from Virar station.

A sub-division counted, 1881

The numbers

The census of 1881 did not count Virar on its own, or if it did the Gazetteer did not print it. [n46 §10] It counted the Bassein sub-division, and the Gazetteer’s Part II volume gives the result. The sub-division covered 221 square miles and held 68,967 people, 312 to the square mile; a footnote says the revised total was about 300 more than the figure printed earlier. Its land revenue in 1880 was £12,671. [n46 §8, §10; n21; Gaz XIII-2 pp. 678–680] In 1872 the same area had held 61,089, so it had grown by about thirteen per cent in nine years. [n21]

Of the 68,967, 52,578 were Hindus (76.23 per cent), 2,292 Muslims (3.32), 14,070 Christians (20.40) and 27 Parsis. [n46 §8; n21; Gaz XIII-2 p. 680] One person in five in the sub-division was Christian. The castes counted, with the warning that the digits in this scan are fuzzy and any single figure should be checked, included 5,382 Brahmins, 827 Kayastha Prabhus, 880 Vanis, 80 Jains, 8,461 Agris, 5,978 Kunbis, 839 Sonars, 519 Sutars, 876 Shimpis, 2,375 Mangela fishermen and 77 Machhis, 8,334 Bhandaris (the first digit may be a 3), 7,308 Varlis, 7,048 “Konkanis”, 1,600 Katkaris, 967 Vaitis, 698 Thakurs, 114 Dublas, 321 Chambhars, 1,482 Mhars and 50 Mangs. [n21; n46 §8; Gaz XIII-2 p. 680] The town of Bassein had 10,356 people; Agashi 6,823; Sopara about 1,700. [n21] Virar is missing from every table.

The land

The same pages describe the land. “Tungar hill” stood in the centre of the sub-division, and to the north-west the hills Nilemora, Baronde and Jivdhan. “In a narrow belt of coast land about three miles broad the soil is a rich alluvial with a good supply of water a few feet from the surface”, suited to plantains, sugarcane and coconuts. [n46 §8; n21 §2; Gaz XIII-2 pp. 678–679] There were 191 ponds and 2,624 wells, 25 of them with steps. [n21 §2] Of the land, 64,098 acres were arable, 2,859 unarable, 328 grass, and 70,635 acres, more than half, village sites, roads, ponds and river beds. [n21 §2; Gaz XIII-2 p. 678] Rice covered 29,687 acres in 1880–81 and sugarcane 1,188. [n21 §2; n46 §8] The rainfall had averaged 71.87 inches in the ten years to 1881. [n21] The livestock, as read from a poor scan, included 2,997 carts, 5,308 ploughs, 8,160 oxen, 4,879 cows, 6,466 buffaloes, 128 horses and 8,142 sheep and goats. [n21 §2; Gaz XIII-2 p. 680]

These are the fields and animals whose produce filled the goods wagons at Virar. The carts carried it to the station; Virar’s road to Agashi was built for them.

Plantains by rail

The garden economy

The railway arrived in a district that already grew for distant markets, and it gave that trade a faster road. The chief products of Sopara and its neighbourhood, the Gazetteer said, were “molasses, rice, plantains, and betel-leaves”. [n48 §1; Gaz XIV p. 315] Sugarcane was “grown all over the district, especially in Bassein where sugarcane and plantains are the chief watered crops”; about 750,000 canes went yearly “chiefly to Bombay, Surat, and Broach” at Rs 14 the hundred. Oil-cake from the Gujarat ports came back as manure for the cane, called phod by the Christian growers and khap by others, and was “in great demand”. [n48 §1; Gaz XIII-1 p. 291; Gaz XIV p. 316]

The plantain trade can be counted, roughly. A footnote in the Gazetteer, from a Mr K. J. Ebden, gives yearly sales for the nine Bassein varieties: green basrai, the bulk of the trade, about 220,000 bunches at Rs 24 the hundred; red tambdi, about 50,000 bunches; mutheli, 12,000; rajeli, the drying plantain, about 5,000 bunches; with smaller numbers of lokhandi, bright-yellow sonkeli, wild bankeli, karanjeli and narsingi. [n48 §1; Gaz XIII-1 p. 292 n. 1] The OCR’s digits are shaky, and the notes advise quoting ranges rather than exact figures; the total was of the order of 300,000 bunches a year, mostly basrai. [n48 §1; n21 §2] Most of the green fruit went to Bombay. The train made that possible: a bunch of ripe plantains does not survive a slow boat.

Dried plantains

The famous product of the Virar side was the dried plantain, made only in Agashi, Vagholi, Vatar, Bolinj, Koprad, Nala, Umrale, Rajodi and Murdes. [n48 §1; n21 §2; Gaz XIII-1 p. 292] At Agashi, Vatar and Koprad, between October and January, about eighty-five families of Samvedi Brahmins, fifteen of Pachkalshis and about seventy-five of native Christians, themselves “originally Samvedis and Pachkalshis”, dried ripe rajeli plantains on booths by the sea, smearing them with clarified butter for seven days. [n49 §1a; n21 §2; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 292, 395] It is the one craft of the period in which the Gazetteer shows the communities of the Virar side working side by side: Hindu Brahmins, Hindu Pachkalshis and Christian descendants of both, in the same villages, at the same booths, in the same season. [n40 A2 Inferences]

Every one of the nine plantain-drying villages lies on the Virar side of the old island, and the notes infer that in 1882 Virar station was their outlet both to Bombay and to Gujarat. [P1; n48 §1 Inferences] The Gazetteer says plainly that the north-bound rail freight from Virar was molasses, rice and betel leaf, and the south-bound freight plantains and betel leaf in “large quantities”. [n46 §8; Gaz XIV p. 316] It does not say which way the dried plantains went. Agashi’s dried plantains were “the best in the district”. [n48 §1]

When the dried-plantain trade ended is not recorded. [n48 §4 Gaps] By 2010 the city development plan still called the coastal belt “famous for Bananas & beetle leaves”, and in the 1980s many growers moved from mogra, betel leaf and velchi bananas to sonchafa flowers for Dadar’s market. [n48 §4] The Gazetteer’s footnote that sonchafa trees were planted among Bassein’s coconut palms in 1882 “because their strong perfume drives off the bees” shows the flower was already an old garden tree when it became a cash crop a century later. [n48 §1, §4 Inferences]

Salt and newcomers

The other industry of the coast changed in these decades too, and brought new people. The Bassein salt works were embanked from the tidal flats, with a reservoir (khajina), brine pits (topavnis) and evaporating pans (kundis); salt-makers worked from January to May for £2 to £5 a season. [n48 §1, §5; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 365–366] In 1876 the salt trade was thrown open to private enterprise, and quality improved. [n21; Gaz XIII-1 p. 370] About the same time “three or four hundred Dubles and Dhodias” were “lately brought from Surat by Mr. Manekji Kharshedji for the manufacture of salt at his pans in Bassein”, producing much better salt than the district’s earlier product. [n48 §1, §5; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 365–366] Manekji Kharshedji’s name marks him as a Parsi; he is one of the very few named entrepreneurs of the Bassein coast in the period, and his Dubla and Dhodia workers from Surat were among the first labour migrants recorded on this coast in the railway age. The census of 1881 counted 114 Dublas in the sub-division. [n21; Gaz XIII-2 p. 680] Where his pans lay the Gazetteer does not say. Bassein salt, with Ghodbandar’s, held about three-quarters of the local trade and went “along the railways” inland. [n21; Gaz XIII-1 p. 370]

The sea’s other gifts

Fish went to Virar’s market from the Kolis’ boats, and fish manure went into its plantain gardens. [n46 §8; n48 §1] The professional fishermen of Bassein in 1882 were “the Uraps and other Christian Kolis”, the mass of the sea-fishers Son Kolis; coastal fishermen as far as Bassein had begun using jolly-boats and outrigger canoes; and the boats lay idle in June and July. [n49 §1f; n48 §1] No catch or boat counts for Bassein appear in the Gazetteer. [n21] Cholera in 1881 “began among the fishermen of Kelva Mahim in April and prevailed in Bassein from May to July”. [n21; Gaz XIII-2, Health] The sea gave and took.

The peoples of Virar in the station era

Who lived here

No source lists the households of Virar in these decades. The peoples of the village and its hinterland can be assembled only from the district record, and the picture is of a meeting place rather than a single community. [n49 §1]

The Kolis and Varlis of the Saturday market are the two peoples the Gazetteer names at Virar itself. [n46 §8] The Kolis were the fishing people of the creeks and the coast; the Varlis (Warlis), 7,308 in the sub-division in 1881, the forest people of the hills to the east, with the Katkaris (1,600) and Thakurs (698). [n21; n49 §1f-bis] The Varlis’ presence at the market fits the Gazetteer’s account of the Nihir Varlis of Bassein and its remark that most of them would not go far from their woods: Virar’s market, a mile from Jivdhan’s forests, was within reach. [n49 §1f; P1]

The Urap Agris were at Virar by name, Christian Agris who had become Hindus in 1820 or 1828 and still bore surnames such as Gomes and Furtado. [n49 §1e; n46 §6] The Sud and Das Agris, who would not marry or eat with them, held the salt lands and the rice flats; Khanivade, to the north-east, was a Dasagri village. [n49 §1e, §3c] The notes’ inference is that the Hindu Agri population of old Virar village included Urap families whose surnames still mirrored Portuguese Christian names. [n49 §1e Inferences]

The Samvedi Brahmins and Pachkalshis held the gardens west of the line. Virar’s Vartakwada carries a Pachkalshi surname, and the notes suggest that Vartakwada and Naringi were wards within the Virar gaothan, the old village settlement; each is a candidate for a Hindu Pachkalshi ward, but no source confirms it. [n40 B11] The Kuparis, Christian Samvedis, lived in the villages from Bolinj and Umrale west to Agashi and Nandakhal; Virar’s Catholics belonged to the Nandakhal parish. [n46 §6; n40 A3] The Bhandaris tapped the palms; a rise in the palm-tree cess from 1877 pushed many of them into farming and labour. [n49 §1f-bis] Kunbis farmed the embanked rice land. [n49 §1f-bis] The Mhars served as village servants, and one of them is the hero of Virar’s only recorded legend. [n46 §8]

The Hindu garden castes

The Hindu Samvedis and Pachkalshis who shared the plantain booths with the Christians left their own marks on these decades. The Samvedis, “on the whole prosperous”, married their daughters before ten, paid bride-prices of Rs 200 to Rs 1,000, held a thread ceremony before the age of ten with a mock “pilgrimage to Benares” in which the maternal uncle promised his daughter, and forbade widow marriage. [n49 §1a; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 82–83] Grierson in 1905 estimated them at about 2,700 and heard in their speech a mixture of Thana Marathi and “vulgar Gujarati”, with genitives in -ho and -ha and the sound ch passing to s and then h. [n34 §4; LSI VII] The Pachkalshis’ Vadval division was counted in 1901 by “an independent agency” at 7,916. Their weddings differed from other Pachkalshis’: the groom’s side asked for the bride’s horoscope, there was no compulsory cash dowry, a sword or dagger was held aloft through the canopy, and widows might remarry; they worshipped both Shiva and Vishnu, with Mahalakshmi, Ekvira, Vajreshwari and Jogeshwari as tutelary goddesses. [n49 §1d; Enthoven III pp. 160–165] Enthoven, publishing in 1922, recorded their claim to Somvanshi Kshatriya rank. [n49 §1d] Among them, too, the railway years opened new paths: the same Vartak surname that marks a ward of Virar would later be borne by notable public figures of Vasai, among them Hari Govindrao Vartak and Bhausaheb Vartak. [n40 B11]

The Kuparis of the Virar villages

Of all the communities around Virar, the Kuparis are the best recorded in these decades, and their villages lay along the road between the station and the sea. The Gazetteer’s seventy-five families of “Native Christians originally Samvedis and Pachkalshis” at Agashi, Vatar and Koprad were, it said, the “gardeners who grow the fruit” that the Hindu Samvedis dried. [n34 §1; Gaz XIII-1 p. 395] Sugar in Bassein was made by “Pachkalshis, Malis, Native Christians and Samvedi Brahmans”. [n34 §5; Gaz XIII-1 p. 395] At Agashi 1,500 of the 6,823 people of 1881 were Christians. [n46 §8] The Gazetteer judged that “the most hardworking and skilful husbandmen are the Christians of Bassein … who grow sugarcane and plantains, and have turned the light sandy country about Bassein into an evergreen garden”. [n49 §1f-bis] The plantains and molasses in Virar’s goods wagons were, in large part, Kupari produce.

Their parishes were the Kupari belt’s institutions. In 1881 the Gazetteer listed nine churches for Bassein under nine priests; three of them, Nirmal (Holy Cross, “St. Cruz”), Koprad (Holy Spirit) and Agashi (St James, “St. Iagos”), served the Samvedi Christian villages. [n34 §3; Gaz XIII-1 p. 206 n. 3] Nirmal’s congregation was 2,184, Koprad’s 2,415; each vicar received a small monthly grant from the British government, and Nirmal and Agashi had small schools whose masters played the violin in church. [n34 §3, §8; Gaz XIV pp. 2, 210, 293] Wealthy families who were village headmen and owners of rich garden lands, the Gazetteer said, “often bring up one or more of their sons for the Church”, and “a considerable number who know English are employed in Bombay chiefly as clerks. The morning trains from Andheri and Bandra are crowded.” The Christians “pride themselves on never taking household service with Europeans”. [n34 §5; Gaz XIII-1 pp. 205–206] That passage describes the Salsette Christians nearer the city more than the Kupari gardeners of the north, whose parish history shows them still choosing farming over English schooling in 1923; but it shows where the railway was leading. [n40 A3]

In 1887, in Bombay, about thirty leaders of the Catholic community met on 26 May to change their designation from “Bombay Portuguese” to “Bombay East-Indian”, founding the Bombay East Indian Association and a weekly paper. The name claimed a century and a half of subjecthood to the East India Company and a place as “sons of the soil”, and drew a line against the Goans. [n34 §7; Mendiratta pp. 234–236] The Catholics of Bassein were included under the new name, labelled “rural” or “samvedi” East Indians; the classification that later counted five groups (Kulbis, Samavedi Christians “commonly called Kuparis”, Koli Christians, Vadvals and Salsette Christians) is from Elsie Baptista’s book of 1967, which the researchers could not read. [n34 §7] In the 1920s the Association complained of “the neglect of education in the districts of Salsette and Bassein” and “the paucity of vocations to the priesthood among the community”, and the Bassein villages stayed under the Portuguese Padroado while urban East Indians moved to the new Archdiocese of Bombay. [n34 §7; Mendiratta p. 236] By 1925 Bassein had twelve parishes, against nine in 1881. [n34 §6] Nandakhal’s first local-born priest, ordained in 1943, answered the Association’s complaint two decades late. [n40 A3]

The Kuparis were a people with a name among themselves before they had one in print. Grierson’s Linguistic Survey of 1905 still counted the Hindu Samvedis (about 2,700) and the Christians’ “Kiristav” Marathi as separate entries; no census ever counted the Samvedi Christians as a group; and the word “Kupari” appears in no printed source the researchers found before the late twentieth century. [n34 §2, §4, §8] Their dress, the red cap and the red-and-checked lugda remembered as community markers, would survive into the twentieth century and fade only later. [n34 §8]

There were small numbers of others. Muslims were 2,292 in the sub-division; at Agashi there were 155, and the Naitas traded at Sopara; the saints’ tomb at Arnala was endowed. [n21; n49 §1h] Jains and Vanis were 80 and 880 in the sub-division; the Jain temple at Agashi had been built by a Bombay Vani about 1820, and Gujarati Lad Vanis had settled at Sopara about 1760. [n21; n49 §2a; n40 A15] Parsis numbered 27, among them presumably the salt-pan owner Manekji Kharshedji or his agents. [n21; n48 §1] Dublas and Dhodias from Surat worked the salt pans. [n48 §5] The Chitpavan and Deshastha Brahmins were present in Thana district in numbers (8,722 and 1,006 in 1872), but the notes find no village-level data and infer that their presence at Virar proper belongs to a later “railway-and-office era migration”. [n49 §1c Gaps]

Who did not yet live here

Two groups who would dominate Virar’s later population are absent from every source for this period. Gujarati speakers, who made up about 28 per cent of Virar in the twenty-first century by Wikipedia’s unsourced figures, and Hindi speakers from the north, about 15 per cent, have no recorded presence at Virar before 1947 beyond the Gujarati Vanis of Sopara and Agashi. [n49 §1h; n40 B11] The notes found no source on when Gujarati, Marwari or north Indian families arrived, or on any Virar mosque’s founding. [n49 §1h Gaps] The great migrations into Virar are the subject of Chapter 29.

Disease, decline and the missing census

Cholera, plague and influenza

The station decades were years of epidemic. Cholera was absent from the district only in 1873 and 1874; it was in Mahim and Bassein in February 1878; in 1879 it began in April in Bassein and lasted to the end of the year; in 1881 it prevailed in Bassein from May to July. [n21; Gaz XIII-2, Health] In 1914, the parish history of Gokhivare records, that village east of Vasai Road was “severely affected by an epidemic of cholera”, and its people vowed a chapel to St Roque. [n40 B8; EI Gokhivare]

Plague reached the Bombay Presidency in 1896. The 1901 census report has a section on famine and plague, but the Thana and Bassein figures could not be read in the OCR; the report says that Thana district’s population fell by only about one per cent against its estimate, the least among the districts. [n21; Census 1901 Vol IX Pt I] Bassein’s own plague deaths are not known. [n21] The influenza of 1918 struck Bombay City, Kolaba and Poona first, then Thana. The 1921 census reconstructed the loss for Thana with Bombay Suburban: 912,756 people recorded in 1921, 36,642 influenza deaths added back, a net reconstructed population of 949,398, against 882,309 in 1911. [n21; Census 1921 Vol VIII Pt I p. 25] No taluka figure was found.

The taluka counts

The census of 1911 counted Bassein taluka at about 225 square miles, two towns and 90 villages, 15,328 occupied houses and 79,978 people (40,978 males), against about 80,251 in 1901; the population had barely moved in a decade of plague. [n21; Census 1911 Provincial Table I] The 1921 report described Bassein, with Bhiwandi, as a town “outside the suburbanisation zone” with no marked increase; but it also excluded Thana from one of its analyses because the talukas of North Salsette, Kalyan and Bassein “have come under the influence of suburbanisation”. [n21] That phrase, in an official report of 1921, is the first recognition that the railway was turning the Bassein coast into a suburb of Bombay.

In 1931 the census gave Bassein taluka an area of 209 square miles, but the OCR lost the population. It gave the Bassein municipality 12,689 people (6,640 males, 6,049 females). [n21; Census 1931 Vol VIII Pt II] In 1951 the taluka held 133,523 people on 203 square miles, of whom 41,130 were urban. [n21; Census 1951] Between 1911 and 1951 the taluka grew by about two-thirds, though the boundaries changed between counts and the figures may not be strictly comparable. [n21 Inferences]

A series that is not Virar’s

A web table labelled “Vasai-Virar Historical Population”, which cites the 2011 census, gives what looks like a series for Virar’s region: 10,702 in 1901; 9,598 in 1911; 10,366 in 1921; 12,689 in 1931; 13,969 in 1941; 26,873 in 1951; and so on up to the 2011 city total of 1,222,390. [n46 §10; findeasy.in] The researchers rejected it as a series for Vasai-Virar: a unit of 10,702 people in 1901 cannot be the whole area, whose taluka held about 80,000. [n46 §10] One figure in it identifies the unit. The 1931 entry, 12,689, is exactly the population of the Bassein municipality in the 1931 census tables. [n21; n46 §10] The 1881 Gazetteer gave Bassein town 10,356 people and the 1872 census 9,356, both close to the table’s 10,702 for 1901. [n21] The table is almost certainly a series for Bassein (Vasai) town, relabelled. That is this chapter’s inference, and it means that no census figure for Virar exists anywhere in the notes before 1981, when the Tata Institute’s history of the region gives “23,303 people” for Virar. [n46 §10; TIFR] How many people lived at Virar in 1881, 1911 or 1941 is simply not known.

Unrest in the hills, 1897–98

One episode of these decades shows the forest people of the hills and the coast towns pulling against the state. In 1897–98 disturbances spread through Thana district. The Commissioner who put them down, A. C. Logan, wrote that the grievance at first “was confined to a few hundred forest people, who at great damage to the forests supplied the Brahmin and other wealthy inhabitants of Bassein and other coast villages with cheap firewood”. When the trade was banned, the forest people “defied the interdict, overpowered the forest guards and brought their loads to Bassein with riotous demonstrations”. The unrest spread: Kolis “rose to the cry of free liquor and free salt”, the Agris turned “turbulent”, a mob at Mahim attacked the treasury and was fired on, and “a few” were killed; the “Native Christians who shared the Koli discontent at the price of liquor emulated them”. [n43 §1; SM-II pp. 636–637] Logan’s is a colonial official’s account, and his reading of the forest people’s “Brahmin allies” is his. [n43 §1] The notes do not place any event at Virar. But the firewood that the forest people carried to the coast came down from hills like Jivdhan’s, and the Kolis, Agris and Christians who joined the protest were Virar’s market-goers. The grievances were about forests, liquor and salt, not about national independence, and they should be called that. [n43 §1 Inferences]

Churches, temples and schools

What is known for Virar itself

The institutional record of Virar itself between 1864 and 1947 is almost empty. The notes found no dated school at Virar before 1947; the schools they found (St Augustine’s, 1972; N. G. Vartak High School, 1986; Annie Besant, 1998) all came later. [n46 §12] They found no Virar parish founded before 1947: the Catholic centres of Virar East and Virar West were later cut from Nandakhal, and the Syro-Malabar church of St Thomas held its first Mass only on 23 July 1989. [n46 §12; n49 §2b] They found no founding date for any temple at Virar town, Hindu, Jain or other, before 1947, apart from the undated shrine on Jivdhan; a Marathi search for Virar’s Hanuman, Datta or Vithoba temples and for the histories of its Brahmin and Vartak associations reached nothing. [n46 §12 Gaps] The heritage survey of 2008 lists Virar’s talavs (Naringi, Vartakwada, Totale, Manvelpada, Ranele) and a Naringi Zilla Parishad school, without dates. [n40 B11; CRIT Vol I]

So the story of faith and learning around Virar in these decades has to be told from the villages around it, which is where its people went to church, to temple and to school.

The Nandakhal parish

Virar’s Catholics belonged to the parish of the Holy Spirit at Nandakhal, the old Franciscan church between Agashi and Nirmal, which by its own tradition had escaped the destruction of 1739. [n46 §6; n40 A3] Its twentieth-century history, as told in a Wikipedia article based on a 2016 parish document (and flagged there as needing citations), runs through these decades. In 1907, “following a government recommendation”, the Collector granted forest timber free to the church from the Shirsat forest. In 1908 Dinda Kurel donated a large bell. In 1910 four metal pillars were added for a new upper floor, and in 1920 an “Alpari” section was built. In 1922 Mgr Francesco De Silva cleared land, built a compound wall and planted coconuts; in 1923 he opened an English-medium school, which later closed for want of pupils, the parishioners saying, in the parish’s account, that “we get bread and butter from farming and not by studying”. Marathi schools began in 1935, and in 1939 Fr Raymond Mendes founded St Joseph’s Marathi school. On 5 January 1943 Fr Frank Lobo was ordained, the first priest born in the parish, “after 370 years”. [n46 §12; n40 A3; Wikipedia: Holy Spirit Church Nandakhal] The parish’s own website gives other dates for its founding (1557 for the arrival of Christianity, “officially established in 1858”, “formally established as a parish in 1886”), which conflict with the 1573 of the Wikipedia article. [n46 §6; n49 §2b] The heritage survey dates renovations of the church to 1910 and a porch to 1930. [n40 A3; CRIT Vol III]

The parish story gives a rare glimpse of attitudes in the Kupari villages near Virar. A school in English was offered in 1923, and the farming families let it close, because their living came from the land. Twelve years later they accepted schools in Marathi. Twenty years after that, one of their sons became a priest. The first local vocation in three and a half centuries came in 1943, four years before independence. [n40 A3]

Arnala’s chapel

At Arnala, the Koli Christians of the fort village had belonged to Agashi’s parish. In 1919 Fr Ismail Da Costa, assistant at Agashi, built a hut chapel there; in 1921 a tin shed dedicated to St Peter went up with the Kolis’ help; and on 27 December 1931 Archbishop Joaquim Lima blessed the church. A Hindu community association gave Rs 3,000 towards it, and “Kolis consider Fr Da Costa as the true Apostle of Arnala”. [n40 C1; EI Arnala; CRIT Vol III] The source is a community calendar, and the Hindu gift is its claim; but it is the clearest sign in the notes of Hindus and Christians on the Virar coast cooperating over a place of worship in these decades.

Other parishes nearby

Further south the old Bassein parishes divided in the same years. At Gokhivare, after the cholera of 1914, two hundred villagers built a chapel of forty feet by eighteen, blessed on 30 June 1930, and it became a parish in 1943. [n40 B8] At Giriz the foundation stone was laid on 14 February 1917, and the villagers finished the church “without foreign aid” a year later. [n40 B9] Gass, the southern Kupari village of the Sopara agar, petitioned for a parish in 1941 and got one in 1942; Bhuigaon became an independent parish on 2 June 1946. [n40 A4, A6] At Agashi, the parish of St James was extended by Fr Jovito V. Dias in 1900, and an English primary school, “Holy Martyrs”, opened in 1938, named after the orphans of 1540. [n40 A2; EI Agashi] The Catholic villages around Virar were reorganising themselves into smaller, closer parishes, building with their own money, and opening schools, in the decades when the railway was binding them to Bombay.

Temples, libraries and schools

The Hindu record nearby is thinner but real. At Agashi the Garde family built a private Ram Mandir in 1885, by a 2019 heritage blog; a public library, the Sarvajanik Vachnalay, was founded in 1914 by Seth Prabhudas Bhagvandas, and its present building dates from 1939; and a Zilla Parishad school opened in 1886. [n49 §2a; n40 A2; CRIT Vol I; Heritage Chronicles] The heritage survey dates other village schools of the taluka: Juchandra in 1872, Bolinj in 1879, Rangaon in 1917. [n40 A11, B10, C2; CRIT Vol I] The Samvedi community’s tradition remembers a visit by the Shankaracharya in 1926, commemorated by a large yajna; it comes from a community blog and is not otherwise attested. [n49 §1a-bis]

On Jivdhan, somewhere in these decades, the cave shrine of 1882 probably acquired its first built temple. [n45 §1 Inferences] No document records it.

Platforms and wires

The 1913 remodel

The railway record for Virar between 1882 and 1947 is the fullest record the village has, and it is still thin. The Wikipedia article on the station, quoting the BB&CI Magazine of December 1923, says that Virar station “was remodeled alongside Borivali after or in 1913”, with a Down platform and an Island platform, and two signal cabins, A and B. [n46 §9; n45 §2] “After or in 1913” is the article’s own hedge. Two platforms and two cabins describe a station built to turn trains round: an island platform between the tracks for the terminating locals, and a separate platform for the through line. The railway company’s own record of the 1913 works was not found. [n46 §9 Gaps]

Electric trains

On 5 January 1928 the first electric suburban trains, running on direct current, began on the Western line between Colaba and Borivali; the railway enthusiasts’ archive IRFCA gives 1 May for the inauguration, so even the day is disputed. [n46 §9; n45 §2] Virar was not yet included. By 1936, according to Wikipedia’s article on the line, “all four lines between Bandra and Borivali and two lines between Borivali to Virar were electrified, thus eliminating steam locomotives”; IRFCA says the Borivali–Virar section was “finished in 1936”. [n46 §9] These dates were read through search summaries rather than the full pages, and no source gives the day of the first electric train at Virar. [n46 §9] A community website says electric commuter trains reached Virar after the electrification of 1927 on the other railway, the Great Indian Peninsula; that date belongs to the other line and should not be used for Virar. [P1; n05]

What the electrification meant is clear enough. From about 1936 Virar was the northern terminus of Bombay’s electric suburban railway, sixty kilometres from Churchgate, and it would remain so until 2013. [n46 §9; P1] The notes infer that Virar’s role as the suburban terminus from 1867 through the 1930s is why the station area, with its market and shops, grew between 1864 and 1936 while the older village west of the line remained a village. [n46 §9 Inferences] The two-sided Virar of 1882, station and market on the east, old village on the west, with the metalled road to Agashi, already matched the later division into Virar East and Virar West. [n46 §8 Inferences]

Toward freedom

The district in the national movement

Thana district was not a centre of the national movement, and the police abstracts of 1930 call it one of the districts with “comparatively little agitation”. [n43 §3; SM-XII] But the government’s own source books, read in full for this book, show the Bassein taluka taking part in four documented ways, and Virar in one. [n43]

The first was the Khilafat and Non-Cooperation period. On 16 December 1919 a Caliphate Committee meeting at Hati Mahal Street in Bassein, attended by about 250 people (thirty Hindus, six Bohras and the rest Muslims), censured the Bassein municipality for “wasting money on the Peace Celebrations” and marched with two black flags; on 1 February 1920 a joint Hindu-Muslim meeting at Bassein under D. K. Phadke, district pleader, adopted the Amritsar Khilafat resolutions. [n43 §2; SM-X p. 24] On 6–8 May 1921 Bassein hosted the first Maharashtra Provincial Conference, presided over by Dr B. S. Moonje, with an audience of 1,000 to 1,700, “for the most part Hindus”. Gandhi arrived on 7 May, spoke on the Mulshi Peta satyagraha, condemned the violence at Malegaon and criticised Maharashtra for carrying on non-cooperation “in a half-hearted manner”; about Rs 2,000 was collected on the spot. His speech began: “Your love has drawn me here, I am sorry I cannot stay in your midst for long.” He came by train from Surat in the night and left for Bombay the same evening. [n43 §2; SM-IX pp. 91–93] Whether his train stopped at Virar is not recorded; the reports say only that he arrived at Bassein. [n43 §2 Gaps] In July 1921 the Collector estimated Bassein’s contribution to the Tilak Swaraj Fund at Rs 1,500, a modest sum beside Bhiwandi’s and Umbergaon’s Rs 4,000 each. [n43 §2; SM-IX p. 106]

1930: salt, and three thousand at Virar

The Civil Disobedience movement of 1930 brought Virar its one documented political event. A secondary summary of the 1982 Thane District Gazetteer, which the researchers could not open in full, says that the Thane District Congress Committee opened its satyagraha centre at Arnala, where leaders sold salt they had made; that at Virar a meeting on 15 April, chaired by D. Gokhale, called for breaking the salt laws and seventy-five people took part; that officials posted at Arnala were boycotted; and that “at various places, viz., Palghar, Vasai, Bhiwandi, etc. people broke the salt law in large numbers”. [n43 §3; n46 §11] The heritage survey of 2008 also says that the Salt and Forest Satyagraha of 1929–30 was centred on Arnala, where a Gandhi memorial now stands. [n40 C1; CRIT Vol I] These April events are reported, not established; the government volume that would cover them, Source Material Volume XI, has only its front matter in the digitised text, and the Arnala leaders’ names were not given. [n43 §3 Gaps]

The September event is in a primary record. The Bombay Police Abstract of Intelligence for the week ending 20 September 1930, reprinted in the government’s Source Material Volume XII, reads: “Thana. Week ending September 20. There was a gathering of 3,000 persons at Virar to receive Jamnadas M. Mehta after his release from jail. Audiences at other meetings were below 500.” [n43 §3; n46 §11; SM-XII p. 245] Jamnadas Mehta was a Bombay labour and Congress leader; the report does not say why he had been jailed. [n43 §3] Three thousand people at a village station was a large crowd, six times the size of any other meeting in the district that week. The notes do not say where the crowd stood, but a leader arriving by train would have been received at the station, and the obvious open ground nearby was the market by the pond. That is an inference. What is documented is the number, the week and the man.

The abstracts then record the movement continuing round Virar through the autumn. A Thana District Liquor Prohibition Mandal was formed on 31 October and “started work in Bassein taluka”. In the week ending 15 November, prohibition was being pushed “in the villages in Bassein taluka from Sopara as centre”. In the week ending 29 November, “there are rumours of a mass raid on salt works by the people of Juchandra in Bassein taluka”. In the week ending 27 December, six of fourteen meetings concerned “remission of land revenue and a general revision of the recently increased land assessment”, and “Bassein taluka is threatened with a week of prohibition propaganda”. [n43 §3; n46 §11; SM-XII pp. 251–259] The same abstracts thought that “Enthusiasm for the Congress appears to be definitely on the wane” by November. [n43 §3] The Juchandra raid is a police rumour, not a recorded event. [n43 §3]

Put together, the record gives a modest but real picture, centred on the Virar–Sopara–Arnala coast: salt made and sold at Arnala, a salt meeting at Virar in April (by a secondary summary), a crowd of three thousand at Virar in September, and prohibition and revenue work run from Sopara through the winter. [n43 §3 Inferences] The land-revenue grievance ties the garden farmers of the belt to the national agitation. The prohibition campaign struck at the toddy and palm-spirit trade of the Bhandaris and the drinking of the Kolis and Christians, the same grievance that had brought them out in 1897 from the other side. [n43 §1, §3]

1942

The second Civil Disobedience of 1932 left no Bassein entry that the researchers found. [n43 §3 Gaps] In January 1942 Congress held a meeting of 1,500 at Bassein, and by March “the People’s Volunteer Corps at Bassein, Thana district, is gaining ground and some 500 volunteers have been enrolled”; this was a civil-defence drive during the Japanese advance, not a Quit India force. [n43 §4; SM-IV pp. 9, 23] After the Quit India resolution of 8 August 1942, the Congress was banned and the weekly digests fall silent on Bassein until 1944. [n43 §4]

The Quit India movement brought one recorded death to the taluka. The ICHR Dictionary of Martyrs lists Bal Samant, born in 1927 in Vasai village, a labourer, who “received severe bullet wounds in the police firing on 9 September 1942 at Chowk Bazar, Vasai, while participating in a rally that was fired upon by the British police and died on the same day”. He was fifteen. The entry rests on a single government listing, and no other source for him or for the firing was found. [n43 §4; DoM-3 p. 23] Five people were killed at Palghar on 14 August 1942. [n43 §4] For Virar itself there is nothing: no record of a 1942 meeting, an arrest, a strike, railway sabotage at the station or an underground worker. A search for “Bassein Road 1942 sabotage” found only station histories. [n43 §4; n46 §11]

The last years

In June 1945, at a meeting of about 250 at Bassein, the Marathi writer and activist Sane Guruji said that innocent people had been killed in 1942 and urged his audience to join the Rashtra Seva Dal, and spoke the next day to students of the New English School, Bassein. In January 1946 he returned on a tour of Thana district and at Bassein defended the Congress’s refusal to withdraw the August resolution. [n43 §4; SM-IV pp. 130–131, 143] The Warli revolt and the Kisan Sabha’s organising of 1945–47 took place in Dahanu, Talasari, Umbergaon and from late 1946 Palghar; no source puts them in Vasai taluka. [n43 §6] And on 15 August 1947, the day of independence, no source in English or Marathi records anything at Vasai or Virar at all. [n43 §6]

What is not known

This chapter has drawn on the richest single source Virar has, the Gazetteer of 1882, and on railway, census and police records around it. The gaps are large and should be stated plainly. [n46 §13]

People. There is no census figure for Virar village or town for any year from 1872 to 1941. The Gazetteers give sub-division totals and figures for Agashi and Sopara only; the web series often quoted is Bassein town’s. [n46 §10] The village tables of the census, and the 1951 District Census Handbook for Thana, which gives village populations from 1901, were not reached. [n46 §10 Gaps] Almost no resident of Virar between 1864 and 1947 is known by name.

Institutions. There is no dated school, church, mosque or temple at Virar itself before 1947. [n46 §12] The founding of the hilltop temple on Jivdhan, the building of the steps and the formation of the trust are undated. [n45 §3] Whether Virar had a municipality or a village panchayat in these decades, and when its later municipal council was founded, is not recorded in the notes. [n45 §6 Gaps]

The railway. The company’s opening notice, the record of the 1913 works and the date of the first electric train at Virar were not found; the 1867 date rests on a 1923 company magazine quoted by Wikipedia and a newspaper. [n46 §9 Gaps] The date when Nallasopara got its station remains open.

Politics. The April 1930 salt events at Virar and Arnala rest on a secondary summary. Nothing is known of Virar in 1942. The Arnala leaders of 1930 are unnamed. [n43]

The objects. Whether the Portuguese church ruin, the well, the tower, the cow-and-calf grant stone, the carved stone by the lake and the cow’s feet in the rock survived the twentieth century is unknown. [n46]

The end of the line

By 1947 Virar had been a railway terminus for eighty years. It had a station with an island platform and two signal cabins, electric trains that turned there and went back to Churchgate, a market by the pond where the Gazetteer had seen Kolis and Varlis, a road to Agashi, a goddess on the hill whose cave had probably become a temple, and an old village west of the tracks whose Catholics went to Nandakhal to Mass. It was still, by every sign, a village and not a town: the census did not count it, and its first secondary school was a generation away.

The Bassein taluka around it held 133,523 people in 1951. [n21] In 1981 Virar alone would hold 23,303, and in 2001, by one official count, 118,945. [n46 §10; P1; n47] The next chapter tells how the village at the end of the line became one of the largest commuter towns in India.
