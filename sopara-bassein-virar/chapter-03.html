Part Two — The First Coast

A core from the outer shelf

Seventy-five metres down, on the floor of the Arabian Sea far out from Bombay, a team of marine scientists drove a tube into the sediment and pulled up a column of mud 26.5 metres long. It is not much to look at: grey-brown layers packed with tiny rounded grains called ooids and with the shells of foraminifera, single-celled sea creatures no bigger than a grain of sand. But the ooids form only in warm, shallow, agitated water, and the foraminifera in the core were of kinds that live in shallow water. They were lying, in other words, in a place where shallow-water creatures could not possibly live today. The sea above them had once been only a few metres deep. [n24; Nigam et al. 1992]

The scientists, Rajiv Nigam, N. H. Hashimi, Menezes and Wagh, published their reading in Current Science in 1992 under the title “Fluctuating sea levels off Bombay (India) between 14,500 and 10,000 years before present”. About 14,500 years ago, they concluded, the sea off Bombay stood about 101.5 metres below where it stands now. From there it rose, with two minor pauses, at about 13,000 and about 11,500 years ago, and it had largely risen before 10,000 years ago. [n24; Nigam et al. 1992]

That core is the nearest thing this coast has to a diary of the end of the ice age. It was not taken at Vasai, and it does not mention Sopara. It is the work of the National Institute of Oceanography, and the book knows it only through an abstract and a catalogue record, not from the full paper. But it is a measurement made on this stretch of shelf, off the city that lies a few hours’ walk south of Vasai Creek, and it tells what the last chapter could only infer from global numbers: that within the span of human memory, if humans had kept written memories then, the sea here lay a hundred metres lower than today, and that it came back in surges and pauses. [n24; Takeaway; Nigam et al. 1992]

Everything in this chapter happens on that moving shore. It begins about twenty thousand years ago, at the coldest point of the last ice age, when the coastline lay far to the west on what is now sea floor. It ends about five thousand years ago, when the sea had come back to the foot of the hills, flooded the river valleys into creeks, filled them with mangrove mud and drawn, for the first time, the outline of the tidal island on which Sopara would one day stand. In between, people lived on this coast: hunters and gatherers who made tiny stone blades and left them on the low hills of Salsette, within sight of the mouth of Vasai Creek. They are the first people of this story whose traces we can hold in our hands. They left no names, no graves and no dates. This chapter tries to tell what can honestly be said about them, and where the honest answer is that nobody knows.

A note on the evidence, which matters more in this chapter than in any other. Almost nothing here comes from Vasai itself. The sea-level data come from cores and corals on the shelf off Bombay and Ratnagiri. The stone tools come from Kandivli, Madh, Erangal, Manori, Marve and Padan, on Salsette Island, fifteen to thirty-five kilometres south of Sopara. The genetic evidence comes from Warli and Kokna people sampled at Jawhar, in the hills of Palghar district. Where a statement rests on evidence from elsewhere, the book says regional. Where it rests on something found inside Vasai taluka, it says Vasai-specific. For the twenty thousand years this chapter covers, the Vasai-specific evidence can be counted on one hand, and none of it is a human trace. [n24; Status and labels] [n01; Inferences]

The world at the glacial maximum

The last chapter followed the ice age from the outside. Global sea level, as Lambeck and colleagues reconstructed it in 2014, fell rapidly by about forty metres near 30,000 years ago and then slowly to about 134 metres below present between about 29,000 and 21,000 years ago. Yokoyama and colleagues, in 2018, put the lowest point of the Last Glacial Maximum, the LGM, at about 125 to 130 metres below present, around 20,500 years ago. Older estimates range from 105 to 163 metres, many of them near 120. [n23; Clark and Tarasov commentary, PMC4234583; Yokoyama et al. 2018]

These are global figures, averaged from many coasts and corrected for the way the earth’s crust sags and rebounds under the weight of ice and water. The Bombay shelf has its own figures, and they are not quite the same. The NIO’s own synthesis, Hashimi, Nigam, Nair and Rajagopalan’s “Holocene sea level fluctuations on western Indian continental margin: an update” of 1995, drew a curve with a low near minus 100 metres at about 14,500 years ago and about minus 80 metres at about 12,000 years ago. [n24; Hashimi et al. 1995] There is no local measurement for the glacial maximum itself: Nigam’s core starts at 14,500 years ago, and no published reconstruction of the LGM shoreline for the Mumbai shelf was found. The figures from Pandarinath and from Hashimi give a gradient, not a line on a map. [n24; Gaps]

So the picture of the coast at twenty thousand years ago is an inference, and it should be called one. It runs like this. The continental shelf off western India is unusually wide, about 280 kilometres from Bombay to Ratnagiri and wider to the north, and its outer edge, the shelf break where the shallow floor drops into the deep, lies at about 90 to 120 metres down (other sources say 90 to 200). If the sea stood a hundred metres or more below its present level, most of that shelf was dry land, and the shore lay near its outer edge, on the order of a hundred kilometres or more west of where Arnala and Kalamb beaches are now. [n23; LGM, Konkan shelf] [n14; J. Micropalaeontology 24:39; J. Coastal Research; Inferences]

On that dry shelf, the features that now define Vasai did not exist. There was no Vasai Creek and no tidal Vaitarna estuary. The site of Sopara was not by the sea at all. It was inland, on a low ridge in front of the hills, and the rivers that now end in creeks, the Vaitarna in the north and the Ulhas in the south, ran on westward across a broad plain to a distant shore. [n23; Inferences] Salsette was not an island. Kandivli, where hand-axes and microliths would later be picked out of the clay, stood on the same continuous land as Sopara and Virar. [n01; Inferences]

The research found almost nothing about what that plain looked like: what grew on it, how much rain fell, what animals crossed it. The notes do record, from a review of Konkan mangrove and monsoon studies, that the early Holocene, after about 11,000 years ago, had a dry phase followed by stronger rain, which implies (though the source does not say so for the glacial maximum) that the monsoon of the ice age was not the monsoon of today. [n24; Frontiers in Earth Science 2021 review] The large mammals of Pleistocene India, the straight-tusked elephant Palaeoloxodon namadicus, the Indian aurochs Bos namadicus, the hippopotamus Hexaprotodon, are known from river valleys far inland, the Narmada, the Purna, the Manjra at Latur, and not from this coast. Whether they ever walked the plain west of Vasai is plausible but undocumented. [n23; Turvey et al. 2021; Latur radiocarbon paper]

What can be said is that the land was there, a great deal more of it than now, and that it was waiting to be drowned.

Reading the drowned shelf

Geologists have tried to read the history of the shelf from its shape, and the shape is complicated.

On the western shelf there are submerged terraces, flat steps in the sea floor, between about 50 and 115 metres down, described by Wagle and colleagues in Marine Geology in 1994. Along the outer shelf there is a barrier reef system, a line of old reefs at 85 to 136 metres, described by Vora, Wagle and colleagues in 1996 and interpreted as having formed during the rise of the sea after the glacial maximum. [n24; Wagle et al. 1994; Vora, Wagle et al. 1996] An older paper lists terraces between Bombay and Karwar at about 92, 85, 75 and 55 metres, perhaps marking places where the sea paused long enough to cut a shoreline, but warns that an unequivocal chronology for them is “not possible at present”, partly because the coast may have moved. [n23; Springer, J. Geol. Soc. India] [n14; Springer]

That warning is the key to the whole problem. The western margin of India is not perfectly stable. Rao and Veerayya, in 1996, inferred that a terrace now at about 130 metres has subsided by about forty metres since about 12,000 years ago. If so, a shoreline that formed at ninety metres below sea level now lies at a hundred and thirty, and any attempt to read sea level directly off the depth of a terrace or a reef will be wrong by the amount the crust has sunk. Relative sea level on this margin, the notes conclude, is affected by local tectonics. [n24; Rao & Veerayya 1996; Inferences]

The same problem appears in the corals. In the early 1970s the radiocarbon laboratory of the Tata Institute of Fundamental Research (TIFR) in Bombay, run by D. P. Agrawal and Sheela Kusumgar, dated a series of samples that the Archaeological Survey of India printed in its annual review, Indian Archaeology 1971-72 – A Review, in a list of radiocarbon dates from Maharashtra. One sample, laboratory number TF-970, was coral from the continental shelf off Bombay, taken at a depth of 150 metres. It gave an age of 11,035 ± 400 years before present, which the list rendered as 9085 BC. Another, TF-1200, was coral from the shelf off Ratnagiri, at a depth of 88 fathoms, about 160 metres, and gave 10,415 ± 260 BP, or 8465 BC. [n24; IAR 1971-72, pp. 83–84]

Corals that build reefs grow in shallow, sunlit water. A coral living 11,000 years ago at a place now 150 metres deep should mean that sea level then was well over a hundred metres lower. But Nigam’s core says that by about 11,000 years ago the sea off Bombay was already rising past the minus-80-metre mark and was largely risen by 10,000. The two cannot both be read at face value. Either the coral was not growing where it was found, having been broken off and carried down the slope, or the shelf has subsided since, as Rao and Veerayya proposed. The notes treat the coral as showing that “the shelf was being drowned at the Pleistocene-Holocene boundary”, and they caution that mapping shelf depth to age “must be treated cautiously”. [n24; Takeaway; Inferences]

A third piece of evidence comes from a summary by S. N. Rajaguru and Sushama Ghate of Deccan College, Pune, titled “Quaternary sea levels and archaeological sites in coastal parts of Western India”. They report shelf carbonate dated to 9,000 to 10,000 years ago about 180 metres below sea level, roughly a hundred kilometres west of Bombay (citing Hashimi), and say it “clearly show[s]” a low sea level at the end of the Pleistocene, with sea level reaching about the present level around 6,000 years ago (citing Kale and Rajaguru 1986). [n24; Rajaguru & Ghate] Again the depth is far greater than the age suggests, and again the explanation is probably subsidence, reworking, or both.

None of this undermines the main story, which every source agrees on: the shelf was dry land at the height of the ice age, and the sea took it back between about 15,000 and 6,000 years ago. What it undermines is precision. Anyone who tells you exactly where the shore off Vasai stood on a given date at the end of the ice age is telling you more than the evidence knows.

The sea comes back in steps

The rising sea did not come smoothly. Nigam’s core shows two minor pauses, at about 13,000 and about 11,500 years ago, when the rise slowed or stalled. [n24; Nigam et al. 1992] Globally, the warming that began the end of the ice age came with a great surge of meltwater, “Meltwater Pulse 1A”, which began about 14,200 years ago by Peltier’s chronology, or about 14,600 by others, and raised sea level fast. [n23; Peltier 2005] To the north, the Gulf of Kachchh was flooded by at least 15,000 years ago. [n14; IAS repository] [n23; IAS repository] Neither of those global events has a documented local signature on the Konkan, but the Bombay core’s numbers, from 101.5 metres down at 14,500 years ago to nearly risen by 10,000, describe a rise of something like ninety metres in four or five thousand years. On a shelf as gently sloping as this one, that is a coastline advancing, on average, by several kilometres a century, faster in the surges and slower in the pauses. [n24; Inferences]

For anyone living on that plain, the effect would have been visible within a lifetime: a familiar beach drowned, a fishing ground moved inland, a creek mouth where a river valley had been. No one recorded it. But the people of this coast, as the end of this chapter will show, kept a story about the sea and the land that it is hard not to read alongside these numbers.

The Bombay core also connects to an older record from closer to home. In 1984 the geomorphologists V. S. Kale, J. N. Kshirsagar and S. N. Rajaguru published “Late-Pleistocene beach rock from Uran, Maharashtra” in Current Science. Uran lies across Bombay Harbour from the city, on the rim of Thane Creek, and its beach rock, sand cemented into stone on an ancient shore, is the only dated Pleistocene coastal deposit on the Thane Creek and Bombay Harbour rim that the research found. [n24; Kale, Kshirsagar & Rajaguru 1984] A 2022 overview of Late Quaternary studies of the Konkan coast, by Gawali, Hanamgond, Sangode, Herlekar and colleagues, summarises that paper and others as cataloguing several advances and retreats of the sea on the Maharashtra coast: a low sea level 30,000 to 35,000 years ago, a rise after 30,000, a fall again, a rise back about 15,000 years ago, and a maximum near the middle of the Holocene. [n24; Gawali et al. 2022, pp. 146–147] The book has not read the 1984 paper itself, and its dates are not known to the research. Uran is still the nearest named place where the ice-age shore left a rock behind.

The Holocene begins

About 11,700 years ago, by the standard division, the Pleistocene ended and the Holocene began: the warm epoch in which all of recorded history has happened. [n23; Gaps] On the Konkan, its first millennia were a time of rising water and returning rain.

The sea was still low at the start. Pandarinath and colleagues, in 2001, found low Arabian Sea levels in the latest Pleistocene and early Holocene, between about 10,760 ± 130 and 9,280 ± 150 years ago, followed by a transgression, the sea advancing over the land, and then stabilisation at present levels after about 6,400 years ago. [n24; Gawali et al. 2022, p. 146]

The rain came back too. A 2021 review in Frontiers in Earth Science gathers what is known about the south-west monsoon from records along the west coast. It reports, from a 2017 study by Sandeep and colleagues, a dry phase between about 11,100 and 10,700 years ago; stronger rain from 10,700 to 8,600; an abrupt weakening at about 8,200 years ago; a decreasing trend from 8,600 to 4,500; and fluctuating, decreasing rain after 4,500. A Kerala record shows intense rainfall near 6,500 years ago. [n24; Frontiers in Earth Science 2021 review] These are regional records, mostly from farther south, and the review itself is a second-hand summary. But they agree on the broad shape: the early and middle Holocene was wetter than the late Holocene, and the monsoon that soaks the Konkan every June was strong in the millennia when the sea was coming back.

And as the sea rose, a new kind of forest grew at its edge.

Mangroves are trees that live in salt water, rooted in tidal mud, flooded twice a day. Along the west coast of India, from Kerala up to the Konkan, the remains of mangrove swamps lie buried in the sediments as dark organic layers, and because mangroves grow only between the tides, those layers mark where the shore was when they grew. K. P. N. Kumaran and colleagues, in a 2005 paper in Quaternary Research, dated such mangrove layers along the west coast from about 10,760 to 4,540 radiocarbon years before present: from the very end of the ice age to the middle of the Holocene. [n24; Kumaran et al. 2005] [n23; Kumaran et al.] [n01; Kumaran et al.] As the sea climbed into each river valley, the mangroves climbed with it, colonising every new stretch of mud.

The sea reaches the hills

At some point in the middle Holocene the rise stopped. When, and how high the sea came, are two of the most important questions for this coast, and the answers are not settled.

Rajaguru and Ghate put sea level “at about the present level around 6000 BP”, citing Kale and Rajaguru 1986. [n24; Rajaguru & Ghate] Pandarinath’s study, as above, has it stabilising after about 6,400 years ago. [n24; Gawali et al. 2022] One record cited in the 2022 Konkan overview has the sea “steady from 6 ka BP to the present”, but the same overview reports a rise of one to six metres inferred between 6,000 and 2,000 years ago. [n24; Gawali et al. 2022, p. 146]

There is a widely quoted figure for the highest point. The geologist S. S. Merh, in 1992, held that the sea in the middle Holocene stood two to six metres higher than present on the coasts of Gujarat and Maharashtra. [n24; Vora, Gaur, Sundaresh & Tripati 2006, citing Merh 1992] For the wider northern Indian Ocean there is better-dated evidence: a highstand above 1.5 metres at about 6,500 years ago in the Maldives and Sri Lanka, followed by a decline. But a 2019 review in Quaternary Science Reviews of 213 sea-level index points across South and South-East Asia found that tectonics and the compaction of sediment dominate local histories, and the notes warn that the Maldives figure must not be applied to Vasai without local data. [n23; QSR 219 (2019)]

And there are no local data of that precise kind. No Konkan-specific sea-level index point, a dated sample whose height relative to the sea of its time is known, with its error and a tectonic correction, was found in anything the research could read. The geographer B. N. Karlekar, in 2001, “opined [that the] relative chronology of Konkan sea-level changes is not comprehensive”. [n24; Gawali et al. 2022, p. 146; Gaps] Some of the coast has moved on its own account: at Velas, on the south Konkan coast, lignite more than 40,000 years old now lies about a hundred metres from the sea, which Rajshekhar and Kumaran in 1998 read as a sign of neotectonic uplift or subsidence. [n24; Gawali et al. 2022, p. 146]

The closest thing to a local clue is a set of shells. Among the TIFR dates in Indian Archaeology 1971-72 are three samples from Manori, on the western shore of Salsette, facing the open sea just south of the mouth of Vasai Creek. Shells taken 5.5 metres below the ground surface, at a point 6 metres above low-water level, gave 4,245 ± 85 years before present (laboratory number TF-1192). Shells from 3.5 metres below the surface gave 4,540 ± 100 BP (TF-1193), and from 1.5 metres, 4,385 ± 110 BP (TF-1194). The list’s calendar figures, 2295, 2590 and 2435 BC, are simple subtractions from 1950, not calibrated dates. [n24; IAR 1971-72, pp. 83–84]

Marine shells, deposited in sediment, six metres above today’s low-water mark, about four and a half thousand years ago. The notes are careful about what this means. It shows that by the middle Holocene the sea was depositing shell-bearing sediment on the Salsette coast at heights above today’s low-water line. Whether that reflects a sea standing higher than today, or storm waves and beach-building throwing shells up onto a raised foreshore, cannot be decided from the list entry. It is “the strongest Mumbai-region hint of a mid-Holocene stand at or above present”, but it is not a published sea-level index point. [n24; Inferences] Nor are the shells human food remains: the list gives them as natural marine samples, submitted by the Geology Department of St Xavier’s College and by Deccan College. [n24; IAR 1971-72; Prehistoric sites, Cited Findings]

One more regional record fills in the climate of those centuries. Limaye and Kumaran, in a 2012 paper in Quaternary International that the research could not retrieve but found summarised in the 2021 review, inferred increased rainfall and higher sea level in the southern Konkan between about 5,600 and 5,330 years ago. [n24; Frontiers in Earth Science 2021 review] So the evidence, imperfect as it is, points one way: around six to five thousand years ago the sea on this coast had come back to roughly where it is now, perhaps a little higher, in a wetter climate than today’s, and the mangroves stood thick in the drowned valleys.

How the dates were made

Since so much of this chapter rests on a single list of numbers in a government review, it is worth pausing on where those numbers came from.

In the late 1960s and early 1970s the Tata Institute of Fundamental Research in Bombay ran one of India’s first radiocarbon laboratories. Radiocarbon dating measures how much of the radioactive form of carbon is left in something that was once alive, a shell, a coral, a piece of charcoal; since that carbon decays at a known rate after death, the amount remaining gives an age. The laboratory’s samples were given numbers prefixed “TF”, for TIFR, and its results were reported by D. P. Agrawal and Sheela Kusumgar and printed each year in the Archaeological Survey’s review. [n24; IAR 1971-72, pp. 83–84]

The 1971-72 list for Maharashtra is a small archive of this coast’s Holocene. Besides the corals off Bombay and Ratnagiri and the shells of Manori, it records shells from Madh Point, St Xavier’s College in Bombay, Mahim, Chaul, Korlai and Murud, and charcoal from the inland Chalcolithic village of Inamgaon. Several of the coastal samples were submitted by the Geology Department of St Xavier’s College and by Deccan College in Pune. [n24; IAR 1971-72, pp. 82–84] The scientific framework behind them was probably the programme described in D. P. Agrawal, R. K. Avasia and S. J. Guzder’s paper “A multidisciplinary approach to the Quaternary problems in Maharashtra”, published in 1973 in the volume Radiocarbon and Indian Archaeology; later NIO authors cite it as the radiocarbon reference for the Maharashtra Quaternary, but the research did not read it. [n24; Vora, Gaur, Sundaresh & Tripati 2006, reference list]

Two cautions go with every one of these numbers. First, the values are printed as radiocarbon years “before present”, where “present” means 1950, and the calendar-style “BC” figures beside them are simple subtractions of 1950, not calibrated dates. Radiocarbon years are not quite calendar years; converting them requires a calibration that the 1972 list does not apply. The book gives the numbers as printed. [n24; IAR 1971-72, note on BP values] Second, these are marine and geological samples. They date the death of a shellfish or a coral, not a human act. Not one of the coastal dates in the list comes from an archaeological layer. [n24; Prehistoric sites, Cited Findings]

That is why, in a chapter about the first people, there is not a single date for a person. The coast has dates for its sea. Its people, so far, have none.

The making of the island of Sopara

It was in those millennia that the landscape of Vasai, as every later chapter of this book knows it, took shape.

Picture the coast from the hills of Tungareshwar and Kamandurg, looking west. Below them lies a narrow coastal plain, the eroded western edge of the Deccan lavas, capped here and there with laterite and fronted by young sediment. As the sea rose into the plain, it flooded the lower valleys of the rivers. The Vaitarna, coming down from the Ghats in the north, ended now in a broad tidal estuary. The Ulhas, in the south, ended in what would be called the Bassein or Vasai Creek, which also separates the mainland from Salsette. Between them, running parallel to the coast, a narrower channel joined the two: the creek that later sources call the Sopara–Bolinj creek. The land enclosed by the Vaitarna on the north, the Vasai Creek on the south, the sea on the west and that inner channel on the east was, in effect, an island. [n01; Takeaway; Pandit, Live History India 2017] [n01; Inferences]

That is the “island of Sopara”, later the “island of Bassein”, which the 1882 Gazetteer of the Bombay Presidency still called “Bassein island”. [n01; Gazetteer of the Bombay Presidency, Thana] The archaeologist Suraj Pandit describes it as the setting of the ancient port: a large island bounded by the Vaitarna and the Vasai Creek, crossed by a coast-parallel channel on which the port probably stood, sheltered by islands to the west and the mainland to the east, with a lake, since dried up, that was once part of the harbour. [n01; Pandit, Live History India 2017] Brahmanical tradition drew the bounds of Shurparaka the same way: the Sahyadri to the east, the Arabian Sea to the west, the Vaitarna to the north, the Ulhas to the south. [n01; Shah et al. 2020]

When exactly the inner channel opened, the evidence does not say. The notes describe it as shaped by Holocene sea levels and mangrove swamps, and add that for the last two thousand years siltation has been filling it in. [n01; Takeaway] No pollen core or mangrove core from Vasai Creek, Thane Creek or the Vaitarna is documented in anything the research could reach. [n24; Gaps] So the island’s birth is an inference from the regional record: when the sea stood at or near its present level, and perhaps a little above it, a coast-parallel tidal channel would have filled with salt water at every high tide, cutting the strip from Arnala to the later fort of Vasai off from the mainland. [n01; Inferences]

What filled the creeks is better known, from just across the water. Two botanists, Vishnu-Mittre and Statira Guzder, studied “the stratigraphy and palynology of the mangrove swamps of Bombay”. They found the swamps of Bombay and Salsette to be up to five metres of dark and brown mud filling undulating rocky basins. They hoped to build a radiocarbon-dated pollen sequence from the mud, which would have shown how the vegetation changed as the sea came and went, but the sediment was too poor in organic matter and pollen to allow it. [n24; Vishnu-Mittre & Guzder] Five metres of mud in a rocky hollow: that is what several thousand years of mangrove life leaves behind. The creeks of Vasai, Bhayandar and the Vaitarna are lined with the same kind of swamp.

An older geography is preserved in names and in how the 1882 compilers read the land. In a note on Nirmal, the old holy place north of Sopara, the Gazetteer records the tradition that the name comes from the demon Vimal, whom Parashurama defeated (the story is told later in this chapter), and then offers a second derivation: from the Dravidian nir, water, and mal, hill, meaning “a wooded mound rising from bare flats once under water”. [n10; Gazetteer 1882, p. 292 n.] The derivation is the compilers’ guess, not a linguistic finding. But as a description of what the Holocene sea did to this coast, it is exact: low rises of basalt and laterite standing out of tidal flats that the sea had covered and was slowly leaving.

Even in 1882, the islands of the Salsette group could still be reached at low tide by walking across the tidal inlets between them. [n01; Salsette Island, Wikipedia, paraphrasing the 1882 Gazetteer] The world of creeks and islands that the mid-Holocene sea created lasted, in recognisable form, into the age of railways.

There is one more hint, from the Archaeological Survey’s own reports, of how this coast’s terraces record the sea. In the winter of 1957-58, the archaeologist B. B. Lal made a preliminary survey of the country between the coast and the Ghats, and between the Ulhas river in the north and Bombay in the south. In the Ulhas valley, between the Ghats and the coast, he observed “as many as four terraces … not counting the youngest one”, which is flooded at high tide. A similar set of terraces lay in the Dahisar valley between Kanheri Hill and Manori Creek. The stretch of the Ulhas between Thana and Ghodbunder, Lal wrote, “provides an ideal link for correlating the different terraces with ancient sea-levels”. [n24; IAR 1957-58, p. 20] Ghodbunder stands on the south bank of Vasai Creek. Lal was describing, in effect, the staircase that the Ulhas had cut as the sea rose and fell over the ages, right at the threshold of Vasai. No dating followed in anything the research found. [n24; Inferences]

An island that would shape everything after

It is hard to overstate how much of the later history of this coast was decided in these millennia, by the height of the sea and the shape of the mud.

The island the Holocene sea created was a natural harbour. Its inner channel, sheltered from the monsoon swell by the island itself and from the land by the hills, ran from one river to another, so that a boat could pass from the Vaitarna to the Ulhas, and from the Ulhas up into the Deccan trade, without facing the open sea. When the 1882 compilers of the Gazetteer asked why Sopara had been “a chosen settlement”, they pointed to its height above the marsh, its fresh water, and its links to the Vaitarna and the Ulhas, and they reasoned that its water link and the land route up the Tansa valley “made it an early centre of trade”. [n24; Gazetteer Vol. XIV, p. 316] That is the compilers’ inference, but the ground they were reasoning about was laid down by the sea in the age this chapter describes.

The same channel served for more than two thousand years. A seventeenth-century sea manual from Kutch records a regular cargo route through the Bolinj–Sopara creek, and Agashi, on the island’s northern shore, was a shipbuilding centre into the seventeenth century, where, by one account, vessels of up to 500 tonnes were built. [n01; Shah et al. 2020, citing Arunachalam 2002] Specialists cited in a 2020 study put sea level about half a metre above today’s in the second and third centuries BCE, when Sopara first appears in the written record. [n01; Shah et al. 2020, citing Arunachalam 2002] A 2020 survey by the University of Warsaw and the Indian National Heritage and Cultural Research Foundation (INHCRF) mapped old channels now lying half a metre below present sea level. [n36; Shah et al. 2020; Brancaccio 2023]

And the same process that made the island began, slowly, to unmake it. Over about two thousand years the channels silted. Deforestation in the colonial era sped the process; the railway, built on a high embankment in the second half of the nineteenth century, cut the channel apart and left its middle stretches dry; and urban building has filled in much of the rest. [n01; Shah et al. 2020, citing Arunachalam 2002] The tidal island of Sopara is today part of the mainland. Its creek survives as a string of ponds, lakes and low wet ground running north and south through Nallasopara.

Every port, every fort, every village of the later chapters, from Ashoka’s edicts at the Bhatela pond to the Portuguese walls of Baçaim and the plantain gardens of Agashi, was built on the stage that the sea set here between about fifteen and five thousand years ago.

Who was here when the sea came back

The people who watched this happen were modern humans. How and when their ancestors first arrived on this coast is a question the last chapter raised and could not settle, and it is worth gathering what is known before following them onto the hills of Salsette.

The most widely held view, as the notes summarise it, is that modern humans reached India along a southern route out of Africa about 65,000 to 50,000 years ago, following the coasts of southern Asia, and that travellers on that route had reached Australia by about 50,000 years ago. [n14; Peopling of India (Wikipedia); PBS NOVA timeline] Their descendants carry an ancestry that geneticists call AASI, for Ancient Ancestral South Indian, which underlies the genomes of every South Asian population. [n14; Narasimhan et al. 2019] There is no direct date for the first modern humans in Maharashtra. The best-known claim for very early modern humans in India, at Jwalapuram in Andhra Pradesh, where similar stone tools lie below and above the ash of the Toba eruption of about 74,000 years ago, is contested both as to who made the tools and what the ash did. [n14; Gaps; Live Science; UOW, Petraglia et al. 2009]

Nearer at hand, an exploration team working on Salsette has reported larger stone tools at Khindipada, in the Sanjay Gandhi National Park, which its members “believed to be over 30,000 years old”. That figure is their own estimate, given to the press, not a laboratory date, and one member said they still had to rule out a stray find. [n01; Homegrown; Deccan Herald archive] [n24; Salsette Archaeological Exploration Project] If the estimate is right, people were on Salsette during the ice age, when Salsette was a hill on a wide coastal plain and the sea was far away. If it is wrong, the earliest human trace near Vasai is younger. Either way, it is not a fact yet.

Did the first people walk along the coast, or come down from the plateau through the valleys of the Ghats? No direct evidence decides it for the north Konkan. Palaeolithic tools occur both inland, at Bori in the Kukdi valley near Pune, and on the coast, at Kandivli and at Susrondi in Ratnagiri. The later record, the one this chapter is about, is strongly coastal. A fair statement, the notes conclude, is that people probably reached this coast both ways: by walking the exposed coastal plain and by descending the Ghat valleys. [n14; Inferences] Any camps they made on the plain west of today’s shore would now lie under the sea, on the drowned shelf. That is a hypothesis, not a finding, but it is a reminder that the archaeology of the ice-age coast is, quite literally, underwater. [n14; Inferences]

There is a tantalising, poorly documented hint of that drowned world in the notes. Rajaguru and Ghate’s summary records that Sushama Ghate collected microliths at a place called “Karal”, a site which, as cited, is “now far below sea level”. [n24; Rajaguru & Ghate] The research could not retrieve the page, and nothing more is known about where Karal is, how far below the sea the tools were, or how they were recovered. The same paper uses the name “Karal” for a beach-dune rock near Nala Sopara, which the next chapter will meet. [n24; Rajaguru & Ghate; VASAI-SPECIFIC, weakly documented] Whether the two are the same place is not clear from the notes.

Commander Todd and the hills of Bombay

The search for the first people of this coast began, as the last chapter told, with a naval officer. Commander K. R. U. Todd spent the 1930s walking the hills and gravel pits of Bombay and Salsette, and he published what he found in three papers: “Prehistoric man round Bombay” in 1932, “Palaeolithic industries of Bombay” in 1939, and “The microlithic industries of Bombay” in 1950. [n01; Antiquity] He claimed a long sequence: “Chellean” hand-axes at Worli, “Mousterian” tools at Colaba, “Aurignacian” tools at Worli, and at Kandivli, in a gravel and clay pit 21 miles north of Bombay, a continuous stratified sequence with hand-axes, a cleaver and a rostro-carinate in the lower layers and later tools above. [n01; Antiquity] [n24; Ruikar] Mortimer Wheeler, in 1959, accepted an Upper Palaeolithic horizon there. [n01; Wheeler]

Todd also found the later tools, the ones this chapter is about. In 1935 he excavated at Marve, near Malad on the west coast of Salsette, and found microliths, potsherds and hearths. In 1937 he reported Mesolithic implements in Nature from the hill and caves of Padan, near Kandivli and Malad. [n01; Antiquity; Grottomap: Padan Cave 1] Micro-burins from his collection, the waste pieces left when a toolmaker snaps a blade to shape a microlith, were studied by the Cambridge prehistorian J. G. D. Clark and may be the first reported from India. [n01; Antiquity] And Todd, in his 1950 paper, placed Bombay’s microliths early relative to the southern site of Jalahalli, and proposed that the people who made them had moved along the coast, comparing them to the “strandloopers” of South Africa, beachcombing foragers who lived on the shore. [n24; Ruikar] [n01; Antiquity]

Others came after him. P. P. Kalapesi, in 1946, reported Palaeolithic implements from Kandivli. S. C. Malik, in 1959, agreed with Todd’s stratigraphy but could not find his “Clactonian” or Late Acheulean tools. Statira Guzder, who studied the Quaternary environments of the Konkan coast in the 1970s and 1980s, and Sushama Ghate, in 1988, found more surface sites. [n01; Antiquity] [n24; Rajaguru & Ghate; Ruikar] By the 1980s the record of the Bombay littoral had grown into a scatter of surface finds: Todd in 1939, Malik in 1959, Guzder in 1981, Ghate in 1988, most of them on low hillocks near the shore. [n24; Rajaguru & Ghate]

None of these sites has an absolute date. Not one. The stone tools of the Mumbai coast have been arranged, rearranged and argued over for ninety years, by typology and by their position in gravels and clays, but no laboratory has ever put a number on any of them. [n24; Prehistoric sites, Takeaway] The research went looking in the Archaeological Survey’s own annual reports to see what the official record said.

What the Archaeological Survey found

The ASI’s Indian Archaeology – A Review has appeared every year since 1953-54, and for the prehistorian it is the closest thing to an official register of sites. The research hit an obstacle at once. Of the 49 yearly volumes scanned on archive.org, about forty carry an unreadable text layer, a garbled Hindi-script recognition of English pages, in which the word “excavation” appears zero times. Readable text exists only for a handful of years. To get round this, the research ran its own optical character recognition over the page images of several volumes, and recovered two entries that bear directly on this coast. Neither is inside Vasai. [n24; Method note; IAR entries found by local OCR, Takeaway]

The first is B. B. Lal’s survey of 1957-58, already met above for its river terraces. Its opening sentence is a summary of the problem: Kandivli “has long been known to yield palaeoliths and later Stone Age tools, but the exact chronological positions of these industries in terms of the different epochs of the Pleistocene yet remain to be determined.” Lal concluded that the industries Todd had found in the nullah section behind Padan Hill near Kandivli “were not all in situ”, meaning they had been moved from where they were made; that the older ones might belong to a higher terrace to the east, where palaeoliths came out of weathered reddish earth and gravel; and that tools of “Series II”, the later, Late Stone Age and microlithic type in the Survey’s scheme, lay on a terrace in the Dahisar valley near the National Park. [n24; IAR 1957-58, p. 20]

The second, and the more decisive, came three years later.

Sankalia at Kandivli, 1960–61

In the field season of 1960-61, Hasmukh Dhirajlal Sankalia of Deccan College, Pune, the most influential Indian prehistorian of his generation, went to Kandivli to settle the question of Todd’s sequence. With him were two younger archaeologists, G. C. Mohapatra and V. N. Misra. The Survey’s review printed their report as “Exploration in District Bombay Suburban”, entry 22. [n24; IAR 1960-61, p. 22]

They found two sections in the Kandivli deposits, “assignable respectively to a higher and a lower terrace”. The lower terrace “seems to be the result of re-deposition by a small stream”. In both sections they found tools of chert and of a flint-like jasper, “which, however, did not include any handaxe or cleaver”. The section that Todd and Malik had divided into many sub-groups “could not really be done so”: at most there were two divisions, and both yielded Series II tools. The German geologist F. E. Zeuner, who also examined the sections, agreed that there were two deposits, not several. [n24; IAR 1960-61, p. 22]

And then the report adds a sentence that is the nearest thing this chapter has to a portrait of the first people’s country: “The hillocks at Kandivli, as also at Madh and Yerangal Point, are full of microliths and tools of Series II.” [n24; IAR 1960-61, p. 22]

Madh and Yerangal, or Erangal, are headlands on the western shore of Salsette, facing the open Arabian Sea, a short way south of Manori and the mouth of Vasai Creek. Full of microliths. Sankalia’s reading, the one the Survey published, was that the Kandivli record is a Late Stone Age, microlithic record, scattered on terraces and on low coastal hills, not the long sequence from hand-axe to microlith that Todd had claimed. [n24; Inferences] Sankalia had already disputed Todd’s stratigraphy and typology in a 1959-60 paper, “Stone Age cultures of Bombay – a re-appraisal”, and found no hand-axes at Kandivli, although Todd’s published drawings do show bifaces. [n24; Ruikar] [n01; Antiquity] The disagreement has never been fully resolved: the notes record both Todd’s illustrations and Sankalia’s failure to find what they show. For this chapter, the important point is the one both sides agree on. The hills of western Salsette were covered in the small tools of a late hunting and gathering people.

The book’s quotations from these reports come from the research’s own machine reading of page images, cleaned of obvious run-together words, and should be checked against the scans before print. [n24; Gaps]

The hillocks full of microliths

Who were the microlith-makers, and what were they doing on those hills?

Microliths are very small stone blades, points and geometric pieces, often a centimetre or two long, struck from cores of fine-grained stone like chert, chalcedony and jasper. On their own they are too small to use. They were made to be set, often several at a time, into hafts of wood or bone, to make composite tools: arrows tipped and barbed with stone, harpoons, sickles, knives with a cutting edge of stone teeth. When a part broke, it could be replaced. It is a technology of mobile people who hunted small and fast game, fished, and gathered, and it spread across much of the world at the end of the ice age. (Chapter 2 introduced them at Manori.) [n14; Manori Hill; INFLIBNET Mesolithic Cultures]

The coastal Mesolithic sites of western India, Rajaguru and Ghate observed, cluster on low hillocks and rock outcrops near the shore, and marine foods probably formed a major part of the diet. [n24; Rajaguru & Ghate] [n23; Mesolithic Cultures of India (INFLIBNET)] [n14; UNESCO Silk Road paper] That pattern fits Salsette exactly. Two botanists studying the Bombay mangroves, Vishnu-Mittre and Statira Guzder, summarised the finds: “Middle and Late Stone Age tools have been found by Todd (1939), Malik (1959), Sankalia (1962) and by one of us (Statira Guzder)”, both in the nala sections at Borivli and Kandivli and on the surface of the coastal headlands at Erangal, Manori and Gorai. [n24; Vishnu-Mittre & Guzder] Erangal, Manori and Gorai: three headlands in a row along the western shore of Salsette, looking out over the sea toward Arnala and the mouth of the Vaitarna.

The newest finds add to the same picture. In January 2018 the Hindustan Times reported that archaeologists of the Salsette Archaeological Exploration Project had found microliths on a hill beside Manori beach, a possible tool “factory”, provisionally dated to between 10,000 and 15,000 years ago; the headline called the tools “Middle Stone Age” but the article put them at about 15,000 years. The same team reported microliths at the Tulsi Lake area of the National Park and larger tools at Khindipada and Padan. [n14; Hindustan Times via PressReader; IBTimes India] [n24; Salsette Archaeological Exploration Project] These finds are still unpublished in peer-reviewed form and undated by any laboratory method. [n24; Salsette Archaeological Exploration Project]

The dates matter, and the cautions are real. Microliths were made over a very long span, from the end of the ice age into historic times, so a scatter on the surface does not date itself; a 2022 review in PLOS ONE notes that “the paucity of securely dated sites with microlithic remains has compounded the confusion” about what “Mesolithic” even means in India. [n24; PLOS ONE 2022 review] [n23; PLOS ONE microlith review] Vishnu-Mittre and Guzder suggested one way out: if the stratum that holds the tools could be tied to the base of the raised-beach deposits, the tools could be placed in a known chronological horizon. The notes read this as implying that the tool-bearing layer is older than the mid-Holocene marine shells, such as those of Manori, but stress that it is the authors’ hypothesis, not a result. [n24; Vishnu-Mittre & Guzder; Inferences]

What can be said with confidence is modest. Between the end of the ice age and some time before the historic period, people who made microliths lived on the low hills of western Salsette, from Kandivli and Padan to Madh, Erangal, Manori and Gorai, and on the edge of the Dahisar valley; at Marve they made hearths, and at some point, if Todd’s potsherds belong with his microliths, which is not established, they or their successors used pottery. They lived within sight of the shore, and probably drew much of their food from it. [n24; Inferences] [n01; Antiquity]

A day on the hill at Manori

What would such a place have been like? The evidence does not allow a reconstructed scene with named people and dated events, and this book will not invent one. But some things are generically true of the setting, and of microlithic foragers on a tropical coast, and they can be put together.

A low hill of dark basalt, perhaps capped with red laterite, stands a little back from a sandy shore. To the west is the open sea, its edge moving: in the early Holocene it is still rising, year by year, and a few generations may see the beach move noticeably inland. To the north, across a broad stretch of water that is becoming the mouth of Vasai Creek, are the low hills and swamps of what will become the island of Sopara. Mangroves are spreading along every creek. The monsoon arrives each June, as it did, by the regional records, more strongly in the early and middle Holocene than later; for four months the hills are green and the streams run brown; then the long dry season. [n24; Frontiers in Earth Science 2021 review]

On the hill, someone sits with a core of chert or jasper and a hammerstone, striking off thin blades and snapping them into shape. The waste, the chips and the micro-burins that Todd’s collection preserved, falls around them and stays there for ten thousand years. The finished pieces go into hafts. What the hafts were used for is not known on this coast. Rajaguru and Ghate’s suggestion that marine foods were important comes from the location of the sites, close to the shore, not from excavated shell middens or fish bones; the notes found no dated food remains from any of these sites. [n24; Rajaguru & Ghate; Gaps] The shell dates from Manori are natural, not kitchen refuse. [n24; IAR 1971-72]

That is as close as the evidence lets us come. The people of the hills of Salsette are known by their tools and their choice of ground, and by nothing else: no burials, no bones, no art, no laboratory date. They were the strandloopers of Todd’s imagination, perhaps, or something else. They were certainly there. And since the hills they chose are the hills that front the mouth of Vasai Creek, they are the right stand-ins for the people who must have lived, unrecorded, on the hills and shores of Vasai itself. [n24; Inferences] [n01; Inferences]

The silence inside Vasai

It needs saying plainly, because it is easy to slide from “near Vasai” to “at Vasai”. No Palaeolithic, Mesolithic or Chalcolithic site is reported from Vasai taluka, from Palghar or from Dahanu in any source the research could read, including the readable volumes of the Archaeological Survey’s annual review. [n24; Prehistoric sites, Takeaway] No microliths have been published from the Tungareshwar foothills, from Kamandurg, from Jivdani hill above Virar, or from any of the low rises of the island of Sopara. [n01; Gaps] [n14; Gaps]

There are several possible reasons, and they are not the same as “nobody lived here”. The Survey’s annual reports for most of the years from 1955 to 2001 survive online only with an unreadable text layer, and any Thana or Palghar entry hidden in them is unchecked. [n24; Gaps] Much of the Konkan has seen very little archaeological work at all; the archaeologist Kurush Dalal is quoted as saying that what is known of the region derives largely from Shilahara-period inscriptions. [n24; Live History India, Chaul] The coastal plain of Vasai has been farmed for centuries and built over in decades. And the coast itself has moved: if foragers camped on the low shore of the early Holocene, their camps may lie under the creeks, under five metres of mangrove mud, or under the sea. [n14; Inferences] The notes put the principle in five words: absence of publication is not absence of sites. [n24; Gaps]

So the Vasai-specific record for this whole chapter is the land itself: the creeks, the island, the mud. The human record is regional, and it comes from across the water.

The far south: carvings on the laterite

There is one more body of evidence from this age on the Konkan, and it is far from Vasai, but it belongs to the same coast and it is the only art.

On the laterite plateaus of Ratnagiri and Sindhudurg districts, two hundred and fifty kilometres and more to the south, roughly 1,500 to 1,600 carvings have been recorded at more than 150 sites: figures cut into the flat red rock, animals and humans and shapes whose meaning is unknown. They are called Mesolithic because no domesticated animals appear among them. Official statements give their age as “20,000–10,000 BCE”, other estimates about 12,000 years; no laboratory dating method is cited for any of them. In 2025 the sites were added to India’s Tentative List for UNESCO World Heritage status, and in June 2026 the Maharashtra government announced a four-year, ₹15-crore documentation project across 107 villages using drones and artificial intelligence. [n24; Drishti IAS] [n14; Drishti IAS; Swarajya; Free Press Journal] [n23; Drishti IAS; Swarajya]

Nothing like them is known from the north Konkan. But they show that somewhere on this coast, at some time between the end of the ice age and the coming of herding, people stood on the open laterite and cut pictures into it. If the age estimates hold, those carvers were contemporaries of the microlith-makers of Salsette. [n24; Cited Findings, south Konkan]

Farther south still, at Susrondi in Ratnagiri, the archaeologist Ashok Marathe from 2001 investigated what has been described as the first coastal Acheulean cave site in India, with Late Acheulean artefacts. [n14; Academia.edu, “Peopling the Koṁkaṇ Coast”] The Konkan has a deep human past. It is simply very thinly explored.

The people of the forest

Every account of the first people of this coast eventually turns to the hills east of Vasai, and to the people who have lived in them for as long as anyone has records: the Warli, the Kokna, the Katkari, the Thakur and the hill Kolis. They are the Adivasis, the “original inhabitants”, of the Palghar hills. Their own history before the colonial record is unwritten, and the archaeological record of the hills is blank. But in the last two decades a new kind of evidence has been added: their genes.

The best study for this district is by Debortoli and colleagues, published in Scientific Reports in 2020 under the title “Novel insights on demographic history of tribal and caste groups from West Maharashtra (India) using genome-wide data”. They sampled six groups, all of them speakers of Indo-European languages: Deshastha Brahmins, Kunbi Marathas, and four tribal peoples, the Kokana, the Warli, the Bhil and the Pawara. The Warli and Kokana samples were collected at Jawhar, in Palghar district, at 19.91° north, 73.23° east, about seventy kilometres north-east of Vasai; the paper notes that the two peoples “live in the same geographic region in the Palghar district”. [n01; Debortoli et al. 2020, PMC7308293]

The result was striking. Of all the groups tested, the Warli showed “the highest AASI-related contribution”: by one method of measurement (qpAdm) about 54 per cent of their ancestry, and by another (an ADMIXTURE analysis with three components) about 91 per cent of the AASI-like component. The tribal groups as a whole carried much more AASI ancestry than the two caste groups. [n01; Debortoli et al. 2020] An earlier study, the large survey by Narasimhan and colleagues in 2019, which included only three Warli samples, had also found one of the highest AASI-related contributions among them. [n01; Debortoli et al. 2020, reporting Narasimhan et al.]

AASI, it will be remembered, is the ancestry that descends from the first modern humans to reach South Asia. A people of the Palghar hills, today, carries one of the strongest signals of that ancestry measured anywhere in the subcontinent. [n14; Narasimhan et al. 2019] [n01; Takeaway]

The study found more. The Warli and the Kokana share a mutual affinity, measured as segments of DNA identical by descent (about 10.17 centimorgans per pair between the two groups), though weaker than the affinity between the Bhil and the Pawara. Within the Warli, the sharing was very high, 33.4 centimorgans per pair, a sign of strong endogamy, marriage within the group, or of descent from a small founding population; among the Kokana it was 13.84. And the Warli were the only tribal group in the study whose effective population had not declined recently: it had grown over the last twenty or so generations. [n01; Debortoli et al. 2020]

What the paper did not do matters as much. It gives no dates for when the Warli or Kokana ancestries mixed. It does not discuss any relationship to the Austro-Asiatic or Munda-speaking peoples of eastern India, which some older writers had suggested. [n01; Debortoli et al. 2020] And these are the genomes of living people. No ancient DNA has been recovered from anywhere on the Konkan, in Mumbai, or on the Maharashtra coast; the large ancient-DNA studies include no individual from Maharashtra at all. [n24; Genetics, Gaps] [n14; Gaps]

So what may be said? The notes put it carefully. High AASI ancestry combined with an Indo-Aryan language suggests that the Warli descend largely from the region’s ancient indigenous population, which adopted an Indo-Aryan language later. That is consistent with calling them a “first people”. It does not prove that they were in Vasai specifically, or that their ancestors made the microliths of Salsette. [n01; Inferences] Todd’s strandlooper idea and the genetic result can be set side by side, as a hypothesis and a genetic backdrop; neither proves the other. [n01; Inferences]

There is one further warning in the genetic literature. The AASI component is defined statistically, and its nearest living relatives are disputed. Reich and colleagues in 2009 found the Andamanese to be related to the Ancestral South Indian population without later northern admixture; Yelmen and colleagues in 2019 argued that the AASI component is distinct from, and not closely related to, the Andamanese, and that the Paniya of south India are a better stand-in. [n14; Reich 2009 abstract; Genetics of South Asia (Wikipedia summary)] The words “deep ancestry” are true of the Warli. The words “the same as the first people” would claim more than any study shows.

What the forest peoples say about themselves

When colonial officials began writing down the peoples of the Thana district, they asked the Warli where they had come from, and found no answer they could record.

R. E. Enthoven’s The Tribes and Castes of Bombay, compiled between 1920 and 1922 from district officers and informants, put it in one sentence: “Regarding their origin nothing is known.” Enthoven counted 151,693 Varlis in 1901, with their headquarters in Dahanu, and described them as “an aboriginal tribe only slightly influenced by Brahmanism”. He recorded four divisions, the Shuddha, the Murde, the Davar and the Nihir, the Nihirs living in south Mahim and Bassein. [n35; Enthoven III, “Varli”, pp. 445–447] The missionary scholar John Wilson had derived the name from varal, a small cultivated patch, meaning an upland, and the 1882 Gazetteer agreed that the Varlis were “probably originally Varalis or uplanders”, and that they gave the name Varalat to the sixth of the seven Konkans. [n35; Enthoven III] [n08; Gazetteer 1882, pp. 182–183] Both derivations are scholars’ guesses. But they preserve something real: that in the colonial reckoning, and perhaps in older ones, the Warli were the people of the uplands, and the uplands of the north Konkan were theirs.

The Gazetteer’s 1872 count gives their distribution: 70,015 Varlis in the district, of whom 45,330 lived in Dahanu, 6,804 in Mahim and 6,099 in Bassein. [n08; Gazetteer 1882, pp. 182–183] Before the 1870s, according to a study by Chattopadhyay, the Warli had been the primary landowners in the Umbergaon, Dahanu and Palghar talukas. They were traditionally semi-nomadic, living in small groups under a headman, and are now mainly farmers of rice and wheat. [n01; Warli people, Wikipedia] Enthoven noted their huts of karvi-reed walls on cow-dung floors, and their seasonal wandering for labour in the dry weather. [n35; Enthoven III]

Their language carries layers too. George Grierson’s Linguistic Survey of India (1905) found about 89,000 speakers of Varli in Thana, more than half the population of Dahanu, and classed it as originally a Bhil dialect, now influenced by Marathi “except in the extreme north”, where it takes a Gujarati form. He noted peculiar words such as nang-ne, “to see”, and a habit of clipping words short. [n22; LSI vol. VII] Enthoven added tal, “here”, davar, “old man”, and jhinguor, “blanket”. [n35; Enthoven III] Warli today has about 390,000 speakers by the 2011 census, and UNESCO lists it as potentially vulnerable. [n22; Wikipedia: Varli language]

Their gods are the gods of the forest and the field. At the centre of the ritual square, the chauk, in Warli wall paintings sits the mother goddess Palaghata, linked to fertility; male gods are unusual and are associated with spirits that took human form. [n01; Warli people, Wikipedia, citing Tribhuwan & Finkenauer 2003] The tiger or leopard god Waghoba is worshipped across Palghar district: a peer-reviewed study in 2021 recorded 150 Waghoba shrines in Mumbai Suburban, Thane and Palghar, found at least one in every Warli village visited, and named Vasai among the Warli areas surveyed. A typical shrine shows a feline under a sun and moon, carved in stone or teak and smeared with vermilion, often at the entrance of the village; the wooden figures are renewed every fifteen to twenty years. [n10; Frontiers in Conservation Science, 2021] Among the fisher Mitne Machhis of Mahim and Dahanu, the Gazetteer recorded the worship of only two forest gods, Chaitya and Hirva, with no Brahman priests. [n08; Gazetteer 1882, p. 148]

How old are these beliefs and these images? Nobody knows. The art historian Yashodhara Dalmia, in The Painted World of the Warlis (1988), dated the painting tradition to 2500–3000 BCE and compared it with the rock art of Bhimbetka; other writers call this unsupported, and place the tradition’s origin around the tenth century CE, which has no documentary basis either. [n01; Warli people, Wikipedia; GKToday] It is a striking claim, and the book reports it as a claim. What is not in doubt is that the paintings were women’s work, white rice paste on red ochre or cow-dung walls, built from the circle, the triangle and the square, until the late 1970s, when the artist Jivya Soma Mashe made them a studio art. [n01; Warli people, Wikipedia]

The neighbouring forest peoples tell other stories of where they came from, and these at least have a shape. The Katkaris, the catechu-makers (from kath, catechu, and karne, to make), told Enthoven’s informants that they were descended from Rama’s monkey army, blessed into human form. Enthoven himself thought them “probably of Bhil origin”, come from the north and first settled in the Gujarat “Athavisi”, the district of Surat, pointing to a division of the tribe still called Athavar. [n35; Enthoven II, “Katkari”, pp. 171–173] The Thakurs of Thana had a tradition that a Rajput element entered the tribe when fugitives from Gujarat took refuge among them in the reign of the Sultan Mahmud Begada (1459–1511); Wilson thought the Thakurs and the Warlis by then “indistinguishable”. [n35; Enthoven III, “Thakur”, pp. 376–377; Enthoven II, p. 245] These are traditions about migrations in the historic period, not about the first people, and they are recorded here only because they show how the hill peoples remembered their own past: as a past with movement in it, often from the north.

The coastal Kolis, the fishers of the creeks, are a different case, and their story belongs to later chapters. Enthoven called “Koli” “a term of vague meaning”, probably used for centuries “to describe the greater part of the original inhabitants of the Presidency”, and Wilson counted the Son Koli “the original inhabitant of Bombay Island”. [n35; Enthoven II, “Koli”, pp. 243–246] Their own tradition, as Enthoven’s contributor recorded it, ties most of the present Son Kolis to immigrants from the hills about four hundred years before 1920, a tradition he himself doubted. [n35; Enthoven II, p. 259] Grierson noted that the Kolis “have often been connected” with the Munda-speaking Kols of eastern India, a past opinion and a weak one. [n22; Gaps; LSI vol. VII] No genetic study of the Son Kolis, Mangelas, Vaitis or Agris has been found. [n01; Gaps]

The notes offer a layered model for how the peoples of this coast may have formed, and they mark it clearly as an inference, not an established history: ancient hunter-gatherers carrying AASI ancestry, the microlith-makers among them; then farming and rice cultivation spreading in, with the Warli, Kokna and Koli lineages forming from local and incoming mixtures and shifting to Indo-Aryan speech; then fishing and trading specialists on the creeks as Sopara’s port grew; and then, mostly after the third century BCE, the Brahmin and Kshatriya-claiming garden castes and the Jain merchants. [n01; Inferences] Only the first of those layers belongs to this chapter. The next chapter will test the second against the evidence and find that the farming layer, on this coast, is almost invisible.

The coast’s own memory of the sea

There is one more kind of evidence about this age, and it comes not from the ground or the genome but from the people of the coast themselves. It is a story about the sea.

In the Sanskrit tradition, the land between the Ghats and the sea was not always land. It was won from the ocean by Parashurama, “Rama with the axe”, the warrior-sage, son of the sage Jamadagni, counted as an incarnation of Vishnu. The Brahmanda Purana, in its fifty-seventh and fifty-eighth chapters, tells how Parashurama threw his sacrificial ladle, the sruva or shurpa, into the ocean, and the sea retreated as far as it flew; the land so reclaimed was named Shurparaka. [n01; Green Message summary of Brahmanda Purana] The Harivamsa, an appendix to the Mahabharata, describes Shurparaka as “a city 500 bows that is about a thousand yards broad, and 500 arrows … high”, built “on the belt of land recovered by the arrow shot by Parshuram when he won the Konkan from the sea”. [n15; Thana Gaz. XIV, p. 316] The Mahabharata and the Puranas, as F. E. Pargiter summarised them in 1922, say that Shurparaka was reclaimed from the sea as Parashurama’s dwelling, and so became a tirtha, a holy crossing-place. [n15; Wikipedia, “Nala Sopara”, citing Pargiter 1922] Sopara was called Parashuramatirtha. [n01; Shah et al. 2020]

The Mahabharata’s pilgrimage section, the Vana Parva, sends the Pandava brothers through this country. In the 1882 Gazetteer’s translation: “the Wish-Granted One saw the very holy Shurparaka. Then, crossing a narrow belt of sea” (the compilers glossed it as the Bassein creek) “he came to a world-famed forest, where, in times of yore, gods had done penance and kings sacrificed … Here the Long and Sturdy Armed One saw the altar of the son of Richika, foremost among bowmen.” The son of Richika’s line is Parashurama. The Gazetteer itself suspected the passage might be “a late Brahmanical interpolation”. [n15; Thana Gaz. XIV, Kanheri n.]

The same story is told, in local forms, about almost every holy place of Vasai. The Nirmal Mahatmya, recorded in 1882, tells how a demon named Vimal harassed the Brahmans near the Vaitarna. Parashurama could not kill him, because Shiva kept restoring the demon’s limbs, and won only with Shiva’s help; the pardoned Vimal fell, and Parashurama set up a linga on the spot called Vimaleshvar. “From Vimal it came to be called Nirmal or stainless.” Modern versions add that Parashurama’s arrows made the twin lakes of Nirmal and Vimal. [n10; Gazetteer 1882, p. 292 n.; Heritage Chronicles; Inditales] The Tungar Mahatmya, part of the Padma Purana, tells how Parashurama drove the demons led by Vimal into the sea, and how Vimal settled on Mount Tungar, won Shiva’s favour by penance, and had his hill honoured with a holy pool and a linga. [n10; Gazetteer 1882, p. 366 n.] Nearly every major Hindu shrine in Vasai, the notes observe, tells the same story: a Puranic Parashurama landscape stretching from Shurparaka to Nirmal, Vimal and Tungar. [n10; Takeaway] [n08; Wikipedia: Samvedi]

These are texts, and late ones. The Brahmanda Purana, the Harivamsa and the Mahabharata were compiled over many centuries and none can be dated securely; the Mahatmyas were recorded in the nineteenth century. [n24; Earliest texts, Inferences] [n01; Takeaway] There is a further claim, from a single unreliable source, that people from Shurparaka carried the land-from-the-sea myth south to Kerala, where Parashurama is likewise said to have reclaimed the coast; the book notes it and does not rely on it. [n01; Shurparaka mirror] Nor is there a linguistic study confirming that the name Shurparaka comes from shurpa, the ladle or winnowing basket: that derivation is traditional. [n01; Inferences]

And yet. The legend says that this land was once sea, that the sea was driven back, and that a holy place was founded on the land that emerged. The geology says that this coast was dry land in the ice age, that the sea came over it, and that in the middle and late Holocene, as the sea stood still or fell slightly and the creeks filled with mud, a strip of tidal flats and low wooded rises emerged at its edge: the island of Sopara. The notes call this “a strong documentary juxtaposition”, and insist that it be presented “as resonance, not proof”. [n01; Inferences] The legend cannot be a memory of the ice age; the sea’s last great advance was over thousands of years before any text was written, and it went the other way, from sea toward land. But a people living on a coast of silting creeks and emerging flats would see, within a few lifetimes, land growing where water had been. The Gazetteer’s guess at the meaning of Nirmal, “a wooded mound rising from bare flats once under water”, is the same observation made by a nineteenth-century surveyor. [n10; Gazetteer 1882, p. 292 n.]

The coast remembered the sea as something that had been pushed back. It is not history. It is the oldest story this place tells about itself.

What is not known

At the end of a chapter like this one, the list of what is not known is longer than the list of what is. It is worth setting down in full, because every later chapter stands on it.

  • The ice-age shoreline off Vasai. No published reconstruction exists. The “hundred kilometres or more” is an inference from global sea levels and the width of the shelf. Nigam’s core begins at 14,500 years ago, not at the glacial maximum. [n24; Gaps; What is not known] [n14; Gaps]
  • The height of the mid-Holocene sea on the Konkan. Merh’s two to six metres is for Gujarat and Maharashtra generally; the Manori shells hint at a stand at or above present; no Konkan sea-level index point with a known height, error and tectonic correction has been published. Karlekar (2001): the chronology is “not comprehensive”. [n24; Gaps]
  • The history of the creeks themselves. No pollen or mangrove core has been documented from Vasai Creek, Thane Creek or the Vaitarna. When the Sopara–Bolinj channel opened is unknown. [n24; Gaps]
  • The age of any stone tool on the Mumbai–Thane coast. No Palaeolithic or Mesolithic site from Kandivli to Manori has a laboratory date. Khindipada’s “over 30,000 years” and Manori’s “10,000 to 15,000” are provisional estimates given to the press. [n24; What is not known] [n01; Gaps]
  • Any Stone Age site inside Vasai, Palghar or Dahanu. None has been reported. The Survey’s annual reports for most years from 1955 to 2001 could not be searched. [n24; Gaps]
  • What the microlith-makers ate, how they buried their dead, what they looked like. No food remains, burials or skeletons have been reported from any site on this coast. [n24; Gaps]
  • The ancient DNA of the Konkan. None has been recovered. The Warli result is from living people. [n24; Genetics, Gaps]
  • The age of the Warli painting tradition, of the Waghoba cult, and of the Parashurama legend. Each has claims, none has dates. [n01; Warli people, Wikipedia] [n10; Gaps]

Three things, on the other hand, are known. The sea off Bombay stood about a hundred metres lower near the end of the ice age and came back, with pauses, by six thousand years ago. Microlith-making foragers lived on the coastal hills of Salsette, facing the mouth of Vasai Creek. And the forest peoples of the Palghar hills carry one of the strongest genetic legacies of the first modern humans of South Asia.

Toward the farmers

About five thousand years ago, as the dates in this book reckon it around 3000 BCE, the coast of Vasai had become recognisably itself. The sea stood near its present level. The Vaitarna and the Ulhas ended in broad tidal creeks, lined with mangroves. Between them, the island of Sopara lay in its channels, its low rises standing out of the flats. On the hills of Salsette, across the creek, lay the scattered tools of the people who had lived there; whether their descendants still lived there, or anywhere on the island, the evidence does not say.

Two hundred kilometres to the north-west, on the coast of Gujarat, and two hundred kilometres to the east, beyond the Ghats, the world was about to change. People were building towns of brick, sailing ships to the Persian Gulf, sowing barley and wheat, herding cattle and firing painted pots. Within a few centuries there would be a dock at Lothal and a town of five thousand people at Daimabad. The next chapter follows those changes as they came toward this coast, and asks a hard question: did any of them reach it?
