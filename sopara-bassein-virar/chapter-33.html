Part Eleven — The Kuparis

The Kuparis are the Catholic community whose villages lie round Vasai. Their story is the one that ties the whole book together, because it passes through nearly every period: Brahmin migrants from the Samaveda tradition, the Portuguese baptisms, the Maratha restoration and the modern parish. These six chapters tell it from the first written trace of the community to the village feast of today.

Period covered: from the first written trace of Samaveda Brahmins on this coast (1026) to the eve of the Portuguese conquest (1534), read backwards through the records of 1882–1920

In this chapter. Where do the Kuparis come from? This chapter reads the earliest traces backwards from the Portuguese conquest: a stone, a copper plate, Christians before the Portuguese, and the legends of Bimba and of Odisha.

A stone beside a headman’s house

In the village of Koprad, on the northern edge of the old garden island of Sopara, about ten miles north of Bassein, there lay in the early 1880s an inscribed stone. It was written in Devanagari, and it carried a double date: Shaka 1386 and Hijri 868, which is the year 1464 of the common era. It named Mahim, Thana and Koprad itself, and it named a place called “Bimbstan”. It also bore Muslim names. The compilers of the Gazetteer of the Bombay Presidency recorded it in their volume of places of interest, and they recorded one more detail, almost in passing: the stone lay near the house of a Samvedi Brahman, the headman of the village. [n03; TG-XIV Koprad and Sopara entries; TG-II p. 441 n. 4]

That small scene is the best place to begin the story of the Kuparis, because it holds, in one frame, nearly everything that can and cannot be said about where they came from. Here is a Samvedi household in the 1880s, at the head of a village at the centre of what is now the Kupari country. Here is a stone four centuries older, from the years when the coast was ruled by the Sultans of Gujarat, naming a “Bimbstan” that echoes the legends of a King Bimba. And here is the silence between them. The stone does not mention Samvedis. The Gazetteer does not say how long the headman’s family had lived there. Nobody wrote down whether his ancestors had known the men who carved it. The stone and the house sit side by side, and the historian has to resist the temptation to join them with a line that no source draws.

This chapter is about the people the Kuparis came from. The Kuparis, the Catholic families of the villages around Nirmal, Agashi, Nandakhal, Bhuigaon, Gass and their neighbours, are described by themselves and by others as descendants of Samvedi Brahmins who became Christians under Portuguese rule. That description is old enough, as the next chapter will show, to have real support in the documents of the 1880s. But it raises a harder question underneath it: who were the Samvedis? Where did they come from, how long had they been on this coast, how did they live, and what did they speak, before a Franciscan friar walked into Agashi in 1535?

This part of the book is written by a Kupari, about the author’s own people. That is a reason for more care, not less. Every community carries a story about its own beginning, and the Kuparis carry several. Some of those stories are old and some are recent. Some have documents behind them and some have none. The aim here is neither to flatter them nor to sweep them aside, but to set each beside the evidence and say plainly what kind of thing it is. Where a claim rests on a dated document, the text says so. Where it rests on a scholar’s reasoning, or a census, or a nineteenth-century official’s description, or a family’s memory, or a website, the text says that too.

One warning belongs at the start. The richest description of the Samvedis that survives was written in 1882, nearly three and a half centuries after the conversions began. It describes a community that had lived all that time beside its own Christian kin. It cannot be read as a photograph of 1534. It is the oldest detailed picture there is, and it will be used, but always with that distance in mind.

The garden island

To understand the Samvedi world, start with the ground.

The old city of Sopara did not stand on the open coast. It stood on what the 1882 Gazetteer called a “garden island”, cut off from the mainland by a backwater that ran from the Manikpur creek in the south, past Sopara, to the Bolinj creek in the north. Before the Portuguese it was called the island of Sopara; under the Portuguese it became the island of Bassein. The Gazetteer laid out the villages of this island, which it called by the local word agar, a garden belt: Bolinj, Koprad and Umbrala to the north, Nirmal and Vagholi to the west, Gass to the south, and Sopara and Mardesh in the centre. [n37; Gaz. XIV pp. 314–315]

Every one of those names will come back in this part of the book. Bolinj, Koprad (Kofrad), Umbrala (Umrale), Nirmal, Vagholi (Wagholi), Gass and Mardesh are the heart of what later writers would call the Samvedi villages and, later still, the Kupari villages. The overlap between the Gazetteer’s map of the garden island and the modern community’s map of itself is the first and most solid piece of evidence in this chapter. Whatever else is uncertain, the Samvedi world was this island and its edges.

The Gazetteer explained, in a single long sentence, why people had chosen this ground. “Its height above the surrounding salt marsh, its rich soil and fresh water, and its nearness to the holy Vaitarna, Jivdhan, Tungar and Nirmal must early have made Sopara a chosen settlement. Its distance from the coast and its broad backwater guarded it from pirate raids or the forays of the wild hill and forest tribes, and its water connection with the Vaitarna on the north and the Ulhas or Bassein river on the south … made it an early centre of trade.” [n37; Gaz. XIV p. 316] It is a colonial official’s reconstruction, not a document from the founding, but it fits the land. A raised island of sweet water and rich soil, wrapped in a tidal creek, with holy places all round it and boats able to reach it from two rivers, is a place where farmers, priests and traders would all want to live.

The creek was once “a considerable arm of the sea”. By the nineteenth century it was silting fast, the Gazetteer said, “especially since the long railway embankment has dammed the flow of water from the south”. [n37; Gaz. XIV pp. 314–315] The silting was older than the railway. A Portuguese writer in 1728 reported that the creek between Sopara and Bolinj could be forded at low water over about 4,000 feet, and asked that it be dug out to a man’s height. [n37; Gaz. XIV p. 322] Yet even in 1882 small boats could still come up as far as Gass, about a mile from Sopara, and Gass, Bolinj and Vagholi served as the landing places from which molasses, rice and betel leaves were shipped to Surat, Broach, Jambusar, Bhavnagar and Gogha. [n37; Gaz. XIV pp. 315–316] The villages of the Samvedi island faced the sea and Gujarat as much as they faced the Deccan. That orientation will matter when we come to their speech.

North of the island, across the Bolinj creek and on the road to the Vaitarna, lay Agashi. In the Gazetteer’s judgement Agashi was “probably an old timber trade and ship-building centre”. In 1630 a Portuguese writer found it poor in buildings but rich in timber trade, building ships “as good as Portuguese ships, able to make the voyage to Europe”. When the Portuguese first came raiding, in 1530, they burned the town and three hundred boats. [n37; Gaz. XIV pp. 1–2] Agashi in 1881 had 6,823 people, of whom 5,168 were Hindus, 1,500 Christians and 155 Muslims, and it drove “a great trade with Bombay in plantains and betel leaves”. [n21; Gaz XIV p. 2] [n34; Gaz XIV pp. 1–2] Its villages, with Vatar and Koprad, were the places where the 1882 Gazetteer would find Hindu Samvedis and Christian ex-Samvedis drying plantains side by side.

The modern community has its own lists, and they agree with the old map. A Marathi Wikipedia article on the Samvedi dialect, and a 2026 article in the Marathi daily Saamana, name sixteen villages where Samvedi is spoken: Bhuigaon, Wagholi, Kofrad, Gass, Umrale, Bolinj, Gavdhani, Vatar, Valunje, Vanda (Mardes), Padhai, Karmale, Navale, Somadi, Bankar and Mandpe. Saamana describes the core as twelve coastal villages running “from Agashi to Bhuigaon”. [n20; mr.wikipedia: सामवेदी बोली; Saamana, 22 Mar 2026] An English Wikipedia page on the Samvedis puts their villages around the Nirmal sarovar, the lake, “earlier called Shurparak”, and names Agashi, Vatar, Kofrad, Bolinj, Nala and Umrale. [n08; Wikipedia: Samvedi / Kupari mirrors] A community blog of 2009 lists the Kupari villages of north Vasai as Nirmal, Agashi, Nandakhal, Gass, Bhuigaon, Umraala, Naanbhat, Shrilaiwadi, Bolinj, Mardes, Wagholi, Gomes Ali and Rajodi. [n07; D’Mello blog] These are modern and weakly sourced lists. But they draw the same circle the Gazetteer drew in 1882: the garden island of Sopara, the Nirmal lake, and the strip north to Agashi and the Vaitarna.

The holy lake

At the western edge of the island lies Nirmal, and its lake.

Nirmal was a place of pilgrimage before anyone wrote down the Samvedis. The Gazetteer reported that the Skanda Purana and a local text, the Nirmal Mahatmya, made it a sacred tirtha. [n34; Gaz XIV p. 293] The Mahatmya’s founding legend tells of a demon named Vimal, defeated by Parashurama, the axe-bearing hero of the Konkan, who set up the linga of Vimaleshvar there. The Gazetteer treated this explicitly as a legend, and added a speculative etymology of its own, deriving the name from the Dravidian nir, “water”, and mal, “hill”. [n37; Gaz. XIV p. 292 note] Neither the legend nor the etymology is evidence of anything except what people in later centuries believed. But the belief itself is old enough and strong enough to have shaped the place. Parashurama is the hero who, in the Harivamsha’s story, won the land of Shurparaka back from the sea with an arrow. [n37; Gaz. XIV pp. 316–317] At Nirmal the same hero founds the holy lake. The whole island lived inside that story.

There is one hard trace of Nirmal’s early antiquity. In front of a village house there in 1882 lay a fragment of a Sanskrit inscription of about the seventh century. [n37; Gaz. XIV pp. 292–293] Again a stone in front of a house; again a stone that the house’s owners did not write. It proves that literate Sanskrit was carved at Nirmal more than a thousand years before the Gazetteer, but not who carved it.

Nirmal also holds the samadhi of a Shankaracharya, and this is where the Samvedis’ own story enters. In the community’s tradition, as reported on the Wikipedia page “Samvedi”, their Vedic practice had lapsed under Buddhism and was restored by Adi Shankaracharya at Nirmal, whom they regard as their chief preacher; their community deity is Nirmaleshwar on the Nirmal hillock. [n08; Wikipedia: Samvedi] Gerson da Cunha, the Goan scholar who wrote the history of Chaul and Bassein in 1876, mentioned “a Shankaracharya of Nirmal”. [n37; da Cunha p. 127] But which Shankaracharya lies at Nirmal is unresolved in the sources read for this book: the Marathi press calls him only “Shrimad Jagadguru Shankaracharya”. [n20; Lokmat snippet; Gaps] Today the Nirmal yatra begins on Kartiki Ekadashi and runs for about fifteen days, with a students’ dindi and a palkhi carrying the Shankaracharya’s image, bathing in the Vimal talav, and stalls along the road from Nirmal Naka to Bhuigaon Phata selling, among other things, Vasai sukeli, the dried bananas of the Samvedi gardens. [n20; Lokmat snippet]

What matters for this chapter is that the Samvedis’ memory of themselves is tied, by their own tradition, to Nirmal. When the next chapter comes to tell how the Portuguese pulled down Nirmal’s temple and destroyed its linga, and how a Christian parish of the Holy Cross grew up there, it will be telling the story of a community whose holy centre was broken and then remade in two forms. [n37; Gaz. XIV pp. 292–293]

The oldest document: a copper plate of 1026

The first time a Samaveda Brahmin appears in the written record of this coast is in a royal grant of the year 1026.

The kings of the north Konkan at that time were the Shilaharas, a dynasty that had ruled as feudatories of the Rashtrakutas and became independent in the late tenth century. Their grants were engraved on copper plates and given to Brahmins and temples. Aparajita issued the Bhadana plate of 997 from Shristhanaka, which is Thana. Arikesarin’s Thana plate of 1017 or 1018, found in 1787 while the foundations of Thana fort were being dug, styles him lord of the “Konkan 1400” villages. [n03; TG-II pp. 424–425] And in 1026, which is Shaka 948, King Chhittaraja issued a plate that was later found at Bhandup. Its donee was a Samavedi Brahmin of the Chhandoga shakha, the branch of the Sama Veda, from Nour, which is Nahur, in Shatshashthi, the “sixty-six villages” that we call Salsette. [n03; TG-II p. 425 n.]

This is the documentary anchor for every theory of Samvedi antiquity, and it is worth being exact about what it shows. It shows that by 1026 there was at least one Brahmin family of the Sama Veda living in Salsette, important enough to receive a royal gift. It shows that the Shilahara kings of this coast patronised Samavedi Brahmins. It does not show that this family lived in the Vasai villages, since Nahur is in Salsette, some distance to the south. And it does not show that the modern Samvedi caste of the Bassein villages descends from that donee. The researcher who compiled the medieval notes for this book put it carefully: only traditions survive, “plus the 1026 Samaveda grant, which does not prove the modern caste’s continuity”. [n03; Gaps] Enthoven, in 1920, still counted the Samavedis as the tenth of twelve divisions of Maharashtra Brahmins. [n35; Enthoven I, p. 241–242, 249] The name survived. Whether the family did is a separate question.

The plate also places the Samvedis’ first appearance in a particular world. The Shilahara coast was plural. The court was liberal to Arab merchants, “allowing them to have mosques and a headman to settle disputes”. [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 432] A grant of 1094, the Kharepatan plates of Anantadeva, exempted the ships of two merchant-ministers from tolls at the ports of Shristhanaka, Nagapur, Shurparaka, Chemuli and others; Sopara was still a working port. [n03; TG-II pp. 425–426] A later Shilahara grant, on a slab at Sandor dated 1255, gave a garden “in the part called Nivayi within the limits of Sandor village” to an astrologer named Kheidev. [n37; Gaz. XIV p. 402] Parsis had left Pahlavi inscriptions at Kanheri in 999 and 1021. [n35; Enthoven III, pp. 192–195] The coast’s medieval notes conclude that by about 900 to 1300 the Vasai–Sopara–Sanjan coast held Shaiva kings, Brahmin grantees from Karnataka and Karhad, Samaveda Brahmins, Jains, Parsis, Arab and Persian Muslims and Nestorian Christians. [n03; Inferences] The Samaveda Brahmin of 1026 was one figure in a crowded scene.

Christians before the Portuguese

That crowded scene included Christians, two centuries before the Portuguese, and in the very heart of what would become the Kupari country.

The Greek merchant Kosmas, writing about 535, said a bishop appointed from Persia sat at Kalyan. In 942 there were Christians at Chaul. [n37; Gaz. XIII-I p. 200] And in 1321–22 a Dominican friar, Jordanus, spent fifteen days at “Supera”. He found “many Nestorian Christians and a church dedicated to St Thomas”. He buried there four companions who had been martyred at Thana, and he baptised people. Between Thana and Sopara he made thirty-five converts, and he found the pagans, Parsis and Hindus, “willing to listen”. [n37; Gaz. XIV p. 322; Gaz. XIII-I p. 200] The Gazetteer suggested, with a hedge, that the Kalyan and Sopara Christians may have been reinforced by Nestorian refugees from Byzantine persecution in the fifth century. [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 432 note 4]

So there was a Christian church at Sopara in the fourteenth century, dedicated to St Thomas, served by the Church of the East, with a Latin friar’s converts beside it. It is tempting to see in this a deep root for the later Christian villages. The sources do not allow it. The Gazetteer stated plainly: “No trace seems to be left either of the Nestorian or of the Latin converts.” It did record, in a footnote, that “one recent report mentions a Christian village in Bassein that claims to be older than the Portuguese”, and added at once: “No confirmation of this statement has been received.” [n34; Gaz XIII p. 200 n.7] The claim of a pre-Portuguese Christian village is therefore itself a tradition recorded in 1882, unconfirmed then and unconfirmed now. It does not name the village.

The honest conclusion is double. The ground where the Kuparis live had known Christianity before, in another form and another language. But nothing connects those fourteenth-century Christians to the families baptised after 1535, and the Kuparis’ faith, as far as any record goes, came with the Portuguese.

Bimba, the chronicle and the kings of Mahim

The second great body of origin stories on this coast gathers round a king called Bimba, or Bimb, or Bhima, or Bhimdev.

His story is told in a Marathi chronicle, the Mahikavatichi Bakhar. Its oldest layer is credited to a writer named Keshavacharya about 1448, and the text was finished in a seventeenth-century recension. It was edited by R. P. Rane in 1877, by V. K. Rajwade in 1924 and by R. C. Dhere in 1973. [n35; Wikipedia: Mahikavatichi Bakhar] [n08; Wikipedia: Mahikavatichi Bakhar] In the chronicle, Pratap Bimb sets up his capital at Mahikavati, which is Kelve-Mahim in Palghar, and later moves it to Mahim island. [n08] Its kings and its dates are, in the words of the notes for this book, “legendary or uncorroborated”. Rajwade found its geography reliable: of 396 place names, 336 are in the north Konkan. But there is no archaeological, epigraphic or numismatic support for its smaller early dynasties, and its own dates contradict each other. The chronicle places Pratap Bimb’s arrival about 1138; community traditions place Bimbadev about 1294 or 1297. [n03; Raja Bimba section] [n35; Wikipedia: Mahikavatichi Bakhar]

And yet there is a real kernel. An inscription of 1368 from Deonar mentions “Konkan-Bimbasthana”, and the Koprad stone of 1464 with which this chapter began names “Bimbstan”. [n03] A Bimb polity, or at least a place-name that preserved one, existed in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries. The 1882 Gazetteer noted that people generally believed Bimb was a prince of Paithan, on the Godavari, but thought this might confuse Paithan with Patan, that is Anhilvada, the Solanki capital in Gujarat. It observed that copies of local grants referring to Bimb belong to about 1280, “when the Devagiri Yadavas were overlords”, and that Bimbsthan seems to have been an old name of Bhiwandi. Its verdict: “The question is doubtful.” [n03; TG-II p. 437 n.] Elsewhere it noted that the Arab historian Ibn al-Athir names a Bimb, nephew of the Anhilvada king, at Somnath in 1025. [n35; Gazetteer XIII-1, pp. 61–62 and footnotes] Three dates are in play, 1025, about 1138 and about 1297, and they cannot be reconciled. [n35; Inferences]

Which communities claim to have come with Bimb? The 1882 Gazetteer records that the upper coastal castes, the Palshe Brahmans, the Pathare Prabhus, the Pachkalshis, the Chaukalshis, the Somvanshi Kshatris, the Sutars and Malis, and partly the Agris and Bhandaris, say by their own accounts that they came from one settlement “under Bimb, a prince of the Devgiri family who established a chiefship at Mahim”. [n08; Gazetteer 1882, p. 61–62] The Palshe Brahmans take their name, it says, from the village of Palsavli in Kalyan, which according to the Bimbakhyan Bimb gave to his family priest. [n08; Gazetteer 1882, p. 81] The Pachkalshis say they came “with Bhimdev from Paithan on the Godavari at the close of the 13th century”. [n08; Gazetteer 1882, p. 129–130] The Bhandaris, the palm-tappers, also say they came with Bimb. [n08; Gazetteer 1882, p. 149–150]

The Samvedis are not in that list. This is one of the most important findings of the research behind this chapter, and it runs against what many people in the villages have heard. In the two oldest written strata, the Gazetteer and the Bimbakhyan as the Gazetteer reports it, it is the Palshes, not the Samvedis, who are tied to Bimb. [n08; Inferences] The link between the Samvedis and King Bimba appears only in modern tellings. The Wikipedia page “Samvedi” says that under Raja Pratap Bimba the community’s work shifted to growing sugarcane and nagvel, the betel vine; a community blog of 2008 carries the same story. [n08; Wikipedia: Samvedi; nirajpatilsblog] Neither cites an older source. The researcher who worked through the medieval material concluded: “I found no scholarly source for the Samvedi Brahmins’ arrival in the Bimba story.” [n03; Gaps]

The Bimba tradition still matters to the Samvedi story, for two reasons. First, the neighbours with whom the Samvedis lived, farmed and later converted, above all the Pachkalshis, did carry it, so it was part of the coast’s shared memory. Second, the one physical meeting between “Bimbstan” and a Samvedi household is the Koprad stone, lying near a Samvedi headman’s door in 1882. That is proximity, not proof. It is also the kind of proximity from which, over generations, a community can come to feel that a king’s story is its own.

The Gujarat thread

If the Bimba story gives little hard ground, there is another thread that does better: the long, repeated pointing of the evidence towards Gujarat.

It begins with the speech of the Samvedis themselves. In 1905 George Grierson, compiling the volume on Marathi of the Linguistic Survey of India, wrote: “The Samvedis live as husbandmen and gardeners in Bassein and [Mahim]. Their number is estimated at 2,700. They speak a mixed dialect, the chief components of which are the current Marathi of Thana and vulgar Gujarati.” His conclusion was that it was “a mechanical mixture of Konkan Marathi and Gujarati”. [n34; LSI VII] [n22; LSI vol. VII] (Grierson’s printed place-name was read by the optical scanner as “Alabim”. One researcher guessed Alibag; the 1882 Gazetteer’s statement that the Samvedis were “found only in Bassein and Mahim” makes Mahim, the Thana taluka now in Palghar, much the likelier reading. [n34; Corrections] [n22])

Grierson grouped the Samvedi speech with two others. “Lastly there are three small dialects in Thana, viz., Vadval, Phudgi, and Samvedi, which still have preserved many of the characteristic features of Gujarati Bhili.” [n22; LSI vol. VII] The Vadvals, he said, were “supposed to have immigrated from Gujarat”. [n22] [n35; Grierson 1905] The Mangela fishermen’s dialect was “of the same kind as Vadval, Samvedi, etc., with a strong admixture of Gujarati”. [n22]

The same pointing appears in the Gazetteer’s argument about the Palshes and their companions. Against the Paithan origin, the Gazetteer gathered several kinds of evidence. There was no record that Mungi Paithan was sacked by Muslims before 1318. The Prabhu and Palshe records agreed that their first settlement was on the coast, at Kelve-Mahim, Bassein and Salsette. Prabhu speech used Gujarati words for household things: dadar for ladder, takabari for a wall bookcase, ovara for a room, ota for a veranda. The Palshes followed the White Yajurveda, the Gujarat form, and like Gujarati Brahmins forbade marriage between people whose mothers’ fathers shared a family stock. A grant to a Palshe said the priest had come from Pattan and that Bimb was of the Anhilvada family. [n35; Gazetteer XIII-1, pp. 61–62 and footnotes 1–4] Enthoven’s own judgement in 1920 was that the Palshikars “formed part of the large settlement which from Gujarat supplied the foreign element to the Thana district”. [n35; Enthoven I, pp. 248–249] In its three-group account of Thana’s cultivators, the Gazetteer put the coastal gardeners, the “Malis, Chavkalsis, and Somvanshi Kshatris … Panchkalshis”, in a third group “probably of part Gujarat descent”. [n35; Gazetteer XIII-1, p. 124]

The record here is not entirely consistent, and the reader should see the seam. In another passage the Gazetteer reports the view that the Pathares, Panchkalshis and Palshi Brahmins were of Gujarat or part-Gujarat origin, and calls it “doubtful”. [n03; TG-II p. 437 n.] The same colonial compilation argued the Gujarat case in one chapter and hedged it in another.

There is older evidence of Gujarati presence on this coast. The Arab geographer Masudi, writing in 915, said that the Lar language, the speech of Lata, south Gujarat, was spoken in the coast towns as far south as Chaul. [n35; Gazetteer XIII-1, pp. 112–113] Sopara in the 1880s still had about thirty-five families of Lad Vanis, Gujarati traders, most of whom had come from Cambay about 1760, though the Gazetteer thought Masudi’s statement made it “probable that from very early times Lad Vanis had settled along the Thana coast for purposes of trade”. [n35; Gazetteer XIII-1, pp. 112–113] And as we have seen, the Samvedi island’s own boats still sailed for Surat, Broach, Bhavnagar and Gogha in the 1880s. [n37; Gaz. XIV pp. 315–316]

What does all this add up to for the Samvedis? Not a migration story. No source says the Samvedis came from Gujarat. Grierson does not draw that conclusion; he only describes the speech. The researcher who assembled the community-origins notes for this book drew a careful inference: Grierson’s reading “points to a western, Gujarat-linked contact zone, not Odisha”, and the Gujarati strand in Samvedi speech “fits a Gujarat-linked origin for the Samvedi farmers”, though “Grierson does not say so”. [n35; Samvedi] [n34; Inferences] That is as far as the evidence goes. The Samvedi language was formed on a coast that faced Gujarat, among neighbours who either came from Gujarat or traded with it constantly, and it carries Gujarat in its grammar. Whether the Samvedis themselves once crossed from Gujarat, or simply lived for centuries in a world where Gujarati was half the air, cannot be decided.

The Odisha legend

The third great origin story is the most striking, and the least supported. It says that the Samvedis came from Odisha.

In the version carried on the English Wikipedia page “Samvedi”, the community follows the Sama Veda and is locally called “Shamedi”, a name the page derives from musicians at a samadhi shrine. It says they came to Shurparak from Utkala or Kalinga, the ancient names of Odisha, as expert singers, “1500 BC”; that their Vedic practice lapsed under Buddhism; and that it was restored by Adi Shankaracharya at Nirmal. Musicians of the community, it says, play the sanai, choughada, mridanga, bheri and veena at the Nirmal Shankaracharya Samadhi Mandir, the Sureshwar Mandir and the Bhavani Shankar Mandir at Agashi. The page has a single reference, is tagged for original research, and contradicts itself on dates, placing Shankara in 497 BC. [n08; Wikipedia: Samvedi]

A different version comes from one family. In 2011 a blogger visited Bhuigaon and met the Marti family, who call themselves Samvedi Christians, linking the name to “Sam Veda and the Bible”. They said their ancestors came from Orissa and were entertainers, not farmers, and that part of the community converted under the Portuguese and took up farming. [n07; Mumbai Paused blog] It is the oral tradition of a single family, reported once.

The 2026 Saamana article on the Samvedi dialect says the dialect has been spoken in north Vasai “since about the sixth century”, and describes the community as a “singing community”, with a rich store of folk songs, proverbs and stories, its speech marked by a sing-song intonation. [n20; Saamana] The sixth-century date is unsupported.

No scholarly source supports the Odisha origin. The only documentary anchor for Samvedi antiquity is the Samaveda grant of 1026 in Salsette, and the linguistic evidence points west, to Gujarat, not east. [n35; Samvedi] The modern origin story’s dates are internally contradictory. It must be presented, plainly, as the community’s own legend. [n08; Takeaway and Inferences]

Being a legend does not make it worthless. Legends tell us what a people value. The Odisha story makes the Samvedis musicians and singers before it makes them farmers, and a “singing community” is exactly how the 2026 newspaper describes them, and how the Nandakhal “Kupari songs” and the Samvedi folk songs collected by Paul Rumav present them. [n20; Saamana; mr.wikipedia] The legend makes Nirmal and Shankaracharya the heart of their identity, and that, as we have seen, is consistent with everything else. And it gives a people who were farmers in every record a nobler and more ancient vocation. A historian can respect all of that without pretending that a Brahmin troupe walked from Kalinga to Sopara in 1500 BC.

There are smaller origin claims too, and they deserve a line each. The English Wikipedia page on the Kadodi language gives the Kuparis a mixed ancestry of Samvedi Brahmins, Goan Konkani Brahmins and Portuguese “New Christians”, through intermarriage in the colonial period; that section is flagged as uncited. [n07; Wikipedia: Kadodi language] [n34; Rival origin stories] A 2009 community blog says most Kuparis trace descent from Samvedi Brahmins, that some have ties to Vadval and Goan families through intermarriage, and that minor Portuguese ancestry “cannot be ruled out”. [n07; D’Mello blog] These are not origin theories for the Samvedis but descriptions of later mixing, and they belong to the next chapter.

The neighbours: Pachkalshis and Vadvals

The Samvedis did not live alone, and the Kuparis do not descend from them alone. The 1882 Gazetteer, describing the Christian plantain-growers of Agashi, Vatar and Koprad, called them “Native Christians originally Samvedis and Pachkalshis”. [n34; Gaz XIII Part I p. 395] To understand the Kuparis’ ancestors we have to understand the Pachkalshis too.

In 1872 they numbered 15,367, strongest along the coast. They were known by many names: Sutars, Malis, Vadvals, Chaukalshis, Somvanshi Kshatris and Pathares, all of whom interdined and intermarried except the Chaukalshis. The name Pachkalshi was said to come from the five earthen pots, kalash, used in their ceremonies. They claimed descent from Surya-Narayan and arrival with Bhimdev from Paithan at the close of the thirteenth century, though the Gazetteer thought they came from Gujarat with the Pathare Prabhus. [n08; Gazetteer 1882, p. 129–130] Their surnames were Raut, Vartak and Chodhri. They spoke a Marathi that used l for the retroflex ḷ and n for ṇ. They worked as husbandmen, gardeners and carpenters, and a proverb went with them: “Pachkalshi ani kon mhanel alshi?”, “Who can call a Pachkalshi idle?” [n08; Gazetteer 1882, p. 130] In their houses images of Cheda and other local “demon-gods” sat beside Ganpati, Shiv and Krishna. They employed Palshe priests. The wives of the gardeners sold vegetables, butter and milk. [n08; Gazetteer 1882, p. 130]

Enthoven’s 1922 entry, compiled from the community’s own informants, adds more. “Vadval” literally means “the managers of wadis or orchards, viz., of cocoa-nut and palm groves”, and the Vadvals were found in Bassein, Mahim and Dahanu talukas, “formerly known as the Feringana”, the land of the Firangis, the Portuguese. A community census of Vadvals in 1901 returned 7,916. [n35; Enthoven III, “Panchkalshi”, p. 160–161] The Vadvals were said to have separated from the main body “some time in the fourteenth century”, a split “reputed to be due to the admission of a Koli into the caste”, with each side accusing the other; their right to the Panchkalshi name rested on letters from the Shankaracharya in 1836 and 1852 and a resolution of the Palshikar Brahmans of the Bassein taluka in 1855. [n35; Enthoven III, p. 162] The majority of the Vadvals were still farmers, working the land on the khand system of sharecropping and supplying their own cattle. [n35; Enthoven III, p. 163]

And Enthoven records, as part of the community’s self-account, a memory of the Portuguese years that bears directly on the Kuparis: “Under the Portuguese … there was much persecution, and this is said to have forced many members of the caste to fly to the outlying parts of the Southern Konkan. Many were then converted to Roman Catholicism by force and their descendants may still be traced at Bassein and Salsette.” [n35; Enthoven III, p. 162] It is a tradition held by Hindu Pachkalshis in the early twentieth century about their own Christian cousins, and it says “by force”. It will be weighed in the next chapter against the other evidence. Here it is enough to note that the Pachkalshis, like the Samvedis, remembered a split in their own body, a part that went to the church and a part that did not.

The Pachkalshis also prospered when the Portuguese fell. Their help to the Marathas “during the siege of Bassein and the acquisition of adjoining country in 1739–40” earned one family the title of Sarpatil and inam lands, and “the patelships of many families of the Vadval division” traced to the Bassein war. [n35; Enthoven III, p. 162] [n08; Gazetteer 1882, p. 130 footnote] The same community thus remembered both forced conversion under the Portuguese and reward under the Marathas.

Today the word Vadval is used in Vasai for a Christian community too: Catholic Vadvals of the south of the taluka, distinct from the Kuparis of the north. A community site contrasts the “more fun-loving” music of the Vadvals of south Vasai with that of the “more conservative Kupari community”. [n07; Vasaiker] The Gazetteer of 1882 already knew that “the lower classes” of Christians included Vadvals, “among the most skilful cultivators in the district”. [n34; Gaz XIII p. 205–206] So the line between Samvedi and Pachkalshi, between Kupari and Vadval, was a line between neighbours who farmed the same kind of land with the same skill. The Gazetteer’s 1882 phrase, “Samvedis and Pachkalshis”, suggests that at Agashi it did not always seem a sharp line at all. [n34; The Pachkalshi connection]

One ritual world, many castes

The Samvedis, Pachkalshis and their neighbours were separate castes, but they lived inside a shared ritual economy, and the records of 1882 and 1920 let us see some of its threads.

At the centre of it stood the Palshe Brahmans. They called themselves Vajasaneyi Brahmans, followed the Madhyandin branch of the Yajurveda and traced their line to Yajnavalkya. They were priests, physicians and astrologers. Their surnames, Kavle, Joshi, Phatak, Pandit, Chhatre, Mogre, Kirtane, Purandare, Devdhar, Paranjpe, Upadhye, Kshirsagar and others, ran through the coastal villages. And they held, the Gazetteer said, “the privilege of fixing the auspicious time for marriage and thread ceremonies for all classes in Salsette, Bassein, Mahim and Dahanu”. [n08; Gazetteer 1882, p. 81] They served as priests for the Pachkalshis, for the Pathare Prabhus, and at the sunset weddings of the Mangela fishermen. [n35; Enthoven III p. 160, p. 250; Gazetteer XIII-1, p. 147] When the Samvedi bride’s family went to an astrologer’s house to fix the wedding day, the ceremony called tithinischay, the Gazetteer does not say whose house it was. [n08; Gazetteer 1882, p. 82–83] Given the Palshes’ privilege, it may often have been a Palshe’s; that is an inference, not a statement in the source.

Below the Brahmanical layer lay another. The Pachkalshis kept Cheda and other local spirits beside Ganpati, Shiv and Krishna. So did the Bhandaris, who offered Cheda goats and fowl, and the Agris, and the Son Kolis, whose family gods included Khanderao, Bhavani, Bhairav, Babdev, Vir, Kalkai, Cheda and Marubai. [n08; Gazetteer 1882, pp. 116, 130, 149–150] Cheda is a thread that runs across castes in the 1882 record. [n08; Inferences] The Gazetteer does not mention Cheda among the Samvedis, and nothing should be read into the silence either way. What it does record among the Samvedis is the worship of Sati on the fifth day after a birth, and that worship, as we shall see, crossed the line of religion as easily as Cheda crossed the line of caste. [n08; Gazetteer 1882, p. 82] [n34; Gaz XIII pp. 210–211]

The boundaries between castes were real but not fixed. The Vadvals’ split from the main Pachkalshi body was blamed on the admission of a Koli. Katkaris admitted Varlis, Kolis, Thakurs and Kunbis for five rupees. Malhar Kolis became Maratha Kunbis. [n35; Enthoven II p. 172, p. 257; Enthoven III p. 162] The coast’s communities moved, merged and divided over the centuries, and they argued about it in writing. The Palshes defended their rank with Peshwa-era sanads and the recognition of the Shankaracharya; the Vadvals defended their name with the Shankaracharya’s letters of 1836 and 1852. [n35; Enthoven I, pp. 248–249; Enthoven III, p. 162] In such a world, a community’s account of its own origin was never only memory. It was also a claim to rank, made against neighbours, and the Samvedis’ stories of Vedic antiquity and Shankaracharya’s favour should be heard in that key as well.

The ritual economy had one more feature that will matter in the next chapter. At death, the Samvedis gave food on a leaf plate to the village Mhar. [n08; Gazetteer 1882, p. 83] The Mhar was outside the caste order and below it, yet he had his place in its rites. When the Gazetteer later described the Christians of the district, it noted that Mhar converts in 1859 lived outside the villages and could not touch the wells or enter the church. [n37; Gaz. XIII-I pp. 201–202 and note 1] The caste order that the Samvedis lived in did not dissolve at the font. Much of it walked into the church with the converts.

The Samvedis in 1882: a portrait

We come now to the one detailed portrait of the Samvedis that the nineteenth century left. It is short, a page and a half of the Gazetteer’s population chapter, compiled from district officers’ returns in 1881–82. It is worth reading slowly, because almost everything later said about Samvedi life rests on it, and because it describes the Hindu side of a community whose Christian side had lived beside it for three hundred and fifty years.

Numbers and place. The census of 1872 returned 2,563 Samvedis, 1,323 males and 1,240 females, “found only in Bassein and Mahim”. In the same district there were 9,311 Palshes. [n08; Gazetteer 1882, p. 81–82] [n34; Corrections] Grierson’s estimate a generation later was 2,700. [n22; LSI vol. VII] These figures almost certainly count only the Hindu Samvedis; the Christians of Samvedi descent were counted as Christians. [n22; Inferences]

Appearance and character. “Sámvedis … speak incorrect Maráthi … strong, tall, and fair, with regular features … clean, neat, sober, thrifty and orderly.” [n08; Gazetteer 1882, p. 82] Colonial ethnography of this kind graded communities by appearance and virtue, and the reader should treat the adjectives as the opinion of an official in 1882. The opinion was favourable.

Work and houses. “Husbandmen and gardeners by craft, they live in one-storied houses with walls of wood planking and tiled roofs. Their daily food is rice pulse and vegetables.” [n08; Gazetteer 1882, p. 82] In the same volume the Christians’ houses are described as “substantial tile-roofed houses with walls of wooden planks, mud, or brick and stone”. [n34; Gaz XIII p. 204] The two descriptions match closely.

Speech. The Gazetteer recorded a few dialect markers in a footnote: áde for kothe, “where”; hád for kā, “why”; geltu for gelā hotā, “had gone”. [n08; Gazetteer 1882, p. 82 footnote]

Dress. “The men wear short waistcloths, a shoulder cloth wrapped round the body, and red broadcloth caps like Telugu Brahmans; on high days white turbans, coats and waistcloths with silk borders.” The women wore the Maratha bodice and robe. [n08; Gazetteer 1882, p. 82] [n34; Gaz XIII p. 82] That red cap will matter later. A modern polemical article claims the Portuguese forced converts to wear red caps and red saris as markers. [n07; Swarajya] The Gazetteer of 1882 shows Hindu Samvedi men wearing red broadcloth caps, and Christian fishermen wearing them too. [n34; Gaz XIII p. 82, p. 205] Whatever the red cap meant, it was not a mark the Portuguese pinned on converts alone. It looks, on this evidence, like a regional cap shared by the Samvedis and others, which some Christians kept. That is an inference from a sentence, and it should be checked against old drawings and photographs. [n34; Inferences]

Ornaments. Women’s gold ornaments were named: mudaga khudi for the head; lavanga bali, mugdya and gáthe for the ear; putlyáchi mál, vajratik, and flint, coral and gold-bead necklaces for the neck; tode, vále and phule for the feet. Boys wore silver. [n08; Gazetteer 1882, p. 82] Two of those words, mugdya and gathe, appear again in the Gazetteer’s list of Christian women’s ornaments a hundred and twenty pages later. The scanned text is rough and the reading should be checked against the page, but if it holds, the Hindu and Christian Samvedi women of 1882 wore jewellery with the same names. [n34; Gaz XIII p. 82, p. 205 n.1] That is not proof of common descent. It is shared material culture, which is what common descent would leave.

Birth. On the fifth day after a birth, the goddess Sati was worshipped; on the twelfth the child was named and the caste feasted. [n08; Gazetteer 1882, p. 82] Among the Christians of the same district, on the third or sixth night after a birth, the poorer families guarded against the spirit Sathi by strewing gram at the door. [n34; Gaz XIII pp. 210–211] The same spirit, under nearly the same name, watched over the cradle in both communities.

The thread ceremony. Before the age of ten, the boy stood on a raised earthen seat, bahule, with a cloth held between him and his father. When the chanting ended the cloth was pulled away and the drums beat. Then came the mock pilgrimage to Benares: after the boy had walked about fifty paces, his maternal uncle asked him not to go, and promised him his daughter in marriage. The boy came back and begged alms, and each guest gave him a pulse ball and two annas to a rupee. [n08; Gazetteer 1882, p. 82]

Marriage. Girls married before ten. A bride-price of Rs 200 to Rs 1,000 was paid to the girl’s father. The date was fixed at an astrologer’s house, tithinischay. The day before the wedding, twenty to thirty earthen pots were brought from a potter and worshipped. Rice-flour lamps were lit in a bamboo basket, zad, and held over the elders’ heads while the priest called “Vidávida ála ho”, “time for sandal and betel”. The food was pápads, vadás and shev sweets. The groom rode to the bride’s house on horseback. The couple stood on stools facing each other with a cloth between them, and when it was pulled aside they garlanded each other. A varghoda procession followed. Widow marriage was not allowed. [n08; Gazetteer 1882, p. 82–83]

Death. The dead were burned, except children under three. On the third day the ashes were gathered, and food on a leaf plate was given to the village Mhar. Mourning lasted ten days, with rice balls offered on the eleventh. A married but childless caste man, a vánzkuli, was taken outside the village, paid two rupees and fed rice, ámti and vadás, and was feasted again on the thirteenth; food cooked that day could not stay in the house. [n08; Gazetteer 1882, p. 83] No source read for this book says that Samvedis took ashes to Nirmal. [n34; Death]

Community. “They had no headman. Disputes were settled by majority vote, and fines were spent on feeding Brahmans. They send their boys to school and are on the whole prosperous.” [n08; Gazetteer 1882, p. 83]

Set beside the Koprad stone, that last detail is curious. The Gazetteer says the Samvedis had no caste headman, and in the same years it records a Samvedi Brahman as the headman of Koprad. [n03; TG-XIV] The two statements are not in conflict. One is about the caste’s own government, the other about a village office. A Samvedi could be a village patil while his caste settled its quarrels by majority. But the pairing shows what kind of community this was: Brahmins by name and by the Sama Veda, but farmers and gardeners by work, holding village offices, prosperous, schooling their sons.

Enthoven’s 1920 entry adds nothing but a line: “Samavedis are found only in the Thana district, where they chiefly grow garden crops.” That is the entire entry. [n34; Enthoven I, p. 249] [n35; Enthoven I] There is no Samvedi-Christian entry and no Kupari entry in Enthoven at all.

One more detail of the Gazetteer portrait changes how the whole Kupari origin debate should be heard. The Samvedis of 1882 were “husbandmen and gardeners”, not priests. [n34; Gaz XIII p. 82] Even if the Kuparis descend from Samvedi Brahmins, as a good part of the evidence suggests, that origin means descent from a landed farming community that kept Brahmin rites, not from a priestly class. The historian Kenneth Ballhatchet, quoted at second hand, found that by 1850 “it was primarily the cultivating classes” who were Catholic in the Konkan and Bombay. [n34; Ballhatchet via Pinto] A Samvedi origin and a cultivator origin are not opposed. The Samvedis were cultivators.

Wadi farming: sugarcane, betel, plantains

What did the Samvedi gardeners grow?

The community’s modern tradition, as reported on the Wikipedia page, tells it as a sequence: under Raja Pratap Bimba the Samvedis took to sugarcane and nagvel, betel; paddy was added in the late seventeenth century; flower gardens and wadis came in the eighteenth century as Bombay grew. [n08; Wikipedia: Samvedi] The sequence is plausible but unsourced, and its first step leans on the Bimba story already discussed.

The 1882 Gazetteer gives harder facts. Sugar was made in Bassein by “Pachkalshis, Malis, Native Christians and Samvedi Brahmans”. [n34; Gaz XIII p. 395] Plantains were dried at Agashi, Vatar and Koprad by about eighty-five families of Samvedi Brahmans, fifteen families of Pachkalshis and about seventy-five families of Native Christians “originally Samivedis and Pachkalshis”, who were “gardeners who grow the fruit”. [n34; Gaz XIII Part I p. 395] “The dried plantains”, the Places volume added, were “the best in the district”. [n34; Gaz XIV p. 2] Agashi drove “a great trade with Bombay in plantains and betel leaves”. [n21; Gaz XIV p. 2] From Virar station the island sent “molasses, rice and betel-leaves” north to Ankleshvar, Nadiad, Viramgam and Patan, and “large quantities of plantains and betel-leaves” south to Bombay. [n21; Gaz XIV p. 316] The Gazetteer called betel a chief watered crop of Mahim more than of Bassein, which fits a Samvedi community spread across both talukas. [n21; Gaz p. 292]

The dried plantain is still the emblem of these villages. Marathi sources say sukeli is made by sun-drying ripe bananas, mainly of the Rajeli variety, that Agashi is its centre, and that the orchards have declined over time. In Samvedi speech, the word itself is pronounced hukeli. [n20; mr.wikipedia; Vishwakosh snippet] A man from Nandakhal-Parsav, Pius Alex Rodrigues, who died in June 2026, is credited by the Marathi press with carrying Vasai sukeli to markets across India and abroad. [n20; eSakal] The fruit that Samvedi and Christian families dried side by side at Agashi in 1882 was still, in 2026, sold at the Nirmal yatra and shipped overseas.

All this was garden farming that needed water. In 1881–82 the Bassein sub-division had 191 ponds and 2,624 wells, twenty-five of them with steps. [n21; Gaz XIII-II p. 678–679] A 2011 blog from Bhuigaon described about forty large irrigation wells, bhowkals, maintained mostly by women, watering bananas, vegetables and flowers behind a steel cross. [n08; Mumbai Paused] The Gazetteer’s highest praise for the cultivators of Bassein went, in the end, to the Christians: “The most hardworking and skilful husbandmen are the Christians of Bassein”, who grew sugarcane and plantains and “turned the light sandy country about Bassein into an evergreen garden”. [n21; Gaz XIII-I p. 300] That sentence belongs to the Kuparis’ later story, but its roots are here, in the garden island and in a farming skill that the Samvedis and Pachkalshis had before anyone was baptised.

The making of a dialect

The speech of the Samvedis is the clearest evidence we have of the world they lived in, because a language records its contacts whether its speakers mean it to or not.

Grierson’s description of 1905 is the oldest systematic account. In Samvedi, he wrote, ch and chh become s and then h: so hakra, “son”, and hit, “field”. The genitive suffix is -ho, -ha or -a. Original s also becomes h. Strong masculine nouns end in -o in the nominative, “as in Gujarati and Konkani”. The dative is in -do, as in mansa-do, “to a man”, or else in the Marathi -la or -ti. The verb forms agree with Thana Marathi, and the past third person masculine ends in -o. Hence his conclusion: “a mechanical mixture of Konkan Marathi and Gujarati”. [n22; LSI vol. VII] [n34; LSI VII] He printed a specimen, the Parable of the Prodigal Son, as his Specimen No. 37, in Devanagari; the scanned text is unreadable, and the page image is needed to read it. [n22; Gaps] For the neighbouring Vadval dialect he recorded the same shift: s and sh become h, “probably like the sound of ch in loch”, so that hona is “gold” and hat is “seven”. [n22; LSI vol. VII]

The 1882 Gazetteer’s three markers fit his account: áde for “where”, hád for “why”, geltu for “had gone”. [n08]

More than a century later, the shift is still there. A 2024 paper that built a Kadodi–Marathi–English dictionary, “Suman”, from native speakers (949 entries and 942 sentence triples) noted that Kadodi lacks the vowels ऐ and औ and the consonants च, छ, ण and ष in its own inventory; that श and स are commonly replaced by ह; and that it prefers plain dentals to aspirated ones. Its examples: cow dung is haen (Marathi shen); “He makes a living by working as a labourer” is tou majuri karon pout bhartae (Marathi to majuri karun pot bharto); “He is sleeping” is tou nejlay. [n22; Kumar et al., WAT 2024] [n34; Dabre, Dabre and Pereira 2024] Marathi Wikipedia gives hukeli for sukeli and heni for sheni, standard ālo (“came”) becoming ālô and jevlo (“ate”) becoming jivlô, and the answer jivyāsā hāy, “I’ve eaten”, to the question “have you eaten?” [n20; mr.wikipedia: सामवेदी बोली] The s-to-h change Grierson heard about 1900 is a feature the speakers of the 2020s still have: a continuity of at least a hundred and twenty years. [n22; Inferences]

What made this dialect? The evidence allows a layered answer, with each layer marked for its strength.

The base is the Marathi of the Konkan coast. Grierson called its verb forms Thana Marathi, and every later description agrees. [n22]

Over that base lies a Gujarati layer: the -o nominatives, the s-to-h shift that Samvedi shares with Vadval and with Gujarati Bhili dialects, Gujarati words. Grierson saw it in Samvedi, Vadval, Phudagi and Mangela alike. [n22] Whether it came from a Gujarati origin of the speakers or from centuries of contact on a Gujarat-facing coast is not known. [n35; Inferences]

A Portuguese layer was added later, through the church. That is the subject of the next two chapters. Grierson’s Samvedi of 1905 is the Hindu Samvedis’ speech; he counted the Christians, including the Christian Samvedis, under a separate heading, “Kinstav”, as speakers of ordinary Marathi with Portuguese church and kinship words. [n34; Inferences] The modern Kupari dialect is best seen, on the evidence assembled for this book, as a Samvedi base plus Portuguese vocabulary, with later levelling towards standard Marathi. [n34; Takeaway, section 4] No linguist has published a historical study of how that happened. [n34; Gaps]

The dialect’s names deserve a line. Grierson called it Samvedi. Today it is also called Kadodi, and the Suman paper and Saamana both use the name. [n22] [n20] The origin of the word “Kadodi” is unknown to the researchers whose notes this book uses. [n34; Gaps] The modern speaker counts, about 50,000 according to Saamana and about 60,000 according to Ethnologue as reported by Wikipedia, are not comparable with Grierson’s 2,700, because they include the Christians whom Grierson counted elsewhere. [n22; Inferences] [n20] Two scholars have given the dialect book-length care: Dr Naresh Naik of Virar, whose Samvedi Boli – Sanrachana ani Swarup won awards, and Paul Rumav, a teacher from Nandakhal, who edited collections of Samvedi folk songs and proverbs. [n20; mr.wikipedia] Their work belongs to the later chapters of this part.

What the Samvedi world was, on the eve

Gather the evidence together and a picture forms, with its edges drawn honestly.

By the early sixteenth century the garden island of Sopara and the strip north to Agashi were a land of gardens, wells and creek landings. Sopara itself was long past its great age. Before 1300 Thana had become the chief trade centre of the coast, and Bassein, six miles south, had begun to rise. [n37; Gaz. XIV pp. 28, 322] The Gujarat Sultanate controlled Bassein from the fifteenth century. The Portuguese traveller Duarte Barbosa, in 1514, described “Baxay” as a town of Moors and Gentiles, a good seaport of the King of Gujarat. [n37; Gaz. XIV p. 28] Arnala, on its island at the mouth of the Vaitarna, was a strong fort in the hands of the Gujarat Muslims, with domes and Saracenic arches. [n37; Gaz. XIV p. 10] The Gazetteer judged that Thana “seems never to have been the scene of any forcible conversion of Hindus to Islam” in its four centuries of Muslim rule, though another sentence on the same pages speaks of local Hindus converted “by the compulsion of Ahmednagar or Moghal rulers”, and the two are not reconciled. [n37; Gaz. XIII-I pp. 66, 216–217] Of Sopara’s stone temples, “many are said to have been destroyed by the Musalmans in the beginning of the fourteenth century”, but “enough were spared, or repaired, to excite the admiration” of the first Portuguese. [n37; Gaz. XIV p. 323] That claim carries “are said to”, and its footnote does not clearly support it; it is tradition. [n37]

In this world the Samvedis can be placed, with varying confidence:

  • As a community of the Sama Veda, a name attested on this coast since 1026, though not continuously and not, before 1882, in these villages by name. [n03]
  • As landholding farmers and gardeners of the garden island and the Agashi strip, with village offices, prosperous enough in 1882 to school their sons. [n08] [n34]
  • As neighbours, and in places partners, of the Pachkalshi gardeners, the Palshe priests, the Bhandari tappers and the Koli fishermen. [n08] [n34]
  • As speakers of a Konkan Marathi strongly marked by Gujarati, on a coast that shipped its produce to Gujarat. [n22] [n37]
  • As devotees of Nirmal and of a Shankaracharya buried there, in their own memory. [n08]

And they cannot be placed, on the evidence, as immigrants from Odisha, as companions of King Bimba, or as heirs of the Nestorian Christians of Sopara. Those are stories, and they are told here as stories.

There is one more thing the evidence shows, and it is the bridge to the next chapter. When the Gazetteer’s officials walked through Sopara in the 1880s they wrote: “The husbandmen of Sopara are Christians, many of them converted Samvedi Brahmans; Musalmans …; and Hindus, chiefly Samvedi Brahmans and Bhandaris.” [n34; Gaz XIV p. 315] At Agashi, Vatar and Koprad they counted eighty-five Hindu Samvedi families and seventy-five Christian families who had been Samvedis and Pachkalshis. [n34; Gaz XIII p. 395] The Samvedi community of 1882 was a community split down the middle. One half kept the thread, the Sama Veda and the fires of cremation; the other half kept the cross, the Latin Mass and the Christian grave. They grew the same plantains, dried them in the same yards, wore ornaments with the same names, and feared the same spirit at the door of a newborn child.

That split began on a particular day, at a particular place, with particular men. In 1535 a Franciscan friar named António do Porto opened an orphanage for forty boys at Agashi, under the invocation of Our Lady of Light. [n34; Da Cunha 1876, pp. 159–160] Five years later it was burned. What happened in the two centuries that followed, and why some Samvedi families went to the font while their brothers did not, is the story of the next chapter.
