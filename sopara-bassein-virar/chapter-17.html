Period covered: from the creek and the island before any town stood here, through Bassein’s first certain notice (c. 1507), the Portuguese walled city (1536–1739), Maratha Bajipur (1739–1818), the British station town and municipality (1818–1947), to Vasai West in November 2026

In this chapter. This chapter takes one town and walks through it from its creek to its fort, its captains, its siege and its modern streets, as a model of how the coast’s story looks from a single place.

The door dated 1720

On the seaward side of Vasai Fort there is a gateway with a pair of massive teak doors. They are cased with iron bars and studded with iron spikes, the kind of doors a garrison hangs when it expects to be rammed. When the compilers of the Bombay Gazetteer walked through the ruins for the volume printed in 1882, they noticed something on one of the leaves, half hidden behind an iron bar: a date cut into the wood, “The 20th November 1720”, in Portuguese DO 20 DE NOV DE 1720. Gerson da Cunha, the Goan physician and antiquarian who had published his own survey of the ruins six years earlier, recorded the same inscription. [n32; Gaz XIV pp. 38–39; da Cunha pp. 213–214]

That date is the only one on the doors, and it is a late one. By November 1720 the walled city of Baçaim had stood for nearly two centuries. Its first stone had been laid in 1536; its great curtain of walls had been finished in 1554; its churches had been built, burned by storms, rebuilt and enlarged; its captains had come and gone on three-year terms for six generations. The men who hung those doors in 1720 were refitting a city that already felt the pressure of the Maratha power inland. Nineteen years later, in May 1739, the gate they had strengthened was opened to let the defeated garrison march out to the boats, with the honours of war. [n32; Gaz XIV p. 34; da Cunha pp. 148–149]

Step through that gate today, and on the left, just inside, there is a small temple of Hanuman. In 1882 the Gazetteer called it “a small temple of Hanuman”, and da Cunha, who had little patience for it, called its architecture “mean” and read it as a Maratha memento: “the monkey-god has kept his ascendancy.” A local tradition, recorded on a modern Marathi heritage website and not in any older source, says the image was consecrated on 27 July 1739, ten weeks after the fall. [n32; n10; Gaz XIV pp. 38–39; da Cunha p. 214; durgbharari.in/vasai] Whether or not that date is right, the arrangement at the gate is a fair summary of this chapter: a Portuguese door, a Maratha god, and a ruin that later centuries leased to sugar planters, protected as a national monument, filmed for Bollywood songs and a Coldplay video, and still fill once a year with Catholic families keeping the feast of a saint who studied here.

This chapter is the story of that place: the town of Vasai, the Bassein of the British and the Baçaim of the Portuguese, and the fort that is its oldest monument. It goes in time order, from the creek and the island to the Gujarat port, the Portuguese city, the siege of 1739 as it looked from inside the walls, Maratha Bajipur, the empty ruin of the early nineteenth century, the new town that grew half a mile inland around a railway road and a municipality, and the twentieth- and twenty-first-century town. It ends in Vasai West in November 2026. Along the way it describes, as fairly as the record allows, every community that has lived in and around the town: the Christians of the old parishes, among them Kuparis, Vadvals, Kolis and the wider East Indian community; Samvedi, Palshe, Chitpavan, Karhada and Deshastha Brahmans; Pachkalshis; the Kolis of the shore; the Lad Vanis and the betel-nut Vanis of Papdi; Muslims; Parsis; Jews; Jains; and the migrants of the last forty years.

A word on sources. The deepest description of the fort and the old town is in two books of the 1870s and 1880s: J. Gerson da Cunha’s Notes on the History and Antiquities of Chaul and Bassein (Bombay, 1876) and the “Bassein” entry in the Gazetteer of the Bombay Presidency, Volume XIV, Thana: Places of Interest (1882), compiled under James M. Campbell. Both quote earlier travellers, among them Pyrard de Laval (1607), Pietro della Valle (1623), John Fryer (1675), Gemelli Careri (1695) and Anquetil du Perron (1760), and the Portuguese chronicler Bocarro (1634), whose book this study did not read directly. [n32; n15; n36] The other Gazetteer volumes on Thana (XIII, Parts I and II) give the town’s population, trade and communities. For the last century the record thins to census tables, a 2008 heritage survey by the Collective Research Initiatives Trust (CRIT) for the Mumbai Metropolitan Region Heritage Conservation Society, parish pages, newspapers and Wikipedia. [n40; n28; n29] Where the evidence is a nineteenth-century OCR scan with damaged digits, a tradition, a blog or a single newspaper snippet, this chapter says so. Where there is nothing, it says that too.

The broad history of Baçaim is told in Chapters 07 and 08 of this book, the Maratha period in Chapter 09, the British period in Chapters 11 to 12, and the modern city in Chapters 14 to 15. The conversions and the Kupari story are told in Part Seven (K1 to K7), and the churches as a whole in T4. This chapter does not retell those stories at length. It stays with the town and the fort themselves: their ground, their walls, their streets, their people, and what became of them.

I. The land and the creek

An island that is no longer an island

Before there was a town, there was a shape of land and water. The coast north of the Bombay islands is broken by two great inlets. To the south is the creek the British called the Bassein river, today’s Vasai creek, the mouth of the Ulhas. To the north, about twelve miles up, is the wide estuary of the Vaitarna, which the Gazetteer called “the most extensive inlet in the north Konkan”. Between them lies a strip of low coast about three miles broad. [n11; n12; n39; Gaz XIV pp. 10, 314–315]

In the 1880s that strip was still separated from the mainland by a winding backwater. It left the Bassein creek a little above the railway bridge and wound north until it met the Vaitarna. The people of the coast had three names for it, one for each stretch: the Manikpur creek in the south, the Sopara creek in the middle, and the Bolinj creek in the north. Because of that backwater the whole strip had been an island. “Under the Portuguese,” the Gazetteer wrote, “the whole of this rich strip, with its patches of salt marsh, was known as the island of Bassein, and, before Portuguese times, as the island of Sopara.” [n11; n39; Gaz XIV pp. 314–315]

The renaming tells the story of this coast in four words. For more than a thousand years the island belonged, in men’s minds, to Sopara, the ancient Shurparaka, the port in its middle; then, from the sixteenth century, to Bassein at its southern tip. The town at the southern end took over the island’s name as it took over its trade.

The island was already shrinking into the mainland when the Portuguese held it. In 1728 a Portuguese report complained that the creek between Sopara and Bolinj could be forded at low water for about 4,000 feet. General Louis de Mello Pereira had built watch-towers of coconut-palm stems and a bridge across the creek at Gokirva, the modern Gokhivare, and the writer warned that unless the channel was dug out “as deep as the height of a man” it would silt up level with the fields. [n12; n40; Gaz XIV p. 322] It was not dug out. A century and a half later the Gazetteer found that the backwater, “once a considerable arm of the sea”, had silted fast, “especially since the long railway embankment has dammed the flow of water from the south”. At Sopara the creek was dry. Boats of ten or twelve tons could still come up from the south as far as Gas (the Gass of this book) and from the north as far as Bolinj. [n11; n39; Gaz XIV p. 315] Today the railway towns of Vasai Road, Nallasopara and Virar stand on or beside that old backwater, and the island of Bassein is part of the mainland. [n12; n39]

The southern tip: creek, island and sand

The town of Vasai stands at the southern tip of that former island, where the land meets the Vasai creek and the open sea. The fort sits right on the water: the seaward half of the walls faces the creek mouth, and the river-side wall of the Misericórdia church rose straight from the shore. [n32; Gaz XIV pp. 38, 40] The site gave a garrison what it wanted: a harbour, a landing that small sea-going craft could reach, and water on two sides.

In the creek itself, just south of the town, lies Panju, an island village. When the railway was built across the creek in the 1860s, Panju divided the crossing in two: a southern bridge of about 1,314 metres in 69 spans and a northern bridge of about 476 metres in 25 spans, the “two spans of 69 and 25 sixty-foot girders on the Bassein channel” in the Gazetteer’s own words. [n05; n21; Gaz XIII-I pp. 328–329] Panju had 1,358 people in the 2011 census, a rural village in the middle of the creek. [n12] In the 1940s it was one of the places where the freedom fighter Augustine Koli of Uttan worked underground. [n16; n25]

The land behind the town was what made it rich. The Gazetteer described the coast strip as “rich alluvial soil”, watered by Persian wheels from shallow wells, with plantains, sugarcane and coconuts the chief products. [n21; Gaz XIII-II p. 679] It divided the garden strip into three belts, which it called by the Marathi word agar: Agashi in the north, Sopara in the middle and Bassein in the south. [n11; Gaz XIV p. 314] The agar of Bassein is the ground of the next chapter, which walks through its villages one by one. For this chapter it matters as the reason the town existed after its military life was over: it was the market and port of one of the most productive garden belts in the Konkan.

What the name means

“Vasai, that is the settlement,” the Gazetteer explained in a footnote. The Muslims, it said, called the place Basai, and the Portuguese Baçaim. Da Cunha thought the “most plausible” derivation was from the Sanskrit root vas, “to dwell”, and recorded a further conjecture, Vanavasai, “forest residence”. [n37; Gaz XIV p. 28 n.; da Cunha p. 137] The English wrote Bassein, from the Portuguese. The Marathas, after 1739, gave the town a new name, Bajipur, “Baji Rao’s city”, which did not outlast them. [n32; Gaz XIV p. 34] The names follow the rulers. Vasai, the oldest of them, is the one that came back.

The Marathi encyclopedia’s entry on Vasai lists the variants more fully: Bassein, Bashya and Bajipur, and in some places Basai, Bakaim, Baksai and Bushi. [n62] It gave the town 52,398 people in 1981. [n62]

II. In the shadow of Sopara

“It can hardly have been a place of consequence”

For most of the history told in this book, there was no Bassein worth the name. The great port of this coast was Sopara, six miles to the north, the Shurparaka of the Mauryan edicts, the Buddhist texts and the Shilahara grants, whose story Chapters 04 and 05 tell. The Gazetteer put the relationship in one sentence: “As Bassein lies only six miles south of Sopara it can hardly have been a place of consequence so long as Sopara (A.D. 1100) remained a centre of trade.” [n32; n15; Gaz XIV p. 28]

What little earlier record there is, the Gazetteer treated with care. “A doubtful reference makes Bassein the head of a district under the Devgiri Yadavs. There is no certain notice till 1507.” It also corrected a mistake: a Kanheri cave inscription once read as naming “Vasai” had been misread, as the Indian epigrapher Bhagvanlal Indraji showed. [n37; Gaz XIV p. 28] A Wikipedia page claims the Yadavas made Bassein a district seat from 1184 to 1318; it gives no primary source for that, and this chapter does not repeat it as fact. [n04]

Some older stones do touch the town’s neighbourhood. A Shilahara grant of AD 1255 (Shaka 1177), found at the Relbav pond in Sandor, three miles north of the later fort, gives “a garden in the part called Nivayi within the limits of Sandor village” to the astrologer Kheidev; it names a headquarters, Nagapur, and a high road. [n37; Gaz XIV p. 402] One of the people-notes behind this book lists “the Shilahara king Mallikarjuna (Bassein stone, 1160)” among the documented rulers of the Bassein area, without giving the stone’s text. [n25] These stones show that the land around the future town was settled, gardened, granted and taxed under the Shilaharas. They do not show a port town at the creek mouth.

When the town rose

Why did the southern tip rise when it did? The notes behind this book give two causes, both of them about water.

The first is that Sopara’s own access to the sea was failing. Its strength had been its position on the inner backwater, guarded from pirates and hill raiders and joined to both the Vaitarna and the Ulhas. As the backwater silted, the old landings moved outward, first to Gas, Bolinj and Vagholi, and the deep-water anchorage at the creek mouth became more valuable. [n37; n11; Gaz XIV pp. 315–316, 322] By about 1300 the chief trading centre of the whole Thana coast was in any case no longer Sopara but Thana town. [n37; Gaz XIV pp. 28, 322]

The second cause was the arrival of a sea power that needed a fortified harbour. The Gujarat sultans, who held this coast from about 1390, and then the Portuguese after them, wanted a place where ships could be inspected, taxed and protected, and where a garrison could be supplied by sea. The creek mouth at Vasai offered exactly that. [n03; n37; n36] The Portuguese, as later sections show, stripped Sopara of its stones to build their new city. “Sopara’s stone was reused,” one of the notes summarises the Gazetteer: temples and lake banks were stripped in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries and the stone carried to Bassein. [n37; Gaz XIV p. 323] The old port literally went into the walls of the new one.

III. The Gujarat port, 1507–1534

First notice

The first “certain notice” of Bassein that the Gazetteer could find was in the reign of Mahmud Begada, Sultan of Gujarat (1459–1511). Its Part II volume gives the year 1503, when, by the Persian chronicle Mirat-i-Ahmadi, Begada “effected his designs against Bassein and Bombay”; the Places volume gives 1507. [n03; Gaz XIII-II pp. 442–443; Gaz XIV p. 28] Either way, Bassein enters the written record in the first decade of the sixteenth century as a Gujarat possession.

Those were the years in which the Portuguese had just appeared in the Indian Ocean. Bassein’s timber, the Gazetteer says, was sent to Mecca and Egypt about 1505–07 to build the Mamluk fleet that sailed against them. In 1507 the Portuguese seem to have stirred up the Hindu chiefs of the coast, and Begada had to “settle disturbances at Bassein”. [n03; Gaz XIII-II p. 449] Under the sultans, the officials of these coast towns were often Hindus: the governors of Chaul in 1503 and of Surat in 1514 were Hindus, and a “Gujarat and Bassein minister” wrote to Albuquerque in 1510. [n03; Gaz XIII-II pp. 449–450 nn.]

“Baxay”: a town of Moors and Gentiles

The earliest description of the town is by Duarte Barbosa, a Portuguese official who wrote about 1514. He called it “Baxay”, “a town of Moors and Gentiles, a good seaport belonging to the king of Gujarat”, with much shipping, among it boats from Malabar laden with areca nuts, coconuts and spices. [n03; n32; n37; Gaz XIV p. 28] “Moors” in Portuguese usage meant Muslims; “Gentiles” meant Hindus. So the first picture of Bassein is of a mixed trading town, Muslim and Hindu, with a harbour busy enough to draw ships from the far south of India.

Who those Muslims were, the Gazetteer suggests elsewhere. The oldest Muslim community of the coast, the Konkani Muslims, descended “chiefly” from Arab and Persian refugees, merchants and adventurers who had settled on the coast in the eighth, ninth and tenth centuries; their home speech was a dialect of Marathi. The Portuguese physician Garcia d’Orta wrote of the “Naitas of Bassein”, who had married local women and were rich traders when the Portuguese came. [n37; n08; Gaz XIII-I pp. 216–217; Gaz XIV p. 315 n.] The mosques of Bassein were endowed: the treaty of 1534 mentions 5,000 larins of revenue assigned to mosques, which “was to continue to be so applied”. [n37; Danvers I p. 416] Who the Hindus of the town were in 1514, Barbosa does not say, and no other source in the notes does either.

On the eve of the Portuguese take-over the land was “guarded by stockades and fortified posts”. Land revenue was taken in kind, with cash cesses on coconut oil, opium, cotton, palm spirit, vegetables, fish, sugarcane and betel, and on the trades of butchers, dyers, fishermen and shepherds. [n03; Gaz XIII-II p. 451] Da Cunha records that the first half-year’s revenue the Portuguese collected from the Bassein district in 1535 came to 158,475 fedeas, raised from exactly those taxes, including a tax on “Koli fishermen”. [n36; da Cunha pp. 158–159] That list is the first glimpse of the working people around the town: fishers, toddy-tappers, sugar and betel growers, oil-pressers, dyers, butchers and shepherds.

Raids, and Malik Tokan’s citadel

The Portuguese came first as traders, then as raiders. Their factory at Bassein is dated 1526 by the Gazetteer, though one source cited on Wikipedia gives 1518. [n04; n32; Gaz XIV pp. 28–29] In 1528 or 1529, after destroying the Gujarat fleet of Alishah near Bandra, Heitor da Silveira entered “the difficult Bassein river” by night, stormed its cannon-defended works, beat Alishah’s 3,500 men, and plundered and burnt the town. The “lord of Thana” became tributary to Portugal. [n03; Gaz XIII-II pp. 450–451; Gaz XIV p. 28 n.] In 1530 and 1531 the Portuguese burnt Agashi, ten miles north, with 300 boats, and António de Saldanha burnt the coast towns again on his way back from a failed attack on Diu. [n03; n11; Gaz XIII-II p. 451; Gaz XIV p. 1]

“To put a stop to these raids”, Sultan Bahadur Shah ordered Malik Tokan, the governor of Diu and son of the famous admiral Malik Ayaz, to fortify Bassein. The Gazetteer dates this to 1532; Portuguese sources put it in early 1533. Malik Tokan built a citadel with ramparts and a salt-water ditch and garrisoned it with 15,000 men by one count, 12,000 by another. Nuno da Cunha, the Portuguese governor, came against it with 150 sail and about 4,000 men. Tokan offered peace and was refused. Diogo da Silveira and Manuel de Macedo scaled the walls, and the defenders fled. The Portuguese took 400 guns, razed the citadel and sailed away. [n03; n18; Gaz XIV pp. 28–29; Gaz XIII-II p. 451]

That razed citadel is the “fort at Vasai before the Portuguese” that modern accounts sometimes mention. The notes find no trace of it in the present walls, which are Portuguese work begun in 1536. [n03] One later Portuguese writer, Bocarro (1634), as reported by the Gazetteer, described a small round bastion by the pillory yard as the former residence of the “Moors”, which suggests that the Portuguese thought they were building over a Muslim stronghold. [n32; Gaz XIV p. 39] That is the closest the record comes.

The Marathi Vishwakosh adds two details to this story. It says that when the Portuguese first built their factory in 1526 and improved it in 1529 and 1531, they “destroyed the temple of Bibhaleshwar Mahadev”, a detail no other source in the notes gives. [n62] And it says Nuno da Cunha attacked Malik Tokan’s fort with 150 vessels and 400 soldiers. The Gazetteer figure the paragraph above uses is about 4,000 men, so the Marathi text has either dropped a zero or recorded a smaller force; the two should be set side by side, not merged. [n62] The entry also calls the Gujarat ruler who ordered the fort “Muhammad Shah”, which is a slip for Bahadur Shah, the sultan of the year. [n62]

The treaty

What the Portuguese could not win by raiding, they won by diplomacy. Bahadur Shah was at war with the Mughal emperor Humayun and with the Rajputs, and Nuno da Cunha was negotiating with Humayun. “To win them from their alliance with Humayun,” the Gazetteer explains, Bahadur ceded “Bassein and its dependencies” to the Portuguese. Gujarat ships bound for the Red Sea had to call at Bassein for a Portuguese pass, and call again on the way back to pay dues; Gujarat was to build no warships; and there was to be no alliance with the Turks. [n03; n32; Gaz XIII-II p. 451; Gaz XIV pp. 28–29] The modern standard date is 23 December 1534, the treaty signed aboard the galleon São Mateus; the Gazetteer gives 1533, and a second treaty of 25 October 1535 is said to have confirmed the surrender of Bassein and the seven islands of Bombay. [n03; n04] The horse trade was written in: for the first three years after the fortress was finished, all horses from the Straits of Mecca and Arabia were to come to Bassein. [n36; Danvers I p. 416]

The dependencies listed with Bassein included Salsette, Thana, Bandra, Mahim and the islands of Bombay. [n04] So began the strange arrangement that this book returns to more than once: for 127 years, until the island passed to England in Catherine of Braganza’s dowry in 1661, Bombay was a minor dependency of Baçaim. [n04; n15]

IV. Building the walled city, 1536–1600

The first stone, 1536

At the end of March 1536, the Portuguese historian of India F. C. Danvers relates, Nuno da Cunha “went to Bassein”, “found all the materials ready”, “and having fixed upon a site” began the fortress. [n37; Danvers I] The man he left in charge was Garcia de Sá. A secondary account adds that António Galvão laid the first cornerstone. [n04]

The oldest dated inscription in the fort records the beginning. It was cut on a stone of a ruined bastion near the citadel, and da Cunha found it in the 1870s with the help of J. H. Littlewood, who then leased the fort’s interior:

HO PRIMEIRO CAPITAM QUE EDIFICOU ESTA FORTALEZA FOI GARCIA DE SA POR MANDADO DO GOVERNADOR NUNO DA CUNHA ERA DE 1536

“The first Captain who built this fortress was Garcia de Sá, by command of the Governor Nuno da Cunha, in the year 1536.” [n32; n15; da Cunha p. 217; Gaz XIV p. 39] Bocarro, writing in 1634, described the bastion that carried it as a small round work by the pillory yard, unmanned in his day. [n32; Gaz XIV p. 39]

The first fort was not the walled city that survives. The Catholic Encyclopedia of 1907 says Nuno da Cunha “founded the city in 1536” and built the fort in its south-west corner; that small citadel, round in plan, was the nucleus around which the city grew. Fryer in 1675 still distinguished “the Fort in the middle of the City”, which was circular, from the city walls around it. [n04; n15; Fryer/Crooke I pp. 190–193]

The walls of 1554

Eighteen years later the city got its curtain. The inscription on the bastion of São Sebastião records that it was built on 22 February 1554, in the reign of King João III, under the Viceroy Dom Afonso de Noronha and the Captain Francisco de Sá. By 1882 the inscribed stone was lying neglected near the land gateway. [n32; Gaz XIV pp. 42–43] A secondary account calls the 1554 work “a new fortress… enclosing a whole town”. [n04] The name of that bastion is the name the whole fortress carried: the Fortaleza de São Sebastião de Baçaim. [n04] It is also, as Section IX tells, the bastion through which the Marathas broke in, 185 years later.

Fortification went on through the century. The Gazetteer records that fort-building “was begun again in 1590” and finished “about the close of the century”. [n32; Gaz XIV p. 34 n.] What survives is substantially that late-sixteenth-century work, patched in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries.

The shape of the fort

The best physical description is the Gazetteer’s of 1882. The wall, it says, is about one and a half miles round and forms an irregular decagon. It is of stone, 30 to 35 feet high, rising to 45 feet on the west, and “not more than five feet thick”. At each of its ten corners stands a four-sided bastion. There were three entrances: two main double gateways and a postern. Inside, the seaward half was built up and the landward third was empty. [n32; Gaz XIV p. 38] Fryer had made the same estimate of size two centuries earlier: “The City is a Mile and half round.” [n15; Fryer/Crooke I pp. 190–193]

The Gazetteer gives the ten bastions’ names in a footnote:

  • Nossa Senhora dos Remédios (Our Lady of Remedies)
  • Reis Magos (the Three Kings)
  • São Tiago (St James)
  • São Gonçalo
  • Madre de Deus (Mother of God)
  • São João (St John)
  • Elefante (the OCR reads “Klephante”; “Elephante” is the likely reading)
  • São Pedro (St Peter)
  • São Paulo (St Paul)
  • São Sebastião (St Sebastian)

[n32; Gaz XIV p. 38 n. 2] The notes did not find a plan that places each bastion on the walls, or the dimensions and dates of any bastion other than São Sebastião (1554) and the 1536 stone. Which bastion was “Elephante” and which “Madre de Deus” is not recorded in the sources read. [n32]

Not every description agrees. The same Gazetteer, on another page, quotes a late-sixteenth-century account, from the Portuguese Cronista de Tissuary via da Cunha, that speaks of “eleven bastions” mounting 90 pieces of ordnance (27 of them bronze) and 70 mortars (7 bronze), with 21 gunboats of 16 to 18 guns each. [n32; Gaz XIV p. 30; da Cunha p. 209] Anquetil du Perron, who saw the fort in 1760, called it “a regular hexagon” with bastions carrying nine guns on each face. [n32; Gaz XIV p. 34 n.] Fryer in 1675 counted “Forty two great Guns” on the outworks and in the citadel, and thought the place “strong enough against the Indians, but not able to endure an European Foe”. [n15; Fryer/Crooke I pp. 190–193] The safest summary is the Gazetteer’s own walk-round of 1882: ten bastions, two gates and a postern, with the “eleven” and the “hexagon” as variants to be noted rather than resolved. [n32]

The gates

The two main gateways were the Porta do Mar, the Sea Gate, on the creek side, and the Porta do Campo, the Land Gate, facing the fields. [n32; Gaz XIV p. 38] A secondary source calls the land gate Porta da Terra. [n04] The Sea Gate’s teak doors, with their iron casing and the date of 1720, are the doors of this chapter’s opening. The Land Gate’s woodwork was broken by 1882. The postern, behind the Franciscan cloisters, had been closed by the bastion of São Sebastião. [n32; Gaz XIV pp. 38–39] A modern Marathi heritage site speaks of a “Bhui Darwaza”, with a newer Hanuman temple and a lamp tower beside it; it does not say which of the gates that name belongs to. [n10; durgbharari.in/vasai] Some web pages call the São Sebastião bastion “Porta Pia”; the notes found no such name in the Gazetteer or in da Cunha. [n32]

Fryer wrote that the city “has a Gate for every Wind”. [n15; Fryer/Crooke I pp. 190–193] That is a traveller’s flourish; the walls had two great gates, not four. By 1882 two more openings had been cut through for the road to the landing place, and there were one or two breaches along the sea face. [n32; Gaz XIV p. 38]

The empty third

Why was a third of the space inside the walls left empty? Da Cunha offered three explanations, and the Gazetteer repeated them without choosing. The open ground may have been kept clear as a field of fire against an enemy who had got over the wall; it may have been a rice field to feed a garrison in a siege; or it may simply have been emptied by the plague that struck the city in the 1690s, which the traveller Gemelli Careri described. [n32; da Cunha p. 213; Gaz XIV p. 38] The record does not settle it.

V. Inside the walls: a walk through the city

The Gazetteer of 1882 described the ruins as a walk from the Sea Gate to the Land Gate, and da Cunha, six years earlier, had transcribed most of the inscriptions on the way. Following their route gives the best picture of the Portuguese city that the evidence allows. What follows takes each building in their order and adds what the earlier travellers saw when the city was alive.

The Hanuman shrine and the Matriz of St Joseph

Just inside the Sea Gate, on the left, is the Hanuman temple of the opening, a Maratha addition. [n32; Gaz XIV pp. 38–39]

Beyond it stood the principal church of the city, the Matriz, the mother church, dedicated to St Joseph. Writers sometimes called it a cathedral; Hull, the historian of the Bombay mission, insisted it was “a cathedral wrongly so-called, in reality a matriz”. [n37; Hull I pp. 4–5] Its history begins with a royal letter. In March 1546, under the Viceroy Dom João de Castro, King João III ordered a church of St Joseph built, and directed that 3,000 pardaos “formerly spent on mosques” be used to support its clergy. [n32; Gaz XIV pp. 38–39; da Cunha pp. 214–216] The Gazetteer says plainly that “certain Musalman mosques were pulled down and in their place a cathedral of St Joseph was built”. [n10; Gaz XIV, Bassein] One secondary source says the Franciscans founded the Matriz in 1547; another says its dedication was São João, not São José. [n09] The inscriptions favour St Joseph.

The lintel inscription records a rebuilding:

No anno de 1601, sendo Arcebispo Primaz o Illmo. Sr. Dom Frei Aleixo de Menezes, e vigário o Pe. Pedro Galvão Pereira, se reformou esta Matriz

“In the year 1601, the Most Illustrious Dom Frei Aleixo de Menezes being Archbishop Primate, and Father Pedro Galvão Pereira vicar, this Matriz was rebuilt.” To the right of the main altar a black Latin tombstone records that the bones of Pedro Galvão, who “managed and enlarged this temple”, had been brought here; he died at Goa on 19 March 1618. At the west end lay the half-buried tomb of Antonio de Almeida de Sampaio. [n32; Gaz XIV pp. 38–39; da Cunha pp. 214–216]

In 1634, Bocarro recorded the Matriz’s staff: a vicar, four canons, two choir boys, a treasurer, four singers and one instrumentalist. [n32; Gaz XIV p. 39 n.] Gemelli Careri heard mass on 11 February 1695 in what he called “the parish church of Our Lady da Sé”. [n32; da Cunha p. 216] By the 1870s the tower stair had decayed so far that two steps crumbled under da Cunha when he tried to climb it. [n32; da Cunha pp. 214–216] A Bombay journalist who visited in 1987 described the Matriz’s “ruined slender tower” standing beside the Dominican church’s. [n09]

The great square and the State House

At the end of the street to the left of the Sea Gate opened the great square, which served as the market. On it stood the State House, the seat of government. [n32; Gaz XIV p. 39] Fryer saw it in use in 1675 and left the most human sentence anyone wrote about Portuguese Bassein:

“towards the Market appears a State-house Piatzed, where the Governour convocates the Fidalgos every Morning upon consultation, in which they all stand, a Chair not being permitted the Governour, though Gouty: Towards Evening they meet there to Game.”

[n15; n32; Fryer/Crooke I pp. 190–193; Gaz XIV p. 39; da Cunha p. 218] Every morning the gentry of the city stood in council with their governor, who was not allowed to sit even when gout tormented him; every evening they came back to gamble. Nothing else in the record catches the formality and the idleness of the Court of the North so well.

The citadel, the palaces and the granary

Beyond the square stood the citadel, the old round fort, with a portal da Cunha described in detail: Corinthian capitals, a Maltese cross, a coat of arms, a sphere (the armillary sphere that was a Portuguese royal emblem), an “I.H.S.” niche, and the date 1606. The fluted column shafts had been carried off, by da Cunha’s time, to adorn a village church. [n32; da Cunha pp. 216–218, 220; Gaz XIV pp. 39–40] Beyond the portal lay the palaces of the General of the North and of the Captain, the factory, and a very large ambar, a rice granary. [n32; Gaz XIV pp. 39–40]

The granary is worth a pause. A walled city of fidalgos, friars and soldiers did not feed itself; its rice came in from the villages of the district, the aldeias held by Portuguese landholders and worked by their tenants. A great store inside the walls was the city’s insurance against siege. [n32; n36] The villages that filled it, and the people who worked them, are the subject of Section VII and of the next chapter.

The portico of 1631

Further on stood a large building with a portico of five steps and four pillars. What it was is disputed. Some have called it the Captain’s house, others the court of justice; the Gazetteer thought it was probably the Augustinian church and convent. It carries two inscriptions. The first reads, in translation: “Governing the State of India the Viceroy Dom Miguel de Noronha, Count of Linhares, this portal was built, on which St Francis Xavier was placed as patron of this city. 10 May 1631.” The second names the Captain Gaspar de Mello de Miranda and three aldermen, Gonçalo Coelho da Silva, Pero Ferreira and João Boto Machado, in 1631. [n32; Gaz XIV p. 40; da Cunha pp. 219–220] Da Cunha noted that Bocarro, writing in 1634, still named St Sebastian as the city’s patron, so the change to Xavier may not have taken hold at once. [n32; da Cunha p. 220 n.] The OCR of both books is rough on the day; “10 May” is the English reading in both. [n32]

The inscription is a small piece of civic history. Baçaim had a captain and a council of aldermen, the vereadores of a Portuguese municipal chamber, who put their names on public works alongside the viceroy’s.

The Misericórdia

On the river-side wall rose “a massive long pile”, the church and hospital of the Misericórdia, the Holy House of Mercy, the lay brotherhood that ran charities in every Portuguese town. It had a square cloistered courtyard and a finely cut front with a Maltese cross and dragons on the escutcheon. Two tombstones survived, of Pº Cabral de Navais and his son Hieronimo. The hospital received 140 pardaos a month (504,000 réis a year), plus 79,200 réis for rice for the poor. Gemelli Careri heard mass here one Sunday in 1695 and called it “the parish of the city”. [n32; Gaz XIV p. 40; da Cunha pp. 225–226] The hospital is dated “before 1550” in one note, and the poor brothers of St John of God served in it later. [n32; Gaz XIV p. 41; da Cunha pp. 226–227] Parallel to it, by 1882, stood a modern temple of Mahadev. [n32; Gaz XIV p. 40]

Nossa Senhora da Vida

Near the Misericórdia stood Nossa Senhora da Vida, Our Lady of Life, “one of the oldest churches in Bassein”, with three altars in Careri’s day. [n32; Gaz XIV p. 40; da Cunha pp. 226–227] The modern Diocese of Vasai says that the first Latin-rite church in the fort was established in 1536 under that title, which would make it the first parish church of the city, earlier than St Joseph’s. [n09; n04] The CRIT heritage survey also dates “Our Lady of Life” to 1536. [n40]

Its later history is the strangest of any building in the fort. In 1852 the ruined church was turned into a sugar factory. When the foundations for the machinery were dug, the workmen found in a grave the bones of a man and a horse, with a rusty rapier: a burial, da Cunha thought, of a general with his charger. The space between the Misericórdia and N. S. da Vida was dug into a tank for the sugar works, and a walled, stepped enclosure near the sea was built to store sea water. The factory “did not pay”, and closed; the Gazetteer says in 1874, da Cunha in 1876 says “recently”. [n32; n21; Gaz XIV pp. 36, 40; da Cunha p. 227]

The Hospitallers

Near by stood the church and monastery of the Brothers of St John of God, the Hospitallers, a “poor, modern” order in the Gazetteer’s words, which arrived about 1681. Careri thought the house so poor that it could keep only three friars. It was probably the chapel later used as the sugar warehouse. [n32; Gaz XIV p. 41; da Cunha pp. 227–228]

The Jesuit church and college

The handsomest façade in Bassein, in the Gazetteer’s judgement, was the Jesuit church: a noble arch, fluted columns with Corinthian capitals, the monogram IHS, and over the college door the date 1636, which marks a repair. [n32; Gaz XIV p. 41; da Cunha pp. 228–230] The Jesuits had arrived the year before their foundation was laid in 1548 by Father Melchior Gonçalves; the Catholic Encyclopedia credits St Francis Xavier with founding the College of the Holy Name in December 1548. [n32; n04; Gaz XIV p. 41] A secondary source dates the church to 1549 and the large college to 1561. [n09] Modern writers call it the church of the Holy Name of Jesus, Nome de Jesus. [n09]

The college was the engine of the Jesuit mission in the district. “In 1588”, the Gazetteer records, “no fewer than 9,400 Hindus were baptised in Bassein church”. From 1560 a Commissary of the Inquisition sat at Bassein. [n32; n15; Gaz XIV p. 41] Two tombstones survived in 1882: Isabel de Aguiar, who died on 24 January 1591, and Dona Filipa da Fonseca, who died on 20 July 1628. [n32; Gaz XIV p. 41] Fryer in 1675 admired the college library, stocked with historians, moralists and expositors, a copy of Michelangelo’s Resurrection, and fine cloisters. [n32; Gaz XIV p. 41] Pietro della Valle stayed a night with the Jesuits on 29 March 1623, by da Cunha’s date (the Gazetteer gives 1628, a conflict the notes leave open), and supped with them among buildings still ruined by the great storm of 1618. [n32; n15]

This is also the college where, by the standard account, a boy of Bassein named Gonsalo Garcia was educated. Born in the town in the mid-1550s or 1560s (sources give 1556, 1557 and about 1564), to a Portuguese father and an Indian mother, he became a Franciscan and was crucified with twenty-five companions at Nagasaki on 5 February 1597. He was canonised in 1862, and is honoured as the first Indian-born saint. [n09; n04; n07] The Jesuit church is where, every January, his feast is kept inside the ruins (Section XVIII).

The Franciscans: Santo António

The oldest religious house in the fort, and one of the largest, belonged to the Franciscans, the first missionaries of Bassein. Their church of Santo António had an arched main chapel with elaborate mouldings; a great arch near the baptismal chapel in the “flat arch” style of Belém near Lisbon, built of dressed stone with basalt dressings; four side chapels, which Careri thought unusual for India; and cloisters. St Francis Xavier stayed here on his visits, about the end of 1544 and twice in 1548. The tombs included a Councillor of His Majesty who died on 24 August 1558, with his wife Dona Luiza da Silva; Dona Francisca de Miranda (died 10 November 1606); and a stone over Antonio Teles de Menezes (died 26 October 1676). [n32; Gaz XIV pp. 41–42; da Cunha pp. 237 ff.] A journalist in 1987 found fidalgos buried there “with their own coat of arms”. [n09] The Franciscan Antonio do Porto, whom the Catholic Encyclopedia calls “the Apostle of Bassein”, founded many churches in Bassein and Salsette between 1530 and 1540, and is credited with more than 10,000 baptisms. [n32; n04; Gaz XIV pp. 41–42] Some secondary sources date the Franciscan church to 1547; the CRIT survey gives 1537. [n09; n40]

In 1634 the city held 30 Franciscans, 15 Jesuits, 10 Dominicans and 8 Augustinians: 63 friars in all. [n32; n04; n37; Gaz XIV p. 42]

The Dominicans: São Gonçalo

The Dominican church of São Gonçalo is dated 1583 by the Gazetteer and 1564 by a modern website. In 1882 its walls, tower and main chapel with its arch still stood, and the ruined tomb of its patron lay on the gospel side of the altar. Careri noticed its three well-adorned altars. [n32; n09; n15; Gaz XIV p. 42]

The Augustinians: Nossa Senhora da Anunciada

The Augustinian chapel of Nossa Senhora da Anunciada, dated 1596 by a modern website, had a double-arched front and a vaulted roof with painted mouldings. [n32; n09; Gaz XIV p. 43] Near it, in the quarter where the nobles had lived, the Gazetteer found “an ornamental bath-room of hard cement studded with shells and porcelain”, with a confused inscription dated 1617. [n32; Gaz XIV p. 43] By the bastion of São Sebastião there was also a modern English tomb, of Mrs Durham, the wife of the surgeon Andrew Durham. [n32; Gaz XIV p. 43]

The jail and the Inquisition

Outside the walls, near the travellers’ bungalow, lay a slab recording the building of the jail: the Viceroy Pero da Silva, the Captain Rui Dias da Cunha and others had it built, and it was finished under the Captain André Salema, between 1635 and 1639. [n32; Gaz XIV p. 39 n.] Da Cunha described an inscribed slab of the Inquisition’s prison near the bungalow, and explained how the system worked. From about 1580 the Inquisition of Goa had commissaries at Chaul, Bassein and Daman, who collected offenders and sent them to Goa for trial; at Bassein prisoners were held in the jail near the Captain’s palace until “the fleet from the north” carried them south. [n37; da Cunha pp. 235–236; Gaz XIII-II p. 463] The one Bassein case the notes record in any detail is late: in 1840 the Goan scholar Filipe Nery Xavier found a stone set up in 1786 where the Inquisition had razed a house whose family, “dogmatists of the said sect”, had been burnt in the auto da fé of 30 December 1747; the house had been demolished and the ground ploughed with salt. The stone’s location and the family’s name are not in the notes. [n37; Priolkar pp. 60–61] A Bombay journalist’s statement in 1987 that “the Inquisition was never set up in Bassein” is true only in the narrow sense that there was no tribunal in the town; there was a commissary and a prison. [n09; n37]

How many churches?

Counts vary with the counter. Fryer in 1675: “Within the Walls are six Churches, four Convents, two Colleges, one of the Jesuits, another of the Franciscans.” The Gazetteer’s own summary for 1673: “six churches, four colleges and two convents”. Hull, copying da Cunha: “five convents, a cathedral wrongly so-called, in reality a matriz… six other churches, and one asylum for orphans and maidens”. The Gazetteer’s general text: five convents, thirteen churches, an orphanage and a hospital under the Portuguese (counting, apparently, beyond the walls). Bishop Heber in 1825 counted seven churches, Mrs Postans in 1838 eight. A modern newspaper and a modern blog both say seven churches stood inside the fort. [n15; n37; n32; n09; Gaz XIV pp. 32 n., 38; da Cunha pp. 211–212] What the visitor can recognise today, a heritage page notes, is three chapels whose façades are “typical of 17th century Portuguese churches”, the southernmost with a well-preserved barrel-vaulted ceiling. [n09; n04]

The tunnel

Every old fort on this coast has its secret passage, and Vasai’s is the oldest-recorded of them. The Gazetteer wrote in 1882, near its account of the São Sebastião bastion and the ruined pier on the outer wall: “Inside of the wall a passage is said to run to the river. But the air is bad and puts out lights, and the passage has never been explored.” [n32; Gaz XIV p. 43] That sentence is the earliest source for the tunnel tradition. Modern heritage pages repeat it with additions: one gives the tunnel a date of 1554 and a length of 530 feet, figures that no older source supports, and that the search tool used for this book’s research itself flagged as unconfirmed. [n32; livehistoryindia.com] A news report, read only as a search snippet, says that excavation in the Balle Killa, the citadel area, found a “secret room”, artefacts and several wells that had been a major water source. [n32; DNA] Whether the passage exists, and where it goes, is still not known.

Above and below the captain

The captain was not the highest Portuguese officer in the city. Baçaim was the seat of the General of the North, who governed the whole “Province of the North” from Daman to Chaul; his palace stood beside the captain’s in the citadel. [n32; n04; Gaz XIV pp. 39–40] Hull calls the fort “the official residence of the General of the North”. [n37; Hull I pp. 4–5] The province, da Cunha records, was divided into units with names taken from the Persian and Marathi administration the Portuguese inherited: the Saibana de Baçaim, the Caçabe (Persian kasaba) of Tana, the islands of Salcete, Caranja, Bellaflor de Sambayo and Bombaim, and the Praganas (parganas) of Manora and Asserim. The district of Baçaim itself ran about ten miles north to the Agashi river, about 24 miles east to Asserim and Manora, and about 40 miles south to Karanja. [n32; Gaz XIV p. 30 n.; da Cunha pp. 157–158]

Below the captain, the city’s officers in 1634 included a sea bailiff, a customs storekeeper (almoxarife dos armazens) and a “master builder”. [n36; Gaz XIV p. 31] The chief revenue officer of the district, the Tanadar-mor, did not live inside the walls at all, but at “Baçaim de Cima”, Upper Bassein, outside them. [n32; da Cunha pp. 221–222; Gaz XIV p. 31] Where exactly Upper Bassein lay, the notes do not say.

The church, too, had a hand on government. Alexander Hamilton, the Scottish sea captain who knew the coast about 1720, wrote that “the church superintends the General of the North, which makes his government both uneasy and precarious.” [n15; Gaz XIV p. 33 n.]

VII. The people of the Court of the North

Fidalgos

The Portuguese called Baçaim a Corte do Norte, the Court of the North, and its people liked the name. It was, in the Gazetteer’s summary of Portuguese sources, “next to Goa the largest and richest of the Portuguese settlements”, with “straight streets, large squares, stately two-storied dwellings… and many rich and magnificent churches”. “There were more than 100 families of the highest in India and proverbially rich.” [n15; Gaz XIV pp. 30, 33] A man of standing was styled “Fidalgo ou Cavalheiro de Baçaim”, Gentleman or Knight of Bassein. [n18]

Fryer, again, saw them at home. “The Fidalgos (for few Artisans are tolerated within the Walls) have stately Dwellings, graced with covered Balconies, and large Windows two Stories high, with Panes of Oister shell.” [n15; Fryer/Crooke I pp. 190–193] Those oyster-shell panes, thin translucent shells set into wooden lattices in place of glass, were the windows of the Portuguese coast. The great estates of these families lay outside the walls: on his way to Bassein Fryer passed the estate at Ghodbandar of “the Richest Don on this side Goa”. [n15; Fryer/Crooke I p. 190] Fryer also recorded, the Gazetteer says, that the gentry of the province lived “like petty monarchs”, holding the people of their villages “in a perfect state of villainage”. [n37; Gaz XIII-I p. 202]

Who held the land, a generation before the fall, is set out in the Tombo de Baçaim of 1727–1730, a Portuguese register of Crown leases published by André Teixeira and Silvana Pires in 2007. Its holders are Portuguese fidalgos, their widows (each “Dona”), and the Jesuits. The rich village of “Virar Naringuy” was held by Dom Pedro de Meneses at 2,000 pardaus a year, the highest rent among the aldeias; “Sandor” by Jorge da Silva Coelho; “Gris”, the modern Giriz, by Dona Margarida Teles de Meneses; “Agaçaim” and the island of Arnala, the “Ilha das Vacas”, by Dona Domingas Pereira de Azevedo; “Cholna Roba”, the modern Chulne, by the Jesuits’ procurator-general. [n40; n39; Tombo pp. 338–345] The most common surnames among the holders are Pereira, Melo, Meneses, Coutinho, Almeida and Brito. [n39] The next chapter returns to this register village by village.

Counting the city

How many people lived in Baçaim? Two figures survive, both reported second-hand.

The first is for 1634, from Bocarro as reported in a Wikipedia article: about 400 Portuguese families, 200 Indian Christian families and 1,800 slaves. [n04; Wikipedia: Vasai Fort] The second is for the beginning of the eighteenth century, from the Gazetteer: a population of 60,499, of whom 58,131 were “native Christians” and 2,368 Europeans. [n15; n21; Gaz XIV p. 33] (One note reads the Christian figure as 58,181; the arithmetic, 60,499 less 2,368, gives 58,131.) The two figures cannot both describe the same thing. The 1634 count is of households inside the walls; the 60,499 must be the whole district, the city and its villages, because it is about ten times what the walls could hold, and because Tieffenthaler and du Perron, after the fall, said the town itself “never counted more than 3,500 inhabitants” though 30,000 lived in the country round it. [n37; Hull I p. 76]

The 1634 count is worth looking at closely, because it names the three groups that made the walled city: Portuguese families, Indian Christian families and slaves, with the slaves outnumbering the free households nearly three to one. Historians of Portuguese India commonly distinguish, among the Portuguese, the noble fidalgos from the married settlers they called casados; the sources gathered for this book do not use that word, and the 1634 figure simply says “Portuguese families”. This chapter leaves that distinction unresolved rather than guessing at the split.

Slaves

Of the 1,800 slaves of 1634 the notes know almost nothing individually. The Gazetteer records that house slaves were brought from Africa, “made Christians and sometimes raised to be priests” (citing Hamilton), that “African blood” could be traced among the Salsette Christians, and that slaves from Abyssinia were among the imports of the Portuguese ports. [n37; n36; Gaz XIII-II pp. 460 n., 465–468; Gaz XIII-I p. 200 n.] A Jesuit report quoted by the Gazetteer says that “the lower Hindus sold their children to Musalmans and Christians. A child at the breast cost as much as a goat in Portugal.” [n37; Gaz XIII-II p. 461 n.] The slaves of Baçaim were part African, part Indian, and all of them, by the law of the city, baptised. No slave of Bassein speaks in the record.

“None but Christians lodge within the City”

Fryer recorded a rule that shaped the town for two centuries: “None but Christians lodge within the City, the Banyans repairing to the Suburbs upon Tattoo.” [n15; Fryer/Crooke I pp. 190–193] Banyan was the English word for the Hindu merchant, the Vani; tattoo, the evening drum. Hindu traders could do business inside the walls by day but had to be outside by nightfall. So the walled city was Christian by law, and around it, in suburbs the record does not name, lived the Hindus who traded with it.

The converts who lived inside the walls were the “Indian Christian families” of the 1634 count. Who they were by origin, the Gazetteer explains for the whole district. The Christians of Bassein “seem to be the descendants of the converts made first by the Franciscans (1535–1548) and afterwards by the Jesuits”; they came from every caste, “Brahmans, Prabhus, Pachkalshis, Chaukalshis, Sonars, Khatris, Bhandaris, Kharpatils, Kunbis, Kumbhars, Nhavis, Dhobis, Kolis, Bhois, Mhars, Chambhars”, though “the bulk of them are Bhandaris, Kolis and Kunbis”. “Men of rank were admitted into the best Portuguese society and were allowed to marry with Europeans,” while “those of low birth were left in a state little removed from servitude.” [n40; n41; n37; Gaz XIII-I pp. 199–202] Why and how they converted, by persuasion, interest, pressure and law in proportions that historians still dispute, is the subject of Chapter 34 and of T4, and is not retold here. What this chapter adds is the setting. The great baptisms of the Jesuits, the 1,600 of 1573 and the 9,400 of 1588, were held in the city, in the Jesuit church, and the Jesuits made them “an occasion of great splendour and rejoicing”, with processions in which the governor, the nobility and the clergy walked beside a converted Brahman. [n37; n15; Gaz XIII-II p. 461; Gaz XIV p. 41; da Cunha p. 234] Da Cunha, a Goan Catholic who disliked the Jesuits, called the method a “bait alluring and irresistible”. [n37; da Cunha p. 234]

The Kuparis and the walled city

The Kuparis, the Samvedi Christians of this book’s author’s community, were not, on the evidence, a people of the walled city. The Gazetteer’s Christian husbandmen who were “many of them converted Samvedi Brahmans” lived in the garden villages of the Sopara and Agashi agar: Gas, Nirmal, Vagholi, Bolinj, Koprad, Umbrala, Mardesh and the rest, six to ten miles north of the fort. [n40; n11; n34; Gaz XIV pp. 314–315] Their parish churches, Nirmal (dated from 1557 to 1586 in different sources), Agashi (1568) and Nandakhal (1573), were among the first outside the fort, and they were founded by the Franciscans, whose great house stood inside the walls. [n40; n09] So the walled city was, for the Kupari villages, the seat of the orders that baptised them, the court where their landlords lived, and the place where their rice and plantains went to market.

There are threads that tie the Kuparis to the town itself. One is the Jesuit policy, recorded by the Gazetteer for 1570–1590, of seeking “Brahman and other high-caste converts, knowing that if the Brahmans became Christians, many of the lower classes would follow”; the baptisms of high-caste converts, with their processions and banquets, were city occasions, and the Samvedi Brahmans of the villages were exactly the kind of convert that policy sought. [n37; Gaz XIII-II p. 461] Another is the baptism, on 8 September 1565, of the Brahmin astrologer Parashuram Joshi as Henrique da Cunha, with about 250 Hindus, more than 50 of them Brahmins; the record does not say where he lived or what his caste division was, so it cannot be claimed for the Samvedis. [n04; n37] A third is the Kuparis’ own saint. St Gonsalo Garcia, the Bassein boy educated at the Jesuit college inside the walls, is honoured by the Kupari parish of Gass, where the church built in 1942 bears his name, and his feast in the ruined Jesuit church is the one great occasion each year when the Kupari villages come back inside the fort. [n09; n07; n42]

What the Kupari villages gave the city in the Portuguese period, the record shows only indirectly: their lands were in the Tombo, held by fidalgos and Jesuits; their produce went to the city’s markets and granary; and their churches were run from its convents. The surnames they carry today, Pereira, Rodrigues, D’Mello, Lopes, Tuscano and others, are best explained, by the evidence the notes collected, by baptism under the godparents, priests and landholders of that system. The Tombo records a licenciado André Toscano who in 1727 took over several hortas and the aldeia “Sircar” by purchase; whether any Kupari family descends from such a sponsor, no document shows. [n39; n34; Tombo fl. 136–136v] Chapter 38 discusses Kupari names at length.

Other communities around the Portuguese city

The Hindus of the suburbs were not one people. The Gazetteer believed the older coastal castes, the Palshe Brahmans, the Pathare Prabhus, the Pachkalshis and others, had come to this coast under Bimb, a prince of the Devgiri house, in the late thirteenth century, or from Gujarat; the Palshes were the old priests of the coast and fixed auspicious times for marriages for all classes in Bassein and Salsette. [n08; Gaz XIII-I pp. 61–62, 81] Many Pachkalshis, Enthoven reported from the community’s own account, fled Portuguese persecution to the southern Konkan, while “many were then converted to Roman Catholicism by force and their descendants may still be traced at Bassein and Salsette”. [n41; Enthoven III p. 161] Those converted Pachkalshis are the ancestors, on the evidence, of the Christian Vadvals of the southern Bassein parishes round the fort, which the next chapter describes. The 1567 decrees of the Goa Provincial Council, as reported by da Cunha, barred the Christians of Goa and Bassein from lending money to, partnering with or sheltering “Parabus”, the Prabhus, from employing non-Christians as clerks, interpreters, brokers or money-changers, and from renting property to them. [n37; da Cunha p. 142] Hindu merchants and writers could trade with the city, but law tried to keep them out of its offices and houses.

The Muslims of the Gujarat town, whose mosques were pulled down and whose mosque revenues paid for the Matriz and the Franciscans, largely disappear from the record of the Portuguese city. The Gazetteer’s judgement was that the Portuguese “treatment of their chief rivals, the Musalmans, was specially severe”. [n37; Gaz XIII-I p. 201 n.; da Cunha p. 235] A traveller of about 1603–1630, Goez, wrote that “the persecution of the Portuguese had made many Hindus, Mussulmans and Parsees abandon their homes and live in the dominion of Shah Jehan”; between Bassein and Daman, he said, there were few natives and much of the land lay uncultivated. The Gazetteer added that if that account was correct, the districts soon recovered. [n37; Gaz XIII-II pp. 463–464]

VIII. Daily life and trade in the Portuguese city

Ships, timber and stone

Bassein, the Gazetteer wrote, was “never so busy a trade centre as Chaul”, the other great Portuguese port of the north Konkan, but it “was famous for its ship building”. In 1607 the French traveller Pyrard de Laval found it “a great place for ship-building”, with “a large trade in timber and building stone, which was as fine and hard as granite, and was used in all the Goa churches and palaces”. “No place”, he wrote, “had better timber than Bassein.” [n36; n15; Gaz XIV pp. 30–31; Gaz XIII-II pp. 468–469] Between 1550 and 1600 great ships built at Agashi and Bassein made many voyages to Europe. In 1634 the English, through their factory at Surat, had four pinnaces built for the coast trade, two at Daman and two at Bassein; with them began, the Gazetteer says, what became the Bombay Marine. [n36; n32; Gaz XIV pp. 30–31; Gaz XIII-II p. 468; da Cunha p. 221 n.]

Cesare de’ Federici, a Venetian who passed about 1565, had a sour verdict: “After Daman comes Basain with many towns… from which nothing is drawn but rice, wheat, and much timber.” He also thought it “a filthy place compared with Daman”. Ralph Fitch in 1583 named it among the places of most trade in corn and rice, and Abul Fazl, Akbar’s chronicler, called it “Bussy”, an emporium in European hands “through the negligence of the Moghal officers”. [n15; Gaz XIV pp. 29–30]

The timber trade made fortunes for officials. A royal letter of 13 March 1587 complained that the captains of Bassein and Daman supplied timber for the king’s armada from their own stock, “who charged far higher prices than their value in the market”, because they traded on their own account. “The Bassein timber trade,” the Gazetteer concluded, “was chiefly carried on by the captains of forts and other government officers.” [n36; da Cunha p. 144; Gaz XIII-II p. 465]

Customs, revenue and the city’s purse

For all its passes and dues, Bassein never had a royal customs house. “There being no mandovim or custom-house built at Bassein belonging to the king,” da Cunha recorded, “a private house was rented for that purpose, paying 84 pardaos a year.” The ferry boatmen of the Bassein river were paid 200 réis a month. [n32; n36; da Cunha pp. 222–223] The city’s revenue, as the Gazetteer converted it into pounds, rose through the last half-century of Portuguese rule: £8,646 in 1686, £9,737 in 1709, £15,639 in 1718 and £45,706 in 1729. [n32; Gaz XIV p. 34 n.] Da Cunha gives the revenue in Portuguese money as 172,920 xerafins in 1680. [n36] Money for religion was large. From 1550 the Crown gave the Franciscans of Bassein 2,070 pardaos a year, from a fund that before the conquest had paid for lighting the mosques; when the Jesuits came it was halved between them; in 1634 the Jesuits’ share was raised to 3,000 pardaos, under the heading Conversão da Fé. [n37; da Cunha p. 235]

Imports and exports

The Gazetteer’s account of the Portuguese ports of the Thana coast lists what left and what arrived. Out went rice, pulse, vegetables, coconuts and betel nuts; large basalt columns from Bassein for Goa; “great quantities of the finest teak… to Goa, Gujarat, Sindh, and occasionally to the Persian Gulf and the Red Sea”; local cotton cloth and muslins; blankets, silks and sandals. In came dates and raisins, Spanish wine, silks, gold from Sofala, ivory from Abyssinia, spices from Malabar and the Indonesian islands, horses from the Persian Gulf and Arabia, elephants from Ceylon, and slaves from Abyssinia. [n36; Gaz XIII-II pp. 465–468] The boats that carried the coastal trade were the craft Gaspar Correa described: planks sewn with coir or nailed, pitched cloth and cane mats against the sea, cargo holds roofed with woven palm leaf, one great mast and a long lateen sail. [n36; Gaz XIII-II pp. 468–469]

Storm, raid and plague

The city’s seventeenth century was hard. In May 1618 a hurricane, “a general and diabolical storm” in the chronicler Faria y Sousa’s words, “began at Baçaim on the 15th of that month… the sea was brought into the city by the wind… two thousand persons were killed; the fish died in the ponds”. [n15; Gaz XIV p. 31 n.] The storm unroofed the three largest churches and damaged the Franciscan and Augustinian churches and convents and the Jesuit church and house. Plague and famine followed. [n04; n09] When Pietro della Valle supped with the Jesuits five years later, many buildings were still ruins. [n15]

In 1674, 600 Arab raiders from Muscat “landed at Bassein, and, unopposed by the panic-struck garrison, plundered all the churches outside of the walls”. [n15; Gaz XIV p. 32] Fryer, the next year, noted that the fruitful land around, “of Sugar-Canes, Rice, and other Grain”, had “lately been destroyed by the Arabs of Muschat”. [n15; Fryer/Crooke I pp. 190–193] The same year the Maratha general More Pandit forced the Portuguese to pay the chauth, a quarter of Baçaim’s revenues; an English source of the time, quoted by da Cunha, said the Marathas demanded it because the Portuguese “had lately inflicted great severities on many families of the Morattoe religion… because they refused to become Christians”. Shivaji’s forces advanced near Saiwan in 1676. [n04; n37; da Cunha p. 143] The quarrel over orphans ran through these years: an English letter of 1675 says Shivaji’s “chiefest cause of his hatred” for the Portuguese was “forcing orphans of his [caste to] turn Roman Catholicks”, and records that the Captain-General of Bassein took the orphans’ side against the Jesuits and made them restore an orphan’s estate. [n37; Priolkar p. 129]

About 1690 the city was “stricken by a pestilential fever or plague, ‘exactly like a bubo’”, which, “continuing at intervals for several years, robbed the city of about one-third of its people”. [n21; Gaz XIV p. 33] Gemelli Careri, who passed in 1695, wrote of a plague that “carried off whole cityfuls of people” at Surat, Daman and Thana. [n15] It is one of da Cunha’s three explanations for the empty third of the city inside the walls.

Hamilton’s verdict

By about 1720 the shine was gone. Alexander Hamilton wrote that Bassein was “a place of small trade, its riches dead and buried in the churches”, and that “most of its riches lay buried in the hands of lazy country gentlemen, who loitered their days in ease, luxury and pride, without the least sense of their country’s ruin”. [n15; n36; Gaz XIV p. 33; Gaz XIII-II p. 485] He was a Protestant Scot with no love for Portuguese Catholics, and his judgement should be read that way. But his picture fits the other evidence: a city of landholders and convents, rich in rents and churches, weak in trade, and about to face an enemy it could not beat.

Faith shared and forbidden

Two details from travellers show what lay beneath the official Christianity of the city. Tavernier, in the 1650s, noticed “that the Indians worshipped the Virgin Mary as a representation of Sita, pulling off their shoes… If the Portuguese had allowed them they would have anointed the image and offered it fruit.” [n15; Gaz XIV p. 32 n.] And da Cunha recorded that the Inquisition “had put a stop to all pilgrimage” to a healing tank near the church of Our Lady of Remedies, on the road between Bassein and Agashi, “under very severe penalties”; by his day Hindus held a jatra there again. [n37; da Cunha p. 163] The Gazetteer adds that at one famous pool between Bassein and Agashi, the Franciscans converted the pool itself, built a chapel to Our Lady of Healing, and “the pilgrimage and cure-working continued”. [n37; Gaz XIII-II p. 461 n.] Hindu devotion to the Virgin, a Christian chapel on a Hindu healing pool, a ban, and a revival: that is the religious history of the Bassein countryside in miniature.

IX. The siege of 1739, seen from inside the walls

The net closes

The campaign that ended Portuguese Bassein is told in Chapter 08. This section tells it from the city’s side, as far as the sources let one stand inside the walls.

By the winter of 1738–39 the city was alone. The Marathas, under Chimaji Appa, brother of the Peshwa Bajirao I, had been fighting the Portuguese of the north for two years. They had taken Thana and Salsette. They first took the small fort of Arnala, at the northern tip of the old island, and put its commandant and garrison to the sword. They seized the islands of Versova and Dharavi and the creeks. [n32; da Cunha pp. 147–148] Arnala’s fall can be dated: the Marathi inscription over its north gate says it was rebuilt under Bajirao I in 1737. [n11; Gaz XIV pp. 10–11]

The Portuguese commanders divided. Luis de Botelho, governor of Salsette, retired to Karanja, leaving Captain Caetano de Sousa Pereira to defend Bassein. Goa could send nothing, because it was itself threatened by a Bhonsle invasion from the south. The English at Bombay, appealed to by João de Sousa Terras, refused help. [n32; da Cunha pp. 147–148] The city that had once ruled Bombay now begged from it in vain.

The siege

The siege began on 17 February 1739. A Portuguese account quoted on Wikipedia puts the besieging army at 40,000 infantry, 25,000 cavalry, about 4,000 miners, 5,000 camels and 50 elephants, close to 100,000 with reinforcements; inside the walls were about 1,200 Portuguese and Indian auxiliaries. [n04] Those numbers come from one side and should be read as such.

The commandant Sylveira de Menezes was killed. The Marathas did not storm the high walls by ladder; they dug. Some twelve or thirteen mines were sprung under the defences, da Cunha records. Two exploded and made a large breach; a third blew up “hundreds of the assailants”. [n32; da Cunha p. 148; Gaz XIV p. 34] A day-by-day account on Wikipedia gives 1 May for the laying of ten mines, four of which exploded and opened breaches; 2 May for a storm by about 4,000 Marathas; and 3 May for a mine that destroyed the tower of São Sebastião. [n04] The final breach, both da Cunha and the Gazetteer agree, was beneath São Sebastião’s tower: the bastion of 1554, the one that gave the fortress its name. [n32; da Cunha p. 148; Gaz XIV p. 34] Du Perron, who saw the fort twenty years later, noted that the Marathas had “condemned the south-west gate”. [n32; Gaz XIV p. 34 n.] Stone cannonballs about six inches across, da Cunha found, still lay in a magazine in the fort in his day. [n32; da Cunha p. 148 n.]

The cost

The casualty figures are old and round. Da Cunha and the Gazetteer both give about 800 Portuguese, and “about 12,000” Marathas “in killed and wounded”. [n32; da Cunha p. 148; Gaz XIV p. 34] An earlier note in this book’s research called the 12,000 figure unsourced; it is in fact in both primary texts, though both repeat it from an older article in the Bombay Quarterly Review, and it is a combined, unverified number for killed and wounded together. The Maharashtra Gazetteer, as cited on Wikipedia, says Maratha losses are unknown. [n32; n04]

The terms

When most of his senior officers were dead, Captain Pereira asked for terms. Chimaji’s ultimatum, as Wikipedia reports it, was that otherwise “the entire garrison would be slaughtered and the fort levelled”. [n04] The capitulation was signed on 16 May 1739; da Cunha gives 19 May on one page and 16 May on another, and the Gazetteer gives 16 May, which is the better-attested date. [n32; n40; da Cunha pp. 147–148; Gaz XIV p. 34] Its general terms were generous by the standards of the age: “all the garrison, as well regulars as auxiliaries” were to march out “with all the honours of war”, and inhabitants who wished to leave were given eight days to go with their movable property. [n32; da Cunha pp. 148–149; Gaz XIV p. 34] The garrison marched out with colours flying, muskets shouldered and drums beating, and went by sea to Bombay. [n04] The saffron flag was raised over the fort on 23 May, and the Portuguese administration left that day. [n04]

For the Christians who stayed, the terms mattered more than for the soldiers who left. Hull, the historian of the Bombay mission, summarising Portuguese documents, says the capitulation guaranteed free passage for noble families, Christians who wished to go, and all religious orders and priests; promised that Christians would “enjoy liberty of worship”; preserved the privileges of three churches in Bassein, one in the district and one in Salsette, with their curates subject to the Primate of India; and let the images remain. The Gazetteer says the Portuguese secured only five churches: three in Bassein city, one in the district and one in Salsette. [n37; Hull I pp. 75–76; Gaz XIII-I p. 203] The Portuguese text of the capitulation, printed in the Bombay Quarterly Review, was not reached for this book. [n32]

Who left and who stayed

“All the Portuguese ‘reinóis’ and ‘descendentes’,” the historian Sidh Losa Mendiratta writes, “fled or were expelled… leaving only the Indian converted population behind.” [n34; Mendiratta pp. 216–218] The fidalgos, the friars and the soldiers went. The Christians of the villages, Kuparis, Vadvals, Kolis and the rest, stayed on their land. Tieffenthaler and du Perron, a generation later, put it simply: “Portuguese nobles and all the regular clergy left for Goa; the ordinary people remained, a native priest taking charge of their souls with the approbation of the Mahrattas.” [n37; Hull I p. 76]

Not every local community had been on the Portuguese side. A Marathi edition of 1935 of the Sashtichi Bakhar, compiled by Gajanan Govind Naik of Anjur, lists by caste the people who helped the Peshwa in the Vasai campaign: Pathare Kshatriyas, Brahmans, Pathare Prabhus, Somvanshi Kshatriyas (among them the Chaudharis of Umele), Bhandaris, Kolis, Christians and Agris. It quotes an older source saying that without Gangaji Naik and Chuba Naik of Anjur and some Somvanshi Kshatriya gentlemen, “the Maratha army would have been badly ruined”. [n41; Naik 1935 pp. 15, 320 ff.] The government gazetteers agree that the Pachkalshis gave “material help” at the siege and were rewarded with patilships and inam villages; the Bombay City Gazetteer names Bugaji and Gangaji Naik, granted the village of Anjur, in Bhiwandi, with the title of Naik Sarpatel in 1739. [n41; Enthoven III p. 161; Bombay City Gazetteer I p. 238; Gaz XIII-I p. 130 n.] (The Thana Gazetteer’s footnote prints “1743”, almost certainly a slip.) The siege of Bassein, then, was not simply Hindu against Christian. It divided local communities, and some of the people who helped bring down the walls were the neighbours of the people who stayed inside them.

The epitaph

The Gazetteer of 1882, written by British officers who admired the defenders, gave the fall a famous epitaph: “Thus Bassein fell as falls a stately tree never to rise. No fight had been more glorious to the Portuguese; in none since the days of Albuquerque had they earned more unsullied fame.” [n15; Gaz XIV p. 34] It is a fine sentence, and a partisan one. A Maratha writer would have ended the chapter differently: with the saffron flag over the walls, the bells carried off on elephants, and the Peshwa’s new governor riding into Bajipur.

X. Bajipur: the Maratha city, 1739–1818

A new name and a new governor

The Marathas renamed the town Bajipur, “Baji Rao’s city”, after the Peshwa, and made it the headquarters of a governor whose charge ran from the Bankot river in the south to Daman in the north. Da Cunha calls the post the Sar Subha. [n32; Gaz XIV p. 34; da Cunha p. 149] The first Maratha governor of Bassein, by every account, was Shankarji Keshav Phadke, whom the Gazetteer calls “the celebrated Maratha Sarsubhedar of Bassein”. [n04; n10; n12; Gaz XIV p. 367] His name is on more buildings in this landscape than any Portuguese captain’s: the eight temples of Nirmal (about 1750), the rebuilt shrine of Tungareshwar (about 1750), the Bhavani Shankar and Hanuman temples at Agashi, the temple of Vajrabai at Vadavli, and, by one account, the new fort at Arnala. [n10; n11; n12] The modern habit of crediting all of this to Chimaji Appa compresses the commander and the governor into one man. The vow, the legend says, was Chimaji’s; the building programme, the record says, was Shankarji Keshav’s. [n10]

The bells

The Marathas carried off the church bells. A Goanet post of 2020 says they went on elephants as trophies of victory. [n09] At least seven bells from the Portuguese churches of the north, taken in the campaign of 1737–39, hang today in temples across Maharashtra: at Naroshankar in Nashik (said to come from Mount Poinsur at Borivali, not from Vasai), at Bhimashankar (cast in 1729, with a cross and an image of Jesus), at Menavali near Wai (cast in 1707, 650 kg, with the Madonna and Child in relief), at the Durga Devi temple at Murud (said to come from Vasai fort), at Baneshwar near Pune (cast 1683, with a cross), at Loni Bhapkar and at Nimgaon Khandoba. [n09; vasaiker.com] Others are said to hang at Jejuri, Tulja Bhavani and Pali in Raigad. [n04; n09] No bell has been traced reliably to a particular church inside the fort. [n09]

The temples inside the walls

Inside the empty Portuguese city the Marathas set up Hindu shrines. The Gazetteer of 1882 found “two modern Hindu temples inside the fort, one to Hanuman close to the sea gateway and the other to Trivikram. Trivikram’s temple enjoys a yearly Government allowance of Rs 1,061.” That allowance, about £106, was the largest temple grant in the whole taluka. [n10; n32; n11; Gaz XIV pp. 37, 40] Near the Misericórdia stood “a modern Hindu temple of Mahadev”. [n32; Gaz XIV p. 40] Mrs Heber, the wife of the bishop, saw in 1825 a “pagoda” in the ruins “with the sacred bull well carved at its entrance”, which may be the same Mahadev shrine. [n32; da Cunha p. 211]

A modern Marathi heritage site adds two traditions. It calls the Sea Gate shrine “Veer Hanuman”, says it was consecrated on 27 July 1739 and that its image has a carved moustache. And it describes a temple of Shri Nageshwar, destroyed under the Portuguese and rebuilt after the Maratha conquest, beside an old pond called the Nagesh Mahatirtha. [n10; durgbharari.in/vasai] The CRIT heritage survey lists a “Chimaji Appa temple (1739)” among the fort’s assets. [n40; CRIT Vol II] Some tourist guides say a Vajreshwari temple built by Chimaji Appa stands inside the fort; the Gazetteer’s 1882 list of temples there has none, and the famous Vajreshwari temple is at Vadavli in Bhiwandi taluka. [n10] An unverified claim on a fan-wiki page says Chimaji Appa built a Hanuman temple over a church that may itself have stood on a mosque or temple. Nothing in the primary sources supports it. [n10] The date of the Nageshwar rebuilding and the present state of the Trivikram temple are not in the notes. [n10]

The Christians under the Marathas

What happened to the Christians after 1739? The evidence pulls two ways. On one side, the Gazetteer records that “to restore a Hindu population grants of rent-free land were offered, and a tax was levied for the support of Brahmans who were brought to purify the Christians and make them fit to take their place in their old castes”. [n32; n37; Gaz XIV p. 34; da Cunha p. 149] Da Cunha attributes the purification tax to Madhavrao Peshwa, who ruled from 1761 to 1772, a generation after the conquest, and says the land grants and the tax together “brought down a crowd of Hindus from Maharashtra and Gujarat, especially Prabhus”. [n34; da Cunha p. 149] The 1911 census says that the Kirpal Bhandaris, “forcibly converted to Christianity by the Portuguese”, were given the choice of reverting soon after 1739 “which they accepted”. [n34; Census 1911] Mendiratta’s judgement is that “the Marathas probably managed to bring some Catholics back into the Hindu fold through purifying rites, and economic pressure”. [n34; Mendiratta pp. 216–218]

On the other side, the Gazetteer says that “the Marathas allowed them the freest exercise of their religion, their processions and festivals were respected, and many of the Salsette churches were built or rebuilt about this time (1760)”; the native Christians “showed more constancy and the Marathas more toleration than could have been expected”. [n37; n34; Gaz XIII-I p. 203] Under a Vicar General at Kurla, the Goan “Canarin” priests whom the Marathas accepted in place of the European clergy kept the parishes going. [n34; n37] The clearest sign is du Perron’s: in December 1760, at Agashi, he found “the lanes full of people going to church as freely as in a Christian country”, and arrived on the church’s feast day “through the finest orchards in the world”. [n40; n11; Gaz XIV p. 2; da Cunha p. 164]

Both things were true. The town became a Maratha seat with new temples and a Brahman administration; the Christian villages around it kept their churches, with fewer priests and less money, and some families went back to Hinduism.

Bajipur’s new Brahmans: Papdi

The Maratha conquest brought a new Hindu elite to the town. The Palshe Brahmans, the old priests of the coast, had suffered, the Gazetteer says, “at the hands” of “their rivals the Deccan and Chitpávan Bráhmans… since the Maratha conquest of western Thana (1740)”. [n08; Gaz XIII-I p. 81 n.] The newcomers settled where the Peshwa’s officials could reach the fort. Da Cunha describes Papdi in 1876 as “a village about a cannon-shot from Bajipura, chiefly inhabited by Brahmans of Chitpawan, Karada, and Deshastha divisions, as well as Palshas, Sonars, and other inferior castes of the Hindus”, and gives with it a revenue figure of Rs 1,80,630. [n32; n40; da Cunha p. 157] That revenue figure seems to belong to the whole division rather than to one village; da Cunha’s sentence attaches it loosely. The CRIT survey adds that Papdi’s present fabric “shows palimpsests from the 18th century when it was given as a jagir to the community which transported goods, supplies and food to the Maratha army”, and that its Ram Mandir, rebuilt in the early twentieth century, originally marked the Maratha dominance of 1739–1818. [n40; CRIT Vol II] Which community that was, CRIT does not say; one note suggests the Vadval carters, and that is an inference only. [n40] In the Maratha settlement of the division, da Cunha counts 161 villages, four of them inam, with mahals at Manikpura in the south-east, Agashi in the north, and others. [n32; da Cunha pp. 156–157]

Under siege again: 1780 and 1802

The British East India Company took Bassein once before they kept it. Colonel Goddard besieged the fort from 13 November to 11 December 1780, during the First Anglo-Maratha War, and took it. A Persian chronicle of the time called it “the fortress of Bessi, which is one of the strongest and most important fortresses under the Mahratta power”. [n04; n15; FIBIS] The Treaty of Salbai (17 May 1782) gave it back to the Marathas. [n04]

Twenty years later Bassein had its moment in Indian history. In December 1802 the Peshwa Baji Rao II, fleeing Yashwantrao Holkar after the battle of Poona, reached Bassein from Suvarndurg. He was met by Colonel Close and Mountstuart Elphinstone, and on 31 December 1802 he signed with them the Treaty of Bassein, which made him a client of the Company: 6,000 Company troops were to be stationed with him permanently, paid for with districts yielding Rs 26 lakh a year, and he was to make no war or treaty without the Company. [n04; n15; n28; Gaz XIV p. 35] A palm-tree stockade was built at Sopara to guard the passage onto the island while he stayed. He left on 27 April 1803 and marched by Kalyan to Poona, where the Company restored him on 13 May. [n28; Gaz XIV p. 35] The treaty set off the Second Anglo-Maratha War and was, in Wikipedia’s words, “a decisive step in the dissolution of the Maratha Empire”. [n04] So two treaties named for this town bracket three centuries: one in 1534 that began Portuguese rule over the coast, one in 1802 that began the end of Maratha sovereignty.

After the Treaty of Poona in June 1817, “Bassein with the rest of the north Konkan passed to the British”. [n15; n28; Gaz XIV p. 35]

XI. The empty fort, 1818–1864

“Scarcely a house habitable”

The British found a ruin. In 1818 the fort was judged of no military value; “scarcely a house” was habitable, and a small detachment was kept there only for a time. By 1824 it was uninhabited and locked, with a small guard under a conductor of ordnance. [n28; Gaz XIV] In 1825 Bishop Reginald Heber found it “perfectly uninhabited, a melancholy display of ruined houses and churches covered with a rank growth of trees and brushwood”; the ruins were “of mean architecture, but striking from their lofty proportions and from the singularity of Christian and European ruins in India”. He was told that one church had been built by a man who made his fortune in the slipper trade. [n15; Gaz XIV pp. 31 n., 35] In 1838 Mrs Postans described Bassein as “long forsaken with no inhabitants except a few fishermen and hunters”. [n15; n28; Gaz XIV p. 35] An 1882 line says the fort had been “almost deserted” since 1838. [n28]

Those “few fishermen” are the first mention in the British record of the people who would become the Killa Bandar fishing village beside the fort, and the record gives no more about them for a long time.

A market town outside the walls

The life of the place had moved out of the walls. John Vaupell, writing in 1837, found Bassein “the chief market town of a petty division”, with shops mostly held by Gujarati Vanis and a few Muslims. [n28; n32; Gaz XIV pp. 35–36] The town that grew there stood half a mile inland, north of the fort walls, in the village of Malonde. [n32; Gaz XIV p. 28] The Parsis had come early: one rest-house was built by Lavji Sorabji Lakdawala in 1780, under the Marathas, and another by Dadabhai and Maneckji Pestonji Wadia in 1836. [n32; n11; Gaz XIV p. 37] The name Lakdawala suggests a timber trader, and the Wadias were the shipbuilders of Bombay from 1735 onward; the notes do not say what brought either family to Bassein, but timber and ships were what Bassein had always offered. [n36] A travellers’ bungalow was built in 1834. [n32; n28]

In 1827 Bassein got the first Government vernacular school in the whole Thana district; the second opened at Kalyan three years later. [n21; n28; Gaz XIII-II pp. 656–657]

Sugar in the ruins

The British tried to make the empty fort pay, and sugar was their idea. In 1829 a Mr Lingard was given a forty-year rent-free lease of about 83 acres on the fort’s esplanade and an advance of Rs 23,000 to grow Mauritius cane and build a mill; in the same year the land inside the fort was leased to a Mr Cardoza for thirty years at Rs 400 a year. Lingard’s mill was begun about 1830; he died in 1832, and the scheme stopped. [n21; n28; Gaz XIII-I pp. 394–395; Gaz XIV pp. 35–36] The government then leased the estate, two years rent-free, to a Hindu, Narayan Krishna, from 1833 to 1836; that venture failed too. McGregor Brownrigg and Company tried next, took a perpetual lease of about 115 acres in 1841, reported in 1843 that cane did not thrive at Bassein, switched crops, and sold to a Mr Joseph in 1848. [n28; n21] In 1852 Mrs Xavier, a daughter of Cardoza, was allowed to turn the ruined church of N. S. da Vida into a sugar factory, subletting to Littlewood; this is the factory whose foundations turned up the general and his horse. [n28; n32] In 1859 the estate passed to Dosabhai Jahangir and J. H. Littlewood. [n28]

In 1860 the interior of the fort was leased for thirty years to Major Littlewood, as the Gazetteer puts it (the Population volume names Macfarlane and Littlewood, at Rs 271 a year, as the “Bassein Sugar Company”). It was sold in 1861 to Lawrence and Company and bought in 1868 by Littlewood, Worthing and Navroji Manekji. By 1881 the company “has long ceased to exist”, and the factory in N. S. da Vida had closed about 1874. [n21; n28; n32; Gaz XIII-I pp. 394–395; Gaz XIV pp. 36–38] When the Gazetteer was compiled, the widow held the lease and her son cultivated the fields inside the walls. On 24 January 1882 the Collector, W. B. Mulock, wrote that this arrangement made “the proper preservation of the ruins almost impossible”. [n32; Gaz XIV pp. 37–38] The columns of the citadel portal had already been carried away to adorn a village church. [n32; da Cunha p. 217]

In 1856 a road was cut through the old town to the landing place. [n32; n11; Gaz XIV p. 36] It is the road the buses to Killa Bandar still follow.

XII. Bassein Road and the municipality, 1864–1900

The railway

On 28 November 1864 the Bombay, Baroda and Central India Railway opened its line through the district. Bassein Road station, today’s Vasai Road, with the code BSR that still keeps the old name, was on the first passenger run from Grant Road to Ahmedabad. Construction had begun in May 1858; the line crossed the creek on the two Panju bridges described above. [n05; n21; Gaz XIII-I pp. 328–329; Wikipedia: Vasai Road railway station] The station stood not at the town but at Manikpur, about four and a half miles east of the fort, where the railway crossed the channel that separated the island from the mainland. In the 1880s the station itself was often called Manikpur. [n32; n11; n12; da Cunha p. 157; Gaz XIV pp. 314, 341]

On 1 November 1865, by one account, the railway started a local service between Grant Road and Bassein Road, one train each way, with Bassein Road as its northern end; in December Rs 6,522 was sanctioned for a second roofed platform, because two passenger trains were often in the station together. Other sources date the first regular suburban service to 1867, from Back Bay to Virar; the dates conflict. [n05; n28] If the 1865 account is right, Bassein Road was the northern terminus of Bombay’s first commuter train, more than a century before Vasai became a commuter suburb.

A metalled road four and a half miles long linked the station to the town. (The Gazetteer’s OCR prints “44 miles”, a misreading of 4½.) [n32; Gaz XIV p. 28] Traffic grew fast. Passengers at Bassein Road rose from 86,473 in 1873 to 140,837 in 1880, though goods fell from 5,292 tons to 3,278. [n32; n21; Gaz XIV p. 36] Goods, it seems, still went by boat. The station had a waiting room and a travellers’ bungalow. [n21] Near it, a double-storeyed rest-house held two inscribed stones in its compound, brought from Nandui and from “Nila” near Sopara. [n40; Gaz XIV p. 387]

The municipality of 1864

The town got its civic government the same year as its railway. “In 1864 a municipality was established”, the Gazetteer records, by Government Resolution 621 of 15 March 1864, “including besides the old fort and most of the modern town which stand in the village of Malonde, the survey villages of Dhauli, Sandore and Mulgaon, and many gardens and fields between Bassein and Pápdi, about a mile and a half to the east”. [n32; n21; Gaz XIV p. 36] Dhauli is the village of the Remedy church, Sandore is Sandor, and Mulgaon lies beside Pali; so the municipality of 1864 took in the fort, the town and three of the old Christian garden villages around them. Bassein was one of seven of the district’s nine municipalities set up under Act XXVI of 1850. [n21; Gaz XIII-II pp. 653–654]

Its money was modest. In 1880–81 it took Rs 7,141 (about £714) from octroi, house and privy taxes, tolls and market fees, and spent Rs 6,637, of which about £117 went on scavenging, £46 on lighting and £77 on roads. [n32; n21; Gaz XIV p. 36] A census volume of 1872 gives the municipality 9,356 inhabitants and an income of Rs 6,579, though its digits are uncertain. [n21]

The town filled with the buildings of a British taluka headquarters. The mamlatdar’s office, the seat of the sub-division’s revenue officer, was finished in 1869 at a cost of Rs 35,530. In 1872 the Balvantrao Hari Naik dispensary was founded in a house given by Yashvantrao Balvant Naik; it had an assistant surgeon and, in 1880–81, 18,324 out-patients and 43 in-patients, paid for by the government (Rs 1,730), the municipality (Rs 1,700) and local funds (Rs 600). (Another Gazetteer volume gives different figures, probably for another year, and says the building was in bad repair by 1881.) [n32; n21; n28; Gaz XIV p. 36] In 1877 the government granted the municipality a public garden, by Government Resolution 1265 of 26 February 1877. Opposite it stood the Robertson fruit and vegetable markets, named after the Collector James Walker Robertson, with the municipal office on the upper floor. [n32; n11; Gaz XIV pp. 36–37]

Schools and a newspaper

By 1881 the town had six vernacular schools. The chief of them, with room for 150 pupils, met in a school-house built in 1878 at a cost of Rs 5,601. [n32; n21; Gaz XIV p. 36] The Catholic parishes ran their own schools: the Dhauli (Remedy) church had about 15 pupils, Pali about 30 and Papdi 56, the last with a government grant and a violin master. [n32; n11; Gaz XIV p. 37] The town also had a newspaper: the Vasai Samachar, “the Bassein News”, a Marathi weekly of five years’ standing in 1882, published on Sundays at Rs 2½ a year. [n21; Gaz XIII-II p. 662] That makes it one of the earliest newspapers anywhere on this coast.

Port, salt and market

Bassein was still a port. In the five years to 1878–79 its exports averaged Rs 5,14,140 (£51,414) a year and its imports Rs 2,25,200 (£22,520), though both swung widely, exports from about £20,700 in 1876–77 to £97,480 in 1875–76. It had “a good landing place and custom house”. [n32; n21; Gaz XIV p. 36] Together with Agashi and Navghar it made up the Bassein group of ports, whose trade in 1881–82 was estimated at £108,868, mostly exports: husked rice, molasses, coconuts and plantains to Bombay and Gujarat, firewood and tiles to Bombay. In came wheat, pulse, ghee, timber, piece-goods, oil and oil-cake, and lime. [n21; n36; Gaz XIII-I p. 358] The traders were “Musalmans, Marathas, local Vanis, and a few Brahmans”, about half of them natives of Bassein, the rest from Kalyan, Bhiwandi, Bombay and Gujarat, who stayed from December to June. The local boats, mhangiris, machvas, padavs and batelas of fifty to seventy tons, were “built locally by Marathas and Native Christians”. [n36; n21; Gaz XIII-I pp. 358–359] Of all the coasting craft of the Thana coast, the Gazetteer says, “most” were built “in the Bassein sub-division, chiefly at Bassein”, by “Marathas, Christians, Gujarat carpenters, Cutch Musalmans, Kolis, and Pachkalshis”. [n36; Gaz XIII-I] The shipwrights of Portuguese Baçaim had become the boat-builders of British Bassein.

Salt was the other trade of the creek. In 1880–81 the Bassein salt division had seventeen works in six groups, with 11,374 pans on 1,439 acres and thirty owners, “widely scattered on the north bank of the Bassein creek”. Agris worked them, and in the fair season Dublas from Gujarat worked “only on the salt-works near Bassein Road station”. [n21; Gaz XIII-I pp. 364–366, 370]

Plague

The plague that reached Bombay in 1896 came to Bassein in 1897. A search summary of the Bombay government’s plague reports, not checked on the page, gives Bassein town about 11,291 people, with 497 cases and 377 deaths between 1 February and 1 June 1897; Thana was ranked the worst district of the presidency by mortality. These figures are provisional. [n28]

XIII. The peoples of the town

The count of 1881

The census of 1881 gave Bassein town 10,356 people: 6,850 Hindus, 2,623 Christians, 885 Muslims, 29 of what the census called “wild tribes”, 14 Parsis and 5 Jews. [n32; n21; Gaz XIV p. 28] The whole Bassein sub-division, 221 square miles, had 68,967 people: 52,578 Hindus (76 per cent), 14,070 Christians (20 per cent), 2,292 Muslims (3 per cent) and 27 Parsis. [n21; Gaz XIII-II pp. 678–680] So the town was about a quarter Christian, a little more than the countryside, and about two-thirds Hindu. Fifty years later, in 1931, the Bassein municipality had 12,689 people, of whom 7,392 were Hindus and 1,148 Muslims; the Christian figure for the town is not legible in the scan, and the Jains read as “about 3”, a digit the OCR may have damaged. [n21; Census 1931 Vol VIII Pt II, Imperial Table V] The town had grown by only about a fifth in half a century.

Behind those totals were many distinct peoples. This section takes them in turn, saying what the record holds of each in and around the town.

The Christians: four peoples under one name

The 2,623 Christians of the 1881 town were the descendants of the converts of the Portuguese centuries, and they were not one community. The Gazetteer itself noticed that “in matters of marriage the lower classes keep to their old caste distinctions”, that the Bhois, Kumbhars, Nhavis and Dhobis among the Christians formed separate castes, and that the Mhar Christians were not, until recently, allowed to draw water from the village well or enter the church. The upper classes called themselves Firgi, from “Feringhi”. [n37; n34; Gaz XIII-I pp. 199–202] In 1887, in Bombay, about thirty leaders of the North Konkan Catholics resolved to drop the name “Bombay Portuguese” for “Bombay East Indian”, founded the Bombay East Indian Association on 26 May, and gave the whole community the name it still uses. [n34; n04; Mendiratta pp. 234–236] Under that umbrella, the standard modern classification, from Elsie Baptista’s book of 1967 and Rodricks’s later study, distinguishes five groups: the Samvedi Christians, “commonly called Kuparis”; the Koli Christians; the Vadvals; the Salsette Christians; and an urbanised group. [n34; n41; Sahapedia] Three of the five lived in and around Bassein town.

The Vadvals. The Christians of the parishes immediately around the fort, Sandor, Papdi, Remedy, Merces, Pali, Manickpur and Chulne, were mainly Vadvals, the Christian descendants of the Pachkalshi and Chaukalshi gardening castes. The Gazetteer names them: “The traders, Christian cartmen chiefly of Chulna, Mánikpur, Gokhivra, Sándor, and Barhánpur in Bassein, are known as Vadvals or gardeners.” They bought fallen timber from the government forests of Vada and cut green wood in Jawhar; “the Vadvals are the best axemen in the district, and their carts are larger and their cattle stronger than those found in other sub-divisions”; they travelled by night in gangs of five to thirty carts. [n40; n41; Gaz XIII-I pp. 97–98] Elsewhere the Gazetteer calls them “among the most skilful cultivators in the district”. [n41; Gaz XIII-I p. 205] Their speech, Vadvali, a Marathi dialect with Gujarati forms that the linguist Grierson recorded in 1905, is today endangered. [n41; LSI VII p. 145] The 1864 municipality’s survey villages, Dhauli and Sandor, were Vadval villages, so the Vadvals were, from the start, citizens of the municipal town as well as its neighbours. Their villages, parishes and dialect are described in the next chapter, in Chapter 31 and in Chapter 38.

The Koli Christians. “The Uraps and other Christian Kolis in Bassein”, the Gazetteer says, “are professional fishermen, but the mass of the sea-fishing population are Son Kolis.” [n08; n21; Gaz XIII-I p. 55] Koli Christians lived, by a modern Wikipedia page, in Bassein and Agashi as well as Bombay, Bandra and Gorai, and shared customs with Hindu Kolis, among them the shakarpuda engagement; a Koli Christian bridegroom traditionally wore an old Portuguese admiral’s uniform. [n08; n04] The Gazetteer’s description of a lower-class Christian wedding fits: the groom wore “the full European dress of bygone days, a scarlet or black military coat with cocked hat, epaulets, knee-breeches”, a costume hired for five to ten rupees, and the Times of India in 1881 saw one carrying a sword “preserved… as an heirloom”. Koli weddings were held in the afternoon, with native music. [n34; Gaz XIII-I pp. 211–212] The Koliwada beside the fort and the fishing village of Killa Bandar, described in the next section, are the Koli quarters of the town. In 2014 it was “Koliwada youth” who decorated the old Jesuit church for the fort feast. [n07; n38]

The Kuparis. The Kuparis, the Samvedi Christians, were not a people of the town in 1881 in the way the Vadvals and Kolis were. Their villages lay north, in the Sopara and Agashi gardens. But the town was their market, their courthouse, their railway station and, after 1998, their cathedral city.

The Kupari passage that the evidence supports is this. The Gazetteer of 1882 never uses the word “Kupari”; it speaks of “Christians, many of them converted Sámvedi Bráhmans”, and of “Native Christians originally Sámvedis and Pachkalshis”. [n34; n40; Gaz XIV p. 315; Gaz XIII-I p. 395] Their Hindu kin, the Samvedi Brahmans, numbered only 2,563 in 1872 and lived “only in Bassein and Máhim”; the Gazetteer described them as “strong, tall, and fair”, “clean, neat, sober, thrifty and orderly”, husbandmen and gardeners in one-storeyed houses of wooden planking with tiled roofs, who spoke “incorrect Maráthi” with words like áde for “where”. [n08; Gaz XIII-I p. 82] The Christian Samvedis shared much with them. The men of both wore red broadcloth caps; the spirit Sathi or Sati, warded off on the fifth or sixth night after a birth, appears in both communities’ customs. [n34; Gaz XIII-I pp. 82, 204–205, 210–211] Among the Christians of Bassein, “the proposal comes from the boy’s side” in marriage, unlike Salsette. [n34; Gaz XIII-I p. 211 n.] They were, with the Vadvals, the gardeners the Gazetteer called “the most hardworking and skilful husbandmen” of the district, who “turned the light sandy country about Bassein into an evergreen garden”. [n21; n41; Gaz XIII-I p. 300] “Wealthy families who are village headmen and owners of rich garden lands often bring up one or more of their sons for the Church, and a considerable number who know English are employed in Bombay chiefly as clerks.” [n34; Gaz XIII-I p. 205]

What drew Kuparis into the town was school, office and church. The taluka courts, the mamlatdar’s office of 1869, the station of 1864 and later the high schools of the twentieth century were all in or near Bassein. The community’s own accounts say that farming dominated Kupari life until the late 1960s and early 1970s, and then gave way to engineering, medicine, banking, teaching and emigration, much of it through the town’s schools and its railway. [n34; n11; Vasai Kupari blog 2007; Post in Mumbai 2015] Those accounts are uncited and their population figures, 40,000 to 60,000, are estimates. [n34] The town’s own distinct Kupari presence, a Kupari quarter or association in Vasai itself, is not recorded in any source the notes reached.

The strongest single tie between the Kuparis and the town is ecclesiastical. When the Diocese of Vasai was created on 22 May 1998, its first bishop, Thomas Dabre, was born at Bhuigaon, a village on the community’s own list of Kupari villages, and he was installed on 15 August 1998 at the church of Our Lady of Grace in Papdi, beside the town, which became the cathedral. [n40; n34; n39; GCatholic] No source the notes reached states Bishop Dabre’s community; the link is through his birthplace. [n34] The present bishop, Thomas D’Souza, was born at Chulne, a Vadval village. [n40] The Kuparis’ Gonsalo Garcia feast at Gass and the fort feast in the town are the two great annual occasions of the saint. [n09; n07]

The wider East Indian community. Among the town’s Christians there were also, by the twentieth century, families from Goa and Mangalore and later Catholic migrants from elsewhere in India, whom the notes do not count separately. The Gazetteer’s “upper classes”, the Firgi who wore European dress, generally black, and the clerks on the morning trains, are the beginnings of the urban East Indian middle class of the town. [n34; Gaz XIII-I pp. 199–206] By 2011 Christians of every kind were about 8 per cent of Vasai taluka, about 110,800 people. [n34; Wikipedia: Vasai taluka]

The Brahmans: Samvedi, Palshe, Chitpavan, Karhada, Deshastha

The Brahmans of the town and its neighbourhood fell into two layers. The old layer was the Palshes, the Vajasaneyi Brahmans, priests, physicians and astrologers who held the privilege of fixing auspicious times for marriages and thread ceremonies “for all classes in Salsette, Bassein, Mahim and Dahanu”. In 1872 Thana had 9,311 of them; the Gazetteer thought they had come from Gujarat with the Prabhus and Pachkalshis, though their own tradition said 1297, from Paithan, with King Bimb. [n08; Gaz XIII-I p. 81] They were, the Gazetteer said, the losers of 1740: the insulting folk etymology of their name as “flesh-eater” was “probably the work of their rivals the Deccan and Chitpávan Bráhmans at whose hands the Palshes have suffered much since the Maratha conquest”. [n08; Gaz XIII-I p. 81 n.] It was Palshe Brahmans of Bassein who in 1820 and 1828 readmitted the Christian Agris to Hinduism: Ramchandra Baba Joshi, who took Rs 1,200 and was put out of caste for a time, and Vithal Hari Naik Vaidya, “a Palshe Brahman of Bassein”, who readmitted another group on 12 November 1828. [n08; n37; Gaz XIII-I p. 117] The dispensary of 1872 was founded in the name of a Balvantrao Hari Naik, in a house given by a Yashvantrao Balvant Naik; the notes do not state that family’s caste, and the shared “Hari Naik” with the 1828 priest is a coincidence of names, not a proven link. [n32; n08]

The newer layer came with the Marathas: the Chitpavan, Karhada and Deshastha Brahmans whom da Cunha found living at Papdi, “a cannon-shot from Bajipura”, in 1876. [n32; n40; da Cunha p. 157] The Samvedi Brahmans, the third Brahman people of the area, lived in the northern garden villages, not in the town; Chapter 31 and the next chapter describe them. The 1881 caste count gives the Bassein sub-division 5,382 Brahmans of all kinds and 827 Kayastha Prabhus, though the scan’s digits are fuzzy. [n21; Gaz XIII-II p. 680]

The Pachkalshis

The Hindu Pachkalshis, the Somvanshi Kshatriya Pathares, numbered 15,367 in Thana in 1872, “strongest along the coast”, and their surnames were Raut, Vartak and Chodhri. “Who can call a Pachkalshi idle?” ran their proverb. They were husbandmen, gardeners and carpenters, and their wives sold vegetables, butter and milk. In their houses images of Cheda and other local spirits stood beside Ganpati, Shiv and Krishna. [n08; n41; Gaz XIII-I pp. 129–130] Their part in the siege of 1739 is told above. In the twentieth century the most prominent Vartak of Vasai was Hari Govindrao “Bhausaheb” Vartak (1914–1998), Bassein MLA from 1962, a minister under Vasantrao Naik, Padma Shri in 1991, and with Tarabai Vartak (1926–2008) the founder of the Vidyavardhini educational institutions of Vasai. A Wikipedia list counts them among the community; their own pages do not state it. [n41; n08] The same caution applies to the town’s dominant politician of the last thirty years, Hitendra Thakur. [n41]

The Kolis of the shore

The Hindu fishing people of the creek mouth were Son Kolis, Mangelas and Vaitis. The Gazetteer estimated about 8,000 Son Kolis “chiefly along the coast south of the Vaitarna”, sturdy, top-knotted men who wore red broadcloth caps; their women wore glass bangles on the left hand only, because at weddings the bangles meant for the right hand were “consecrated and thrown into the sea, the ocean being invoked to take care of the husband”. Their tribal head was the Vagh Patil at Alibag. [n08; Gaz XIII-I pp. 148–149] The 1881 count gives the Bassein sub-division 2,375 Mangelas and 77 Machhis, and the Machhis of Bassein, the Gazetteer says, spoke Gujarati at home and Marathi outside, fished, hired out boats and worshipped Maruti. [n21; n08; Gaz XIII-I p. 147; Gaz XIII-II p. 680] In 1851 the district had 129 salt-water fisheries, 22 of them in Bassein. Before Act XIX of 1844 the Kolis, Mangelas and Vaitis had paid a poll tax for the right to fish. “Good oysters are found along the Bassein, Mahim and Dahanu coasts.” [n21; Gaz XIII-I pp. 54–56] A modern Wikipedia list includes Vasai and Pachu Bandar among Mangela Koli villages, and in Vasai, as at Worli and Colaba, the Koli Holi pyre is built of areca-nut branches. [n08]

The Vanis of Papdi and the Lad Vanis

The Hindu merchants of the town were Gujarati. In 1837 Vaupell found the shops “mostly held by Gujarati Banias”. [n28; n32] The Gazetteer records that Gujarat Vanis and Brahmans moved to Sopara and Bassein about 1760–66, and that the Lad Vanis of Sopara came from Cambay about 1760, “when its traders fled from the exactions of Momin Khan II”. [n36; n37; Gaz XIII-I p. 63; Gaz XIV p. 315] Lad Vani families lived at Nala, Agashi and Papdi. [n05; Gaz XIII-I] At Papdi the Vanis ran the betel-nut trade of the whole Bassein garden belt. The areca palms of the gardens, grown partly by Christians, “Malis converted by the Portuguese”, and partly by Chavkalshi and Pachkalshi Hindus, were sold wholesale “to the Vanis of Papdi, about two miles from Bassein”, who sorted the nuts into six classes, phulbardi, tambdi, chikni, lavangchuri, pandhri and dagdi or kapkadi. “Bassein is famous for its phulbardi betelnuts.” Prices at Papdi ran from Rs 21 to Rs 36 a maund of 80 pounds, and the nuts went to Bombay, Poona and Gujarat. [n21; n41; Gaz XIII-I pp. 298–300] Papdi’s “Shah Wada” of the 1850s, which the CRIT survey lists, may belong to one of these families; the survey does not say. [n40]

The Muslims

The 885 Muslims of the 1881 town were the heirs of the Gujarat port of 1514. In 1872 the whole Bassein sub-division had 2,025 Muslims, 1,866 Sunni and 159 Shia. [n08; Gaz XIII-I pp. 216–217] The oldest group, the Konkanis, descended from the Arab and Persian settlers of the eighth to tenth centuries; their home speech was a Marathi dialect, and well-to-do Konkani families owned brick houses of two or three storeys. The Naitas of Bassein had been rich traders when the Portuguese came. [n08; n37; Gaz XIII-I pp. 216–217] After the destruction of the mosques in the 1530s and the hard Portuguese treatment of Muslims, the Muslim population of the town recovered under the Marathas and the British; by 1881 Muslims were, with Marathas and local Vanis, among the town’s port traders. [n36; Gaz XIII-I p. 358] In the twentieth century the Muslims of the Koliwada built the Gausia Masjid: it began as a prayer shed, the foundation stone of its present two-storey building was laid in 1982, and it runs an Arabic school with free lodging for about fifty students. [n10; palghar.gov.in] Near Pali, a little east of the town, the CRIT survey records a sixteenth-century dargah with a step well, a dome, minarets and stone lattices, and an eighteenth-century mosque whose architecture mixes Islamic, Portuguese and Maratha wada features; it notes that “before the Portuguese domination” the area had “a significant Muslim population due to the traders and merchants who had settled here”. [n40; CRIT Vol III] The notes found no modern history of Vasai town’s Muslim families. [n08]

Parsis, Jews and Jains

The 14 Parsis of 1881 were the few heirs of the two Parsi rest-house builders of 1780 and 1836. [n32; n21] The 5 Jews of 1881 are a single line in the census; the notes say nothing more about them. [n32] The Jains of the town were very few: the 1881 Hindu caste list gives 80 Jains in the whole sub-division, and the 1931 town table, as legible, about three. [n21] The Jain centre of the area was not the town but Agashi, where a temple of Parasnath was built in the early nineteenth century by Motilal, “a Vani of Bombay”, and Sopara, an ancient Jain tirtha. [n40; n08; n10] Today Gujarati and Marwari Jain families are part of the town’s trading community, but the notes give no figures. [n08]

The hill peoples

The 29 “wild tribes” of the 1881 town were presumably Varlis, Katkaris or Thakurs from the eastern hills; the census does not say. The Bassein sub-division had 6,099 Varlis in 1872, and 1,600 Kathkaris and 698 Thakurs in 1881. [n08; n21] Their lives were in the forests of Tungar and the villages east of the railway, described in the next chapter.

Agris and Bhandaris

Two large Hindu peoples of the countryside appear in the town mainly through their work. The Agris worked the salt pans on the north bank of the creek and the rice land; in 1881 the sub-division had perhaps 8,461 of them (the leading digit may be a 3). The Urap Agris were the Christian Agris who went back to Hinduism in 1820 and 1828, keeping only surnames like Gomes, Soz, Fernan, Frutad and Minez. [n21; n08; n37; Gaz XIII-I p. 117] The Bhandaris, the palm-tappers, perhaps 8,334 in the sub-division in 1881 (again the digit is uncertain), sang while they tapped the trees and carried heavy tapping knives on their left thighs; after the palm-tree cess was raised in 1877, many became husbandmen and labourers, and “the Bassein Bhandaris continued unruly and discontented”. [n21; n08; Gaz XIII-I p. 150; Gaz XIII-II pp. 643–649]

XIV. The quarters of the town

Killa Bandar

Killa is “fort” and bandar “harbour”: Killa Bandar is the fort jetty, and the name the buses to the fort still carry. A village of the same name, Killabandar, lies beside the fort. [n32; n11; n04] The Gazetteer’s good landing place and custom house of the 1880s, reached by the road cut through the old town in 1856, was here. [n32; Gaz XIV p. 36]

What the record does not hold is Killa Bandar’s fishing life: its boats, its catch, its families, the history of its jetty. The research behind this book looked for it in the Gazetteers, da Cunha, English and Marathi newspapers, and found nothing village-specific. [n32; n11; n20] What can be said is general. The “few fishermen and hunters” Mrs Postans saw in 1838 lived in or beside the ruins. [n15] The Koli Christians and Uraps of Bassein were “professional fishermen” in 1882. [n21] The Koliwada youth decorated the fort church for the feast in 2014. [n38] The Gausia Masjid of 1982 is in the Koliwada, and so is a church of St Peter with a wayside cross on its grounds. [n10; n09] So the shore quarter of the town, on the evidence, is Christian, Hindu and Muslim at once. Marathi newspapers name Arnala, Pachu Bandar and Naigaon, not Killa Bandar, as the main fishing harbours of the Vasai coast. [n20]

Vasai Gaon: Malonde

The town that the British knew, the Bassein of the municipality, stood in the village of Malonde, half a mile inland and to the north of the fort walls. [n32; n11; Gaz XIV p. 28] Today it is called Vasai Gaon, “Vasai village”, to tell it from the railway town at Vasai Road. [n11; n40] The mamlatdar’s office of 1869, the dispensary of 1872, the school-house of 1878, the public garden of 1877 and the Robertson markets were all here. [n32] Its streets, on the Gazetteer’s description of the garden country, would have been lined by tiled houses with wooden walls on high masonry plinths. [n11; Gaz XIV p. 315] The notes found no street-level history of Vasai Gaon after 1882.

Papdi

A mile and a half to the east, Papdi was a village of the Maratha Brahmans and Gujarati betel-nut Vanis, and a Christian parish whose church of Our Lady of Grace, rebuilt in 1865 for Rs 18,000, had a congregation of 1,294 in 1881. [n32; n40; n11; Gaz XIV p. 37] It was, and is, the town’s nearest large neighbour, and part of it lay within the municipality of 1864. Since 1998 its church has been the cathedral of the Diocese of Vasai; in 2025 the parish community’s restoration of it won a UNESCO Asia-Pacific Award of Merit. [n11; n40] The CRIT survey’s Papdy Talav precinct, with its Ram Mandir, Bondali Talav, Govardhan Vidyalaya, the Mohanlal Vakil Bungalow, Shah Wada and the Thomas Baptista School, is the nearest thing the record holds to a portrait of a Vasai neighbourhood in the twentieth century. [n40; CRIT Vol II] The next chapter describes Papdi fully.

Parnaka

Parnaka, today one of the busiest junctions of Vasai West, appears in the notes only once, as part of a road name: the CRIT survey’s “Holy Market precinct” at Remedy lies on the “Remedy–Par Naka Road”, where vendors from Sandor, Kaular Budruk and neighbouring villages pay five rupees a day to sell. [n40; CRIT Vol I] Naka in Marathi is a junction or toll point. The origin of the name, and the history of the place, are not recorded in any source reached.

Manickpur and Bassein Road

Manickpur, about four miles north-east of the fort, was the Jesuit parish of St Michael, founded in 1606, destroyed by the Marathas in 1739, and rebuilt in 1851; its Portuguese name was Porim, or Purim, and the village took the name Manikpur, probably in the nineteenth century (a parish history says early in the twentieth, but the Gazetteer already uses “Manikpur” in 1882). [n40; n11; Gaz XIV p. 37] When the railway came in 1864, Bassein Road station was built here, and Manickpur became the hinge between the old town by the sea and the new railway settlement. About 500 or 800 yards south-west of the station lay a place called Puri, “said to be Burhanpur”, named in a Maratha chronicle of the 1738 siege; the Gazetteer weighed it as a candidate for the Shilahara capital Puri and found “no signs of old remains”. [n11; n37; Gaz XIV p. 402] The station’s neighbourhood grew into Navghar-Manikpur, one of the four municipal councils that merged into the city corporation in 2009. [n12; n29] The Vasai Road–Diva line of the Central Railway, which runs east from this junction to Juchandra and Kaman Road, is the twentieth-century railway’s addition; the notes do not give its opening date. [n12]

Diwanman and Ambadi

Two more names of today’s Vasai West, Diwanman and Ambadi, appear in no source the research reached: not in the Gazetteer, not in da Cunha, not in the heritage survey, and not in the newspapers searched. [n11; n32] Their history is a gap.

XV. The twentieth-century town, 1900–1988

A quiet half-century

The first half of the twentieth century is the thinnest stretch of this town’s record. The district gazetteers and census reports of 1901 to 1941 were not found in readable form, and the research behind this book found almost nothing dated and specific to Bassein town between 1900 and 1946. [n28] What can be said comes mostly from the railway, the census and the church.

The census shows slow growth. The taluka had about 80,251 people in 1901 and 79,978 in 1911, in two towns and ninety villages; the 1921 census report called Bassein a town “outside the suburbanisation zone”, with no marked increase, though it also noted that the talukas of North Salsette, Kalyan and Bassein “have come under the influence of suburbanisation”. [n21; Census 1911, 1921] By 1931 the municipality had 12,689 people. [n21] The influenza of 1918 struck Thana district hard, but no figure for Bassein survives in the notes. [n21; n28]

The railway changed slowly. The creek bridges were strengthened between 1896 and 1902, and rebuilt from 1920, with the main work from 1923 and pile-driving trials at Panju on 11 September 1923; the new bridges were well advanced by August 1925. [n05; n28] A road bridge from Ghodbandar to Naigaon is dated 1928 by one weak source; the standard account dates the first road bridge on the highway to 1968. [n28; n12] In 1936 the two lines from Borivali to Virar were electrified, so that electric local trains ran through Bassein Road. [n28] The BB&CI became the Western Railway on 5 November 1951. [n28]

Schools

The church built the town’s modern schools. The Carmelite Convent School in Vasai dates its history from 1939, by its own account, read only as a search summary. [n28] St Augustine’s High School was founded by the Christian Brothers in 1970 or 1972; the sources disagree. [n28] On 9 February 1986 Pope John Paul II, on his Indian journey, spoke at St Augustine’s High School in Vasai; the Vatican keeps the text of the speech. [n40; Vatican] Vidyavardhini, founded in 1970, built the colleges and polytechnic named for Bhausaheb Vartak. [n41]

The town in the Republic

After 1947 the town was the seat of the Bassein taluka of Thana district, and from 1952 to 1972 its seat in the legislature was called “Bassein”; Wikipedia lists no members for those years. From 1978 the Vasai constituency elected Pandharinath Choudhari (Janata Party, 1978), Tarabai Vartak (Congress, 1980), Dominic Gonsalves (Janata Party, 1985) and, in March 1990, a twenty-nine-year-old Congress candidate from Virar, Hitendra Thakur. [n28; n29] Four members in twelve years from three parties, followed by one man for nearly two decades: the political history of the town turned in 1990.

The 1981 census found the whole Vasai–Virar region with fewer than 250,000 people; Vasai town had 34,900 and Virar 23,303. Many towns of the region had actually lost population since 1971. [n05; n37; TIFR] The town had grown nearly threefold since 1931, mostly after independence, but it was still a garden-belt market town with a fort, a dozen parishes and a railway station four miles away.

XVI. From green belt to city, 1988–2009

1988

In August 1988 the state government converted the green belt of Vasai–Virar to residential use, against the advice of the Mumbai Metropolitan Region Development Authority, and without the roads, drains and water that a city would need. [n05; n29; TIFR] By 1991 the region’s population had risen by about 150 per cent; Vasai, Virar, Sopara and Sandor, the TIFR study says, grew by 290 per cent. [n05] The town that had grown threefold in fifty years now grew threefold in three.

Harit Vasai

The resistance came from the town’s own church and its villages. On 1 April 1989 Father Francis D’Britto, then editor of the Catholic Marathi periodical Suvarta, called a meeting of public figures; from it came the Harit Vasai Saurakshan Samiti, the “Save Green Vasai” committee. On 1 October 1989 it held a rally of about 35,000, with the playwright Vijay Tendulkar among the speakers. In 1990 the state made CIDCO the planning authority for the Vasai–Virar sub-region (14 May 1990 by one official document; other sources say 1991), and in September 1990 CIDCO’s interim plan, the committee said, put holding ponds and dumping grounds in the green zone. [n29; Sabrang 2000] On 26 January 1992 about 100,000 people gathered at the Vasai grounds, and about 100,000 signatures went to the Governor; CIDCO later revised its plans to keep the green belt. (The source names the Prime Minister of the day as V. P. Singh, who was no longer in office in 1992; that is an error in the source.) [n29] In 1991, a Times of India report quoted by Sabrang described violence against Christian residents of the area linked to a dispute over tankers drawing well water from the green belt; tankers were later banned from drawing water there. [n29]

Father D’Britto (1943–2024), “of Jeladi”, a hamlet of Nandakhal parish in the Kupari belt, became the movement’s face. He won the Marathi Sahitya Akademi Award in 2014, wrote the Marathi Subodh Bible (2010), and in January 2020 presided over the 93rd Akhil Bharatiya Marathi Sahitya Sammelan at Osmanabad, the first Christian to do so. He died on 25 July 2024, aged eighty-one; about 3,000 people attended his funeral at Nandakhal. “The greenery that you see now is because of the movement,” an advocate, Noel Dabre, said; the state had limited development to 1.5 kilometres on either side of the railway line. [n29; n40; n05] The Harit Vasai movement is told fully in Chapter 15. In this chapter it matters as the moment when the town’s old communities, the Christian and Hindu gardening villages around it, organised to decide what the town would become.

The diocese and the corporation

On 22 May 1998 Pope John Paul II created the Diocese of Vasai, carved from the Archdiocese of Bombay. Its creation was announced at the Shrine of Our Lady of Remedy, at Remedy beside the town, on 28 June 1998, and its first bishop was installed at Papdi on 15 August. [n40; n09] For the first time since 1739, Vasai was the seat of a bishop’s church.

On 3 July 2009 the state constituted the Vasai-Virar City Municipal Corporation from four municipal councils, Vasai, Virar, Nallasopara and Navghar-Manikpur, and 53 villages; some sources give 2010. [n32; n12; n29] The Bassein municipality of 1864, after 145 years, became part of a city of more than a million people. The notes do not record when the Bassein municipality was renamed Vasai, or how its boundaries changed between 1882 and 2009. [n32; n28] The inclusion of the villages was bitterly contested; 49 of the 53 opposed it, and in 2011 the state moved to exclude 29 of them, a decision still in the courts in 2025. [n40; n29]

XVII. The fort in modern times: protection, conservation and controversy

A national monument

The fort is a monument of national importance protected by the Archaeological Survey of India, listed as N-MH-M14, monument number 110 of its Mumbai Circle, with its condition given as “dilapidated”. [n04; Wikipedia: Vasai Fort] The date of its notification was not found in the notes. (Arnala fort, by contrast, has been a protected monument since 26 May 1909.) [n11; n32]

The quarrels over conservation

Conservation has been controversial. Newspaper reports, several of them read only in summary and undated, record conservationists’ complaints: white plaster patches on the old walls; the Mumbai heritage figure Tasneem Mehta’s objection to the use of cement; antiquities from excavations “left in a heap”; and a stolen gate found “dumped in a godown”. [n04; n32; DNA] The excavation in the citadel that is said to have found a “secret room” and old wells was reported by the same paper. [n32] The ASI’s Mumbai Circle announced the conservation of the ruins of three churches, which the report names as St John the Baptist, St Dominic and St Augustine, with work on the Misericórdia and better visitor facilities and security; the date of that announcement is not known. [n32; n04; FPJ] In 2026 the ASI put out a conservation tender worth Rs 72.65 lakh, with bids opened on 29 May 2026; visitors this year may well find scaffolding. [n04]

The quarrel is older than the ASI. In 1882 Collector Mulock complained that the lease of the interior to the Littlewoods’ heirs made “the proper preservation of the ruins almost impossible”. [n32] The fort has been leased, farmed, quarried for columns, used as a sugar factory, planted with fields, and protected, in that order, and each age has left its marks on it.

On film

The ruins have become one of the favourite film locations near Mumbai. Scenes of the Hindi films Josh, Khamoshi: The Musical, Aag and Love Ke Liye Kuch Bhi Karega were shot here, as were the songs “Pyaar Tune Kya Kiya” (Kambakkht Ishq) and “Poster Lagwa Do” (Luka Chuppi), and the British band Coldplay’s video for “Hymn for the Weekend”, which had more than 960 million YouTube views by July 2018. [n04; Wikipedia: Vasai Fort] Shooting has its own frictions: police registered a case against a production company for lighting a stove inside the protected fort. [n04; FPJ] A Bombay journalist who walked the ruins in 1987 recorded that the fort was said to be “haunted by the ghosts of dead fidalgos”. [n09]

XVIII. Killa cha Sann: the feast in the ruins

The feast

Once a year the Jesuit church of the Holy Name fills again. The feast of St Gonsalo Garcia, the Bassein boy who studied in its college and died on a cross at Nagasaki, is kept inside the roofless church on a Sunday in January. Local people call it Killa cha Sann, “the fort’s festival”. [n09; n07; n38; FPJ 11 Jan 2026] The date follows an old rule: the feast falls on “the first Sunday nearest the neap tide after Christmas”, the Daily News and Analysis reported in 2014, when “thousands” were expected and the old church was decorated by Koliwada youth. [n07; n38; DNA 12 Jan 2014] In 1987 a temporary roof was put up over the Jesuit church for the annual mass. [n09] The universal feast of the saint is 6 February, and Indian bishops keep it on 7 February; the fort feast is a local January celebration of its own. [n07]

In 2026 the fort feast was held on Sunday 11 January, with Father Peter Fernandez presiding before hundreds of worshippers. The ASI allows a Sunday service in the church. [n07; n09; n38; FPJ 11 Jan 2026] On the same day the church of St Bonaventure at Erangal, on the Madh shore across the creek, built in 1575 and destroyed in 1739, kept its own feast. At both, families arrive the evening before and camp in enclosures, cooking East Indian moile and stew, meat with vegetables such as kohlrabi (navalkol); in the old days, a man from Erangal told the reporter, “people from Vasai and Versova came by boat”, and others by animal cart. [n09; n07; FPJ 11 Jan 2026]

Whose feast

The feast is the meeting point of all the Christian peoples of the town. The Koliwada of the fort shore decorates the church. The Vadval parishes of Sandor, Papdi, Remedy, Merces, Pali and Manickpur are the fort’s nearest neighbours. The Kupari villages of the north, whose own great church of the saint stands at Gass, built in 1942 as a daughter of Nirmal, come south for the day. [n09; n07; n42; n40] St Gonsalo Garcia is the patron of the Diocese of Vasai and of the East Indian community. [n07; n09] Once a year, the Court of the North is full of the descendants of the people who lived outside its walls.

XIX. Vasai West in November 2026

The town in the city

In November 2026 the old town of Vasai is the southern-western quarter of a city of well over a million and a half people. The census of 2011 counted 1,222,390 in the corporation, which projects 19.85 lakh now; no reliable 2026 count exists. [n12; n40; n06] Vasai West, the old town of the fort, Vasai Gaon, Papdi, Parnaka, the Koliwada and the garden villages, lies between the creek, the sea and the railway; Vasai East, around the station and the highway, is a different world of industrial estates, townships and new towers. [n12] The PIN codes still divide them. [n12]

Politics

The political order the town had known since 1990 cracked in 2024. In the November 2024 assembly election the Bahujan Vikas Aghadi of Hitendra Thakur, which had run Vasai–Virar for about three decades, lost Vasai and Nallasopara to the BJP; in Vasai, Sneha Dube Pandit beat Thakur by 3,153 votes by the Election Commission’s count. [n29; n41; n06] In the corporation election of 15–16 January 2026 the BVA held the city with 71 of 115 seats, but the BJP rose to 43. The Deccan Herald headlined the result “whistle blows lotus off Vasai fort”, the whistle being the BVA’s symbol and the lotus the BJP’s. [n06; n29] On 3 February 2026 Ajiv Patil of the BVA became mayor, and Marshal Dominic Lopes deputy mayor. [n06; n29]

Floods and water

The town’s old ground is flooding. In early July 2026 heavy rain killed ten people in Palghar district in a week, moved 1,261 people into relief centres in Palghar, Vasai and Wada talukas, brought national and state disaster teams to Vasai and Virar, and stopped trains when tracks flooded near Nallasopara; nine rain-related deaths in Vasai–Virar were reported by 13 July. [n29] Western Railway has blamed flooding on construction over salt pans, mangroves and waterholes, on choked culverts, and on overflow from the Pelhar dam. [n06] The salt pans of the creek’s north bank, 1,439 acres in 1881, are the ground in question. [n21] On 15 November 2026 a Marathi daily warned that reservoir levels were falling and scarcity was feared, and that the historic lakes of Vasai–Virar were choked with garbage and water hyacinth. [n06; eSakal 15 Nov 2026] The city depends on bulk water from the Surya scheme, begun in late 2023, and on the old Usgaon and Pelhar dams. [n12; n29]

The church and the fort

The Diocese of Vasai, with about 117,000 Catholics in 49 parishes and four missions in 2022, is led since 15 December 2024 by Bishop Thomas D’Souza, born in 1970 at Chulne, formerly rector of the Remedy shrine. [n34; n38; n40] In June 2026 the diocese designated St Joseph’s Church at Umralla, a Kupari parish, a pilgrim centre for the Year of St Francis declared by Pope Leo XIV. [n38] The fort feast was kept in January. The ASI’s conservation tender was opened in May. The Thirteenth Vasai-Virar Mayor’s Marathon, run by the corporation and the Vasai Taluka Kala Kreeda Vikas Mandal, is due on 13 December 2026. [n29]

What is left

Walk from Vasai Gaon down the road the British cut in 1856, and the old order of the town is still legible. The road runs past the Robertson-era market and the garden villages to the walls of 1554. Inside, the Hanuman shrine sits by the Sea Gate with its doors of 1720. The tower of the Matriz leans over the grass where the fidalgos stood each morning before their gouty governor. The Jesuit façade still carries its IHS and its date of 1636, and once a year it shelters a mass. Somewhere under the São Sebastião bastion, where the Marathas broke in, a passage is said to run to the river, which nobody has ever explored. And on the shore, at Killa Bandar, fishing boats still come in to a jetty whose history no book has written.

What the record does not hold

The gaps are as much a part of this story as the facts. The notes behind this chapter could not find:

  • Bassein before 1507. No certain notice exists. The Yadava district seat is a “doubtful reference”; the Kanheri “Vasai” is a misreading. [n37]
  • Malik Tokan’s citadel on the ground. No trace of it is identified in the present fort. [n03]
  • A plan of the bastions. The ten names survive; their positions, sizes and dates (other than São Sebastião, 1554) do not, in the sources read. The “eleven bastions” and the “regular hexagon” are unresolved variants. [n32]
  • The captains’ missing dates. Several dates in da Cunha’s list are lost in the OCR, and there is a gap of more than half a century after 1554. [n32]
  • The composition of the Portuguese population. The 1634 count is second-hand; the split between fidalgos and other settlers, and the origins of the slaves, are not recorded. [n04]
  • The Portuguese text of the 1739 capitulation, with its clauses on churches and Christians. [n32]
  • The tunnel. Never explored; the 530-foot length is unsupported. [n32]
  • Killa Bandar’s fishing life. No source for its boats, catch, families or jetty. [n32; n11]
  • Diwanman, Ambadi and Parnaka. No history found; Parnaka appears only in a road name. [n11; n32; n40]
  • The municipality after 1882: its renaming, its finances, its boundaries before 2009. [n28; n32]
  • Bassein town between 1900 and 1946: schools, libraries, cinemas, hospitals, the effects of the wars, the freedom movement in the town itself. The notes found no Vasai-specific incident of 1930 or 1942. [n05; n28; n32]
  • The date of the fort’s ASI notification. [n32]
  • A Kupari quarter or association in the town, and any count of Kuparis in it. [n34]
  • Modern histories of the town’s Muslim, Parsi, Jain and Gujarati families. [n08]

From the walls to the villages

The fort was never the whole of Vasai. Even at its height, the walled city of the Court of the North stood on the produce of the villages around it: the rice in its granary, the timber on its slipways, the betel and plantains in its market, the tenants on its fidalgos’ lands, and the converts in its churches. After 1739 the walls emptied and the villages stayed. The Christians of Sandor and Papdi, the Kolis of the shore, the Brahmans and Vanis of Papdi, the Samvedis and Kuparis of Nirmal and Gass, the Agris of the salt pans and the Varlis of the Tungar slopes kept the land and the faiths they had, and it was their garden belt that the British town of 1864 was built to serve. The next chapter leaves the walls and walks out through those villages, one by one.
