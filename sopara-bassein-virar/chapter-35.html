Period covered: from the surrender of Baçaim (16 May 1739) to Indian independence (15 August 1947)

In this chapter. What happened to the Kupari villages after the fall of Baçaim: the terms of 1739, the Hindu restoration, who went back and why most stayed, and the Company’s years.

Lanes full of people going to church

In 1760 a French scholar, Anquetil du Perron, travelled up the coast north of Bassein. The city had been in Maratha hands for twenty-one years. Its Portuguese nobles and its friars were long gone, its great churches unroofed and looted, its bells hanging in temples across the Deccan. When du Perron came to Agashi, he was struck by what he saw: the lanes “full of people going to church as freely as in a Christian country”. [n34; Gazetteer XIV pp. 1–2] [n37; Hull vol. 1 p. 76; Gaz. XIV pp. 1–2]

That sentence is the best single piece of evidence for what happened to the converted Samvedi and Pachkalshi families after the Portuguese left. It does not say who the churchgoers were, or how many, or what they believed. But it says that in the northernmost of the old Portuguese subdivisions, under a Hindu Brahmin government that had every reason to want them back in their castes, a generation after the conquest, the Christians of Agashi were still walking to Mass in the open.

This chapter follows them for the next two centuries: through Maratha rule and Brahmin purification, through the British conquest and the cholera years when some went back, through the rebuilding of the village churches with their own money, through the censuses and surveys that counted and described them, through the invention of the name “East Indian” in Bombay, and down to 1947. Through all of it, one word is missing from the record, and the chapter ends by asking what that absence means. The word is “Kupari”.

The terms of 1739

The siege of Baçaim began on 17 February 1739 and ended with the surrender of the garrison on 16 May. The Portuguese marched out with their colours flying and went by sea to Bombay. [n04; Battle of Vasai] [n32; DC 148; BG 34] The terms of the capitulation, as Hull’s mission history gives them, provided free passage for the noble families, for Christians who wished to leave, and for all the religious orders and priests. The Christians who stayed were to “enjoy liberty of worship”. The privileges of five churches were preserved, three in Bassein city, one in the district and one in Salsette, with their curates “subject to the Primate of India”, and images were to remain. [n37; Hull vol. 1 pp. 75–76, citing Materials vol. 1 p. 213] The Gazetteer gives the same five: “three in Bassein city, one in Bassein district and one in Salsette”. [n37; Gaz. XIII-I p. 203] Mendiratta places the district church in the “Cassabé” subdivision. [n34; Mendiratta pp. 216–218] None of the sources read says which church that was.

Everyone of Portuguese birth or descent left. All the reinóis, those born in Portugal, and the descendentes, their locally born children, “fled or were expelled … leaving only the Indian converted population behind”. [n34; Mendiratta pp. 216–218] The friars left with them. Hull, drawing on du Perron and Tieffenthaler, put it simply: “Portuguese nobles and all the regular clergy left for Goa; the ordinary people remained, a native priest taking charge of their souls with the approbation of the Mahrattas.” [n37; Hull vol. 1 p. 76] Bassein city itself “never counted more than 3,500 inhabitants” under the new rulers, though 30,000 lived in the country around. [n37; Hull vol. 1 p. 76]

The Marathas would accept only non-white priests. So Goan priests, ordained by the secular clergy or by the Oratorians, took over the parishes, and reorganisation began in 1740 from Kurla, in Salsette, under a Vicar General. [n34; Mendiratta pp. 216–218] The Gazetteer gave these native priests the credit for what followed: under the Vicar General at Kurla “the native or … Kanarin vicars managed the churches and kept the bulk of the people from forsaking Christianity”. [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 203] It is worth pausing on the word. Kanarin (Canarim), the Portuguese name for a native of the coast, was “considered a term of reproach” when applied to the native clergy. [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 203 n.1] The men the Portuguese had looked down on were the men who held the church together when the Portuguese were gone. And they existed because, two centuries earlier, António do Porto had filled his orphanages “in times of war and famine” with deserted children and trained them as priests. [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 201] The orphanage at Agashi had been burned in 1540. Its purpose outlived it.

The churches themselves suffered. The Marathas looted the fort churches and carried off their bells as trophies; Portuguese-era bells cast in 1683, 1707 and 1729 hang today in temples at Pune, Menavali, Bhimashankar and elsewhere. [n09; Vasaiker, “Bells of Vasai Fort”; Goanet 2020] The church of Our Lady of Mercy at Merces was destroyed in 1739, and its priests fled. [n09; Goanet; Vasaiker] Nandakhal’s Holy Spirit church, according to its Wikipedia page, survived the attacks of 1737–39 and is therefore called the oldest surviving church in north Vasai. [n09; Wikipedia: Holy Spirit Church Nandakhal]

Bajipur: the Hindu restoration

The Marathas renamed the city Bajipur, “Bajirav’s city”, and made it the seat of a governor whose authority ran from the Bankot river to Daman. [n37; Gaz. XIV p. 34] They set out to restore Hinduism on the coast the Portuguese had made Christian.

“To restore a Hindu population grants of rent-free land were offered, and a tax was levied for the support of Brahmans who were brought to purify the Christians and make them fit to take their place in their old castes.” [n37; Gaz. XIV p. 34] Gerson da Cunha attributed the purification tax to the Peshwa Madhavrao, who “also instituted a tax in support of Brahmans to purify the native Christians, whom they regarded as polluted Hindus, before receiving them into their former castes. Both these measures brought down a crowd of Hindus from Maharashtra and Gujarat, especially Prabhus”. [n34; Da Cunha 1876, p. 149] Madhavrao ruled from 1761 to 1772, so if da Cunha is right, the tax came a generation after the conquest. [n34; note]

New people came. Between 1760 and 1766 “a considerable number of Cambay Vanis, chiefly of the Lad sub-division, and with them several Gujarat Brahmans settled in Supara, Bassein, and other coast towns to avoid the exactions of Momin Khan II”. [n35; Gazetteer XIII-1, p. 63] By the 1880s there were Lad Vani families at Supara, Bassein, Agashi, Nala and Papdi, traders and moneylenders who spoke Gujarati at home and Marathi outside. [n35; Gazetteer XIII-1, pp. 112–113] Priestly Chitpavan and Deshasth Brahmans “settled as priests, and to a great extent ousted the Palshis and other older classes of priests”. [n35; Gazetteer XIII-1, p. 63] By 1876 Papdi, “a village about a cannon-shot from Bajipura”, had Chitpavan, Karhada and Deshastha Brahmans, Palshes and Sonars among its people. [n32; DC 156–157] The Pachkalshis, whose help in the siege had earned one family the rank of chief Patils, prospered. [n35; Enthoven III, p. 162]

At Nirmal, the holy lake of the Samvedis, the restoration was made in stone. “On the fall of Bassein (1739) Nirmal was purified, prints of the feet of Shri Dattatraya took the place of the linga, and a reservoir was built.” About 1750 Shankarji Keshav Phadke, the Maratha Sarsubhedar of Bassein, built eight temples at Nirmal with government grants. The Gazetteer thought the stones the Portuguese had taken from the old temples for their churches were now “probably used to raise the temples of the older gods”. [n37; Gaz. XIV pp. 292–293] [n34; Gaz XIV pp. 292–293] Across the whole district, the Gazetteer said, “On the decline of Portuguese power (1740) Brahmanism revived, and except those that are more modern, most of the present Hindu temples date from the eighteenth century.” [n37; Gaz. XIII-I p. 66]

So by the 1750s Nirmal held two sacred centres side by side: the Hindu temples raised by the Maratha governor on the site of the destroyed linga, and the Christian parish of the Holy Cross. Hindu Samvedis worshipped at one, Christian Samvedis at the other. The village did not choose between them. It held both. The research notes call this coexistence “documented, not just remembered”. [n34; Inferences, section 6]

Who went back

Some Christians did return to Hinduism after 1739. The question is how many, and who.

The Gazetteer recorded “the local story” that after the conquest many churches were destroyed and “numbers of the Christians were … purified by Brahmans and admitted into Hinduism”, “among them probably the Bhandaris known as Kirpals”. [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 202] [n37; Gaz. XIII-I p. 202] The Kirpals were the lowest division of the Bhandari palm-tappers, “once Christians”; the Gazetteer guessed their name came from kriyapal, “allowed Hindu rites”. [n08; Gazetteer 1882, p. 149–150] The Census of 1911 confirmed the story for them: the Kirpal Bhandaris “were forcibly converted to Christianity by the Portuguese, but soon after the conquest of Bassein by the Marathas (about 1739) they were given the choice of reverting to Hinduism, which they accepted”. [n34; Census 1911 Bombay Part I, para 136]

Mendiratta’s judgement is careful: “The Marathas probably managed to bring some Catholics back into the Hindu fold through purifying rites, and economic pressure.” [n34; Mendiratta pp. 216–218] Machado’s modern history, via Wikipedia, says that after 1739 Christianity faced a “severe threat from the Peshwa Brahmins”, that only a few churches remained, and that a heavy, discriminatory religious tax led many to convert to Hinduism, until the native clergy under the Vicar General at Kurla restored the community. [n04; Machado 1999 p. 108 via Wikipedia] A Hindu writer of 2019 says reconversion under the Marathas was voluntary, and names “Fatherwadi” as a once-Christian village that is now largely Hindu; that is a single modern claim, and the research for this book could not check it. [n07; Swarajya]

This was not the first time the Marathas had tried. Shivaji, between 1665 and 1670, “is said to have taken much care that the people should be purified by Brahmans and brought back to Hinduism”; “after Shivaji’s death the spread of Hinduism ceased”. [n37; Gaz. XIII-I p. 202]

There is no Maratha-period document concerning a named Samvedi Christian village. [n34; Gaps, section 6] The sources name the Kirpal Bhandaris; they do not name the Samvedis or the Pachkalshis among those who went back. The evidence that the Samvedi Christians stayed is circumstantial, but strong: du Perron’s crowded lanes at Agashi in 1760, and, 120 years later, seventy-five Christian families “originally Samvedis and Pachkalshis” at Agashi, Vatar and Koprad, and a congregation of 2,184 at Nirmal. [n34; Gazetteer XIV pp. 1–2, p. 293; Gazetteer XIII p. 395]

Why most stayed

Why did the converted families of the northern villages not go back, when the rulers offered purification, the priests of their old castes were at hand, and their Hindu kin lived next door? The sources do not record anyone’s reasons. But they record the conditions, and from those a careful answer can be built.

First, the Marathas did not compel. “The Marathas allowed them the freest exercise of their religion, their processions and festivals were respected, and many of the Salsette churches were built or rebuilt about this time (1760).” The Gazetteer’s judgement was that “the Natives showed more constancy and the Marathas more toleration than could have been expected”. [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 203] [n37; Gaz. XIII-I p. 203] The pressure that existed was the pull of rent-free land and the offer of purification, and the push of a tax; it was not a sword.

Second, the church had a native clergy. The Kanarin vicars under Kurla “kept the bulk of the people from forsaking Christianity”. [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 203] The research notes call this “a good point for the ‘why Christianity stayed’ arc”: the community survived because the Portuguese had trained a native priesthood. [n37; Inferences, section 5]

Third, and this is an inference from everything in the last two chapters, return was not simple. A family that had been Christian for four or five generations by 1740 had its own church, its own feasts, its own godparents, its own graves. To go back meant paying Brahmins for purification, and it meant being received, not as equals, but as returners. When the Urap Agris went back in the next century, they ranked below the Sud and Das Agris, “who neither marry nor eat with them”. [n37; Gaz. XIII-I p. 117] A Brahmin family that had been Christian for two centuries had even more to lose by that calculation: it might be readmitted to a caste that would never again treat it as fully its own. No source records a Samvedi family weighing this choice. But the outcome, a community still divided in 1882 into Hindu and Christian halves of nearly equal size at Agashi, is what one would expect if both halves had made their peace with where they stood.

Fourth, the land. The Samvedi and Pachkalshi Christians were cultivators of the garden island and the Agashi strip. Under the Portuguese, grants of land had been “nominally for three lives”, with “no right in the land” for tenants. [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 460] When the Portuguese landholders left, the people who actually worked the gardens remained. The research notes found no source on taxation, landholding or the privileged landholding class in the Kupari villages after 1739. [n34; Gaps, section 6] But by 1882 the Gazetteer could say that “some of their villages are as rich as any in the Thana district”. [n37; Gaz. XIII-I pp. 204, 216] Whatever the mechanism, the Christian gardeners came out of the Maratha century holding their gardens.

The Company and the cholera

The British came in stages. The East India Company took Salsette in 1774, when it held about 20,000 Catholics and at least ten churches. General Goddard took Bassein on 11 December 1780 after a month’s siege, and the Treaty of Salbai gave it back to the Marathas in 1782. On 31 December 1802 the fleeing Peshwa Baji Rao II signed the subsidiary Treaty of Bassein in the town, and in 1818 the Company annexed the Peshwa’s territories. [n34; Mendiratta p. 219] [n04; FIBIS; Treaty of Bassein (1802)] When the British took over the Bassein subdivision early in the nineteenth century, they found “eight functioning churches and a Catholic population estimated at 5000”. [n34; Mendiratta p. 219, citing Further Papers 1828–36]

The British did not trust the Catholic clergy, whose loyalty ran to Goa and Portugal. A letter of 1819 complained that “they form a sort of separate caste in the middle of the community”. [n34; Mendiratta p. 220] The Christians “did not receive any special encouragement from the British Government”, the Gazetteer admitted, and “in some of the earlier accounts … they are noticed in terms of contempt”. [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 203] In 1824 the Salsette Christians were estimated at about 10,000, “lower orders … indifferent Christians” who kept Hindu symbols; in 1832 they were described as “in the most lamentable state of superstitious degradation”, and in 1838 as “poor, ignorant and drunken”. [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 204] Those were Salsette descriptions, by officials who did not like what they saw.

Then came cholera. In 1818 and 1819 it struck the district so hard that it “did not recover for years”. [n21; Gaz XIII-II] In the cholera of 1820–21, “about 3000 Christians rejoined the Hindu fold”, and a British report blamed the lack of “respectable ministers”. [n34; Mendiratta p. 220] The Gazetteer told the story through the Uraps. In the cholera years 1818–20 and again in 1828, “some of the Christian Kolis, finding that they suffered as much as their Hindu neighbours, took to propitiating the goddess of cholera, and either left or were driven from the Church. Some of these people with the help of a Palshi Brahman became Hindus, and are known as Uraps or Varaps.” [n37; Gaz. XIII-I p. 203 and note 3]

The details are exact. The Bassein Agris who went back in 1820 “had to pay £120 (Rs 1200)”. The priest who readmitted them was Ramchandra Baba Joshi, a Palshe Brahman, who was put out of his own caste for a time for doing it; his successor Bhai Makund Joshi was also put out of caste. The second readmission, on 12 November 1828, was performed by Vithal Hari Naik Vaidya, a Palshe Brahman of Bassein. [n37; Gaz. XIII-I p. 117] [n35; Enthoven I, “Agri: Urap Agris”, p. 13–14] The Uraps’ only remaining sign of their Christian past was their surnames: Gomes, Soz, Fernan, Frutad and Minez. They ranked below the other Agris, who would neither marry nor eat with them. [n37; Gaz. XIII-I p. 117] The Gazetteer derived the name Urap from Marathi orapne, “to scratch or sear with a hot iron”, supposedly for a purifying ordeal; that etymology is a hypothesis. [n37; Gaz. XIII-I p. 117 and p. 203 note 4] An earlier writer, Vaupell, in 1838, gave a different cause for the break: “an attempt to extend the priestly prerogative”. [n37; Gaz. XIII-I p. 203 note 3] “Others of those who were expelled did not become Hindus”, the Gazetteer added, and still attended the parish churches at festivals. [n37; Gaz. XIII-I p. 203]

These are the best-documented reconversions in the whole history of the Bassein Christians. They concern Christian Kolis and Agris, fishermen and salt-workers, in the cholera years. They do not mention Samvedis or Pachkalshis. And they show something the Maratha-era reconversions do not: that going back cost money, and that even the Brahmin who performed the rite paid for it with his own caste standing.

Growth and rebuilding

What followed the cholera years was not decline but growth.

“If these accounts are correct,” the Gazetteer wrote of the Salsette estimates, “the Salsette Christians have, during the last fifty years, more than doubled in number.” [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 204] In Bassein the numbers are clearer still. The British found about 5,000 Catholics in the subdivision early in the century. [n34; Mendiratta p. 219] The census of 1872 counted 13,126 Christians in the Bassein sub-division, about 22 per cent of its 61,089 people. [n21; Gaz XIII-I p. 71] In 1881 there were 14,070 Christians in a population of 68,967, 20.40 per cent. [n21; Gaz XIII-II p. 680] The early figure of 5,000 and the later figures may not cover the same ground, and the earlier one is an estimate; but even allowing for that, the Christian population of Bassein grew through the nineteenth century, not shrank.

And the Christians rebuilt their churches, largely with their own money. The Gazetteer’s entries for the churches near Bassein in 1881 read like a register of village effort:

  • Dhauli (Our Lady of Remedies): built 1821 for Rs 18,600; congregation 3,238.
  • Sandor (St Thomas): side walls of the sixteenth century, a chapel of 1838, a front of 1858; congregation 1,725.
  • Pali (Our Lady): built 1840; congregation 900.
  • Manickpur (St Michael): rebuilt 1851; congregation 800.
  • Papdi (Our Lady of Grace): built 1865 by private subscription; congregation 1,294.
  • Our Lady of Mercy: congregation 974. [n32; BG 36–37] [n37; Gaz. XIV pp. 35–36]

In the north, the two Samvedi Christian centres stand out.

At Nirmal the church of Santa Cruz, the Holy Cross, had “a congregation of 2184 Christians”. It had been rebuilt by the parishioners in 1856 at a cost of about Rs 24,000, a building 96 feet long, 28 wide and 26 high. Its vicar had a house and a monthly grant from the British government. There was a private school, whose teacher played the violin in church. [n34; Gazetteer XIV p. 293] Twenty-four thousand rupees, raised by a farming parish in the 1850s, is a large sum: the whole land revenue of the Bassein sub-division in 1880 was £12,671, about Rs 1.27 lakh. [n21; Gaz XIII-II] The parishioners of Nirmal spent on their church in 1856 a sum equal to about a fifth of the year’s land revenue of the entire sub-division. That comparison is this book’s arithmetic, not the Gazetteer’s, and the two figures are a generation apart; but it gives a sense of what the rebuilding meant.

At Agashi the church, “built after the destruction of Nossa Senhora da Luz”, measured 75 feet by 22 by 16, and kept a school of seventeen pupils who were taught Portuguese, arithmetic, singing, doctrine and music. [n34; Gazetteer XIV pp. 1–2]

The 1881 list of the nine churches of Bassein, each under its own priest, was: Sandor (St Thomas), Papdi (Our Lady of Grace), Pali (Mother of God), Manickpur (St Michael), Davli (Our Lady of Help), Nirmal (Holy Cross), Koprad (Holy Spirit), Agashi (St James) and Bassein (Our Lady of Mercy). [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 206 n.3] The Catholic Encyclopedia, about 1907, gave Bassein nine churches, twelve priests and 16,119 Catholics. [n04; Catholic Encyclopedia] By 1925 Mendiratta counts twelve parish churches in Bassein, and about 50,000 faithful in Salsette and Bassein together. [n34; Mendiratta p. 239 n.50]

The twentieth century continued the work. Nandakhal’s church was rebuilt in 1907–10 with government timber, four metal pillars and an upper floor; a front extension, the “Alpari”, was added in 1920, and a bell given in 1908. [n09; Wikipedia: Holy Spirit Church Nandakhal] At Giriz in the south, villagers laid a foundation stone on 14 February 1917 and built a church of St Francis Xavier in about a year “without foreign aid”. [n09; Wikipedia: Giriz] The church of St Gonsalo Garcia at Gass was built in 1942, as a new parish carved out of Nirmal, and the Holy Family church at Bhuigaon is dated 1946 by a single source. [n07; Catholics India] [n09] The pattern of the century, mother churches founded in the Portuguese period and daughter parishes carved out of them from 1917 onward, is a family tree written in stone. [n09; Inferences]

Through all of this the parishes belonged to the Portuguese Padroado, the royal patronage of the Portuguese crown over the church in its old territories. Vasai was under the Archdiocese of Goa until 1886, under the new diocese of Daman from 1886 to 1928, and under Bombay from 1928. [n09; Wikipedia: Sandor] [n04] The long quarrel between the Padroado and Rome’s Propaganda Fide over who controlled the Catholics of Bombay, Salsette and Bassein runs through the nineteenth century. [n37; Hull] [n34; Mendiratta p. 234] The bishops of Daman tried, between 1887 and 1925, to set up Portuguese schools in the villages; Mendiratta says they “don’t appear to have been long-lasting”. [n34; Mendiratta p. 241]

The Christians of Bassein in the record of 1882

The 1882 Gazetteer’s chapter on the Christians of the Thana district is the fullest description of the Kuparis’ ancestors that the period left. It is not about them by name. It describes all the “Native Christians” of Salsette, Bassein and Mahim together, with a few points marked as specific to Bassein. But read beside its account of the Hindu Samvedis, it lets us see the Christian Samvedi villages as an official of the 1880s saw them. Chapter 36 will return to dress, food, houses and rites in detail; here the purpose is to see the community whole, as the record of its time saw it.

Names. The upper classes called themselves Firgi, from Firangi, “Frank”; the lower classes “call themselves by their caste name”; Hindus called them “Firangis and more often Kiristans”. [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 199–200] Europeans called them “Portuguese” or “Salsette Christians”. [n22; LSI vol. VII] Nowhere is there a Kupari.

Who they were. “Chiefly converted Brahmans, Prabhus, Pachkalshis and Kolis”, with the bulk Bhandaris, Kolis and Kunbis; at Sopara “many of them converted Samvedi Brahmans”; at Agashi, Vatar and Koprad “originally Samvedis and Pachkalshis”. The Gazetteer added that Thana Christians had “two strains of foreign blood, the European and the Negro”, the latter from the African slaves of the Portuguese period. [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 63, p. 200 and n.8; XIV p. 315] [n37; Gaz. XIII-II p. 460 note 3]

Houses and food. “Substantial tile-roofed houses with walls of wooden planks, mud, or brick and stone.” Two meals a day: rice, nagli, vari, pulse, vegetables, mutton, beef, pork, fowl and fish; palm spirit (phul) and the double-distilled bevda. “The whole family eats together, men, women and children.” “Pigs are found in most Christian villages.” [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 204, p. 55]

Dress. The well-to-do men wore European dress, generally black. Poorer men wore knee-length trousers of coloured cotton, a jersey and a cotton jacket. “Cart-drivers and husbandmen wear a long cylindrical white cotton hat or a woollen night-cap with or without a checked kerchief tied round the temples, fishermen wear red broadcloth caps.” “Almost all the women dress in local Hindu fashion”, the robe drawn over the right shoulder, “not like most Hindus over the right temple”, in dark purple, green or black; for church they wore a white sheet-like robe falling from the head. [n34; Gazetteer XIII pp. 204–205] The husbandmen of the Gazetteer’s description wore a white hat, not a red cap; the red cap was the fishermen’s and, as the last chapter showed, the Hindu Samvedis’. [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 82]

Birth and baptism. After a first confinement the wife went to her parents’ house. On the third or sixth night the poorer families guarded against the spirit Sathi by strewing gram at the door, the same spirit the Hindu Samvedis worshipped on the fifth day. The child was baptised between the eighth and fifteenth day, carried by padrinho and madrinha; “the mother never goes to the christening”. The poorer families burned incense at the door before the child entered and feasted with country liquor, dates, gram and molasses. On the fortieth day the mother was purified at church. [n34; Gazetteer XIII pp. 210–211; p. 82]

Marriage. “In Salsette the parents of the girl propose; in Bassein the proposal comes from the boy’s side.” [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 211 n.1] It is a small detail and the Gazetteer marks it as specific to Bassein. Among the Hindu Samvedis, a bride-price was paid to the girl’s father. [n08; Gazetteer 1882, p. 82–83] Whether the Bassein Christians’ custom of the boy’s side proposing descends from that older custom of the boy’s side paying is a question the sources raise but do not answer. Weddings were held between eight and ten in the morning; the groom walked under a long silk umbrella, and in the lower classes wore “the full European dress of bygone days, a scarlet or black military coat with cocked hat, epaulets, knee-breeches”, hired for five to ten rupees, sometimes with a sword “preserved … as an heirloom”. The bride went in a palanquin, duli. The feast had two pork dishes with vinegar and spices; the poor sat on mats and ate off leaf plates. [n34; Gazetteer XIII pp. 211–214]

Death. The priest was called, the church bell tolled, the dead were buried within a day, the poor in a calico habit “like a monk’s robe, in shape like a Franciscan’s and in colour like a Carmelite’s”. A widow removed her ornaments “and unless she is very young, never wears them again”. The brotherhoods attended with candles. There were church prayers and a meal on the seventh day, and services at a month and a year. [n34; Gazetteer XIII pp. 214–215]

Faith in practice. “The Christians go to church regularly.” First fruits of the rice harvest were blessed at church. Each church paid one or two music masters who played the violin, though the people “seem to have lost their old fondness for singing”. Passion plays, introduced by the Jesuits in the sixteenth century, continued. And secret offerings to Ganpati and Gauri, and vows to Shitladevi, were “said occasionally” to continue in outlying villages, disapproved by the priests. [n34; Gazetteer XIII pp. 207–210]

Work and standing. “Wealthy families who are village headmen and owners of rich garden lands often bring up one or more of their sons for the Church.” The Christians “pride themselves on never taking household service with Europeans”. [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 205–206] And the judgement already quoted: “The most hardworking and skilful husbandmen are the Christians of Bassein”, who “turned the light sandy country about Bassein into an evergreen garden”. [n21; Gaz XIII-I p. 300] They also built boats: the coasting vessels of the Bassein ports were “built locally by Marathas and Native Christians”. [n21; Gaz XIII-I pp. 358–359]

Caste. It had not gone. The Bhois, Kumbhars, Nhavis and Dhobis “form separate castes”; Mhar converts were until recently “not allowed to draw water from the village well or enter the church”. [n37; Gaz. XIII-I p. 201] Only the Kolis, the Gazetteer said, had a headman or council of their own. [n34; Gazetteer XIII pp. 207–210]

Read alongside its page on the Hindu Samvedis, this chapter shows two halves of one village culture: the same tiled wooden houses, the same plantains, ornaments with the same names, the same spirit at the door of a newborn child. The Christians ate pork and beef; the Hindus ate rice, pulse and vegetables. The Christians buried; the Hindus burned. The Christians proposed from the boy’s side; the Hindus paid a bride-price. Both sent their sons to school.

Language: “Kinstav”

In 1905 George Grierson’s Linguistic Survey of India recorded the speech of the native Roman Catholics of Thana. Their neighbours, he said, called it “Kinstav” (or “Kiristav”), from the Hindus’ and Muslims’ word for Christians. It had “adopted some Portuguese words, such as pay, father, may, mother, mass, … padron, god-father, madron, god-mother”, and he noted the use of the English word estate. He put the speakers at about 26,600 for Thana, including the Christians of Salsette. And his verdict, based on a specimen of the Parable of the Prodigal Son, was that it was “nothing else than the usual Marathi of the district”. [n34; LSI VII, Kiristav section] [n22; LSI vol. VII]

The Gazetteer of 1882 had said much the same: “Their home tongue is Marathi, very little different from the Marathi spoken by the Kunbis and Kolis of the district. A few know and a considerable number understand an ungrammatical Portuguese, and among some of the higher families and in the Khatri ward of Thana town Portuguese is the home speech. Latin is the language of the Church.” Sermons were in Portuguese or Marathi, and prayer books in both. [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 204, p. 207]

Mendiratta, from later scholarship, gives a slightly different picture: in the 1880s Portuguese was a home language only for well-to-do families in Bandra, Thane, Bassein and Kurla, but “Indo-Portuguese creoles were more widespread in Salsette and Bassein and other areas, and survived until later”, and “the majority of non-educated East-Indians from Salsette and Bassein would communicate either in Marathi or in Indo-Portuguese creole”. [n34; Mendiratta p. 241] A Portuguese creole of the Northern Province, called Norteiro, was described by the Portuguese scholar Dalgado in 1906, but no source read for this book describes a Bassein-specific creole or the date it died. [n22; Cardoso 2006; Wikipedia: Norteiro people] Grierson’s 1905 specimen suggests that by then the Christians of Thana spoke Marathi, not a creole; Mendiratta’s statement suggests a creole lingered among some. The two are not reconciled in the sources. [n22; Inferences]

The important point for the Kupari story is what Grierson did not record. He described Samvedi, the speech of the Hindu Samvedis, about 2,700 people, as a mixture of Konkan Marathi and Gujarati. He described Kinstav, the speech of 26,600 Christians, as ordinary Marathi with Portuguese words. He did not record a Christian Samvedi dialect at all. The Kupari dialect, Kadodi, did not exist as a named category in 1905. [n34; Inferences, section 4] Whether the Christian Samvedis of the northern villages kept the Hindu Samvedis’ s-to-h shift while the Kinstav specimen did not is a question for modern fieldwork. [n34; Inferences] What the evidence does show is where the Portuguese words in the Christians’ speech came from: they are church and kinship words, father, mother, Mass, godparents. The Portuguese strand entered through the parish, not through settlement or trade. [n34; Inferences]

1887: “East Indian”

On 26 May 1887, about thirty “leaders of the community” met in Bombay “to change the designation ‘Bombay Portuguese’ to ‘Bombay East-Indian’”. The words are from the recollection of one of those present, John de Mello, printed in a souvenir of 1937. At the same meeting they founded the Bombay East Indian Association and a weekly newspaper, the Bombay East Indian. [n34; Mendiratta pp. 234–236]

The name was a political act. It evoked a century and a half of subjecthood to the East India Company, and the claim to be “sons of the soil”; above all it separated the local Catholics from the Goans and from the Portuguese, with whom they had been lumped together. [n34; Mendiratta pp. 234–236] The Goans answered with a “Bombay Portuguese Association” and a paper of their own, Portuguez Brittanico. [n34] The meeting fell in the year of Queen Victoria’s Golden Jubilee, and later tradition linked the two; the 1937 souvenir called it a “happy coincidence”, and a modern professor says the community petitioned the Empress, though whether she agreed is unknown. [n07; east-indians.com; The Goan, Jul 2025]

The Catholics of Salsette and Bassein were brought under the new name. In East Indian discourse they would be labelled the “rural” or “samvedi” East Indians. [n34; Mendiratta p. 218] The Association’s grievances in the 1920s included “the neglect of education in the districts of Salsette and Bassein” and “the paucity of vocations to the priesthood among the community”. [n34; Mendiratta p. 236, quoting O Anglo-Lusitano, 13 Mar 1926] And the community divided along the line of the city: the “urbanised” East Indians drifted to the new Archdiocese of Bombay, while those of Salsette and Bassein “remained by and large under the jurisdiction of the Padroado”. [n34; Mendiratta p. 236]

The 1887 politics were Bombay-centred. The Bassein villages stayed under Padroado parishes, so “East Indian” meant something different there. [n34; Inferences, section 7] No Kupari voice from 1887 to 1930 has been found; neither the Bombay East Indian newspaper nor the Association’s souvenir was found online. [n34; Gaps, section 7] What the umbrella did, in the long run, was give the Samvedi Christians of the north a place within a larger identity, while leaving their own village identity intact underneath it. The standard history of the East Indians, Elsie Baptista’s The East Indians: Catholic Community of Bombay, Salsette and Bassein of 1967, is cited by later writers for a list of five cultural groups within the community: Kulbis, Samavedi Christians “commonly called Kuparis”, Koli Christians, Wadvals and Salsette Christians. [n34; Catholic Connect, citing Baptista] Baptista’s book is not online, and the research for this book could not check what she actually wrote. [n34; Gaps]

The censuses, 1901–1931

The censuses of British India counted the Christians of Bassein, but not the Kuparis. They did not tabulate caste for most communities, and they had no category for Christian Samvedis. [n34; Gaps, section 10]

The 1901 census report for the Bombay Presidency, as searched for this book, contains only a Samvedi entry in a caste list, and no Bassein figures readable in the scanned text. [n34; Census 1901] [n21] The Bassein taluka’s population that year was about 80,251. [n21; Census 1911 Provincial Table I] Plague had come to the Presidency in 1896, and the 1901 report has a section on famine and plague, but its Thana figures were unreadable in the scan; it says only that Thana’s population fell by about one per cent between estimate and count, the least of any district. [n21; Census 1901]

The 1911 census put the Bassein taluka at 79,978 people in two towns and ninety villages, at 355 to the square mile. [n21; Census 1911 Provincial Table I] Its report, in paragraph 136, recorded the Kirpal Bhandaris’ reversion after 1739. [n34; Census 1911 Part I, para 136]

The 1921 census called Bassein and Bhiwandi “towns outside the suburbanisation zone”, with no marked increase. [n21; Census 1921] The influenza of 1918 had killed 36,642 people in the Thana and Bombay Suburban district, but the report gives no Bassein figure. [n21; Census 1921]

The 1931 census is the richest. Its provincial tables show about 22,467 Christians in the Bassein taluka, 11,238 males and 11,229 females, though the columns in the scanned table are shifted and the figure should be checked against the page. [n21; Census 1931 Vol VIII Pt II, Provincial Table II] If it holds, the Christians of Bassein had grown by about 60 per cent in fifty years, from 14,070 in 1881. The report itself, in its section on Indian Christians, wrote about the Bassein coast at length. It gave the estate-owner explanation of conversion and surnames already quoted. It said that “the intensive work of the last 60 to 80 years has done much to purge the Indian Christian population of Bassein of practices alien to … Roman Catholicism”; that the bulk of the coastal population was “seafaring”; that “the priests admit the Indian Christian population of Bassein and the coastal area around Bombay is more unorthodox than is desirable”; and that “there is no evidence that this community worships at Christian altars behind which are placed Hindu gods”. [n34; Census 1931 Vol VIII Part I pp. 363–364] Those sentences were written of the fishing coast, not of the northern gardeners. [n34; note] The same report said that at least ninety per cent of the Indian Christians of the whole Presidency descended from the “Sudra section of Hindus”. [n34; Census 1931 para 8]

What the censuses give, then, is the size of the whole Christian population of Bassein, roughly a fifth of the taluka in 1881, and the official mind’s view of it. They do not give the Samvedi Christians as a group, and they never use the word Kupari.

Land, rent and the moneylender

The Christian gardeners of the north lived, under the British, inside a new land system, and the Gazetteer’s revenue chapters let us see its outline, though never at the level of a single Kupari village.

Before 1862 the Bassein lands had been assessed in three different ways, by plough, by contract and by a rate per bigha; collections in the twenty years to 1860–61 ranged from about Rs 86,650 to Rs 1,06,440. In 1842 Bassein, with Mahim and Sanjan, was described as thinly peopled and miserably tilled. The British survey settlement of Bassein ran from 1858–59 to 1861–62, and the rates fixed in 1861–62 were to stand for thirty years, to 1891–92. [n21; Gaz XIII-II pp. 587–588, 610, 679] By 1880 the sub-division had 8,064 holdings and paid a land revenue of about Rs 1,26,710. Its occupied land, 46,011 acres, was assessed at average rates of about nine annas an acre for dry land, a little under three rupees for rice land, and more than six rupees for garden land. [n21; Gaz XIII-II p. 679] Garden land, the land of the Samvedi and Christian plantain-growers, was taxed at more than ten times the rate of dry land and twice that of rice. The figures are averages for the whole sub-division, and the scanned table should be checked against the page; but they show where the wealth of Bassein lay.

The settlement brought the moneylender. The Gazetteer’s account of the Marwari Vanis is harsh, and it is the colonial author’s judgement, to be attributed as such. They had come to Thana “almost entirely since the British conquest”. Their first movement “followed the very liberal and general reduction of rent … between 1835 and 1838. The reductions left a large margin of profit to the landholder and the Marvadis came, advanced money at from 100 to 200 per cent to the husbandmen, and sold them up.” In 1846 the Collector noticed that “the thrifty avaricious Marvadi had begun to settle even in the remotest villages”. [n35; Gazetteer XIII-1, pp. 64, 113–114] The Lad Vanis who had come from Cambay in the 1760s were already “shopkeepers, moneylenders, superior landholders” at Supara, Agashi, Nala and Papdi. [n35; Gazetteer XIII-1, pp. 112–113] No source read for this book records what debt did to the Christian gardeners of Agashi or Nirmal specifically. But the Gazetteer’s statement that “some of their villages are as rich as any in the Thana district” suggests that at least some of them came through the century with their gardens intact. [n37; Gaz. XIII-I pp. 204, 216]

The land fed a trade. In the five years to 1879 the port of Agashi alone exported goods worth an average of about Rs 3,18,470 a year, and imported about Rs 85,650. [n21; Gaz XIV p. 2] The three Bassein ports, Agashi, Bassein and Navghar, had an estimated trade of £108,868 in 1881–82, sending husked rice, molasses, coconuts and plantains to Bombay and Gujarat, and firewood and tiles to Bombay, and bringing back wheat, pulse, ghee, timber, piece-goods, oil and lime. [n21; Gaz XIII-I p. 358] The boats that carried it, the mhangiris, machvas, paddvas and batelas of fifty to seventy tons, were “built locally by Marathas and Native Christians”, and Agashi in 1837 “built very good fishing boats and coasting craft”. [n21; Gaz XIII-I pp. 358–359; Gaz XIV p. 2] The Christian villages of the north were not only gardens; they stood beside a working coastal economy in which their own people built the boats.

Disease kept returning. Cholera, absent from the district only in 1873 and 1874, was in Bassein in February 1878, from April to the end of the year in 1879, and from May to July in 1881. [n21; Gaz XIII-II Ch. XII] A dispensary in Bassein town, founded in 1872 in a house given by Yashvantrav Balvant Naik, treated about fifteen to eighteen thousand out-patients a year by 1880–81. [n21; Gaz XIV p. 36; Gaz XIII-II c. p. 667] Plague reached the Presidency in 1896, and influenza in 1918; the sources give no figures for the Christian villages. [n21]

Schools and priests

Education was the Christian villages’ other ladder, and the records show it being built slowly.

The first Government vernacular school in the whole Thana district was opened at Bassein in 1827. By 1881 Bassein town had six vernacular schools, the chief of them in a school-house of 1878 with room for 150 pupils. [n21; Gaz XIII-II pp. 656–657; Gaz XIV p. 36] A Marathi weekly, the Vasai Samachar, had been published at Bassein on Sundays for five years by 1882. [n21; Gaz XIII-II c. p. 662]

The parish schools were smaller and older in spirit. At Agashi in 1881 the parish school had seventeen pupils, taught Portuguese reading, arithmetic, singing, doctrine and music. At Nirmal there was a private school whose master played the violin in church. [n34; Gazetteer XIV pp. 2, 293] Salsette had eleven parish schools in 1881; Bassein a handful. [n34; Gazetteer XIII pp. 215–216] The well-to-do sent their sons on to St Mary’s or St Xavier’s in Bombay. [n34; Gazetteer XIII pp. 215–216] The Hindu Samvedis, the Gazetteer had noted, also “send their boys to school”. [n08; Gazetteer 1882, p. 83] In both halves of the old community, schooling was valued.

The priesthood was the highest calling the village could offer. “Wealthy families who are village headmen and owners of rich garden lands often bring up one or more of their sons for the Church.” [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 205] The parish priests of the 1880s were Goans or men from Salsette, educated at Goa. [n34; Gazetteer XIII pp. 207–208] Yet forty years later the Bombay East Indian Association was lamenting “the paucity of vocations to the priesthood among the community”. [n34; Mendiratta p. 236] The research for this book found no count of priests or nuns from the Kupari villages for any period before 1947. [n34; Gaps, section 8] The flowering of vocations from these villages, and the first Bishop of Vasai, Thomas Dabre, born at Bhuigaon in 1945, belong to the years after independence, and to Chapter 37.

The railway and the city

In 1864 the Bombay, Baroda and Central India Railway reached the coast, and Bassein Road station, at Manickpur, opened on its line. [n32; Wikipedia: Vasai Road railway station] By 1880 Bassein Road carried 140,837 passengers a year, and Virar station 83,176. [n32; BG 28, 36] [n37; Gaz. XIV pp. 382–383] The produce of the gardens went south by rail: “large quantities of plantains and betel-leaves” to Bombay from Virar. [n21; Gaz XIV p. 316] The railway also changed the land. The Gazetteer blamed “the long railway embankment” for damming the flow of water into the old Sopara creek and hastening its silting. [n37; Gaz. XIV pp. 314–315] The creek landings of Gass, Bolinj and Vagholi, where Samvedi produce had been loaded for Gujarat, gave way to stations.

How far the people of the northern villages moved to Bombay before 1947, the sources do not say. The Gazetteer recorded in 1882 that “a considerable number who know English are employed in Bombay chiefly as clerks. The morning trains from Andheri and Bandra are crowded”; but those stations are in Salsette, and the sentence is about the Salsette Christians. [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 205–206] The well-to-do sent their sons to St Mary’s or St Xavier’s in Bombay. [n34; Gazetteer XIII pp. 215–216] Community blogs of the 2000s say the Kuparis depended mostly on farming until the late 1960s and early 1970s. [n07; D’Mello blog] [n34; Vasai Kupari blog] That suggests the great move to the city came after independence, and belongs to Chapter 37. A recollection of the “Vasaiwale kawadis”, Vasai farmers carrying vegetables on shoulder poles, coming on the four o’clock train to Victoria Terminus and selling at Bora Bazaar and Dadar to the cry “baaju-baaju Vasaiwale aale”, is undated in the source and describes a later time. [n08; India Food Network, 2015]

For the years of the freedom struggle, the research for this book found no source on Vasai’s Christian villages: nothing on the Salt Satyagraha at Agashi, Quit India in Vasai, or the part any Kupari played. [n20; Freedom struggle section] That silence is a gap in the research, not evidence that nothing happened.

The name that is not there

We come, at the end of two centuries, to the strangest fact in the whole documentary history of the Kuparis.

The research for this book searched the full scanned text of fifteen primary and early sources for the words “Kupari”, “Kumpari” and “Cupari”. It searched the 1882 Gazetteer, volumes XIII and XIV; Enthoven’s Tribes and Castes, volumes I and II; Grierson’s Linguistic Survey, volume VII; the Census reports of 1901, 1911 and 1931; Gerson da Cunha’s books of 1876 and 1900; and the Jesuit letters of Documenta Indica, volumes II, IV, V and VII. There were no hits in any of them. [n34; Headline finding]

In those texts the Christians of Bassein appear as “Native Christians”, “Portuguese Christians”, “Firgi” and “Kinstav”. [n34] The first attestations of “Kupari” the research found were modern: a community blog of 2007 and Wikipedia. [n34] Later writers cite Elsie Baptista’s book of 1967 for the phrase Samavedi Christians “commonly called Kuparis”, which, if the quotation is exact, would be the earliest known appearance in print; but that book was not available to check. [n34; section 7] The community’s own scholars, writing in Marathi, use another name: Paul Rumav of Nandakhal titled his book Samvedi Khristi Samaj, “The Samvedi Christian Community”, and the 2026 newspaper article on the dialect speaks of “Samvedi Christians”. [n20; mr.wikipedia; Saamana]

This is a negative result from scanned text, and it has limits. The scanner may have misread the word somewhere; a spelling no one thought of may turn up. [n34; Headline finding] But fifteen texts across three centuries is a wide net, and they include the one book, the 1882 Gazetteer, that described the Samvedi Christians of Agashi and Sopara by village. If the officials who wrote that book heard the word, they did not write it down.

What does the absence mean?

It does not mean the Kuparis did not exist. The people are in every one of those texts: the “converted Samvedi Brahmans” of Sopara, the seventy-five Christian families of Agashi, Vatar and Koprad “originally Samvedis and Pachkalshis”, the 2,184 parishioners of the Holy Cross at Nirmal who rebuilt their church in 1856. [n34] What is missing is the name, not the community.

The likeliest explanation, on the evidence, is that “Kupari” was a spoken word, used in the villages and by their neighbours, that did not reach print until recently. [n34; Inferences, section 2] That would put it beside the other names the record does show. Firgi was what the upper-class Christians called themselves; Kiristan and Firangi were what Hindus called them; Kanarin was what the Portuguese called the native clergy, “a term of reproach”. [n34; Gazetteer XIII pp. 199–200, 203 n.1] Names for communities on this coast have always come in registers, some used by insiders, some by outsiders, some proudly, some as insults, and the written record has caught some registers and missed others.

If “Kupari” comes from compadre, the co-father of baptism, it is a name drawn from the very rite that made the community: the godparent who carried each child to the font while its mother stayed at home, whose Portuguese title the Christians of Thana were still using in 1905. [n34; Gazetteer XIII p. 210; LSI VII] If it comes from kūp, the well, it is a name drawn from a story of humiliation, which a commenter in 2007 said Hindus used as an insult. [n34; Talk:Kupari] Neither derivation has a source. Both are possible. The research for this book suggests that the first fits the documented vocabulary better, and that the second fits the documented folklore better. [n34; Inferences, section 2]

A historian cannot choose between them on this evidence, and should not pretend to. What can be said is this. For two hundred years after the Portuguese left, the people this part of the book is about were described by others as Christians, as Portuguese, as natives, as Firangis, as rural East Indians, as converted Samvedi Brahmins. They called themselves, in the hearing of the record, by their caste names, or as Christians, or as Samvedi Christians. Whatever they called each other in the lanes of Agashi and Nirmal, they kept it among themselves. The name by which they are now known, and by which this part of the book is titled, entered the written world only when they began to write their own history.

That history, the language they made, the dress and houses and food and songs and rites of their villages, is the subject of the next chapter.
